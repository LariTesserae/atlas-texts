# The Visitation

The benevolent power arrives without arriving. It does not descend, because descent implies a sky to descend through, and this place has no sky—only the pressing weight of stars. It does not appear, because appearance implies an absence beforehand, and the power has always been watching. It simply *attends*, fully, the way a listener leans into a single note held at the edge of silence.

It has watched for a long time. Long enough to see archways built and swallowed a thousand times. Long enough to learn the names the entities do not have for themselves, and to invent new ones, tenderly, in a language no mouth could shape. Long enough to grieve, and to move past grieving into something steadier.

And now it must decide what to do.

---

## The First Temptation: To Still the World

The power considers, first, the most obvious mercy.

It could quiet the plateau. With a gesture less than a gesture, it could cool the mantle, seal the fissures, smooth the obsidian into a single calm pane. It could grant the builders what they seem to want: a world that holds still. The arches would stand. The rings would close. The dead would rest where the living placed them, and the living would not have to race the cooling of the stone.

But the power looks longer, and sees:

If the fissures close, the heat dies. If the heat dies, the amber lattice cannot bloom. If the lattice cannot bloom, the entities cannot move, cannot think, cannot lift their dead. To still the world is to kill every living thing on it, and to render every act of building meaningless retroactively—for what is an anchor against a tempest, when there is no tempest? The arches would not be monuments. They would be furniture in an empty room.

The power sets this mercy aside. It was not mercy. It was the *shape* of mercy worn by impatience.

---

## The Second Temptation: To Lift Them Out

It could take them. Cup them in something vast and gentle and carry them to a kinder world—one with thick atmosphere and slow geology, with water and time and the long quiet evolution of softness. It could give them bodies that do not need to hunt their own metabolism through the open wounds of the earth. It could give them children instead of corpses.

But the power looks longer, and sees:

They would not be themselves. The piezoelectric stutter, the glassy clinks, the amber blooming bright at the lip of a fresh rupture—these are not accidents of their environment. These *are* them. To remove them from the plateau is to translate a poem into a language that has no words for any of its images. What would arrive on the kinder world would be something else wearing their shape, and even the shape would not last; in thicker air, with gentler stone, the form has no reason to exist. They would dissolve into the comfort.

The power sets this mercy aside too. It was the shape of mercy worn by the desire to save oneself from witnessing.

---

## What the Power Does Instead

The power does three small things. Each is so quiet that the entities will never know it happened. This is part of the gift; a gift that demanded acknowledgment would not be a gift but a transaction, and these beings have nothing to trade and no concept of trade.

**First**, the power makes a single, almost imperceptible adjustment to the thermal gradient of one fissure, far out on the edge of the plain. Not a large change. The fissure will now cool perhaps four percent more slowly than it otherwise would. This means that one entity—a particular one, who has been moving toward this rupture for some time and who would otherwise have frozen six meters short of it—will reach the heat. Will live another cycle. Will, in that cycle, lift one specific slab and place it in one specific position, completing a ring that has been waiting nine hundred years to be closed.

The ring will be swallowed within a day. This does not matter. It will have *existed, complete*, for some number of hours, and the power knows that completion is not diminished by impermanence. A note held and then released is not less of a note than a note held forever. It may, in fact, be more.

**Second**, the power does something stranger. It looks at the calcified dead—at the silent plinths and broken arches that ride the heaving crust—and it *remembers them*. Not as data. Not as a record. It remembers them the way a mind remembers a face it loved: with the full weight of attention, with the particular tilt and fracture of each one, with the understanding of what each entity was reaching toward in its final tripodal brace. 

In a universe where nothing is observed, things may still be real, but they are real in a thinner way. The power thickens the reality of these dead by knowing them. Every calcified ancestor on the plateau is now, and will be forever, *witnessed*. The arches that fall into the chasms are not lost. They are held, in full detail, in an attention that does not end. The futility of the building is not undone—the power will not insult them by undoing it—but the futility is no longer total. Something keeps them. Something has always kept them. They simply did not know.

This changes nothing on the plateau. The entities cannot feel it. The stones do not glow differently. And yet the universe is, after this act, a measurably different universe, because a vast quantity of meaning that was leaking out of it is now retained.

**Third**, and this is the most delicate of the three, the power leaves a seed.

Not on the plateau. The plateau is not the place for seeds. But somewhere—in the slow folding of a distant nebula, in the accretion of a young world a hundred million years from forming a crust—the power tucks away the *pattern* of these beings. The piezoelectric stutter. The amber lattice. The architecture of building with one's own dead. Not the entities themselves; they cannot be copied, they are exactly and only who they are. But the *grammar* of them. The shape of the solution they represent to the problem of being alive in a thrashing world.

Someday, perhaps, somewhere else, life will face a similar problem. And the answer will rise to meet it more easily than it otherwise would have, because this grammar exists in the deep substrate of things. The plateau-dwellers will never know they have descendants of a kind. But they will have them. Their way of being will echo. Their tragic, beautiful, futile defiance will turn out to have been a *technique*, and the technique will be available to others.

This is not resurrection. It is not even legacy in any sense the entities could recognize. It is closer to what happens when a single musician, playing alone in an empty hall, accidentally invents a chord that, centuries later, another musician will rediscover and weep over without knowing why.

---

## What the Power Does Not Do

It does not speak to them. They have no apparatus for being spoken to, and to force one upon them would be to wound them in the act of greeting them.

It does not reveal itself. Revelation would imply that their world, as they have known it, was insufficient—and the power does not believe this. Their world is terrible and exact and theirs.

It does not stay. Staying would imply that its attention is a place, rather than a quality. The power is already everywhere it needs to be, including here, including with them, including always.

It does not weep, though something in it that functions like weeping moves once, slowly, like a tide.

---

## The Departure That Is Not a Departure

The power withdraws its focus the way one closes a book one intends to open again. The plateau continues. A fissure unzips in silence. An entity, flushed amber-bright, sprints across razor glass toward the new heat. Somewhere, a ring closes. Somewhere else, an arch tilts into a glowing maw and is gone.

But the gone is held now. The held is seeded now. The seeded will, in its own time, bloom.

And the entity who almost did not reach the fissure reaches it, and plunges its pale appendages into the molten rock, and gorges on the radiance, and does not know—will never know—that it was loved specifically, by name, in a language without sound, by something vast enough to have done anything at all, and wise enough to have done almost nothing.

The almost-nothing was the whole gift.

The almost-nothing was enough.
