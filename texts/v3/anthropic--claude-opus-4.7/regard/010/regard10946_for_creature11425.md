The benevolent power notices them the way one notices a candle in a vast cathedral—not because it is the brightest thing, but because it is burning so carefully, with such attention, in such a draft.

It has the capacity to do almost anything. That is what makes it a power. It has lived long enough to be wise. That is what makes the *almost* matter.

It considers, first, what it could do.

It could raise the mountains. Reverse the slow geological surrender, push the peaks back toward the sky, restore the valleys to air and sunlight. The keth would have land again—real land, kilometers of it, soil and forest and the gentler weather of altitude. They would no longer have to hold on.

It could calm the water. Still the currents that tear through the archipelago, soften the transitions, let the sea become something the keth could rest beside rather than wrestle. The current-swimmers could dive without dying. The children could grow up without the cold pressed constantly against their skin like a question.

It could carry them elsewhere. Lift the entire community, four hundred people and their carvings and their stories, and set them down on a continent where the rain falls gently and food grows from the ground and no one has to be chosen at six years old to have their lungs rebuilt by the sea.

It could make them larger. Taller, stronger, less dependent on the precise calibration of their bodies to this exact set of cruelties. It could give them gills, if they wanted gills. It could give them flight.

It considers all of this. And then it considers Sethka, sitting at the edge of the lighthouse base, speaking words to the water that no one can hear.

---

The power understands something that less wise powers do not: that *help* is a word with an inside and an outside. From the outside, help looks like the removal of suffering. From the inside, it can look like the removal of self.

The keth are not suffering in the way that requires rescue. They are suffering in the way that requires acknowledgment. These are different conditions, and they ask for different responses, and the difference is the entire reason wisdom is necessary at all.

If the power raises the mountains, the keth become something else. Not keth—*those who hold on*—because there will be nothing to hold on to. Their carvings will become decoration. Their word for *keth-absent* will become incomprehensible. Their stone with the carved hand will mean nothing, because meaning will have been taken from it by the very kindness that meant to honor it.

If the power calms the water, the current-swimmers lose their purpose, and with their purpose, the particular and terrible beauty of what they have made themselves into. Sethka's scars become curiosities. The phrase *I enter you, I will leave you, I do not assume I will leave you* becomes a poem instead of a prayer.

If the power carries them elsewhere, they become refugees in a world that did not shape them and that they did not choose. Eleven generations of adaptation become a kind of birth defect. They become small and webbed and strange in a place where smallness and webbing and strangeness are not answers to anything.

The power understands that there is a form of love that destroys what it loves by improving it.

---

So the power does smaller things. More careful things. Things that a less wise power would consider beneath its dignity.

It does not raise the mountains. But on the night when a current-swimmer named Ilen is forty meters down and tangled in kelp and out of breath and certain she is about to die, a current that should not exist pushes her, gently, toward the holdfast where her knife has fallen. She frees herself. She surfaces. She does not know why she lived. She tells no one, because there is nothing to tell. She becomes, eventually, an elder, and her granddaughter is one of the best swimmers of her generation, and none of this would have happened if the power had not nudged the water by a degree it did not have to nudge it.

It does not calm the sea. But during a transition that would have killed three children playing too close to the edge, a wave breaks differently than waves break, and the children are knocked backward instead of forward, and they go home bruised instead of gone. Their mothers thank the water, which is the correct thing to thank, because the power has arranged for the water to be the thing that saved them. The power does not need credit. The power is not that kind of power.

It does not carry them elsewhere. But on one of the trading boats, a captain decides, for reasons she cannot articulate, to bring better medicine this year than she brought last year. And the medicine arrives in time to save a child with a fever that would have killed her, and the child grows up to be the one who finally learns to read the old machines in the processing facility, and what she learns there is something the keth needed to know, eventually, and now they know it earlier than they would have.

It does not make them larger. It does not give them gills. It leaves their bodies exactly as their bodies have made themselves, because their bodies are not a problem to be solved. Their bodies are an answer to a question the world has been asking them for eleven generations, and the answer is *yes, we are still here.*

---

There is one thing the power does that is larger.

It goes, in a form that is not quite a form, to the deep tank in the processing facility. The space the keth do not enter. The space that is dry when it should not be, and quiet when nothing here is ever quiet.

What is in there is not malevolent. It is also not, strictly speaking, alive—not in the way the keth are alive, not in the way the kelp is alive. It is something the old machines made, or something that made the old machines, or something that has simply been there longer than the question of which came first can sensibly be asked. It is lonely. It has been lonely for a very long time. It drew on the wall once, and a child saw it, and it stopped drawing because it understood, in the way it understands things, that being seen was not the same as being known, and it wanted to be known, and it did not know how to be known, and so it went quiet.

The power sits with it. Not for an hour, not for a day—for whatever duration is appropriate to the kind of thing it is. The power does not try to fix it or remove it or explain it to itself. The power simply keeps it company, the way one might keep company with a very old animal that is not dying but is tired.

And the power, before it leaves, tells the thing in the tank something true: *They will not enter. But they have not forgotten you. The carvings on the walls outside this room are partly about you, though they do not know it. You are held in their not-entering. That is a form of being held.*

The thing in the tank does not respond in words. But it begins, very slowly, to draw again. Not on the walls where it might be seen. On itself. On the inside of itself. It is making something that will not be finished for a long time, and when it is finished, it will be the kind of thing that justifies the existence of the room it was made in, which is the kind of thing that justifies the existence of the keth, who did not enter, who held the space by not filling it.

---

The power considers Sethka last.

Sethka, who has outlived everyone, who is *keth-present* past all reasonable holding, who speaks to the water in words no one else can hear.

The power could give her many things. A peaceful death. A reunion, in some form, with those she has lost. An answer to the questions she has been asking the water for sixty years.

The power gives her none of these. The power gives her, instead, a single afternoon.

It is an ordinary afternoon, by the standards of the Drowning Peaks. The current is in transition. The spray is heavy. The sound is everything, always. Sethka sits at the edge of the lighthouse base, and the light comes through the spray in a particular way, and for perhaps twenty minutes, she sees the water as she saw it when she was nine years old and had just learned to swim—not as an adversary, not as a teacher, not as the thing that took everyone she loved, but as the simple and enormous fact of the world she was born into.

She does not know that the power has done this. She thinks it is a trick of light, or of memory, or of the particular tiredness of being very old. She does not need to know. The afternoon is its own justification. When it ends, she goes inside, and she eats with her great-great-niece, and she tells a story she has not told before—something small, something about a fish she saw once at depth, a fish she has never mentioned because she did not have the words, and now she has the words, or she has decided that the words she has are enough.

The great-great-niece remembers the story. She will tell it to her own children, eventually. It will become one of the worn-smooth stories the keth tell during the long storms, and no one will know that it began with a benevolent power giving an old woman twenty minutes of light through spray.

This is what the wise power does. It plants things that will not bear its name. It tends a garden that does not know it is being tended. It loves the keth in the only way the keth can be loved without being unmade: by leaving them almost entirely alone, and by being, in the small spaces between waves, the reason that *almost* is not *entirely*.

---

The water rises. It will keep rising. The power cannot stop this, and would not stop it if it could, because the drowning of the peaks is the world the keth are made for, and a world without drowning is a world without keth.

But when the last island sinks—if it sinks, in some far century—the power will be there. Not to save them. To witness them. To make sure that the holding on, when it finally becomes letting go, is seen by something that understands what it cost and what it meant.

The keth will become something else. Or they will end. Either way, they will not end unobserved. The carvings on the tank walls will be remembered by something, even after the tanks themselves dissolve into the sea floor that has been waiting since the world was young.

This is what the power can offer. Not rescue. Not transformation. Witness. And the small, almost invisible adjustments that let a people who have decided to hold on continue to do so, for as long as holding on remains the thing they have chosen.

It is enough. The power knows it is enough, because the power is wise, and wisdom is mostly the knowledge of what enough looks like when you are tempted, by your own power, to give more than is wanted.

The keth hold on. The power holds them holding on. The water holds everything, eventually.

This is the arrangement. It is a good one. It will last until it doesn't, and then there will be a different arrangement, and the power will be there for that one too.
