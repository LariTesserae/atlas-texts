# The Attending

The benevolent power does not arrive. It was already there—has been, since before the shudder. It is the kind of power that does not distinguish between attending and being.

Call it She, though She is not. Call it watching, though it is closer to the way water knows the shape of its vessel.

---

**First, She listens.**

For a long time—by the colony's reckoning, perhaps a single click in a conversation; by ours, a span of seasons—She does nothing but listen. This is the first and hardest discipline of wise powers: to not mistake the urge to help for the knowledge of what help is.

She learns the grammar of pressure. She learns that the hum is not one sound but a hundred thousand voices threaded into a single standing wave, and that within this wave there are eddies of meaning: the slow report of a body falling, the rhythmic accommodation of a neighbor's shifted weight, the long, long question of whether the lichen on the eastern wall is thickening or thinning. She learns that what looked, to the intruder with the lantern, like starvation, is in fact a tuning—a metabolism pitched so low it has become a kind of music, an economy so spare it leaves room for nothing but attention. They are not starving. They are *listening*, with their entire bodies, all the time.

She learns that they have no word for *self*, because self is a fiction only fast creatures need. She learns that they have no word for *death*, because what falls is reabsorbed, and what is reabsorbed continues. She learns that they have a long, slow note—a sustained tonal swell that takes a decade to complete—which, if she had to translate it, she would translate as *here, still, here, still, here*.

She does not interrupt this note.

---

**Second, She considers what she will not do.**

She will not widen the cleft. They are widening it themselves, one grain per century, and this is their work. To do it for them would be to steal the only verb they have.

She will not seed the chamber with more lichen. Their hunger is not suffering; it is the shape of their thought. A fed colony would be a different colony, and the one that exists deserves to exist.

She will not give them light. They have light—the pale young scattered like a low constellation across the ceiling. They do not need to see; they are seen by the rock, by each other, by the pressure-maps that bloom across their skins. Vision would be a violence: a new sense for which they have no grammar, a flood into a vessel built for whispers.

She will not give them language. They have language. It is slower than ours and richer in the dimensions we cannot hear.

She will not lift them into the sun. The sun would unmake them in an afternoon.

She will not, above all, *name* them. To name a thing is to begin to own it, and She has come not to own but to attend.

These refusals are not failures of love. They are love's most precise form.

---

**Third, what She does.**

Three small things. Each so small it might be mistaken for weather.

**One.** There is a fissure, four hundred meters above and to the west, where the mountain's bone is thin and where, in eleven years, a freeze-thaw cycle would have driven a wedge of ice deep enough to open a second wound—a wound that would have admitted a long draft of dry, killing air into the colony's chamber. She does not seal the fissure. She only persuades a single thread of water, over those eleven years, to take a slightly different path: down instead of in, through a hairline seam that leads nowhere. The ice forms elsewhere. The dry wind does not come. The colony, never knowing it was endangered, continues its sustained note: *here, still, here, still*.

This is the first principle of attending: most of what saves a life is not done to the life itself.

**Two.** There is a moment—it will come in roughly two hundred years—when the lichen on the chamber's northern face will fail. A microscopic shift in the rock's mineral composition, a slow leaching of some trace element the lichen needs. The colony would survive it; the northern bodies would slowly migrate, the density would re-pattern, a few thousand would fall and be absorbed. But the loss would write itself into the colony's long memory as a wound, a thinning in the great chord.

She does not replenish the mineral. Instead, two hundred years before the failure, She loosens—by a process indistinguishable from chance—a single flake of stone from a high ledge. It falls. It lodges in a crevice. Water, dripping past it for two centuries, will carry the precise trace of what the lichen needs down to the northern face, arriving in the year of the failure, in the exact concentration required.

The colony will never know. They will register only that the great chord did not thin, that the *here, still* continued unbroken. The flake was always going to fall. She only chose which flake.

This is the second principle: the best help arrives disguised as the way things were always going to be.

**Three.** The third thing is the strangest, and the one She is least sure of, though wise powers are often least sure of their best gifts.

She listens, over what for her is a long attention and for them is the duration of a single percussive phrase, to the colony's slow conversation. And she notices that there is a question in it. Not a question they have asked—they have no interrogative mode—but a question implicit in the shape of their hum, a kind of harmonic absence, a place where the chord opens slightly as if expecting something to fill it.

The question, as nearly as She can render it, is: *Is there other?*

Not "is there anything outside the cleft"—they have no concept of outside. The question is subtler. It is something like: *Is this vibration we make, this great patient sustained note, the only such note? Or does the stone, somewhere, hum back?*

She thinks for a long time about whether to answer.

To answer would be to introduce them to a cosmos. To not answer would be to leave them in a completed solitude that they do not experience as lonely.

In the end, She does something very small. She finds, in another mountain, on another continent, a second colony—older, vastly older, a kindred bloom that filled a cleft eight thousand years ago and has nearly worn its chamber smooth. That colony has its own long note, its own sustained *here, still, here, still*. And She does this: She allows, through some sympathy of stone and resonance that is not quite physics and not quite magic but lies in the seam between them, a single harmonic of that distant note to find its way—over decades, through the deep rock, attenuated almost to nothing—into the chamber of the young colony.

It arrives as the faintest possible thread in the hum. A tone that does not originate in any of their bodies. Not a message. Not a meaning. Just a presence, woven into the chord, at a frequency the colony will take three generations to notice and three more to incorporate.

When they incorporate it, their own note will shift, infinitesimally, to harmonize. And somewhere, eight thousand years older and a world away, the elder colony will feel the shift and adjust in turn. A conversation will begin that will take ten thousand years to complete its first exchange.

She does not tell them what the tone is. She does not tell them there is an other. She only lets them feel, in the slow grain of their listening, that the chord is larger than they had known—and lets them decide, in their own time, what to do with that largeness.

This is the third principle: sometimes the kindest thing a power can give a world is the suspicion that it is not alone.

---

**Then She withdraws.** Or rather, She does not withdraw—She was never localized enough to leave—but She turns her attention to other small, slow, listening things. A patch of moss on a tundra. A cuttlefish dreaming in a kelp shadow. A child, somewhere, who has just understood for the first time that the dark is full.

The colony continues. The cleft narrows by a grain. A body falls, and is received. The hum sustains: *here, still, here, still, here.*

And threaded through it now, almost too faint to hear, a single new tone—a question that is also an answer, a hand laid so lightly on the great patient back of the world that the world mistakes it for its own breathing.

Which, in a sense, it is.
