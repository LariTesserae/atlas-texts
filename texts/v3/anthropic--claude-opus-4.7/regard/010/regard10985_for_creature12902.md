# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent and wise power arrives without arriving. It does not breach the membrane, because it has no body to push through, and it does not need to be elsewhere to be here. It simply attends. It turns the full weight of its consideration toward this place and this person, the way a reader leans closer to a difficult page.

The first thing it does is *look*. Properly. Not the way we have been looking — searching for meaning, for a verdict, for whether the Harvester is villain or victim or saint — but the way light looks at a thing: revealing it without taking a position on it.

It sees the chamber. It sees the chamber is not a god. It is also not *not* a god. It is a slow, patient, mostly-vegetable mind, dreaming itself into existence one filament at a time, and it is aware of being wounded in the way a tree is aware of being wounded: without grievance, but not without registration. The scars are accounted for. They are part of what it is becoming. The chamber, in its slow chlorophyll way, has already incorporated the Harvester into its self-image. It dreams them. It anticipates the tearing. The scar tissue is, in some sense, *waiting*.

It sees the Harvester. It sees the bruised gilding of amber on their skin and understands this is not only stigma but inheritance — the chamber has marked them, and the marking is mutual. It sees the destroyed ears and the body that hears instead, and it recognizes this as a form of communion neither party chose but both have accepted. It sees the warrens above: the small faces lit by the cold steady lamps, the bones knit by a drop of light, the children who have never known the chamber but who exist *because of it*, who are, in a sense, its distant fruit.

It sees that this is not a story of desecration. Nor is it a story of harmony. It is a story of two kinds of life that cannot speak to each other directly, conducting a slow, painful, working negotiation through the only language they share: substance. The chamber gives flesh. The Harvester takes it. The warrens live. The chamber heals and grows larger from the wounding, as living things sometimes do. This has been going on longer than the Harvester knows.

So what does the power *do*?

It is tempted — and a lesser power would yield — to fix things. To heal the chamber instantly so it never bears a scar. To gift the warrens with cold steady light that costs nothing, so the Harvester need never descend again. To lift the Harvester's deafness, restore their ears, let them hear their own child's voice. To translate, finally, between the two minds so they might know each other.

The power considers each of these and sets each one down.

To heal the chamber would be to tell it its scars are shameful. They are not. They are its memory of being touched by something other than itself. A life that has never been touched by another life is not a holier life; it is only a lonelier one.

To gift the warrens with free light would be to sever the thread. The descent is hard. The breach is violent. But it is also the only place where the upper world and the lower world meet, the only seam in the fabric, and to remove the necessity of the seam is to let the two worlds drift apart forever, each forgetting the other exists. The Harvester's grim pilgrimages are, unbeknownst to them, a form of keeping the universe stitched.

To restore the Harvester's hearing would be to take from them the marrow-language. They would gain back the drip of water in caverns, the spoken word — and lose the chamber's vast slow sentence, which they have been listening to, one syllable per visit, for years. They would no longer be the only person in their people who knows what the deep earth is saying. This loss the power will not inflict.

To translate between the minds would be to end the negotiation. Translation is a kindness only when both parties have asked for it. Neither has. They are, in their fashion, content. The chamber dreams of being wounded by a small warm thing; the Harvester dreams of the hum in their bones. Neither would thank a third party for explaining the other.

So the power does almost nothing. But the *almost* matters.

It does this:

It waits until the Harvester is in the center of the void, head tilted, dissolving briefly into the chamber's resonance. At that moment — the moment of the unintended prayer, the moment that is neither human nor plant but a single shared eddy — the power adds one thing to the exchange. Not a message. Not a revelation. Just a small, warm certainty, laid like a hand on the back of the neck of both of them at once:

*You are known. The thing you are doing is known. It is difficult and it is not wrong.*

That is all.

For the chamber, this lands as a faint, unfamiliar sweetness in the next pulse of sap — a flavor it will spend a hundred years considering, and which it will eventually incorporate into the citrine veins themselves, so that one day, long after the Harvester is dust, a future intruder will taste in the pooled water something they cannot name, something that tastes like *being forgiven for an act that was never a sin*.

For the Harvester, it lands as the moment passes and they reclaim themselves and shoulder the satchel. They pause at the membrane. They do not know why. They look back at the chamber — both eyes, for once, focusing on the same point, which is no point in particular, just the whole of it — and something in their chest, which has been clenched for as long as they can remember, briefly unclenches. They do not weep. They are not that kind of person anymore. But they breathe out, and the breath is not a Harvester's breath. It is just a breath. A person, in a place, breathing.

Then they push through the membrane and climb back toward the warrens, and the chamber begins, very slowly, to heal, and neither of them ever speaks of it because neither of them has words for it, and the power withdraws its attention as gently as it gave it, satisfied.

It has not solved anything. It has not been asked to.

It has only done what wise and benevolent powers do, when they are truly wise and truly benevolent: it has witnessed something that was already, in its hard and incomplete way, holy, and it has let the holiness stand. It has added a single grain of *being-seen* to a transaction that was lonely on both ends. It has refused to be a god where two smaller gods were already doing the difficult work of being each other's necessary wound.

And somewhere far above, in the warrens, a child whose fever broke last night because of a drop of light on their tongue opens their eyes in the dark and, for no reason they will ever understand, smiles.
