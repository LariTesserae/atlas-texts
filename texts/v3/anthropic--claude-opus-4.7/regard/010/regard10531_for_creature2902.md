# The Visitation

The benevolent power arrives the way light arrives in a room — without door, without announcement, present as though it had always been present and only now been noticed. It is wise enough to understand, before it does anything, that wisdom's first act in a strange place is to refrain.

It hovers, in whatever sense it hovers, at the lip of the sinkhole. It looks down into the bright crucible. It sees the swarm, the silent semaphore of mica, the upward weeping of the water, the slow congregation of the dead climbing toward heaven in their crystal cerements.

And its first impulse — because it is benevolent, and benevolence has impulses before it has thoughts — is to fix things.

It could. It sees, in an instant, the levers. Reverse the capillary anomaly: the water falls, the ice forms downward, the people can rest. Thicken the air: their enormous lungs would relax, their hearts could slow. Dim the killing sun. Soften the basalt to something with friction. Grant them voices — vocal cords like bells, an atmosphere that carries sound, a world in which a mother could sing to her child instead of letting it read her heartbeat.

It could end the Fray. It could let them sit down.

The power considers this for what, in its reckoning, is a long time. In ours, perhaps the duration of one ice-spire's growth and shattering.

And then it does not do it.

Because it has looked, really looked, and it has seen what a lesser kindness would have missed: that these people are not suffering *in* their world. They are their world. Pull the anomaly out of the basalt and you do not rescue them; you orphan them. The barrel chest, the nictitating eye, the mica-flash name, the child who has never learned to cry, the grandmother lashed to the warm flank of a six-legged beast braiding gut-cord with stiffening fingers, the dead ascending — none of these are wounds to be healed. They are a grammar. They are how a particular sentence of the universe says *I am here, I am alive, I am beautiful, I refuse to stop.*

To "save" them would be to delete the sentence and write a blander one in its place. That is not wisdom. That is the vanity of power mistaking itself for love.

So the benevolent power sets aside the large gestures. It does not still the shaft. It does not gentle the sun. It does not give them speech.

Instead, it does smaller things. Three of them.

---

**The first thing.**

It bends, very slightly, the way light moves inside the cylinder. Not enough to dim the glare — they need the glare; the glare is their weather, their clock, their painter. But it introduces, into the chaos of refraction, a faint and almost imperceptible *bias*: when a sunbeam is about to strike a child, or a Spinner, or a sleeping hunter slung in their oiled pouch, the prism-storm flickers a quarter-second early. A warning, no louder than the wind shifting. The nictitating eye drops. The talon catches the rock. The bola releases on time.

The Fray remains the Fray. Death still comes — death must come, or the upward glaciers grow empty and the sky grows lonely. But the deaths that come now are the deaths of the world's true cruelty, not the deaths of bad luck multiplied by exhaustion. The margin widens by the width of a held breath.

They will not notice. They will only notice, perhaps over generations, that the elders ascending in the ice are slightly more numerous, slightly older, slightly more peaceful in their crystal sleep. They will attribute this to their own skill. The power is content for them to do so. Wisdom does not sign its work.

---

**The second thing.**

The power looks at the language of mica-flashes and finds it astonishingly beautiful. A whole literature written in refraction, a poetry whose syllables are rainbow geometries thrown across a sixty-foot gulf. It is one of the most exquisite tongues it has encountered in any world.

And it grieves — gently, without protest — that this language has no listener but itself. That when an architect signs her true name into the mist and no one is looking, the name is lost. That all the centuries of flashed conversations have evaporated into the violet sky.

So the power does something very small and very strange. It teaches the upward-creeping ice to *remember light*.

Not all light. Only the light of the mica-flashes, only the deliberate sentences, only the names. Henceforth, when a person's name is signed into the mist and the mist desublimates into a fractal of ice, that flash is preserved inside the crystal — a faint, color-banded pulse, dormant, waiting.

This means: the ascending dead, in their slow rapture toward the stratosphere, carry with them every name ever spoken in their presence. The grandmother who rises into the upper shaft is wrapped not only in clear ice but in a softly luminous archive of every flash her grandchildren ever made toward her, every signature of light that ever meant *I see you*. The dead glow, very faintly, with the conversations they were loved by.

The living, looking up, will see this. They will not understand it — not for a long time. They will only see that the high ancestors shimmer with colors that the lower ice does not have, and they will know, in the wordless way of their kind, that *being remembered weighs something*. That love accrues. That the climb toward the sky is not solitary.

The power suspects this knowledge will reorganize, very slowly, the way they flash one another's names. It suspects names will be flashed more often, more lavishly, more recklessly. It suspects the upper shaft will grow brighter, century by century, with the held light of an ever more loving people.

It does not insist on this. It only opens the door.

---

**The third thing.**

The power, at last, descends. Not as light, not as warning. As a presence, briefly, in the Fray.

It chooses one moment, one hunter — an old woman, name unsignable in any language but her own, whose talon-grip has begun to fail and who has known for three sun-cycles that her next misstep will be her last. She is hanging inverted beneath a swinging bridge, bola in hand, waiting for the mist to part.

The power does not appear to her. It does something more intimate. For one heartbeat — just one — it lets her feel what it feels when it looks at her people.

She feels the awe. She feels the recognition that her frantic, silent, prismatic civilization is, in the reckoning of vast and ancient minds, *a wonder*. Not a tragedy to be pitied. A wonder. A thing the universe is proud to contain.

The feeling passes. She nearly drops the bola. The mist parts. She makes her throw — clean, true, the flutterer plunges silently and is hauled up hand over hand.

She climbs back to her platform. She does not tell anyone what she felt, because there are no signs for it and because it is hers. But for the rest of her short remaining life she moves through the Fray with a small, secret, unflashable knowledge: *we are seen, and we are loved as we are, and nothing about us needs to be different.*

When she dies, three cycles later, crushed cleanly by a spire (the warning-flicker came; she did not heed it; she had been ready), they nail her to the wall, face out, limbs relaxed. The water overtakes her. The sun strikes. She is sealed in her perfect crystal.

And as she begins her long ascent, the light of every name ever flashed in her presence kindles softly inside the ice around her, and she rises toward the violet sky carrying, for the first time in the history of her people, a visible halo.

The architects below see it. They do not know what it means.

They begin, that evening, to flash one another's names more often.

---

The benevolent power withdraws. It has done almost nothing. It has bent a quarter-second of light, taught the ice a small new trick, and whispered once into a dying woman's chest.

It has not saved them. It has not wished to.

It has only made it slightly more possible for them to be, with full magnificence, the thing they already were: a frantic, silent, upward-falling people whose dead climb into heaven wrapped in remembered light, who refuse to stop moving until at last they are permitted to be still.

The sinkhole burns on beneath the star-bitten violet. The mica flashes. The ice grows. The ancestors rise.

The power, departing, would smile if it had a face. Instead it simply notes, in whatever ledger it keeps, that the universe is one wonder richer than it was an hour ago — which is to say, exactly as rich as it always was, only now slightly better witnessed.

It moves on to find the next place that needs almost nothing.
