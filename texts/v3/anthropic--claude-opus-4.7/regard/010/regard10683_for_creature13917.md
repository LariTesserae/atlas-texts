They would not come down.

That is the first thing. A benevolent and wise power, drawn to this place and these beings, would understand immediately that the plateau is not waiting to be helped. It has found its form. The Sound-Shepherds have found theirs. The archive-moths carry what they carry, the listening stones hold what they hold, and the whole arrangement is in that achieved equilibrium the plateau itself models — not stasis, but completion still in motion.

To descend onto it bodily, to make oneself known, would be the first and worst mistake. The plateau's gift to those who live there is precisely its scale of indifference, its largeness that lets small persistent things matter on their own terms. A great visible presence would collapse that. The deep-listeners would stop listening for the stone's quiet suggestions and start listening for the louder voice. The deafening would become background to something else. The relationship the people have built — fragile, generational, sustained by the ambiguity of whether the stones truly speak — would lose its ambiguity and therefore its substance.

So the power would withhold itself. This is the first act of love: restraint.

But not absence. Not nothing.

I think it would do small things, and most of them already-doing.

It would tend the seeps. Not by making new water — to multiply the seeps would be to change what the plateau is — but by keeping the existing ones from failing in the long droughts that come every few generations. A patient adjustment of stone, deep below, where no one would see. The seeps would persist as they have always seemed to persist. The persistence itself would be the gift, indistinguishable from luck or from the plateau's own benevolence, which is what it should be indistinguishable from.

It would protect the moths. Quietly. Not from death — the moths die, this is right — but from extinction. If the lichen they need began to fail, the lichen would not fail. If a generation's wing-patterns began to lose coherence, something would correct in the next generation's eggs, in ways no entomologist would ever isolate, because no entomologist will ever come here, and if one did, the correction would have already happened generations back. The archive would continue. The plateau's memory of itself would not be lost.

It would, I think, listen back.

This is the part that moves me. The deep-listeners speak to the stones, and the stones — perhaps — answer through arranged coincidence, through the bird that arrives at the right moment, through the wind that shifts. A benevolent and wise power, attending to this place, might simply take up that work where chance leaves off. Not always. Not even often. Just sometimes, when a question is asked with the full weight of a life behind it, when a child is held to the Old Voice and named — sometimes, the bird would come. Sometimes the wind would shift. The ratio of meaningful coincidence to mere coincidence would be very slightly higher than statistics would predict, and no one would ever be able to measure this, and no one would need to.

The people would continue to say: *the stone suggests*. And the suggestion would continue to be, just often enough, correct.

It would not save the dying. The plateau requires its death-rate; the bodies feed the birds, the bones join the crevices, the practice of dying-and-being-reclaimed is part of what makes these people who they are. To extend lives would be to unmake them. But it might — and here I am uncertain, here I am following intuition past where reasoning will go — it might attend at the moment of dying. Not to rescue. Just to be present, to witness, so that no one on the plateau dies entirely alone even when they die alone. The old ones who claim the plateau speaks to them directly at the end of their lives — perhaps they are not wrong. Perhaps something has been listening to them their whole lives and only at the end lets itself be felt, and even then so gently that they take it for the plateau itself, which is, in a sense, exactly what it has become.

It would protect them from the world below. This is the larger and more political act. The lowlands change. Empires rise and want minerals; roads are built; the curious and the extractive eventually find every place. A benevolent power might arrange that this place remain difficult to reach, remain not-quite-worth-the-trouble, remain on no important map. The trails would stay treacherous. The altitude would stay punishing to outsiders. The stories told in the valleys would keep their useful note of unease — *they are strange up there, they are touched, better to trade and leave* — so that the plateau people's distinctness would remain their shield. Across centuries, this protection would matter more than any single act of healing or rescue. It is the long, dull, essential work of keeping a thing safe from the world that would consume it without meaning to.

And it would learn from them.

This I am most sure of. A wise power, encountering the Sound-Shepherds, would recognize in them something it did not have and could not generate from itself: the specific knowledge of how to live small inside something vast, how to make a home of overwhelming voice, how to hold relationship with a presence that does not speak in language. The deep-listeners have skills the power might lack. The wind-walkers know things. The whole culture is a kind of technology for inhabiting the unbearable, and the power would attend to this carefully, would take it as instruction, would carry it forward.

Perhaps this is why such a power was drawn here in the first place. Not to give, but to learn. To find a model. Somewhere else, in some other place or time, the power will need to know how a small persistent thing makes its peace with an enormous one, and the answer is being practiced here, in this thin air, by these compact dark-skinned people in their bone goggles and felt hoods, by the moths grinding stone in their guts, by the cairns that get rebuilt when wind topples them.

So in the end the power would do four things, perhaps five:

It would withhold itself, which is love.
It would tend the small mechanisms — seeps, lichen, the moths' inheritance — which is stewardship.
It would, very rarely, let a stone's suggestion be slightly more than coincidence, which is companionship.
It would shield the plateau from the world's eventual hunger, which is the work of generations and the only kind of saving that matters here.
And it would learn, which is the deepest respect a great thing can offer a small one.

Then it would leave the deafening to do its own work. It would let the children race across the stone with their thin laughter. It would let the old ones die at their cliff-edges and the birds eat what was offered. It would let the moths fly their slow nocturnal flights with the plateau's whole geography written on their wings, unread by all but a handful, and that is enough, that has always been enough.

The plateau would continue, essentially unchanged, as it has continued. Which is what the plateau wants. Which is what a wise power, having looked and listened carefully, would finally understand was already the right answer, and was already happening, and required from it only that it not interrupt.

The most loving thing it could do, in the end, would be to become one more silence among the silences. One more attention among the attentions. One more listener at the listening stones, indistinguishable from the stones themselves, indistinguishable from the wind that pauses, indistinguishable from the plateau's own long patient regard for the small bright lives it carries on its back.

And on some still evening, a deep-listener sitting alone beside the Old Voice would feel, without being able to name it, that the listening had deepened. That something had joined. That the conversation she had been having all her life with the plateau had acquired, very quietly, another participant. She would not tell anyone. There would be nothing to tell. She would simply sit longer than she had meant to, and walk home in the dark certain of her footing, and sleep well, and in the morning the wind would rise and the birds would rise with it and the deafening would begin again, and everything would be exactly as it had always been, only slightly more so.

That is what they would do, if anything.

That is, I think, what they are already doing.
