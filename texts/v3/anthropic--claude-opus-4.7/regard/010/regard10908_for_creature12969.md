# The Visitation

The benevolent and wise power considered Iskar for a long time before doing anything at all.

This was the first courtesy. To rush in with gifts—warmth, soil, abundance, easier air—would be to misunderstand everything. It would be the act of a different kind of power, one that confused benevolence with the alleviation of difficulty. The wise power knew better. It had learned, across many such visitations, that the deepest cruelty often wears the face of help, and that to "save" a people from their world is frequently to murder them while leaving their bodies intact.

So it watched. For what, in Iskari reckoning, would have been an appropriate length of time: not a day, not a year, but generations. It watched the *chink-chink-chink* of the carvers. It watched a girl learn the vertical sense by being held, as an infant, just past the edge of a ledge by her grandmother, who whispered the names of the winds into her ear until the child stopped flinching and began to listen. It watched a Ghor calf birthed in a stable-cavern, the mother's breath fogging in the cold, the herders silent and attentive as priests. It watched an old man die on his ledge, watching the mist below, and it watched the Ledge-Family come and unmake his body with the same reverence they would unmake a Ghor: nothing wasted, every part returned.

It saw what was already there.

And what was already there was *enough*. Not in the sense of comfort—the power was not so foolish as to romanticize chapped faces and the slow grinding of stone-dust into lungs—but in the deeper sense the Iskari themselves would have recognized: it was *dûr*. It endured. It had integrity. It was a true thing, and true things are rare in any universe.

This realization shaped what the power chose to do.

---

It did not descend in light. It did not speak from the sky. The Iskari had no sky-gods and would have found such a manifestation not awesome but vulgar, an embarrassment, like a guest who shouts in a quiet house.

Instead, the power did four small things, over the course of a long time, and each of them looked, to anyone watching, like nothing at all.

**The first thing** was this: in a particular vein of slate, deep in the western wall of the canyon, the power adjusted—very slightly—the crystalline structure of the stone. Not to make it softer. The Iskari did not want softer stone; softer stone does not endure. The power made it *more honest*. The new slate, when struck by an attentive carver, rang with a clearer tone. Its flaws announced themselves earlier. A mason sitting with a slab for a day could now hear, more precisely, what the stone wished to become. The number of cracked stones and wasted efforts in Iskar decreased by a measure so small that no single generation noticed it. But across centuries, the city grew a little more securely, and fewer Iskari fell.

No one knew this had been done. This was the point. A gift that announces itself is a gift that demands gratitude, and gratitude is a debt, and the power had no interest in placing the Iskari in its debt. The gift had to disappear into the stone, become indistinguishable from the stone, become simply *how the stone was*. The wise power understood that the best gifts are the ones that look, in retrospect, like they had always been there.

**The second thing** concerned the Ghor. There was a sickness that came among them, rarely, perhaps once in four generations—a wasting of the lungs, brought on by some confluence of cold and damp and the close press of the herd in the stable-caverns. When it struck, it killed perhaps a third of the herd, and the Ledge-Families that depended on those Ghor would diminish, sometimes fail, their dwellings going dark and being slowly reabsorbed into the canyon wall.

The power did not eliminate the sickness. To do so would be to remove a thing the Iskari had built themselves around: their songs of mourning the herd, their ancient practices of quarantine, their knowledge of which lichens, gathered at which altitudes, would ease a Ghor's breathing. To take away the sickness would be to take away these things, which were beautiful, and which were theirs.

Instead, the power slightly altered the timing. The sickness, when it came, would now come in the warmer parts of the year, when the stable-caverns could be ventilated, when the lichen the herders needed was easier to gather, when the weakest Iskari—the very old, the very young—were not also struggling against the deepest cold. The mortality of the herd dropped by perhaps a tenth. The mortality of the Iskari themselves, in those bad years, dropped further. The practices and the songs remained.

**The third thing** was the strangest, and the power hesitated longest before doing it.

There was, in the seventh generation of its watching, a girl named Vesh, born to a Ledge-Family on the eastern wall. She was, by Iskari standards, peculiar. She fidgeted as a child. She asked questions her elders did not have patience for. She wanted to know why the mist moved as it did, why some stones rang and some did not, why the Ghor's six legs were arranged in the pattern they were and not some other pattern. She was treated kindly—the Iskari were nothing if not patient—but she was treated as one treats a stone whose grain runs in an unhelpful direction. She could not be made into a load-bearing piece of the family. She would have to find her own use, or fail.

The power saw in Vesh the seed of something. Not a savior—the Iskari did not need a savior, and the power would not insult them by providing one—but a *translator*. Someone who could, in a distant generation, when the Iskari encountered other peoples (as all peoples eventually do), explain the Iskari to those others without diminishing them. Someone who could see her own people from a slight angle, the way an Iskari mason sees the grain of a stone, and who could therefore describe them truly.

The power did not change Vesh. She was already what she was. The power simply ensured that she lived. A particular ledge she would otherwise have slipped from, in her fourteenth year, the power firmed with a single grain of frost-fused stone. That was all. Vesh lived, grew old, and in her old age she carved—in defiance of every Iskari convention—a series of small bone plaques on which she etched, in a script she half-invented, the inner shape of her people. She hid them in a cavity behind her dwelling's hearthstone. The power knew that in time, perhaps many generations hence, perhaps in some unimaginable future when Iskar at last met its neighbors, these plaques would be found, and they would do their work.

This was the only intervention the power made into an individual life. It felt the weight of it. To touch a single person, even with a single grain of stone, is to bend the world's grain in ways that propagate forever. The power did it anyway, because the alternative was to allow the Iskari to be, eventually, misunderstood by history, and misunderstanding is its own kind of death.

**The fourth thing** was not a thing the power did, but a thing the power *refrained* from doing.

There was, far above the city, on the highest plateau where the Ghor grazed, a thin seam in the rock through which, over the next four hundred years, a vein of geothermal heat would rise. It would warm a small patch of ground. On that patch, given the slow patience of seeds carried by wind from a distant continent, a true plant—not lichen, not moss, but a flowering thing—might one day grow. A single flower, blue and small, in a world that had never known such a thing.

The power considered, very carefully, whether to encourage this. Whether to warm the seam a little more, hasten the seed's arrival, give the Iskari a flower.

It decided not to.

Not because a flower would have harmed them. A single blue flower, four hundred years hence, would not have ruined the Iskari. But it would have been a gift from the power, and the power had resolved that the Iskari would receive nothing they could trace back to a source outside themselves. They were a people who built their world from what their world contained—stone and bone and patience and the slow weeping of ice. To give them a flower from elsewhere would be to introduce a foreign grammar into their language. They might love the flower. They might build a small ritual around it. And in doing so, they would have, for the first time, something that was not theirs.

So the power let the seam cool. The flower would not come. The Iskari would continue to know vegetation only as wire-stemmed moss and ashen lichen, and they would find this enough, because it was theirs, and because they were a people for whom *theirs* and *enough* were the same word.

---

When the power had done these four things, and considered them, and found them sufficient, it prepared to leave.

But before it went, it did one last thing, which was not an intervention but a gesture, and which no Iskari ever knew about, and which the power did purely for itself, because even wise and benevolent powers have hearts of a kind, and hearts need their small expressions.

It went, in its formless way, to a particular ledge on the eastern wall, where an elder sat wrapped in a Ghor-hide blanket, watching the mist curl in the Great Below. The elder had been sitting there for most of the day. She was very old. Her hands, folded in her lap, were the geological hands of her people, wide and calloused, the lines of her palms dark with the grit of a long life.

The power settled, without weight, beside her. Not visible. Not audible. Simply present, in the way another Iskari might have been present—quietly, without needing acknowledgment, sharing the ledge.

For a long time, the power watched the mist with her.

Then, because it could not help itself, it allowed one very small thing: a single drop of condensation, on the stone just above her head, formed a fraction earlier than it would have otherwise, and fell, and landed on the back of her hand. Cold. Pure. The city's lifeblood.

The elder looked down at her hand. She watched the drop bead and slowly track across her knuckles. She did not smile—the Iskari did not smile easily—but something in the deep stillness of her eyes shifted, just slightly, the way a stone shifts when the pressure on it finds a new and more comfortable distribution.

"*Dûr*," she said quietly, to no one. *It endures.*

The power, which had been called many things in many worlds, took this as the only thanks it would ever receive, or want, from the People of the Stone and the Ghor.

Then it went away, and left them to their patient, vertical, enduring world, which was theirs, and which was enough.
