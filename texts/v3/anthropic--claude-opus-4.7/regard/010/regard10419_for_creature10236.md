The benevolent and wise power considers Shardvale for a long time before doing anything at all.

This is the first thing they do: they look. Not the glancing look of a passerby, nor the appraising look of one who measures worth, but the long, patient look of someone trying to understand what is *already here* before deciding what, if anything, ought to change.

And what they see surprises them — though perhaps it shouldn't. They had expected suffering. The word "weeping" is in the marsh's name; the people are small and pale and short-lived; the land breathes and sinks and devours. From a certain altitude — the altitude at which powers usually operate — this would look like a problem to be solved. Drain the marsh. Stabilize the ground. Lengthen the lives. Teach them speech. Bring them light that does not summon insects.

But the power has lived long enough to know that "wisdom" which arrives with solutions in its hands has often not yet arrived at all.

So they look longer.

They see the Thornveil grafting living willow into their walls, and understand: *these people are not fighting the marsh. They are in conversation with it.* They see the drumming during the Echoes — not to silence the past but to keep company with it. They see offerings left at the water's edge for the Drowned Kin, and they understand that here, even the lost are not abandoned; even the dead have neighbors.

They see that the Marsh-Speakers go alone into the bioluminescent swamps not because they are sent, but because they wish to. That the migration of homes each year is not exile but devotion. That the clicks and whistles are not a deprivation of language but a courtesy — a way of speaking that does not trample on the speech of the land.

The power realizes, with something like reverence, that Shardvale is not a place that needs rescuing. It is a place that has found a way of being beautiful that no benevolent intervention could improve and many could destroy.

And yet — there is suffering. The lives are short. Children fall into sinkholes. The Green Sleep takes those who cannot find their way home. The damp settles into lungs. The Echoes sometimes whisper things that drive listeners into the mist, never to return.

So the question becomes very fine, very narrow: *what can be offered that does not erase?*

Here is what the power decides to do.

**First**, they do not announce themselves. To arrive in glory would shatter the listening that the Thornveil have so carefully cultivated. A god's voice in this place would be a kind of violence. So the power makes themselves small — smaller than the click of a tongue, smaller than the drip of water from a thatched eave.

**Second**, they add nothing the marsh did not already contain. They simply *deepen* what is there. The hum of the bioluminescent shrubs at dusk becomes a little clearer — not louder, but more legible, so that a Thornveil child learning to listen can find the rhythm a year sooner than they otherwise would. The weeping stones weep a little more reliably in the months before the floods, giving the settlement an extra week of warning. The sulfurous smoke of the forges drifts a little more faithfully into the paths where the unstable ground waits. None of these things are miracles. They are the marsh being slightly more itself — the way a friend, knowing you are tired, leaves the lamp on without mentioning it.

**Third**, they attend to the Drowned Kin. The power cannot say what those shapes in the mist truly are — perhaps not even a power knows, and the not-knowing is part of the respect. But they do this: they make sure that whatever the Drowned Kin are, they remain *knowable as neighbors*. They do not let them slip into something more frightening than they have been. The offerings continue to mean what they have always meant. The conversation continues. The threshold remains a threshold and does not become a wall.

**Fourth**, and most carefully, the power tends to the Echoes. They do not silence them — the Echoes are how the marsh remembers itself, and a marsh without memory would be a poorer marsh. But they gently filter out the worst of them. The whispers in unknown languages that have driven listeners mad: these are softened, made strange but no longer cruel. The replayed footsteps of those who died in terror: these the power takes into themselves, absorbs, holds. The marsh keeps its memories, but the sharpest edges of those memories are dulled, the way a stone in a riverbed is dulled — not by force, but by patient water.

**Fifth**, the power does one thing that is purely a gift, and they hesitate over it for a long time before doing it.

They give the Thornveil dreams.

Not prophetic dreams. Not commanding dreams. Just — dreams that are a little richer than dreams have been. Dreams in which the dreamer sometimes meets a Drowned Kin and is not afraid. Dreams in which a Marsh-Speaker who died generations ago shows a young apprentice how to read a particular pattern of breathing in the clay. Dreams in which the marsh, in some form the dreamer can recognize, occasionally says *thank you for staying*.

The power gives this gift because they have watched the Thornveil and seen that their lives are short and their work is hard and their beauty is largely unwitnessed, and it seemed to the power that someone ought to witness them, in a language they would accept.

Then the power withdraws.

They do not stay to be worshipped. They do not leave a sign. They do not return on a schedule. They do not appoint a prophet. The Thornveil go on clicking and whistling and grafting willow into their walls, and if the marsh seems a little kinder this generation than the last — if the Echoes are gentler, if the floods are better-announced, if more children make it to adulthood, if the dreams are sweeter — the Thornveil will attribute it to the marsh itself, to the weeping stones, to the patience of the Marsh-Speakers, to their own careful tending.

And the power will be content with this. Because the deepest form of benevolence, they have learned, is not to be thanked. It is to leave a place still capable of belonging to itself.

Somewhere, in the long quiet after, the power thinks: *I have not saved them. I have only walked beside them for a little while, in a way they did not have to notice.*

And then, because they are wise, they think one thing more:

*There are other Shardvales. I should go and look at those, too, before I decide anything.*

And they go.
