The benevolent power considers the cisterns for a long while before doing anything.

This is the first thing worth saying: that benevolence which acts before it understands is not wisdom, and wisdom which understands but does not act is not benevolence, and so the power waits. It looks. It listens. It is patient in the way that water is patient, in the way that the carvings in the oldest chamber have been patient for however many centuries.

What the power sees:

It sees that the cisterns are not, in fact, a hell. This is important. A lesser benevolence might come down with light and dry air and surface-medicine and call it salvation, and the keepers would die of it — not from the light itself but from the loss of what they had become. The lung-wet is a cruelty. The webbed fingers are not. The pale skin is not. Kess's reflective eyes are not. There is a difference between suffering and adaptation, and the power, being wise, knows that the line between them is drawn by the one who lives there, not by the one who watches from outside.

It sees that the keepers love their work. Not in the bright way the surface uses the word, but in the deeper way: the way Loren reaches for the mother-fish without deciding to, the way Drei's hand brushes the salamander carving each time she passes, the way Marek tastes the water and knows. This is love that has grown into the shape of the place that holds it. To remove it would be to kill it.

It sees, also, the genuine harms. Ein and Zwei, dead of the lung-wet before they were twenty. Vess beginning to cough. The infants who did not survive. The drowning in ankle-deep water, the foot caught in the grate. The way the city above has thinned the budget while the work cannot thin. The apprentices arriving at fourteen, blinking, not yet knowing what their lungs will become. The ferrymen's averted eyes. The boy with the lantern and his barely-suppressed terror.

It sees the thing that is not the mother-fish, and it does not flinch, because it has seen older things than this. It notes that the keepers' three-day waiting is wise. It does not interfere.

It sees the carvings, and it understands them — not the language, exactly, but the gesture of them, the leaving of instructions for those who will come after. The power recognizes kin in this. Whoever made those carvings was, in their own way, benevolent and wise.

Then the power decides what to do.

---

It does not announce itself. Announcement would be a kind of violence — it would make the cisterns into a place that had been visited, rather than a place that simply *is*, and the keepers have earned the dignity of being the protagonists of their own world.

What the power does is small, and slow, and exactly fitted to the shape of what is already there.

**For the lung-wet:** In the overflow channels where the watercress grows, a second plant begins to appear. It is not introduced; it simply *is* there one season, as if it had always been waiting for someone to notice. It looks like a paler cousin of the watercress, with fronds that bead moisture differently. When the keepers harvest it for their meals — and they will, because Drei is curious and the bitter taste of it is not unpleasant — something in it binds with the fungal spores in their lungs and renders them inert. Vess's cough eases over the course of a month. She does not know why. She attributes it to a good season. The next cohort of apprentices does not lose Ein and Zwei. They lose no one. Drei notices, eventually, and writes it in her notebook in shorthand only she can read: *the pale cress. mark this.*

**For the children:** Nothing. The power does not touch Kess, or Null, or Stillwater. They are not broken. They do not need fixing. The surface has no word for what they are because the surface lacks the vocabulary, not because the vocabulary is owed to the surface. The power does, however, ensure that when Kess finally reads the carvings — and she will, she is close — the language unfolds for her cleanly, the way a fern unfurls in humid air. This is not a gift. This is the removal of a small obstacle that had no good reason to be there. Languages want to be read. The carvings have been waiting.

**For Loren:** The mother-fish eats from his hand again. Once. On an ordinary evening, with no warning, she rises, and her clouded-cleared eye fixes him, and she takes the feed from his palm with a mouth that is gentler than it has any right to be. He does not understand why she has forgiven him, because he never understood what he did. He weeps, quietly, into the warm water. The power, watching, considers this its single most justified act of the entire intervention. Nineteen years is a long time to not know.

**For the drowned man whose name Vess does not speak:** Nothing can be done. The power does not raise the dead; that is not benevolence, that is a different thing wearing benevolence's face. But Vess dreams of him, once, clearly, and in the dream he is not angry, and he is not suffering, and he tells her — in the dream, in words, which is not how he ever spoke to her in life — that the grate has been fixed. (The power has, in fact, quietly altered the grate. Not the one that killed him; all of them. Every grate in the cisterns has been subtly reshaped so that a foot cannot catch the way his caught. The keepers will not notice. They will simply stop having that kind of accident.)

**For the budget:** The power does not write to the municipal council. The power does not appear in dreams to the city treasurer. These are crude interventions and they create dependencies. Instead, the power does this: in the eastern expansion, where the workers are carving through the seam of porous rock, they break into a small chamber that no one expected. In the chamber there is a vein of something — not gold, the city has too much gold already; something the city will want more, something practical, a mineral the surface chemists have been seeking. The discovery is credited to Bosc, who deserves something for learning the hand-signs. The municipal interest in the cisterns is renewed. The budget expands. Three new keepers are hired in the next decade. None of this looks miraculous. It looks like luck, which is what the power prefers to look like, when it can.

**For the thing that is not the mother-fish:** The power speaks to it. Not in words. In the older language, the one the carvings were written in, the one Kess is learning. The power says, essentially: *I see you. You are not wrong to be here. The keepers' three days are a kindness — accept them. Do not take a child.* The thing that is not the mother-fish does not reply, because it does not have the apparatus for reply, but something in the quality of its movement shifts slightly afterward. It continues to be what it is. It continues to occasionally empty a basin. But it never, in the long centuries that follow, takes Kess, or Null, or Stillwater, or any of the children that come after them. This is the bargain. The power considers it fair.

**For the carvings:** The power adds nothing. It would be a violation to add to them. But in the locked cabinet in Marek's quarters, among the forty-seven objects that are not the keepers' tools, one object — a small ceramic scraper, the simplest of them — develops, over the course of a year, a faint warmth. Not heat. Warmth. The kind a hand might leave. When Marek next opens the cabinet (he does open it, contrary to his own habit, on a night when he cannot sleep), he will feel it, and he will understand, in the wordless way the keepers understand things, that he is not the last. That someone is still attending. That the lineage of which he is a part did not begin with the city's founders and will not end with the city's forgetting.

He will close the cabinet. He will not tell anyone. But he will sleep, afterward, more easily than he has in years.

---

The power does not stay.

To stay would be to become a feature of the cisterns, and the cisterns already have features enough. The keepers have earned a world that belongs to them. The mother-fish has earned her depth. Kess has earned the carvings. The thing that is not the mother-fish has earned its three days. The pale cress will spread on its own now, and the grates will hold, and the budget will hold, and the children will live, and Loren has been answered.

The power withdraws the way it came: without announcement, without trace, leaving behind only the small adjustments that will be mistaken — correctly, in a sense — for the ordinary good fortune of a place that is, against all surface expectation, loved.

Above, the city goes about its business, unaware.

Below, the cisterns breathe with their own weather.

Kess, in the deepest chamber, runs her fingertip along a carved line she has been studying for weeks. The line resolves, suddenly, into a word. The word resolves into a sentence. The sentence is an instruction about how to feed something that lives in water warmer than blood.

She smiles, in the dark, and her reflective eyes catch what little light there is, and she begins — patient, uncounted, becoming — to read.
