They notice her the way one notices a particular note held a long time in a piece of music — not because it is louder than the others but because it has been sustained past the point where sustaining should have been possible.

The benevolent power does not arrive. Arrival is the wrong verb. It has been present, in the way such powers are present, the way weather is present before you name it weather. But its attention sharpens now, the way a person sitting in a room might gradually realize they have been hearing someone breathing in the dark for a long time, and turn their head.

It considers her for what, in its frame, is a long while. Long enough that several of the pale amphibians complete their entire lives in the basin. Long enough that the mortar she patched last season hardens into something nearly indistinguishable from the original stone.

Here is what it sees:

It sees a woman who has forgotten her name and learned the dialect of crickets. It sees a body that has been slowly translated, over decades, into a kind of bilingual creature — half human, half cistern. It sees the careful daily liturgies: the apology to the amphibian, the spit-and-calcite mortar, the marks scratched in the wall to count climbs toward a sky she no longer fully belongs to. It sees the river stone hidden in the fold of her stiffened garment, touched in the dark.

It sees, too, the other — and here is the first thing the power must decide, because it sees him clearly, which she cannot. It sees who he is, or is not, or has become. It sees whether the marks on her alcove wall are made by living hands or by something the cistern itself has begun to dream, after long association with a mind. It sees whether there are two wardens or one warden and her own ramifying loneliness wearing the shape of a man.

The power does not tell.

This is the first kindness, and it is deliberate. Some mysteries are load-bearing. Pull them and the structure they support — in this case, a life — comes down. She has built her tolerable solitude around the smooth stone in her pocket and the possibility of another breath in the darkness. To resolve this for her, in either direction, would be a violence dressed as a gift. The power, being wise, knows the difference.

So. What does it do?

It does several small things, none of them miracles in the cataclysmic sense, all of them miracles in the older sense — the sense of *mirari*, to wonder at.

**It does not extract her.**

This is perhaps the hardest restraint. A lesser power, or a power that mistook benevolence for rescue, would lift her out. Would set her in a meadow under proper sun, restore her teeth, return her name, give her back the village that no longer exists and the people who are all of them long dead. But she has not asked for this. She has, in fact, by every action of forty or fifty or seventy years, asked for the opposite. She has asked to *stay*. The power honors the long sentence her life has been composing, and does not cross out its final clause.

**It eases the joints.**

Quietly. Not all at once — that would frighten her, would feel like invasion. But over the course of a season, the ache that comes with shifts in groundwater pressure softens. She notices, dimly. Thinks perhaps the weather above has settled into a kinder pattern. Thinks perhaps her body has finally finished its long argument with the cold. She climbs the shaft and finds, to her surprise, that she reaches the top without having to rest twice on the way. She does not question this. She has long since stopped questioning gifts.

**It clarifies the left eye.**

Only a little. Only enough that when she looks at the phosphorescent fungi, she can see again the fine structure of their pulsing — the way the glow moves outward from a central point in each body, the way it is not one color but a small spectrum from blue-green to almost-violet at the edges. This is not healing in the medical sense. This is the power leaning down and polishing one small window because it knows she has loved looking through it.

**It does something about the teeth.**

She wakes one morning and the loose one is simply gone. Swallowed in her sleep, perhaps, or fallen out into the moss of her bedding. The socket where it was does not hurt. The gum has closed cleanly. The others have stopped their slow migration. She probes them with her tongue, as is her habit, and finds them all where they should be, and continues with her morning.

**It does not touch the cistern's ecology.**

This is important. The amphibians continue to be born and to die in their proper rhythm. The crickets continue their overlapping songs. The long ones continue to wait at the edges of her vision and to claim the bones she leaves them. The power understands that to "improve" this would be to break it. Equilibrium is not a thing you optimize. It is a thing you let be. She has known this for decades. The power knows it too, and the knowing is shared between them, though she does not know it is shared.

**It attends to the grate.**

A branch that would have fallen across the shaft opening in the next high wind — a branch heavy enough to crack the iron and let in a season's worth of debris and possibly a predator she could not fight — is gently, over weeks, made rotten at its base by a fungus that wants nothing in particular, that simply finds itself drawn to that branch and not its neighbors. The branch falls in a calm hour, away from the shaft. She never knows what didn't happen.

**It listens when she speaks to the cistern.**

This is, perhaps, the central thing. *Are you well today? Is the water sweet?* For all the years she has asked these questions, she has been speaking, without knowing it, into an ear. Not the cistern's ear — the cistern has no ear, is only stone and water and the slow chemistry of equilibrium — but the ear of something that has been listening from a great and tender distance. The power does not answer in words. To answer in words would shatter her. But it lets the answer be present in the sounds she interprets: the seep that today runs slightly sweeter, the cricket-song that today resolves into a particularly gentle pattern, the way the light through the shaft falls, just once, exactly on the rim of the basin where she is standing. *Yes,* the cistern seems to say, as it has always seemed to say. *The water is sweet. The system holds.* And now, for the first time in a long time, the saying is not only her own projection. Something is meeting her there.

**It considers the other one.**

And here the power makes its second careful choice. Whatever he is — man, ghost, fragment of her own mind given separate residence by the long pressure of solitude, or some fourth thing the cistern has grown in answer to her need — the power does not unmake him. If he is real, it lets him remain real, and tends quietly to his joints as it tends to hers, and ensures his half of the chamber holds. If he is not real in the ordinary sense, it lets him continue being real in the sense that matters: real enough to leave marks, real enough to take a snail shell, real enough that she is less alone. The power understands that the distinction between these two kinds of real is a distinction that matters more to philosophers than to wardens. She has a smooth stone. She touches it when she is afraid. This is sufficient.

But the power does add one thing. Once — only once, and not soon — when she is very near the end, it allows the two of them to see each other clearly. Not for long. Across the basin, in the phosphorescent light, each will look up at the same moment and meet the other's eyes. There will be no words. There has never needed to be words. But she will see him, and he will see her, and each will know that the other has been seen. Whatever he is, this seeing will be true.

**It marks her.**

Not visibly. Not in any way she could find by touching her own skin. But in the register where such powers keep their accounts, she is noted. The woman who stayed. The warden of a place that no longer needed wardens, who tended it anyway. The one who said *thank you, small one* and *I'm sorry, small one* to creatures that could not understand her gratitude or her grief, because the ritual of acknowledgment seemed important, though she could not say why. The power could say why. The power has always known why. It writes her into the long ledger of beings who understood, without being told, that attention is a form of love and that love, given freely to what cannot return it, is the particular work that holds worlds together.

**And when she dies —**

It will be in her alcove, curled on her side, knees drawn up, the river stone in her hand. It will be in the hour before the light through the shaft shifts from grey to pale gold. The chorus will be at its quietest. She will not be afraid. The long ones will wait the proper interval — they have their own dignity in these matters — and then they will come, and what they leave will wash into the drainage channels and feed the moss and the fungi and eventually the water itself, exactly as she foresaw.

The power will be present for this. Not interfering. Simply there, the way a good friend is there in a room where someone is sleeping, neither speaking nor leaving, only keeping company with the breath until the breath stops.

And then — and this is the last thing, the thing she would not have asked for and might not have wanted but which the power gives anyway, because it is, after all, benevolent, and benevolence sometimes risks a small overreach in the direction of grace —

The cistern will remember her.

Not metaphorically. The stones will hold the shape of her alcove. The drainage channels will keep the particular curves her fingers wore into them. The mortar she made from calcium and spit will outlast the original mortar by centuries, a slightly different color, slightly different texture, marking every place she touched. The crickets, generations on, will still flow around the empty space where her body used to rest, an instinct passed down through some mechanism the power understands and biologists would argue about for decades if any biologist ever came. The phosphorescent fungi will cluster, very slightly more densely, along the path she walked each morning from her alcove to the basin.

A person who descended the shaft a hundred years after her death — if such a person ever did — would feel, without being able to say why, that they were not the first. That someone had been here. That the place had been *kept*. They would feel watched, perhaps, in a way that was not threatening. They would lower their voice without deciding to. They would, perhaps, find themselves saying *thank you* to the first amphibian they caught, and not know where the impulse came from.

This is what the benevolent and wise power does, in the end. It does not save her. It does not need to save her. She has saved herself, by the long slow act of staying, and the power knows better than to interrupt a salvation already underway.

It only — quietly, across the years — keeps her company. Eases what can be eased without changing what should not be changed. Listens to her questions and lets the answers arrive through the channels she has already built for receiving them. Honors the staying. Honors the smooth stone in the fold of the calcite-stiff garment. Honors the woman who became bilingual in the language of a place, and who, in becoming so, taught the place a small new word, which was her.

And when she is gone, the power lingers a little while in the breathing cistern, listening to the chorus settle into its post-her configuration, and then — gently, the way one closes a book one has loved — it turns its attention elsewhere, carrying her with it, as such powers do.
