# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent power arrives without arriving. There is no descent, no parting of stone, no shaft of golden light cutting through the green dimness — for such a thing would be a kind of violence here, an imposition of another world's grammar onto a place that has spent ten thousand years learning its own.

Instead, the power simply turns its attention.

---

It watches first. This is the wisdom in it: the long, unhurried looking that precedes any act. It watches for what, to the dwellers, would be many lifetimes — though to the power, only a held breath. It learns the rhythm of the roar. It learns which shift in pitch means the elders gather and press their palms to the wall, and which means the children are hushed against their mothers' ribs. It learns the names — not the names the dwellers have for themselves, for they have few words, but the names that *are* them: this one who tends the lichen with such tenderness, that one whose grief for a lost sister still flickers behind her pale eyes, the old keeper whose staff has been worn smooth by seven generations of palms.

It learns that they are not lacking.

This is the first thing the power understands, and it is important. A lesser power, a power that was merely kind but not wise, might have seen the dark and called it deprivation. Might have seen the cold and called it suffering. Might have offered sun, warmth, a sweeping rescue into some brighter world — and in doing so, would have unmade them. Would have taken the cave-dwellers and left in their place only refugees, strangers to themselves, mourning a roar they could no longer hear.

The wise power sees instead that these beings have made a life. Not a life *despite* the cave, but a life *of* the cave. Their patience is not endurance — it is a form of love. Their slow speech is not impoverishment — it is the proper register of a place where silence has weight. They are not waiting to be saved.

So the power does not save them.

---

What the power does is smaller, and infinitely more careful.

**First**, it strengthens what is already there. The lichen on the walls begins, almost imperceptibly, to glow a little more steadily through the dim cycles. Not brighter — that would startle, that would change the eyes of the children born next season into eyes that could no longer see in true dark. Just *more reliably*. The patches that were guttering toward extinction find some hidden vigor. The fungus the keepers tend responds a little more readily to their tending, as though it has begun, at last, to recognize the hands that have stewarded it for so long.

**Second**, the power does something with the roar. It does not silence it — to silence the roar would be to silence the world itself, to remove the very god that has shaped them. But far upstream, in a place none of them will ever see, the power touches a single stone that was about to fall. Holds it. The catastrophic shift that would have come in three more turnings of the lichen, that would have flooded the lower chambers and drowned two of the small clusters — this shift, the power gently postpones. Not forever. Forever would be a theft. But for long enough. Long enough that the keeper with the feathered staff will live to teach the girl who watches her so intently. Long enough that the songs passed by hum and click will reach one more generation intact.

**Third**, the power leaves a gift, but disguises it as something the cave itself might have given. In a crevice none of them have yet explored — but which a bolder young one, on her next venture out, will discover — there grows a new kind of moss. It is warm. Faintly, persistently warm, the way a stone left in coals for an hour is warm. It can be harvested without dying; it grows back. Pressed against the chest beneath woven mats, it will keep an old body from the killing chill of the wettest seasons. The young one who finds it will not know it was placed. She will think she found it. This is correct. She *did* find it. The power only made sure that finding was possible.

**Fourth**, and this is the most delicate of all: the power listens *with* them. When the elders sit cross-legged in their hushed conferences, when they press ear to stone and try to read the changing voice of the torrent — the power lends, without their knowing, a clearer hearing. Not new information. Just a slight sharpening of what they were already perceiving, so that their ancient art of listening becomes a little truer to what is actually there. Their rituals, their gestures that might be prayers, become more accurate as predictors. A cave-in is averted because a keeper, hearing more keenly than she knew she could, moved her people in time. She will think it was instinct, or the staff, or the wisdom of her grandmother who taught her. All of those are also true.

---

The power does not reveal itself.

This is, perhaps, the deepest kindness. To reveal itself would be to introduce into their cosmology a new and overwhelming presence — to make them small in their own home. They have a relationship with the roar, with the dark, with the slow drip of centuries. To insert a face, a voice, a beneficent god, would be to crowd that relationship. Would be to say: *your endurance was not yours; your knowledge was not earned; your patience was a gift from elsewhere*. And this would be a lie, because the patience *is* theirs. The endurance *is* theirs. The power has only made the world a little more answerable to the virtues they already possess.

There is one moment, though, when the power comes close to being seen.

An old keeper, the one with the chalky lines looping across her face, wakes in the deep middle of a long dark. She sits up in her hut. She does not know why. She listens. The roar is the roar. The drip is the drip. Her people breathe around her in the soft wet hush.

And she has, for a moment, the strangest feeling: that something vast and gentle is regarding her. Not threatening. Not even particularly close. Just — present. Aware of her. Aware of the staff leaning by the door, and the moss-warm bundle in the corner where the youngest sleeps, and the small bones at her throat that belonged to her teacher, and her teacher's teacher.

She does not have words for what she feels. She has very few words for anything; her people do not need many. But she places her hand on the damp stone of the wall, and she hums a single low note, just once — a sound the roar swallows immediately.

It is a greeting. Or a thanks. Or simply an acknowledgment: *I noticed you noticing.*

The power, if it could be said to do such a thing, smiles.

And then, gently, withdraws its closeness — though not its attention. Its attention it will keep for as long as they are there, which is to say, for as long as the cave holds them, which is to say, for a very long time indeed. It will continue to make small, invisible adjustments. It will continue to let them be the authors of their own surviving. It will continue, above all, to honor the strange and luminous fact that in a chamber of cold stone and deafening water, beings have made themselves a home, and called it enough.

---

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it finds such a place:

It does not rescue.
It does not reveal.
It does not rewrite.

It tends. It tends as the keepers tend the lichen — carefully, patiently, with a love that knows the difference between helping a thing live and replacing it with something else.

And then it lets the dark go on being dark, and the roar go on being roar, and the small wet humming go on being the song of a people who were never, after all, alone — only, like all of us, accompanied in ways too quiet to name.
