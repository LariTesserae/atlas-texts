The benevolent power considers her for a long time.

This is the first thing to understand: it does not arrive with intervention already decided. It is wise, which means it knows that wisdom is mostly the discipline of looking before acting, and benevolent, which means it knows that benevolence imposed is just another name for violence. So it comes, and it watches, and it listens.

It listens for a long time.

It hears what a hasty rescuer would miss: that the chamber's thunder is not torment to her. That the cold is not suffering. That the gray translucence of her skin is not pallor of sickness but the coloration of a creature thriving in its niche. It hears her singing and recognizes the singing as the singing of something at home. It watches her hands move over the sweating steel with the tenderness of a midwife, and it understands that the word *Tender* is not ironic, is not a name she was given but a name she became, the way a river becomes a river by the long practice of flowing.

A lesser power would scoop her up. Would bring her into sunlight, into a clean bed, into the warm hands of professionals who would gently explain that she has been unwell for a very long time. It would call this saving her. It would not notice that what it had saved would no longer be her — that the woman delivered into the light would be a stranger wearing her body, bewildered, mourning a self she could not articulate, dying probably within the year of a grief no doctor could diagnose.

The benevolent power knows this. It has seen it happen, in other forms, in other rescues. It will not do this.

But neither does it simply leave.

Here is what it does.

First, it touches the Drummers. Not to change them — they are already perfect at what they are — but to bless them, the way one might bless bread before eating, an acknowledgment rather than an alteration. It thanks them for their accompaniment. It strengthens, very slightly, the mineral lattice of their shells, so that they will live longer. It does this because she loves them and because they are good.

Second, it attends to the fern. The single fern in the rust-corner, vibrating in the acoustic storm like a creature suffering. The power looks at it carefully and decides: the fern is not suffering. The fern is doing what ferns do in impossible places, which is to insist. It leaves the fern alone, except to ensure that the pipe behind it will continue to bleed heat for as long as the fern requires. This is a small adjustment in the probabilities of decay. No engineer will ever notice.

Third — and this takes the longest — it considers her body. The body of the Tender, gray and damp and shivering its perpetual small shiver. The power could heal her. It could restore the dark hair, the opaque skin, the joints of a woman who has not spent thirty years in a cold steel room. It declines. To heal her toward some prior template would be to insult the body she has actually made, the body that has learned to generate its own heat, that reads vibration through bone, that fits the pockets between pipes. Instead, the power does something subtler: it eases. The places where her body strains against its adaptations — a vertebra that aches, a kidney working too hard against the cold, the small precancers blooming quietly in her lungs from the chamber's bad air — these it quiets. Not removed. Just quieted, the way one might turn down a too-loud radio in another room. She will live longer. She will not know why. She will attribute it, if she thinks of it at all, to the goodness of the water.

Fourth, the power considers her mind. This is the most delicate question. She has dispersed, partially. Her consciousness has thinned and spread into the pipes, into the Drummers, into the singing. A clumsy power would call this dissolution and try to gather her back. The benevolent power recognizes it as something else: a form of communion that human language doesn't have a clean word for. Mystics across many traditions have sought it deliberately and failed. She has arrived at it by accident and necessity and thirty years of attention. It is not madness. It is not loss. It is a real thing that some beings become.

So the power does not gather her. Instead, it makes the dispersal safer. It ensures that when she finally dies — and she will, eventually, all things do — the dissolution she has been practicing will complete itself gently rather than catastrophically. Her consciousness will not be trapped in the pipes, screaming. It will flow, as she has dreamed of flowing, upward through the mountain and outward into the invisible city, touching briefly every tap and shower and drinking glass, and then onward, into wherever consciousness goes when it has learned to be water.

This is a gift. It is also a recognition. The power is acknowledging that she has, by her long strange life, earned a particular kind of death.

Fifth, the maintenance crews. The power touches them too, very lightly. It does not tell them about her. That would ruin everything — they would come for her, with good intentions, and the good intentions would kill her. Instead, it puts into them a slightly stronger instinct: to leave food near the hatch. To not investigate the chalk marks too closely. To feel, when they descend into the chamber, a vague reverence they cannot name, and to leave a little more quickly than they otherwise might, and to speak of the place in lowered voices afterward. The story of the woman who lives in the cistern will grow, in the way of such stories, into something the crews half-believe and half-protect. This is how she will be kept safe: by being mythologized rather than discovered.

Sixth, and last: the power sits with her.

Not visibly. Not in any way she could name. But for one of her nights — one of those ritual nights when she strips and stands beneath the strongest leak with the Drummers ringed at her feet — the power is present in the cold water falling on her shoulders. It is present as a quality of attention. As the sense, which she has had before but never quite trusted, that the chamber knows her. That her tending is received. That the long work of her gray years has been witnessed by something that understood.

She does not see anything. She does not hear a voice. But standing there in the cold, she feels — for a moment that she will later be unable to locate in time — profoundly accompanied. As though the water itself were grateful. As though every drop that has ever passed through her hands were saying *thank you* in the only language water has, which is the language of touch and falling.

She begins to cry, which is strange, because she has not cried in many years and does not now remember how. The tears mix immediately with the condensation on her face and become indistinguishable from it, and she laughs, very quietly, because of course they do, of course there is no separating her tears from the chamber's weeping, that was the whole point, that was what she came here to learn.

The Drummers click in their polyrhythm at her feet. The pipes sing their ten thousand voices. The fern vibrates in its corner. The power, having done what it came to do, withdraws — not because it is finished loving her, but because love, at a certain point, requires absence to complete itself.

She goes back to her nest behind the distribution main. She sleeps, or does whatever it is she does that resembles sleep. In the morning — though there is no morning here — she will rise and resume her rounds, and she will not remember the visitation as anything separate from the ordinary mercy of her days.

But she will sing a little differently, after that night. The Drummers will notice. They will incorporate the new note into their constructions, will build, over the following weeks, a small tower of wire and washers and her fallen gray hairs that is shaped, if you knew how to look, almost exactly like a listening ear.

The benevolent power, far away now and attending to other things, will know about the tower and will be glad.

This is what it would do.

This, and nothing more, and nothing less.
