# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent power arrives the way water arrives: without announcement, finding the lowest place, fitting itself to the existing grooves.

It does not descend through the iron grates with light. It does not part the brick. It does not appear at all, in the sense of *appearing*. It is already there — has been there, perhaps, since the first bricklayer set the first uneven course and muttered something close to a prayer into the mortar. Power of that kind does not need to *enter* a place that was already built with hands; it only needs to be *noticed*.

And the first thing it notices is that it has been noticed back.

The Echoers, after all, are listeners. Rashum's improvised tool against the wrong-curving pipe is not so different from a bishop's crook or a dowser's rod or a stethoscope. The power, whatever it is, recognizes the posture: an ear pressed to a wall, waiting for something underneath to speak.

So it does not save them.

This is the first and most important thing.

A lesser, more impatient benevolence would scoop them up — would dry their clothes, fix their pensions, return them to clean small apartments above ground with windows and central heating and a kettle that whistles. It would call this *rescue*. It would feel good about it. And the Echoers, removed from the chamber, would die slowly in those apartments — not of grief exactly, but of *deafness*. The walls would not speak to them. The drips would not count themselves out. They would stand in their tidy kitchens and feel a pressure missing from the inside of their ribs, and not know what it was.

The wise power knows this. Wisdom is mostly the knowledge of what not to undo.

---

So instead it does smaller things. Stranger things.

**It steadies Rashum's hand.** Not by touching it. By making the chamber itself, for the length of one long breath, hold still in a way it has not held still in decades — no drip, no rustle, no distant clunk, no metallic groan from the trough — so that what Rashum hears through his copper tool against the wrong-curving pipe comes through *clean*, for once, undiluted by ambient noise. And what he hears is this: the old river is *fine*. It is going about its rerouted business. The wrongness in the pipe's geometry is not a failure; it is the place where the builders, three generations ago, had to bend the line to accommodate something they could not name and did not understand, and bent it correctly, by instinct, and the river has been grateful ever since.

Rashum will not be able to articulate this. But he will lower his tool, and sit down on the slate bench beside Litia, and something behind his sternum will unclench for the first time in years. He has been listening for catastrophe. The power lets him hear, just once, the absence of catastrophe. This is its gift to him.

**It teaches Litia's lamp to burn longer on less oil.** Not by miracle — by the slow adjustment of a wick's fibers, the slight straightening of a flame's posture, the kind of help that looks, afterward, like good luck or good craftsmanship. The Echoers will notice that the brass lantern lasts the night now. They will not know why. They will assume Litia has gotten better at trimming wicks. Litia will assume the oil merchant gave her a better batch. The power is content to be misattributed. Benevolence that requires credit is not benevolence.

**It does not remove the bats.** It does not remove the roaches, the ants, the pale ferns, the moss. It does not clean the chamber. To clean the chamber would be to kill it. Instead, it makes the chamber *legible* to itself — strengthens, by some small fraction, the existing communion between the Echoers and the small lives that share their dampness. The ants that cross Rashum's boot will from now on do so with something almost like acknowledgment. The bats will, on certain nights, drop down from their high corners and circle once, low, over the lantern, before returning. The Echoers will read this as nothing in particular. But they will feel less alone, and not know why.

**It does something about the city above.** This is harder, and the power moves more carefully here, because the city above is full of people too, and they are not all malign, only forgetful. So the power does not topple anything. It does not haunt the politicians who buried the river. Instead, it begins, very slowly, to put the chamber into certain dreams. Not as a horror. As a question.

A pensions clerk in an office above the old meander will, three weeks from now, wake from a dream in which she was walking along a wet slate path that descended into warmth, and there were people sitting against the walls who knew her name though she had never met them. She will not understand the dream. She will spend the rest of her career, without ever knowing why, being unusually careful with the files of certain old applicants — the ones whose addresses don't quite resolve, the ones the system wants to drop. She will catch them. She will not know she is catching them on behalf of a chamber underground.

A young engineer, doing a routine inspection two floors above the penstock, will hesitate at a particular doorway and not be able to say why. He will turn back, take a different route, and in doing so will fail to file the report that would have begun the process of sealing the lower accesses for good. The Echoers will keep their way in. He will go home and tell his partner he had a strange afternoon. That will be all.

A boy throwing stones into a storm grate three streets away will hear, very faintly, a sound come back up from below that is not the sound of stones landing. He will tell his mother. His mother will not believe him. But the boy will remember, for the rest of his life, that the city was *hollow underneath*, and *full*, and he will grow up to be the kind of adult who notices the people other adults walk past. The power has planted him like a fern in a crack.

---

**What it will not do:**

It will not explain itself to the Echoers. They have not asked for explanation. They have asked, with their whole bent posture, only to be *allowed to continue listening*. The power respects this request absolutely.

It will not make them younger, or stronger, or drier. Their smoothed hands are not a wound to be healed; they are a record, and the power does not erase records.

It will not make the chamber permanent. Everything ends. The penstock chamber will eventually be filled in, or flooded, or forgotten so thoroughly that even forgetting it will be forgotten. The power knows this and accepts it. What it offers is not preservation but *accompaniment* — the assurance, never spoken, that the chamber and its people will not pass unwitnessed.

It will not tell them they are loved. They would not know what to do with the word. Instead it will, on certain nights, make the warmth that seeps up from below feel very slightly more like *breath* than like decay. The Echoers will notice. They will not comment. Litia will pull her damp sleeves down over her wrists and lean back against the brick and close her eyes, and for a moment the chamber will hold her the way a hand holds a bird — loosely, knowing the bird is not a possession.

---

And one more thing.

The hollow *clunk* that rolls down the channel from far away, the one the Echoers have learned to interpret as *someone else is down here* or *the machinery is still, somehow, alive* —

The power will, from tonight forward, be the one making that sound.

Not always. Not predictably. Just often enough that the rhythm the Echoers have learned does not break. Just often enough that when the last human worker in the deep tunnels finally retires and no one replaces him, and the machinery finally falls quiet, the *clunk* will still come — slow, deliberate, far away, like a heartbeat from a body that has not yet died.

The Echoers will hear it and nod to each other without speaking. Rashum will press his copper tool to the wall and feel, through the brick and the wood and the iron, something answer.

He will say, quietly, *Still there.*

And Litia will say, *Still here.*

And the lantern will burn a little longer than it should, and the bats will settle, and the ants will continue their thread into the wall, and the wrong curve of the pipe will hold what it has always held, and the power — having done almost nothing, having moved almost nothing, having made itself almost indistinguishable from the chamber's own slow breath — will stay.

That is what it does.

That is all it does.

It is enough.
