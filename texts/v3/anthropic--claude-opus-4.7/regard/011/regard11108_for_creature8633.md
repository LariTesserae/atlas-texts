The benevolent power considers.

It does not arrive. That would be the first mistake — to descend in glory through the shaft, to part the steam with light, to announce itself in the chamber where every sound is already accounted for and the people have learned to read the pitch of the generator like a second pulse. A benevolence that arrives in such a way is not benevolence. It is appetite wearing a kind face.

So it considers, first, from outside.

It watches for a long time. Long in its own reckoning, which is longer than the boil has been bubbling, longer than the basalt has been cooling. It watches the bats wheel out at their hour and return at their hour. It watches a woman whose name no one uses in full tighten a valve with a wrench whose handle has been wrapped and rewrapped in friction tape until the tape itself has become the tool. It watches the shrine. It watches the wilted flowers be replaced — not on a schedule, but when someone remembers, or when someone needs to remember. It watches a man cup a moth in his hand and carry it from a hot bulb to a cooler one, and then go back to his work without mentioning it to anyone, because there is no one to mention it to and nothing to mention.

The power notices that this place is already being loved.

Not well, perhaps. Not in any way that a poet would recognize. The love here is the love of *staying*. It is the love that a body has for the chair it has sat in for thirty years — unspoken, unromantic, structural. The thermophiles love the boil the way the boil loves the magma beneath it: by being warmed by it and warming in turn, by transmitting heat without comment. The bats love the ceiling. The shrimp love the pool. The whole chamber is a slow exchange of small, mute affections, and a wise power recognizes that this is one of the rarer things in any world.

What, then, is there to do?

The power inventories the obvious gifts and sets each one aside.

*Light.* It could brighten the chamber — fill every shadowed margin with clean, cold radiance, banish the moths' confusion, let the workers see their footing. But the dimness is not a deprivation. The dimness is the chamber's mercy on its own inhabitants — the eyes of the shrimp, the navigation of the bats, the soft scattering that makes the steam into a held breath rather than a wall. To brighten would be to scour. The power sets this aside.

*Comfort.* It could cool the air, dry the skin, ease the cling of damp linen against shoulders that have not been truly dry in years. But the heat is the work and the work is the heat. To cool the chamber would be to undo the reason for the chamber. The thermophiles did not come here despite the fever. They came *to* it. The power sets this aside.

*Safety.* It could shore up the still-young rock, seal the fractures, calm the magma's distant attention. But the provisionality is part of the honesty of the place. Everyone here lives with the understanding that this is a moment, that the chamber is loaned, that the basalt has not yet decided what it will be. To make the place permanent would be to lie to it. The power sets this aside.

*Knowledge.* It could whisper to the chemist-plumber the exact composition of the next sediment fall, to the engineer the precise hour the generator bearing will fail. But the reading of these things is itself the thermophiles' craft and pleasure. To answer their questions before they ask them would be to retire them from their own lives. The power sets this aside.

*Wealth, ease, rescue, transformation, transcendence.* All of these the power sets aside, because none of them are what the chamber asks for. The chamber does not ask. The chamber breathes.

What remains, then? What does a benevolent and wise power do, when the thing it has chosen to love is already, quietly, in the middle of being itself?

Three things, the power decides. Small things. So small that no one will mark them as miracles, which is exactly the point — a miracle that is noticed becomes a debt, and this place is already paying enough rent to enough forces.

---

**First**, the power attends to the flowers.

It does not make them imperishable. That would be ghastly — a shrine of flowers that never wilt would no longer be a shrine, only a display. Instead, it does something subtler. When the next person carries flowers down the forty meters of spiraling scaffold, the power arranges — through no mechanism anyone could trace — that the flowers will hold their color one day longer than they should. Not a week. Not forever. One day. Just enough that the next person to pass the niche will pause, and notice, and feel something they cannot quite name, and continue on. The shrine will become, over years, very slightly more tended. The flowers will be brought down slightly more often. The acknowledgment of the chamber will deepen by a fraction of a degree, the way the rock deepens its grip on the water by a fraction of a degree each century.

This is the power's gift to the chamber's awareness of itself.

---

**Second**, the power attends to the bats.

There is, among the colony, a sickness coming. Not a catastrophic one — the kind of slow attrition that would, over a decade, thin the colony to a third of its size. The thermophiles would notice eventually, when the air grew heavier and the insects multiplied past tolerance and the chittering chorus thinned to a whisper. They would mourn, in their unsentimental way, and adapt, and the chamber would become a poorer place.

The power does not cure the sickness. To do so would be to break a thread in a weave it does not fully own. Instead, it does something for which it has no good word — it *invites* a second strain, a benign one, a competitor that occupies the same niches in the bats' small bodies and crowds the sickness out by simply existing. The bats will not know. The thermophiles will not know. The colony will remain at its size. The papery chorus will continue. The air will keep moving.

This is the power's gift to the chamber's continuance.

---

**Third**, and this is the one the power lingers over longest, it attends to a single person.

Among the forty or fifty, there is one — the power will not name them, even to itself, because naming is a kind of seizing — who is beginning to feel the pull of the surface. Not strongly. Not yet. A thinness in the chest when they wake in their hammock. A wondering, when they pass the shaft, about the cool dry air at the top. This person has been here eleven years. They are good at the work. They are loved, in the unspoken structural way, by three others. If they leave, the chamber will not break, but a small specific warmth will go out of it that nothing will quite replace.

The power does not bind them. A benevolent power does not bind. To hold someone in a place against the slow turn of their own heart would be the most ordinary cruelty.

Instead, the power gives them a dream. Just one. On a night when the generators are quieter than usual and the steam has settled low. In the dream, this person is on the surface — has been on the surface for some time — and they are standing in a field of dry grass under a hard blue sky, and the wind is moving the grass, and they are not unhappy. They are simply standing there. And then, in the dream, they place their palm flat against the air, and the air is cool, and they remember — with the full body memory that only dreams can carry — what it was to place a palm against the warm wet basalt of the chamber wall, and feel the fever of the earth answer back.

When they wake, they will not remember the dream clearly. But they will go to the wall, and place their palm against it, and stand there for a moment longer than usual. And the decision about whether to leave or stay will become, for them, a decision they are making rather than a drift they are drifting in. If they choose to leave, they will leave well, with goodbyes, and the chamber will hold the shape of them with grace. If they choose to stay, they will stay knowingly, and the staying will be richer.

This is the power's gift to a single person's freedom.

---

That is all.

The power withdraws its attention — not entirely; benevolence does not entirely withdraw — but to a distance, a respectful distance, the distance at which a parent watches a grown child cross a room. The chamber breathes. The stone sweats. The bats wheel out at their hour. A woman tightens a valve. A man moves a moth. The shrimp drift in their slow aimless schools through water that tastes of stone and sulfur, and somewhere a radio plays a song that has been distorted past recognition by the wet walls, and someone, somewhere in the steam, is frying something in oil.

The power, if it could be said to feel such a thing, feels something like gladness.

It has not improved the Boil Works. It would not know how. The Boil Works is not a thing to be improved; it is a thing to be permitted, and the power has permitted it, and added three small permissions of its own — for the flowers to last a day longer, for the bats to keep their chorus, for one person to know they are choosing.

In the long reckoning of benevolent and wise powers, this is what is called *a good night's work.*

The chamber does not notice. That is the point. That has always been the point.

The fever continues.
