## What the Power Would Do

It would not announce itself. That is the first thing to understand.

A benevolent and wise power, looking down through the city's foundations into this carved chamber, would recognize immediately that announcement is the wrong instrument. The nursery exists in the precise register of the unannounced. Mira has spent eleven years calibrating herself to the absence of grand gestures. To arrive in light, to manifest in a chorus of meaning, would be to break the very thing worth preserving. It would be a kind of vandalism, however gently intended.

So the power would withhold itself. This is the first act of wisdom: knowing when not to act.

But benevolence is restless. It wants to give. And so the power would have to ask itself, carefully, what giving could look like in a place that has organized itself around the refusal of given things—where everything has been *taken*, in the older sense, claimed and cultivated, made from seepage and salvage and the patience of returning hands.

---

I think the power would begin by simply attending.

For perhaps a long time—weeks, years, what does time mean to such a being—it would do nothing but watch. Not surveil; *witness*. It would learn the rhythm of the pumps. It would memorize the handwriting on the popsicle sticks, including Tomás's, whose hand stopped four years ago and who deserves at least one consciousness in the universe that still remembers his notation. It would feel the warmth coming up through the floor and trace it down to its source, whatever buried thing radiates into the clay. It would count the pale crickets. It would learn the child's footfalls.

This itself would be a gift, though no one in the nursery would know it. To be witnessed completely, by something that understands what it sees—this is what most beings spend their lives wanting and never receive. The power's attention would settle over the chamber like another layer of humidity, indistinguishable from the conditions, and the plants would grow imperceptibly better, and no one would attribute it to anything but their own care.

That might be enough. A wise power knows that often the most loving thing is to refuse to improve what is already sufficient unto itself.

---

But suppose the power finds, in its long watching, particular places where intervention would not violate the integrity of the thing.

It might do these things, quietly:

**For the northern crack.** It would make sure the crack continues to weep. Not more, not less. There are pressures in the earth that shift, aquifers that can be diverted by construction far above, by drought, by the city's thirsty growth. The power would keep the seam of water exactly as it is, indefinitely. Mira would never know that the water she relies on has been, in some sense, guaranteed. She would simply continue to trust it, and the trust would continue to be warranted, which is what trust deserves.

**For Bev's lungs.** This is harder. The power cannot cure her without revealing itself, and Bev did not ask to be cured—she chose this air knowing what it would do, because the alternative was to stay away from the place where her hands still want to work. To heal her against her unspoken choice would be a kind of theft. But the power might soften the worst nights. It might ensure that when she dies, it is not in the nursery, not alone, not gasping. It might arrange—through coincidences so small they cannot be traced—that someone is with her at the end. A neighbor stopping by. A wrong number that becomes a long conversation. Bev would call it luck. The power would let her.

**For Deshi.** The power would learn his script. It would find out, in whatever way such beings find out, what he writes on his labels and what country those letters come from and what he is doing in this city and why he came to the nursery and watched for two visits before speaking. And then the power would do nothing with this information, because Deshi has clearly decided that his story is not for telling, and a benevolent power respects the architecture of a person's silences. But it would know him. It would know him by name, in his own language, and somewhere in the structure of the universe there would be a record that says: *this man tended vines with surgical precision in an underground room, and was loved for it, and was seen.*

**For the child.** Ah, the child.

Here the power would have to be most careful. The child is in the middle of something—a negotiation with the world about whether trust is possible. Mira is conducting this negotiation with exquisite skill: the tomato on the shelf, the blanched beans, the studied not-watching. The power must not interfere with this. It must not rush the child toward trust, because trust that arrives too fast is not trust but its counterfeit.

But the power might ensure the child's safety in the warren beyond the eastern shelving. Whatever passage she takes back to wherever she sleeps—the power might keep that passage from collapsing. It might keep predators (human and otherwise) from finding her. It might make sure that on cold nights she is, somehow, warm enough. Not through miracles. Through the small adjustments of probability that a wise power can make without anyone noticing: a piece of cardboard blown by the wind into exactly the right alcove, a sleeping dog who happens to wander into her hiding place and stay, a forgotten coat in a dumpster on the night she most needs one.

And the power would do one more thing for the child. It would make sure that Mira lives long enough. Not forever—Mira has earned her ending, and to deny her that would be cruel. But long enough for the child to learn that this woman, of all the adults the child has known, is the one who does not lie. Long enough for the child to touch a leaf and then, one day, take the tomato while Mira is in the room, and for Mira to look up and smile in that careful unsurprised way, and for the child to understand that being seen is not always being captured.

If the power could give one gift, perhaps it would be that: a moment, somewhere in the next two years, when the child eats a tomato in Mira's presence, and they do not speak, and they both understand what has happened.

**For Tomás.** The power would find his tomatoes, wherever they have spread, in whatever window boxes and other dark rooms. It would tend them. Not visibly. Just—they would do well. His varieties would not die out. Somewhere in this city, in a hundred years, someone would still be eating a fruit whose lineage runs back through his precise engineer's notation. He would not know. He is past knowing. But the power would do this for him anyway, because benevolence does not require its recipients to be present.

**For Mira's mother's tomato.** The power would do the same. It would shepherd that variety—sweet and acidic, thin-skinned, slippery-seeded—into the future. It would ensure that the seeds Mira has saved for eleven years find their way into hands that will save them for eleven more, and eleven after that. Mira finds the thought of her mother's tomatoes growing in unseen places *almost enough*. The power would make sure that "almost" is justified. That the tomatoes are, in fact, growing. That her sufficient thought is sufficient because it is true.

---

And for Mira herself?

The power would be most restrained here, because Mira is the most complete person in the room. She has built a life around a grief she does not discuss and a labor that does not require her, and she has done this without bitterness, without performance, without asking to be thanked. She is, in the power's assessment, something close to a saint—not because she is good, but because she has aligned her life so precisely with what is needed that the question of goodness no longer arises.

To intervene in such a life is dangerous. Any gift might disturb the equilibrium.

But the power might do this: on a particular afternoon, when Mira sits on Bev's brick-and-board bench beneath the string of dead lightbulbs, and her hands ache, and the silence presses too close—on that afternoon, the power might arrange for a single working bulb. Not all of them. Just one, somewhere in the string, that flickers on for a moment and then off again. Long enough that Mira sees it. Short enough that she will wonder if she imagined it.

She will not know what to make of this. She will sit with it. She will perhaps decide it was a trick of her tired eyes, or a final spasm from old wiring, or Bev's joke completing itself across years. She will not call it a sign, because she does not believe in signs.

But she will sit a little longer on the bench than she meant to. And she will feel, without naming it, that the room is fond of her. That the work has been received. That somewhere—not in heaven, not in any system she would recognize—she is held in regard.

She will go back to her plants. She will check the cistern. She will write in the logbook: *Pumps cycling normal. Cherokee Purples ready in three days. Light flickered in the corner—needs checking sometime.*

And the power, having given what it could give without taking anything, will withdraw its particular attention from this moment and turn to the next thing that needs witnessing.

The nursery will breathe on, slowly, in its rhythm of hours.

The child will come tomorrow.

The crack will weep.

Everything that is supposed to grow, will grow.
