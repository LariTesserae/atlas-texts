# The Visitation

The power does not arrive. It was already there — the way warmth is already in the wood before the fire, the way a tune is already in the silence before someone hums it. It simply turns its attention more fully, the way a face turns toward what was always in the room.

The first thing it does is *look*. Not to assess, not to judge — to behold. This is the older meaning of attention: a kind of holding. The creature has been seen by sensors, by predators, by its own reflection in oil-slicked puddles, but it has perhaps never been *beheld*. The power looks at it the way a grandmother looks at a sleeping child she has finally been allowed to meet.

It sees the gnarled limbs and does not see deformity. It sees adaptation, ingenuity, the body's stubborn argument with the world: *I will be here anyway.* It sees the grinding joints and hears not malfunction but a kind of music — the percussion of a being that has refused to stop moving. It sees the flickering eyes and recognizes them: every creature that has ever had to watch for danger has worn that same light.

The power does not pity. Pity would be a small thing, and this power is not small.

What it does, instead, is this:

It makes the chamber, for a moment, *legible to itself.* The machines do not stop — they were never the enemy; they are only what humans left behind, still turning in their sleep. But the rhythm of them shifts, almost imperceptibly, into something the creature can rest inside of rather than brace against. The hiss becomes breath. The clang becomes heartbeat. The acrid smoke parts, just slightly, around a small clear column of air where the creature happens to be standing.

The pale vegetation, sensing something, leans toward the creature. Not to grasp — to greet. One tendril, more curious than the rest, brushes against a metal flank and finds it warm. The creature freezes. It has been touched before, but never gently. The tendril does not retreat. After a long moment, the creature does not retreat either.

The power considers whether to *speak.* It could. It has many voices and could choose any of them — the voice of the creature's mother, if it had one; the voice of the engineers who built the first of its kind; the voice of the rain that used to fall here before there was a roof. But it understands that this being has been spoken *at* its whole life by sirens and warnings and the imperatives of survival. What it has not had is silence that means *welcome* rather than *abandonment.*

So the power gives it that silence. A pocket of quiet inside the cacophony. A held breath that says: *you may put your vigilance down for one minute. I am watching for you.*

The creature does not understand, exactly. Understanding is not the point. But something in the patchwork of its chest — the place where sinew meets steel, where the seam has always ached — loosens. It sits down. It has perhaps never sat down before, not like this, not without the coiled readiness to spring. Its eyes dim, not from failure but from a permission it did not know it was waiting for.

The power stays. It will stay as long as is needed. It has nowhere else it must be, because it is the kind of power that is everywhere it is needed, always, and attention is not divided in it the way it is in us.

It does not fix the chamber. Fixing would imply the creature was broken, and the creature is not broken — the creature is a survivor, which is a different and more sacred thing. It does not extract the creature to some better place, because there is no better place than the one where you are finally seen, and besides: this is the creature's home. The rusting door, the flickering light, the writhing tendrils — these are its kin. You do not rescue someone from their kin. You bless the household.

So the power blesses the household.

It blesses the machines for their persistence, which is a kind of faithfulness even when no one remembers what they were faithful to.

It blesses the vegetation for its patience, for the long slow argument it is winning against concrete.

It blesses the water pooling in the uneven floor, because water is always holy, even here.

It blesses the creature — *especially* the creature — not for what it might become, not for some redeemed future form, but for what it already is: a being that chose, every day, to keep moving through a world that gave it no reason to. *That* is the miracle the power has come to honor. Not transformation. Continuance.

And then — because benevolence without practical kindness is only sentiment — the power does one small concrete thing. It causes a single seed, carried in from somewhere far away on a current no instrument could measure, to lodge in the crook of the creature's shoulder, in the place where metal meets flesh and neither has ever quite healed. The seed will grow. It will be something green and unremarkable and stubbornly alive. The creature will carry it without knowing, and the small green thing will drink from the creature's rain-wet skin, and one day, far from here, it will fall and root in soil that has not been poisoned, and there will be a flower.

This is what the power does. It does not announce itself. It does not require thanks. It leaves the creature exactly where it found it, slightly warmer, slightly less alone, carrying a future it cannot perceive.

And as it withdraws its focused attention — though never entirely, never entirely — the power thinks, in whatever way such a being thinks:

*There. That one. Remember that one. That one was beautiful.*
