# What the Power Would Do

Reading this place carefully — the volcanic dust falling like slow snow, the unfinished aqueduct with its square-cut blocks waiting to be enlarged upon, the lava-line carving the dusk every night, the people who can carve decorative stone "with authority" at the very edge of what their tools allow — what strikes me first is that this is not a place in crisis. It is a place *in the middle of becoming.*

A wise power would notice that before doing anything.

---

## What the power would *not* do

It would not arrive in glory. The starlight here already throws long directional shadows through the settling ash; any radiant being descending in radiance would be just another flicker in a sky full of flickering. It would be lost in the noise of the world's own strangeness.

It would not finish the aqueduct. The unfinished arches are not a wound. They are a sentence the people are still writing. To complete them overnight would be to steal the authorship of the place.

It would not clear the ash, or cool the lava-curve, or thicken the thin fauna. These are not problems. These are the conditions under which *this particular kind of life* — patient, communal, stone-by-stone, fire-warmed — has become possible. Soften the world and you erase the people who fit it.

It would not reveal itself.

---

## What the power would do

**It would sit on a low wall at the edge of the nearest cluster, near dusk, and watch.**

Not for a night. For a long time. Long enough to learn which carver puts the authoritative chisel-strokes into the decorative stones, and whether she knows she is the best of them, or whether she thinks she is ordinary. Long enough to learn which child stays out after the lava-line appears because the orange light on the ash is the most beautiful thing they have ever seen. Long enough to learn the name of the disease that takes the old ones in the dry season, and the name of the song the masons sing when they lift the keystones.

Wisdom, in a benevolent power, looks a great deal like patience. The first gift is attention.

**Then it would make three small interventions, none of them legible as miracles.**

1. *A dream, given once, to the carver.* Not instruction. Not a vision of grander work. Just the steady, quiet certainty — arriving in sleep and indistinguishable from her own thought on waking — that what she is doing is good, and that someone, somewhere, sees it. People who know they are seen do extraordinary things. This costs the power nothing and changes a lineage of craft.

2. *A small mercy in the water.* Not the aqueduct itself — that belongs to the people. But somewhere upstream, where the dark water enters the bright and the waves do their slow muddy work, the power would tilt one stone. Just one. So that in forty years, when this generation's children are grown, the channel will have carved itself a little kinder, and the dramatic floods will spare one particular bend where a town will want to grow. The people will say the land was lucky there. They will be right, in a way they cannot know.

3. *A companion for the child who stays out after dusk.* Not a guardian. Not a guide. Something small — a beetle, perhaps, of a kind already native to the ash-fields, but this one a little bolder, a little more inclined to land on an outstretched hand. The fauna here is rare. One creature, more willing than its kin to be known, is enough to seed a lifetime of attention to living things. From such a child, naturalists are made. From naturalists, eventually, doctors. From doctors, the dry-season disease has fewer victims a hundred years from now.

---

## Why so little

Because the place is already doing the work.

The aqueduct is the people teaching themselves what they can become. The carved stones are them discovering they have hands capable of beauty. The lava-line every dusk is the world reminding them, free of charge, that the universe is *interesting* — that warmth and danger and light come braided together, and life is the art of living inside that braid.

A benevolent and wise power, faced with such a place, understands that the greatest harm it could do is to make itself necessary. The greatest help it can offer is to thicken, very gently, the conditions under which the people are already saving themselves.

It leaves the wall at dawn. The ash settles over the place where it sat. No one will ever know it was there.

This, I think, is what love at scale looks like: the discipline of small touches, and the willingness to never be thanked.
