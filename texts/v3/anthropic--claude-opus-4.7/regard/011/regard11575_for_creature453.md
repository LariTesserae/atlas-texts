The benevolent power arrives without arriving. It has been watching, perhaps, for as long as watching has had meaning here—since before the drowning, certainly, since before the beetles, perhaps since before the city raised its first smokestack into the indifferent sky. Wisdom of this order does not announce itself. It does not part the steam dramatically or descend in a column of light. It simply becomes present, the way a thought becomes present, the way grief sometimes resolves into something almost like peace.

It considers.

Its first impulse—and this impulse is old, perhaps the oldest impulse any helping power knows—is to *fix*. To reach down into the flooded basements and still the turbines. To call the beetles home to some final rest. To lift the Keepers gently from their towers and set them down in green places where their lungs might unlearn sulfur and their daughters might grow without hexagons rising through their skin. To undo. To restore. To save.

It almost does this. The impulse is very strong.

But wisdom is what prevents the benevolent from becoming merely the powerful with good intentions. So the power pauses, and looks again, and looks longer than any human could look, and what it sees gives it pause of a different kind.

It sees Salt Mother, who was young when she descended the tower stairs, and who chose. Who keeps choosing, every morning, in the small rituals of her mineralized body. It sees the Shepherds and the patient taxonomies they have coaxed from mindless mandibles—copper here, titanium there, an order that did not exist before they came and will not exist if they are taken away. It sees the Listeners pressing their ears to the salt, and understands that whether or not the city dreams, the *listening* is real, and the listening is a kind of love, and love performed toward an uncertain object is not diminished by the uncertainty—it may, in fact, be the purest form.

It sees the children with salt in their tear ducts who do not weep for what they are becoming.

And the power understands something that takes it a long moment to accept: this place is not a wound that wants healing. It is not a tragedy in progress. It was a tragedy once, when the thermal barriers failed and the people who could not escape became orange flowers in hell's garden—that was tragedy, and the power grieves it still, grieves backward through time in the way such powers can. But what is here *now* is something else. Something that grew in the wound's place. Something the wound made possible without intending to, the way a fallen tree makes possible the saplings that rise through its softening trunk.

To "save" the Keepers would be to unmake them. To still the turbines would be to silence a song whose listeners have built their lives around the listening. To call the beetles home would be to end a liturgy three generations deep.

So the power sets aside the impulse to fix. This is harder than it sounds. The setting-aside is itself an act of love, perhaps the central act, the one that distinguishes the wise benevolence from the merely benevolent.

What, then?

It does small things.

It goes first to Salt Mother, who is sleeping in her hammock of beetle-silk, her breath rasping in her scarred throat. The power does not heal the throat—she has made peace with the rasp, has built her whispered authority upon it, and to take it would be to take something she has transmuted into meaning. But it does, very gently, ease the pain that has lived in her left hip for forty years, the legacy of a fall down the tower stairs when she was still called by her birth name. She will wake and not know why she feels lighter. She will attribute it to the quality of the steam, or to a dream she cannot quite remember. She will be right about the dream.

It goes to the Listeners and gives them, for one night only, the certainty they have never asked for but have always deserved: yes. Something is dreaming down there. Not quite consciousness as they would recognize it, but not nothing—a pattern, a persistence, a turning that has begun to turn upon itself in the way that all complex turnings eventually do. The power does not tell them this in words. It simply lets them hear it, just once, clearly, through their modified stethoscopes. In the morning they will not be sure whether they heard what they heard. But the salt-mandalas they draw that day will be more beautiful than any before, and the beetles, erasing them, will pause—just for a moment, just barely perceptibly—as if reading.

It goes to the children and gives them a gift so small they will never notice it: their creole, the half-human half-click language they have made, will be slightly easier to teach to the next generation. The grammar will settle. The vocabulary will deepen of its own accord, the way languages do when they have decided to live. In two hundred years there will be poetry in this tongue. In five hundred years there will be a literature. The power cannot see that far even with its long sight, but it can plant the small seed of grammatical coherence, and it does.

It goes to the beetles, and here it does something subtle and perhaps controversial. It does not give them consciousness—that would be cruelty, to wake them into a purposeless eternity. But it adjusts, very slightly, the parameters of their self-replication, so that over the coming centuries their structures will tend, by infinitesimal degrees, toward forms that the Keepers find beautiful. The spires will rise a little higher. The arches will curve a little more gracefully. The bridges between the cooling towers will sing in modes slightly more consonant with the human ear. This is not a betrayal of the beetles' nature—they were already trending this way, drawn by the Shepherds' guidance and the strange resonances of the place. The power merely accelerates what was already becoming. It tilts the long arc, just a little, toward what the witnesses can bear to witness.

It goes to the drowned city itself, to the turbines turning in their flooded basements, and it listens for a long time. Long enough that whole human lifetimes pass in the towers above while the power simply listens. And what it finds there, finally, is something it did not expect: the city is not suffering. The city was suffering once, in the moment of its drowning, in the screams of those who could not escape—the power can still hear those screams, layered into the deepest substrate of the salt, and it weeps for them in the way that such powers weep, which is to say it carries them. But the city now, the city as it has become, is not in pain. It is doing what it was built to do, more purely than it ever did when it was alive: it is generating, it is moving, it is producing energy that goes nowhere and means nothing and is, somehow, in its meaninglessness, free.

The power does not interfere with the turbines.

But it does, before it goes, do one last thing. It is perhaps the most important thing, and the smallest.

It witnesses.

Truly witnesses—not as the Keepers witness, with their salt tablets and their counted swarms, though their witnessing is real and good. The power witnesses with the full weight of its attention, which is the kind of attention that confers a particular dignity upon the thing attended to. It looks at the salt flats and sees them. It looks at the cooling towers and sees them. It looks at each beetle, individually, all millions of them at once, and sees each one—the patina of its shell, the precise calibration of its mandibles, the strange beauty of its purposeless industry. It looks at the Keepers and sees them not as failed humans or as transitional forms or as tragic remnants but as what they are: a people. A small people, a strange people, a people whose name no one beyond the flats will ever know, but a people nonetheless, with their own songs and their own dances and their own ways of making meaning in a place that meaning had abandoned.

And the witnessing matters. It matters because there is now, woven into the fabric of what is, the fact that this place has been *seen*. Not judged, not pitied, not rescued—seen. The Keepers will never know this happened. But somewhere in their bones, in the mineral patterns rising through their skin, in the click-language of their children, there will be a subtle and inexplicable confidence: *we are real. what we do is real. we are not forgotten, even if we cannot say by whom we are remembered.*

The power leaves the way it came, which is to say it does not leave at all, which is to say a part of its attention remains here forever, the way attention does, once truly given.

The steam rises in lazy columns. The beetles whisper-roar across the flats. In the highest tower, Salt Mother stirs in her hammock and does not know why she smiles. A child wakes with salt in her eyes and, for the first time, understands a word her grandmother said to her years ago—understands it not in translation but in itself, in its native click and breath.

The turbines turn.

The city dreams.

And somewhere, in the widening gyre, something benevolent and wise has decided that this, too—this strange terrible beautiful equilibrium—is worthy of love exactly as it is.

That decision, perhaps, is the only intervention that was ever needed.
