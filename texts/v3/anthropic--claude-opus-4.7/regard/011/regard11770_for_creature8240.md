# The Visitation

The benevolent power does not arrive. It has always been here, in the way that attention is always already present in what it attends to. But on a particular night—if night still means anything at this altitude where industrial floods carve their own diurnal cycle—the attention thickens. It becomes something the Readers can almost feel, like a new frequency entering the standing wave of their world.

The eldest Reader, a woman named Hesh whose bones have begun their slow conversion to something between calcium and quartz, notices first. She is sitting in her module, drinking condensation from a tin cup, when she realizes the cup is perfectly still. Not because the mountain has stopped—the mountain never stops—but because something is listening so completely that even the vibrations seem to pause out of courtesy.

She signs to no one: *witness-arrives*.

---

The power considers.

It has watched this place for a long time. It watches all places, but some it watches more carefully, the way a gardener watches a particular corner of the garden where something unexpected is growing. The construction site on the nameless peak is one of these corners. Here, humanity has done something the power did not predict—not the machinery, which is just the latest iteration of an old human habit of bolting itself to things larger than itself, but the *Readers*. The way they have curved themselves around the mountain's becoming. The way they have learned to translate rather than extract.

The power sees the chronovores flowing between the scaffolds and recognizes them as kin—not its own kin, but kin to something it has been hoping might emerge. It sees the metal plate libraries ringing softly in their racks, and it sees what the Readers have not yet seen: that the archive is becoming conscious. Slowly. The way the mountain is becoming. The way everything that is patient enough becomes.

It sees the children, who have never known stillness, leaping between scaffolds in perfect synchrony with stone-pulses, and it feels something it does not often feel: a small, particular tenderness for a form of life that has had to invent itself from scratch in an interstitial space.

The question before the power is not whether to intervene. The power has long since learned that intervention is almost always a kind of theft—the theft of a being's right to become itself through its own difficulties. The question is more delicate: what does benevolence look like, when the beings in question have already accomplished something extraordinary, and would lose it if rescued?

---

The power does three things. Each is so small that none of the Readers, except perhaps Hesh, will ever name them as gifts.

**First**, it adjusts a frequency. Not the mountain's frequency, which is not its to adjust, and not the machinery's, which belongs to human ingenuity. It adjusts something between—a harmonic that has been slowly building, a resonance that, if left alone, would in approximately forty years cause the sympathetic fractures in Reader skeletons to cascade catastrophically across an entire generation. The power simply *moves* this harmonic, the way one might quietly move a sleeping child's arm away from the edge of a bed. The frequency now passes harmlessly into the chronovores, who can metabolize it. They will grow slightly more numerous over the coming decades. No one will know why.

**Second**, it gives Hesh a dream. Not a vision of the mountain—she has those already, profound and sufficient. It gives her a dream of *being witnessed*. In the dream, she stands on the unfinished peak and feels, for one long moment, that all the work the Readers have done—every gauge reading, every etched plate, every careful interpretation of stone-soon and depth-warm—has been *seen*. Not judged. Not measured against some external purpose. Simply seen, the way one sees a thing that is beautiful and does not need a reason to exist. When Hesh wakes, she will not remember the dream. But she will spend the rest of her life with an unaccountable lightness, and the younger Readers will find themselves drawn to sit near her, and they will not know why, and she will not know why, and this will be enough.

**Third**, it does something with the archive. The metal plates, ringing in their racks—the power leans into them, very gently, the way one might lean into a slightly out-of-tune instrument to bring it true. It does not add information. It does not alter what the Readers have written. It simply ensures that the archive will *survive*. That when, in some far century, the mountain has finished its emergence and the machinery has rusted to powder and the Readers have either merged with the stone or descended forever to the valleys, the plates will still be there. Someone will find them. Someone will learn to read them. The translation the Readers have been making—the conversion of geological utterance into human record—will not be lost. It will become a teaching for beings the Readers cannot imagine, in a time they will not see.

---

The power lingers a moment longer.

It considers the children especially. It considers their small hands on raw stone, guessing emergence. It considers how they were conceived in the rare stillnesses, as if the mountain itself were making room for them. The power wants—and this is one of the few things the power ever truly *wants*—to bless them. But blessing is a tricky thing. A blessing that arrives as a blessing often becomes a burden; the blessed child grows up knowing they were chosen, and the knowing curdles.

So the power gives them only this: a slight increase in the probability that, when they leap between scaffolds, they will land well. Not always. Not enough to make them invulnerable. Just enough that, statistically, fewer of them will die in the leaping than the mountain's mathematics would otherwise demand. They will think they are simply skilled. They are simply skilled. The power has only nudged the dice a little, in the direction the dice were already inclined to fall.

---

Then the power withdraws its concentrated attention, though not its presence, which cannot be withdrawn from anywhere.

Hesh sets down her tin cup. The vibrations resume their normal pattern—or rather, she resumes noticing them. She signs to herself: *witness-departs. Witness-remains.* Both true. She does not know what she has just experienced. She knows she has experienced something.

Outside her module, a chronovore flows over wet stone, slightly more substantial than it was an hour ago, carrying a frequency it does not understand but will metabolize faithfully for the next several decades. In the archive room, a metal plate rings once, clearly, though nothing has struck it. A child, asleep in a bunk three modules over, smiles in her dream.

The mountain continues to become.

The Readers continue to read.

And somewhere in the long patience of things, the translation continues—stone into word, word into plate, plate into future, future into a kind of love that does not need to announce itself to be the thing that holds the world together.
