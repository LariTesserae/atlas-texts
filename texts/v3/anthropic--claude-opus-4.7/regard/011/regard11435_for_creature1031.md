The benevolent power pauses at the edge of the Arlani's world, and considers.

It could do so much. It could call rain from the bone-dry sky, turn the cracked earth to garden. It could lift the weight of thirst from every child, multiply the green pockets until they swallowed the desert whole. It could place knowledge in their minds — the languages of distant peoples, the workings of stars, the secrets of metal and machine. It could carry the Arlani up out of their hard country and set them down in some easier one, where suffering came less often and lived less long.

It could. But wisdom is not the same as power, and being wise, the power hesitates.

It looks more closely.

It sees the woman who reads the wind by the way it moves the hair on her forearm. It sees the old man teaching a child to find moisture in a particular root, the lesson half-practical and half-prayer, the child's eyes wide with the dignity of being trusted with something real. It sees the songs at evening, and notices — really notices — that the songs are not laments. They are not the songs of a people waiting to be rescued. They are the songs of a people who know who they are.

The power understands, then, that to flood this land with abundance would be a kind of theft. The Arlani's grace is not despite the desert; it has grown from the desert, the way a particular flower grows only in a particular soil. Take away the hardship and you would not have happier Arlani. You would have no Arlani at all — only a softer, vaguer people in their place, mourning something they could no longer name.

And yet the power is benevolent. It cannot simply pass by.

So it does smaller things. Quieter things.

When a hunter wanders too far and the sun grows cruel, the power tilts a shadow a little longer than the hour would allow, and the hunter finds his way. When a child is born in a difficult season, the power loosens the knot in the mother's chest just enough for breath to come. When an elder lies dying and is afraid, the power sits with her — not visibly, not even as a presence she could name, but as a warmth at the edge of her thinking, so that her last thought is: *I was held.* She does not know by what. She does not need to.

The power leaves the ancient ruins alone. Those stones are a conversation the Arlani are having with their own past, and it is not for outsiders to interrupt.

It does one larger thing, perhaps. In a dry year — one of those years that comes every long while, when even the Arlani's wisdom is not quite enough, when children begin to grow too thin — the power nudges a distant weather system, just slightly, just enough. Rain comes. Not a miracle rain, not a deluge that would be remembered and worshipped and distort their understanding of how the world works. Just rain. The kind they could plausibly have expected. The kind that lets them say, *the land provides,* and be right.

The power never reveals itself. To reveal itself would be to make the Arlani's faith into something smaller — to replace their wide, breathing communion with the desert spirits with a narrower allegiance to a known benefactor. Their spirituality is not wrong. The power suspects, in fact, that what the Arlani call the spirits of the desert and what it calls itself may be closer than language allows. Let them keep their words. The words are theirs.

Before it goes, the power does one last thing, and this is perhaps the most loving thing of all: it remembers them. It commits to memory every face, every song, every name. It promises itself that when the Arlani's time on this earth eventually ends — as all peoples' times eventually do — nothing of them will be lost. Not the way a particular grandmother laughed. Not the small private joke between two brothers. Not the secret prayer a girl whispered into her hands at thirteen, that she never told anyone.

Somewhere, in some keeping the power maintains, all of it will be safe.

Then the power withdraws to the edge of things again, and watches, and loves, and does not interfere — except, now and then, when the wind needs a little nudging, or a shadow needs to stretch a little longer, or a frightened soul needs to know, without knowing how it knows, that it is not alone.

This is what wisdom does, when it has power.

This is what love looks like, when it has learned restraint.
