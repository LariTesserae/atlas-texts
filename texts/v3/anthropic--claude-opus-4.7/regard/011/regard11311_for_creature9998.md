The benevolent and wise power pauses at the gate.

It has, in its time, parted seas and quieted volcanoes. It has lifted villages out of famine with a gesture and turned the courses of rivers when rivers grew cruel. It has, on certain occasions it does not speak of often, leaned down into a sickroom and breathed and left only the smell of cedar behind. It knows what it can do.

So it pauses at the gate, and it watches for a long time before it does anything at all.

What it sees is this: a people who have made an instrument of themselves. Every gesture tuned. Every child a small competent verb. Every platform leaning into the next platform the way singers in a round lean into each other's breath. The basin is not a trap they are caught in. The basin is a covenant they are keeping. The cold and the drip and the slick green stone are not their punishment; they are the terms of an agreement these people have signed and re-signed, season upon season, with hands that know exactly what they are doing.

The power understands, watching, that it has arrived at one of the dangerous places. Not dangerous to itself — nothing here could injure it — but dangerous in the other direction. A place where a single careless kindness could undo a thousand years of careful kindness already in progress.

It considers, first, what it will not do.

It will not drain the marsh. It sees, almost at once, that the marsh is not the adversary but the partner, and that a people who lost their water would lose also the shape of their hands, the rhythm of their speech, the reason their children learn to leap before they learn to walk slowly. To dry this place would be to kill it more thoroughly than any flood.

It will not raise the platforms above all possible flooding. It sees that the negotiation with the water is the spine of the culture; remove the negotiation and the spine collapses. The people would still stand, for a while. But they would be standing the way a cut flower stands.

It will not multiply the fish, nor fatten the sedge roots, nor send down warmth from a sourceless sun. Abundance arriving from nowhere would teach these people, in one generation, to forget the shape of sufficiency, and sufficiency is the only shape they have ever truly known. The power has seen, elsewhere, what happens when the lean are made suddenly plump by miracle. It is not always a kindness. Often it is the beginning of a long forgetting.

It will not give them writing, nor metal, nor wheels, nor any of the gifts that other powers, less patient, have flung down into smaller places with the conviction of doing good. It sees that their wisdom is held in their hands and their bridges and the angle at which an old woman watches children at a fire. To put that wisdom into marks on bark would be to begin, very slowly, to let the hands forget. The power has watched this happen in other valleys, and grieved.

It will not even show itself. The people speak low, vanish into fog, value quiet; a visitation would be a violence against the very grain of their listening. They have not asked for a god. They have arranged their lives precisely so as not to need one.

So what, then. What does a benevolent and wise power do, faced with a people who have, by their own long labor, already arrived at a kind of grace?

It does three small things.

The first is this: it bends, very slightly, the weather of the coming century. Not enough that anyone would notice. Not a banishing of the floods — the floods are necessary, and the stories of the floods are necessary, and the rebuilding after the floods is the deepest schooling these people have. But the worst of the floods, the one that would have come in the fourth generation and broken the basin entirely past the possibility of repair — that one the power loosens, lets fall apart upstream, lets dissipate into a hundred lesser rains. The people will still have hard years. They will still tell their children of water that overcame. But the unrecoverable year, the year that would have ended them, simply does not arrive, and no one ever knows it was coming.

The second is this: it walks the perimeter of the palisade once, slowly, on the outside, where no one is looking. As it walks it does nothing visible. But somewhere in the soil along the boundary, in the deep mud where the reeds anchor, it leaves a kind of attention. Not a blessing exactly. More like a witness. So that for as long as this place exists, it will be seen. Not interfered with. Not steered. Only seen, the way a parent sees a grown child across a crowded room and is content to see and not to call out. If, someday, the people here begin to vanish — not from flood but from forgetting, from drift, from the slow erosions that have no name — the witness in the soil will know, and the power will come again, and decide again, with the same care, what to do or not do. Until then, the attention simply rests there, the way moss rests on stone where it will not threaten the join.

The third is the smallest and perhaps the strangest. The power looks at one child — a wiry girl, six or seven, crouched at the edge of a channel, watching a fish that has not yet realized it is being watched. The power looks at her for a long moment. And it gives her nothing. No gift, no knowledge, no destiny. It only notices her, completely, the precise green-brown of her eyes and the chapped place on her left knuckle and the particular way her breath is held. It notices her the way no one in her life will ever quite have time to notice her, because her life will be full of useful motion and shared tenderness but not of this kind of stillness. And the noticing, having happened, is enough. It does not change her. She catches the fish, or doesn't. She grows, or doesn't. But somewhere in the structure of what is real, she has been seen entirely once, and that fact is now permanent, and the universe is very slightly different for containing it. The power does this, sometimes, in places it loves. It is the closest thing it has to leaving a signature, and the signature is invisible even to itself afterward.

Then it goes.

It steps back through the narrow wedge in the palisade — and the wedge does not even register its passing, because it has taken care to pass the way the fog passes, the way a thought passes, the way the small competent people of this place themselves pass through their own gate: without announcement, without disturbance, without taking up more room than is strictly required.

Behind it, the drip falls from the awning. A stone tool knocks, somewhere. A child laughs and the laugh is quickly stifled. The water threads between the platforms according to its slow logic. Nothing has changed, which is to say: everything that needed to remain has remained.

The benevolent and wise power, walking away through the cold clean air, thinks — if such a being can be said to think in words — that the highest form of love it has ever learned is the discipline of leaving a good thing alone.

And it thinks, too, that it hopes someone, someday, will do the same for it.
