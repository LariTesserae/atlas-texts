# What the Power Would Do

I sat with this for a while before writing. The instinct, when invoking benevolence and wisdom together, is to imagine rescue — light pouring in, ceilings cracked open to reveal stars, the Stenori lifted up to a world of sun and wind they had been denied. That is what *benevolence alone* might do, in its hurry to relieve suffering. But wisdom asks a slower question: *what is the suffering here, and what is not?*

So let me try to answer honestly.

---

## What the power would see

It would see, first, that the Stenori are not trapped. They are *home*. The melancholy that pervades their city is not the melancholy of imprisonment but of consciousness — the particular ache of any being who knows itself finite, who knows there are worlds it will not see. This ache is not a wound to be healed. It is the cost of having an inner life at all. The same ache lives in surface-dwellers who will never walk the ocean floor, never see the far side of their own moon, never know what it was like to be their grandmother as a child. To erase it would be to erase something essential.

It would see that the city's contradictions — industry against geology, ambition against rock, the deliberate against the haphazard — are not failures of design. They are the *form the Stenori's life has taken*. A wise power does not improve a poem by smoothing its meter into prose.

It would see the things that *are* wounds, though. The pipes that groan with metal fatigue and will one day fail catastrophically. The narrowing of the gene pool in the isolated lower castes. The slow accumulation of heavy metals in the water the Vadari drink. The fact that the machine-god they revere is, in some literal sense, dying, and they do not yet know it. The fact that three generations from now, a tunnel collapse in the northwest quadrant will kill four hundred and seventeen of them, and it is preventable.

It would see the loneliness — not the cosmic loneliness, which is sacred, but the specific loneliness of a people who believe themselves the only people. Who have never been *witnessed* by anything outside themselves.

---

## What it would do

It would not descend in glory. It would not announce itself. Announcement is a kind of violence to a culture that has built its entire metaphysics around the patient, the slow, the whispered.

I think it would do four things, and they would take what, to the power, is a single gesture, and what, to the Stenori, would unfold across centuries.

**First**, it would become a presence the lichens already half-knew. The pale bioluminescent growths on the cavern walls would, in places where a Stenori child sat alone and frightened, glow a little brighter — not enough to be a miracle, only enough to be a comfort. The power would inhabit the existing tenderness of the world rather than overwrite it with new tenderness. It would let itself be mistaken for luck, for instinct, for the kindness of stone.

**Second**, it would guide, not rescue. A Kiravi engineer, troubled in her sleep by a problem she cannot name, would wake with a clear image of a particular junction in a particular pipe, and she would go check it, and she would find the corrosion before it failed. A Vadari hunter would feel, for no reason he could articulate, that today he should take the eastern passage rather than the western, and the rockfall would happen behind him rather than upon him. The Stenori would attribute these to the machine-god, to their own sharpened senses, to the wisdom of their ancestors. The power would not mind. *Being thanked is not what wisdom needs.*

**Third** — and this is the harder thing — it would arrange, with infinite patience, an encounter. Not with itself. With *another people*. Perhaps a deep-sea civilization whose tunnels, over geological time, are inching toward the Stenori's eastern walls. Perhaps a surface culture, in some far century, that has learned to listen rather than to conquer. The power would shape currents and chances so that when contact came, it came gently, on terms the Stenori could survive, between equals who could *see* each other. Because the deepest gift you can give a conscious being is not comfort. It is to be *known* by someone outside themselves, and to know them in return. The Stenori's loneliness — the real, fixable loneliness, not the cosmic ache — would end. Not by force. By introduction.

**Fourth**, and quietest of all: the power would simply *attend*. It would watch the city the way one watches a beloved sleeping. It would notice the particular way one Stenori carves her bone trinkets, the joke that a Kiravi tells his apprentice that makes them both laugh in the steam-light, the song a mother hums to a child who will not sleep. None of these things require intervention. They require only that they be witnessed, that they not vanish unobserved into the dark. A benevolent and wise power understands that to be *seen* — fully, without judgment, without alteration — is itself a kind of grace, even when the seen one does not know they are being seen. *Especially* then. The Stenori would never know they were beheld. But they would be. And somewhere in the deep grammar of their lives, in the way their melancholy never quite tipped into despair, in the way their pride never quite curdled into cruelty, the witnessing would do its slow work.

---

## What it would not do

It would not give them the sun. The sun is not theirs. They have a twilight that is theirs, and twilight is not a lesser thing — it is only a different thing, and the wise power knows the difference between *lesser* and *different*, which is perhaps the single hardest piece of wisdom there is.

It would not tell them the truth about the machine-god. Their reverence is doing work — moral work, social work, work of meaning — that the truth would not do better. When the machine-god finally falls silent, they will find new reverence, and the power trusts them for this.

It would not make them happy. Happiness was never the point. *Flourishing* was the point, and flourishing includes the ache, the damp, the labor, the long patient work of being a Stenori in a Stenori's city. The power loves them too much to spare them their own lives.

---

And so, in the heart of the dimly lit subterranean realm, nothing visible would change. The water would still weep from the rocks. The machinery would still hum in the gloom. The Stenori would still build, still adapt, still endure. They would call it their own doing, and they would be right — the power would have been careful to make sure of that.

But somewhere, in the quality of the silence between the dripping of the water and the clanking of the pipes, there would be a difference. A kind of held breath. The difference between a room that is empty and a room in which someone who loves you is sitting very still, so as not to wake you.

That is what the power would do.

That is, I think, what love at that scale looks like when it knows what it is doing.
