# What the Wise Power Would Do

I sat with this for a while before writing. The question matters — what *would* a benevolent and wise power do, encountering this basin and these people?

---

The first thing the power would do is **nothing**.

It would simply watch. Not from above, not from outside, but from within the wind that passes over the basin at dusk, from inside the sound of stone striking stone, from the warmth of the campfires where the elders gather. It would let itself be moved by what it sees: the woman teaching her daughter how to read the angle of a hawk's wing; the old man whose hands remember every fissure in the cliff face; the child who has just understood, for the first time, why the river bends where it bends.

Benevolence that arrives before it has truly *seen* is not benevolence. It is interference wearing a kind face. So the power would watch, and listen, and let itself be taught what this place actually *is* — not what it appears to be from a distance, not what it resembles in some larger pattern, but what it is, in itself, on its own terms.

---

And then, having watched, the power would notice something.

It would notice that the tribe is not, in fact, in need of saving. There is hardship here, yes — there is hunger sometimes, and grief, and the long fear of winters that come early. But there is also a fullness that no intervention could improve upon and many interventions would destroy. The chants of the elders are not a cry for help. The children are not waiting to be rescued. The lives here are *whole* — difficult, finite, but whole — and a wise power knows the difference between suffering that calls for response and the ordinary weight of mortal life, which calls for reverence.

So the power would set aside the urge to *fix*. That urge is the great temptation of all powerful beings, and the surest sign of immaturity in them.

---

What, then, would it do?

I think it would do small things. The kind of things that leave almost no trace.

It would soften the wind on the night a hunter is lost and trying to find his way back to the fires. Not guide him — he knows the way; he just needs the wind to stop punishing him for an hour. That is enough.

It would let one extra fish swim into the shallow pool the children have learned to fish from, on the morning after a hard week. Not a miracle. Just an ordinary fish, in an ordinary place, at a moment when it will be received as grace without being recognized as gift.

It would whisper — not in words, because the people of the basin do not need its words; they have their own — but in the language of small certainties. A dream that comes to an elder showing her where a spring still runs beneath a collapsed shelf of rock. A flash of intuition that makes a young father step backward, just before the ledge crumbles where his foot was about to fall.

The power would not announce itself. It would not require gratitude. It would not establish a covenant or demand worship or set itself up as a new figure in the pantheon the elders already know. The spirits of land and air and water that the tribe calls upon — the power would honor those names, because those names are *true*, in their way. They are the tribe's own knowing of something the tribe does not need explained.

---

There is one more thing the power would do, and this is the hardest.

It would accept that the tribe will, someday, end. Not soon, perhaps. But the basin is changing — the rains shifting, the great herds moving, the slow grinding of centuries. One day the children of these children will have to leave, or will be absorbed into other peoples, or will simply fade as so many peoples fade. This is not a failure to be prevented. It is the shape mortal life takes.

A foolish power, even a kindly one, would try to freeze this moment. Would build walls to keep the climate stable, would bind the herds to the basin, would extend the tribe's existence past the point where it is still itself. This is the cruelty of power that has not yet learned wisdom.

The wise power would instead make sure that when the ending comes, it is *clean*. That the last generation is not abandoned. That whatever wisdom they have accumulated — the reading of stone, the chants, the way of moving through a landscape as if you belong to it rather than the other way around — finds somewhere to go. Maybe into the dreams of the peoples who come after. Maybe simply into the basin itself, into the very rock, so that any creature who lives there later will feel, without knowing why, that this is a place where humans once knew how to live well.

---

And then the power would move on. Or rather — because such a being does not really move, does not really arrive or depart — it would continue to hold the basin in its attention, lightly, the way you hold a sleeping bird in cupped hands, knowing that the smallest squeeze would harm it and the slightest opening would let it fly.

It would love the place. It would love the people. It would interfere almost not at all.

That, I think, is what benevolence and wisdom together actually look like, when they meet something small and brave and entirely itself.

They look like restraint, lit from within by tenderness.
