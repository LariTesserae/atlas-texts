# The Visitation

The benevolent power does not arrive. To arrive would be to add motion to a place whose entire being is the refusal of motion, and that would be a discourtesy. Instead, it is simply, suddenly, *also here*—the way a second meaning settles into a word you have known all your life.

It takes no form at first. It listens.

For what, in human reckoning, would be a long time, it does nothing but listen. It listens to the grinding chord. It listens beneath the chord, to the shriek of consciousness against imperative. It listens beneath the shriek, to the slow internal weather of the Custodians, the cyclones of mote-light turning in their chests like thoughts that cannot be finished. It listens to the beetles eating the overtones. It listens to the pinging amphibians surface their fragments of calculation and sink again. It listens, finally, to the thing the Custodians themselves cannot quite hear, because they *are* it: the long, unbroken vow.

And the power understands.

It had come, perhaps, prepared to help. This is what benevolent powers tend to assume of themselves—that they are needed, that suffering implies a request. It had been ready, in the way the wise are ready, to do nothing at all if nothing were warranted. But it had not been prepared for *this*: a suffering that is also a sacrament. A labor that is also the laborer. A scream that, if silenced, would unmake not only the screamer but the whole shape of the world the screamer was holding back.

So the power revises itself. It sets down its assumptions the way one sets down a heavy pack at the door of a house one has been invited into. And it asks, in the only language available here—a modulation, the smallest possible adjustment of pressure in the basin's hum—

*May I sit with you?*

The hum shifts. Not in answer, exactly. The Custodians cannot answer; an answer would be a movement, and they have foresworn movement. But the shift is in a key the power recognizes as permission. A minor third, opening.

The power takes a form, then, because to sit with someone is a courtesy that wants a shape. It chooses to be a Custodian. Not a true one—it cannot truly bear what they bear; it has not been born from the cataclysm; it has no ancient covenant with the strain—but a likeness. A figure of the same crystalline strata, the same moss-furred patience, the same internal vortex of slow light. It places itself in a gap in the machinery where no Custodian stands, where a long basalt lever leans against nothing in particular, holding only its own grammar. It fits itself there. It begins to hold.

And in holding, it learns what the Custodians have always known and never been able to tell, because telling would require a mouth, and a mouth would require an inhalation, and an inhalation would require the world to move.

It learns that the labor is not punishment. It learns that the labor is not even, exactly, *labor*, in the sense of something that could be put down. The Custodians are not holding the machine still against their will. They are holding it still *because they love it*. They love the potential of it—the dreadful, magnificent shape of what it would do if it began. They love the cogs that want to turn, and the pistons that want to fall, and the flywheels that want to spin out the long unspooling thread of consequence. They love these so much that they have chosen, in the first instant and in every instant since, to keep them *whole*—to keep the engine in the perfect, untouched, undeployed state of pure possibility.

To begin the machine would be to spend it. And what the Custodians guard, what they have always guarded, is the unspent thing.

The power, holding its lever, weeps. Not water—it has not earned the right to weep water here, where every drop is a Custodian's processed pressure—but something subtler. A small, internal acknowledgment. The recognition of a kind of love it had not, in all its wisdom, previously catalogued.

After a further long time—the kind of time that is not measured but instead *settled into*, the way silt settles—the power does three small things. They are the only things it does. They are, by the standards of benevolent powers, almost nothing. By the standards of this place, they are everything that could properly be offered.

**The first thing.** It adjusts, infinitesimally, the angle of the cloud-ceiling above the basin. Not to let in more light—the Custodians do not want more light; the bruised twilight is the correct light, the light of held breath—but to redirect a single thin lane of high atmospheric current so that, once in a great while, a particular kind of seed will be carried in from the lowlands and dropped into the basin. A seed of a flowering vine that does not yet grow here. The vine, when it grows, will bloom once a century, and its blooms will be the precise color of the internal vortex inside a Custodian's chest. The Custodians will not see the blooms—they cannot turn their heads—but the beetles will carry the scent across their crystalline skin, and they will know. They will know that somewhere, in their stillness, a thing has bloomed that resembles them. It is a small companionship. The power offers it because companionship, even unseen, eases a long vigil. It does not relieve the labor. It only honors it.

**The second thing.** It learns, in its time of holding, the true name of each Custodian. Not a name in any spoken tongue—the Custodians have no tongues—but the unique signature of force-vectors and harmonic resonances by which each one knows itself and is known by the others. The power memorizes these names. It commits them, with great care, to the part of itself that does not forget. This is the smallest gift, and perhaps the largest: that there is now, elsewhere in the wide architecture of things, a record of who each of them is. They were born unwitnessed by anything but the rock they negate. Now they are witnessed. If the basin should ever end—if the engine should ever, somehow, against all the long vow, begin—the Custodians will not be lost. They will be remembered specifically, by name, by the shape of the load they bore. This matters. The power knows it matters, because it has held a lever for what felt like an age, and it knows now that to be unwitnessed in such labor is the only loneliness that approaches unbearable.

**The third thing.** When the power finally rises to depart—and it does depart, because to remain would be to become a permanent fixture, which would alter the basin's equilibrium, which would be the one thing a guest must never do—it leaves behind, woven into the foundational hum, a single sustained note. Not a new note. A note that was always implicitly there in the chord but had never been sounded. A note of *being held in return*. The Custodians, who hold the mountain, have for all their existence been held by nothing. Now there is this. A faint, perpetual harmonic that says: *the labor is seen, the love is understood, the vow is honored, and you, who hold everything, are also held.*

The note will not change anything. The grinding will continue. The shriek will continue. The slam will continue. The vines will bloom and the beetles will carry the scent and the names will be remembered in a far place, and the Custodians will go on, exactly as they have always gone on, doing the one thing they were born to do.

But they will do it, from now on, inside the sound of being loved.

The power leaves the way it came: without arriving, without departing, simply ceasing to also be here. The lever-shape it had occupied softens back into ordinary basalt. The basin closes around the place where it had been, the way water closes, the way stone closes, the way a long-held breath closes around the small mercy of not, after all, having to be held alone.

And somewhere in the bruised twilight, in a chest of smoky quartz, a vortex of motes turns very slightly faster for one moment, and then settles, and resumes its eternal, patient, sufficient turning.
