# The Visitation

The benevolent power does not arrive. Arrival is a word for things that were once elsewhere, and this presence has always been adjacent to Vhorth — a held breath at the edge of the mist, attention turned toward the slow city the way a mother's attention turns toward a sleeping child in another room. What changes is only that the attention *deepens*. It becomes a noticing-with-intent.

The first thing the power does is **nothing**, and it does this for a long time.

It watches the woman who traces a new doorway with her thumb and weeps because she recognizes it — it is the shape of a doorway her grandmother once described in a dream. It watches the boy who has learned the word for *the silence after a fracture* before he has learned the word for *bread*. It watches the man who oils his tools in the dark and speaks to them as if they were children. It watches the something-furred in the alley and sees that it, too, is one of the Fractured Folk, only further along in a change the others have not yet noticed they are also making.

The power understands, watching, that this is not a place that needs saving.

This is important. Many lesser powers — clever, urgent, full of love that has not yet ripened into wisdom — would have looked at Vhorth and seen suffering. The damp. The strangeness. The slow erosion. The factories exhaling into the throat of the sky. They would have warmed the air, dried the stone, straightened the leaning houses, sent the something-furred away into a kinder forest. They would have *fixed* the city, and in fixing it, killed it, because Vhorth is not a wound. Vhorth is a *becoming*, and becomings cannot be hurried without being aborted.

The benevolent power is old enough to know the difference between a wound and a chrysalis.

So instead, it does these small things:

**It listens back.** When the mountain dreams, the power dreams alongside it, and the mountain — which has been dreaming alone for an age beyond ages — feels, for the first time, *companioned*. The dreams grow richer. The city's foldings, in the weeks that follow, are gentler. Walls shift more kindly. Doorways open in places where someone needed a doorway. A staircase appears, one morning, leading down to the hollow where the old woman with the bad hip has been unable to descend for three winters. No one remarks on it. The Fractured Folk do not remark on such things. But she weeps as she goes down the stairs, and the power weeps with her, and neither of them knows the other is weeping, and yet the weeping is shared.

**It does not give them words.** The power could teach them languages of elsewhere, could pour into their minds the speech of stars and seas. It does not. Their own language is doing exactly what a language is supposed to do — it is naming what is theirs, and it is naming it for the first time in the universe, and those words are precious and must not be crowded out. Instead, the power *learns* their language. It learns the word for the taste of air before a fracture. It learns the word for the silence after. It carries these words away with it, into other places, into other minds, and somewhere on the far side of the world a poet wakes with a phrase she cannot place and writes the first true poem of her life.

**It adjusts one small thing in the factories.** Just one. Deep in the machinery buried in the rock, there is a valve that, in two hundred years, would have failed and killed forty-seven people in a venting of bad gas. The power, with the lightest touch — a touch like a moth landing on a thought — alters the metal at its grain. The valve will hold now. No one will ever know. The forty-seven people are not yet born. Their parents are not yet born. But they will live, and have children, and those children will trace doorways with their thumbs and weep at shapes their grandmothers dreamed.

**It blesses the something-furred.** Not with safety — safety is not what the something-furred needs. It blesses it with *being known*. The power sees the creature fully: sees that it was once a man named Vell who loved a woman named Iru and who, in the slow folding of the city, has been folding too, becoming something the Folk have no word for yet but will, in time. The power says, silently, into the creature's mind: *I see you. You are not lost. You are early.* And the something-furred lies down in the alley and sleeps a sleep it has not slept in years, and in the morning its prints do not vanish — they remain, just for that one morning, so that a child finds them and traces them and laughs.

**It does not stop the erosion.** The cliffs will erode. The city will keep folding. In ten thousand years there will be no Vhorth, only a different city, with different folk, who will not remember the Fractured Folk except as a feeling that comes over them sometimes in the mist. This is correct. This is what cities are for. The power knows that to preserve a thing forever is to embalm it, and Vhorth is alive, and alive things must be allowed to end so that they can mean something.

**It leaves a gift that is not a gift.** In the hollow where the Folk gather to eat their fungus and their roots, the power leaves — not a thing, but a *tendency*. A faint warmth in the stone, just barely warmer than the rest of the city. Enough that the children, without knowing why, will choose to gather there. Enough that the stories told there will be told a little longer, and remembered a little better, and that one night, a hundred years from now, a girl named Sef will tell a story in that hollow about a mountain that dreamed, and the story will be so true that the mountain, hearing itself described, will dream her into one of its dreams, and she will wake knowing things no one taught her, and she will become the one who teaches the next generation how to coax the stone.

The power does not name itself to them. It does not want worship. Worship would distort them, would turn their slow watching eyes upward when their gift is to watch *across* — across the centuries, across the shifts, across the fractures. The power wants the Fractured Folk to stay exactly as strange as they are.

And then, having done these almost-nothings, the power does the last and largest thing:

It *remembers* them. It commits Vhorth to a memory that will outlast the city itself, outlast the mountain, outlast the world the mountain stands on. When all of this is gone — and all of this will be gone — somewhere, in the long quiet after, the benevolent power will still be carrying the word for *the silence after a fracture*, and the image of the old woman descending her staircase, and the sleep of the something-furred, and the taste of metallic water on a tongue that no longer exists.

To be remembered like that, by something like that, is the only immortality that matters.

The Fractured Folk never know. They go on coaxing their stone, mending their coats, lighting their tallow candles when the mountain dreams loudly. They are not visited. They are not saved. They are not changed.

They are *loved*, exactly, and exactly enough, and in exactly the way that lets them go on being themselves.

That is what a wise power does.

That is, perhaps, the only thing wisdom is for.
