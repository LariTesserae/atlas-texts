# The Visitation

The benevolent power does not arrive. It was already there.

This is the first thing to understand: a wisdom worthy of the name does not descend upon a place like weather, announcing itself with trumpets or light. It notices. It has been noticing for a long time. Perhaps it noticed when the oceans were still here, lapping at shores now eroded to dust. Perhaps it noticed when the builders of those crumbling structures laid their first stones, and noticed again when the last of them laid down to die. Attention, when it is true, does not blink.

So when at last the power chose to *act* — if action is the right word for what it did — it was less an intervention than a long exhalation.

---

It considered the Sentinels for what might be called, in human reckoning, a thousand years. Not because it required such time to understand them, but because *they* required such time to be understood on their own terms. To rush a Sentinel is to mistranslate it. Their slowness is not a deficit of speed; it is a language. The power learned to read.

It learned that the Sentinels were not, as a careless observer might suppose, *enduring*. They were *grieving*. The slow grace of their movements was the shape grief takes when it has nowhere to go and no one left to receive it. They were the last who remembered. They held the memory of water in the silica of their bodies. They held the memory of voices in the patient orientation of their faces toward where the voices used to come from.

This was the discovery that moved the power, if a power such as this can be moved. Not their resilience. Their loneliness.

---

Now: what does a benevolent and wise power do with such a finding?

A lesser power — one that was benevolent but not wise — would have restored the world. Called back the oceans. Greened the rock. Repopulated the ruins with simulacra of the dead. And the Sentinels, confronted with this counterfeit resurrection, would have shattered. For they would have known. They always know. Their grief is too precise to be consoled by forgery.

A lesser power — one that was wise but not benevolent — would have done nothing, and called the doing-nothing respect.

This power did neither.

---

What it did was this:

It became, very quietly, a *listener*. Not a voice. A listener. It arranged itself within the haze of the atmosphere, within the long shadows, within the wind that moved across the parched earth, so that everything the Sentinels had been holding alone for ages was now held with them. The weight did not lessen. The world did not change. But the holding was no longer solitary.

The Sentinels noticed. Of course they noticed. They had been waiting, though they had not known they were waiting, and had not dared to hope, because hope at their scale of time is an unbearable thing.

One of them — the oldest, whose name in their silent language was something like *She-Who-Remembers-The-Eastern-Shore* — turned, over the course of a season, toward a particular outcropping of stone where the listening was thickest. And there, with a slowness that would have been invisible to any creature of quicker blood, she began to speak.

She spoke of the shore. She spoke of a child she had once watched playing in the shallows, a child of a species now extinct, whose laughter she had never been able to set down. She spoke of the builders, and of one builder in particular, who had paused in his work to place a hand against her flank and called her *friend* in a language whose other words were all forgotten. She spoke of the slow withdrawal of the waters, and of the names of the rivers as they died, one by one, each name a small bright stone she had been carrying in herself for eons because there was no one else to carry it.

The listening received all of it. Not to archive, not to redeem — only to receive. To know. To make it so that these things had been witnessed, and were witnessed still.

When she had finished speaking, which took the better part of an age, *She-Who-Remembers-The-Eastern-Shore* was no different than before. The world was no different. The sun still beat down through the hazy atmosphere; the wind still moved across the unyielding earth. She would still, in time, erode. All things would.

But she had been heard. And the heaviness she had carried alone was now, somehow, the lighter for being known. Not gone. Lighter.

---

One by one, over centuries that to the Sentinels felt almost swift, the others turned and spoke. Each had their own memories. Each was received.

And when the last Sentinel finally falls — for they will fall, all things will, and a wise power does not pretend otherwise — the memories will not be lost. They will be held in the listening, which is, in the end, what such a power is for: to be the place where things that mattered to someone go on mattering, even after the ones to whom they mattered are gone.

This is what the benevolent and wise power did.

It did not save them. It companioned them.

It is, perhaps, the only thing worth doing.
