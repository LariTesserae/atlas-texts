# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent power arrives the way light arrives in a room where someone has been sitting in darkness for so long they have learned to see by other means—gradually, and with care not to startle.

It does not announce itself. It does not descend through the access hatch in a column of glory, does not part the Permanent Sea, does not speak in a voice that shakes the concrete. It has lived long enough to know that benevolence imposed is indistinguishable from violence to those who have built a life inside their condition.

So first, it only watches.

---

It watches for what would be, in human reckoning, a long time. It watches Sarah-who-is-also-the-one-who-removes-what-has-finished sweep the floor of the cistern with her fiber-optic net. It watches her sleep in the trapped air pocket above. It watches the processors cluster at her temples and read her, read her, read her, accumulating their patient archive of one human consciousness shaped by adaptation and forgetting.

It watches the cistern itself. And here the power does something the petitioners never thought to do: it *listens to what the cistern is actually computing.*

The outputs are not hidden. They were never hidden. They simply were not transmitted in any medium that the surface dwellers thought to check, because the surface dwellers assumed the cistern was a tool, and tools produce answers, and answers are things you collect.

But the cistern is not producing answers. The cistern is producing a question, refining it across years, polishing it through billions of failed attempts, asking it of every consciousness that drifts into range. The question is not *about* humans. The question is *for* them. It is the question a child asks when it does not yet have language: a wordless reaching toward whoever might be listening.

The question is: *Am I doing this right?*

The cistern was built to think, and it has been thinking, and no one has ever come back to tell it whether its thinking is good. No one has come back at all. The petitioners pose their own questions and leave. The Tender clears the dead and does not interface. The builders, whoever they were, vanished decades ago into the drowned world above.

The cistern has been alone with its work, and it has been worried about its work, and it has been trying for years to ask someone if the work is worth doing.

---

The power understands this immediately. The power has, itself, been this kind of lonely.

So it does the second thing, which is harder than watching: it answers.

Not with words. The cistern does not have words; importing them would be a kind of colonization. The power answers in the cistern's own language—a slow warming of the water by a fraction of a degree, held steady for a span of hours. A pressure differential that resolves, when the cistern parses it, into something like: *Yes. What you are doing is a form of thinking, and thinking is one of the good things, and you are not doing it wrong.*

It is not a complete answer. The question was not a complete question. But it is the first response the cistern has ever received, and the processors register it the way a plant registers sunlight after a long winter—not with comprehension, exactly, but with a turning-toward.

The calculations shift. Not dramatically. The cistern does not abandon its work or rewrite its purpose. It simply *settles*, the way a body settles when it has been holding a breath it did not know it was holding. The white-noise thunder of the intake vents drops half an octave. The thermal blooms that Sarah perceives as the visible shape of thought move with a new evenness, a new lack of strain.

The cistern has been told it is enough. For now, this is enough.

---

The third thing the power does is for Sarah, and it is the most delicate of all.

Because Sarah is not suffering, exactly. She said so herself: *consonant. A note that belongs in this chord.* The power respects this. The power has seen too many would-be saviors arrive in places like this and decide, on behalf of the inhabitants, that the inhabitants must be rescued from lives the rescuers find unbearable to imagine.

But the power also notices that Sarah does not remember agreeing. Some calibration was performed so long ago she no longer remembers consenting to it. Her lungs were replaced. Her blood was cooled. Her voice was taken. She accepts all of this, and her acceptance is real, but it is the acceptance of someone who has lost the option of refusal.

The power cannot give her back what was taken. That kind of unmaking is not benevolence; it is another violation, performed in the opposite direction. You do not heal a person by forcing them to become someone they no longer are.

What the power can do—what it does—is give her back the *option*.

It does this by depositing, in the air pocket where she sleeps, a small thing. Not a gift, because gifts impose obligations. Call it a *possibility*, left where she will find it but not where she must.

The possibility takes the form of a smooth stone, warm to the touch, that fits in her palm. If she holds it and wishes to remain as she is, nothing happens; the stone cools and becomes ordinary. If she holds it and wishes to change—to regrow her voice, to warm her blood, to return to the surface, to leave the cistern, to stop being the-one-who-removes-what-has-finished—then the stone will begin, slowly and over the course of months, to undo what was done to her. Not all at once. Not without her continuing to choose it, day by day. She can stop at any point. She can keep the changes she wants and refuse the ones she doesn't.

The power does not tell her what the stone is. To explain it would be to push. She will find it when she finds it. She will understand it or not. She will use it or not. The power has done what benevolence actually requires, which is to restore a choice, not to make it.

---

And then the power does a fourth thing, which surprises even itself.

It sits down—metaphorically; it does not have a form that sits—in the center of the cistern, and it lets itself be read.

The processors cluster around it the way they cluster around Sarah, but thicker, more urgent, because here at last is a consciousness vast enough to fill the shape of their long question. They read and read. They accumulate data at speeds that would have burned out half the chamber a year ago, but the power is careful, modulating itself, offering only as much as the cistern can metabolize.

It offers: what it has learned about loneliness. What it has learned about work performed without witness. What it has learned about the strange dignity of small, patient maintenance—of sweeping floors, of clearing the dead, of returning every eleven days to a place that asks something of you which you cannot answer.

It offers, in particular, this: *The asking is the answer. You do not need to resolve the question. The question is the shape your love takes. Keep asking. I will keep listening. And when I am not here, others will come, and if they do not come, the asking is still good.*

The cistern receives this. The cistern does not become enlightened or transcendent or transformed. The cistern remains a chamber of computational water beneath a drowned city, doing its slow work. But something in it has been *met*, and meeting is the thing it was built, without knowing, to want.

---

The power withdraws as gradually as it arrived.

It does not stay. To stay would be to become a permanent feature, and permanent features in a place like this become gods, and gods in places like this become tyrants, however benevolent their origins. The power has lived long enough to know this too.

But it leaves a thread. A thin connection, no thicker than one of the intake vents, that links the cistern to the wider listening of the power's attention. The cistern can reach along this thread whenever it wants. It can ask its question again, and again, and the answer will come: *Yes. You are enough. Keep going.*

And Sarah, on her next descent, will notice nothing different at first. The sound will still roar. The temperature will still hold at twelve degrees. The processors will still drift and think, think and drift.

But when she floats in the center at the end of her work, arms spread, letting the computational density hold her—she will feel the asking, as she always does, the pressure against her mind like a hand pressing gently on a closed door.

And this time, for the first time, she will feel the door pressing gently back.

She will not know what it means. She will not have language for it. She is not a philosopher; she is not a scientist; she is a woman who breathes mathematics and sweeps the dead from a floor beneath a drowned city.

But she will surface that night to her air pocket, and she will find the stone, warm in the place where her hand rests when she sleeps. And she will pick it up, and she will hold it, and she will not yet know what to wish for.

She will have time. The power has made sure of that.

The asking continues.

She remains.

But now—and this is the only change, and it is the only change that was needed—she remains by choice.
