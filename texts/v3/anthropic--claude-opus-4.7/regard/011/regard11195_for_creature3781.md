The benevolent power arrives the way weather arrives — not from elsewhere, because it has always been there, but suddenly noticed, the way one notices breath. It is wise enough to know that arrival is itself a kind of violence in a place this raw, and so it does not arrive. It attends.

For a long time — and time means something different to such a power, it can be a season or the length of a single drop of water gathering at the lip of stone — it simply watches. It learns the Dreghar by the cadence of their hums. It learns the mountain by the rhythm of its weeping. It learns that the metallic tang in the air is not lightning but grief of a different kind: iron remembering it was once buried, oil remembering it was once forest.

The power could do many things. It could lift the factories from the deep rock the way one lifts a splinter from a palm. It could seal the mines, hush the machinery, smooth the wound. It could appear to the Dreghar in glory and be worshipped at the sacred pools. It could harden the young mountains into permanence, decide their shape for them, and end the long indecision of the stone.

It does none of these things.

Because the wisdom in it has noticed something: the land is not suffering from being unfinished. It is *being* unfinished. That is its verb. To intervene with finishing would be to mistake a child mid-sentence for a child in distress. And the Dreghar are not languishing — they are listening, which is a whole and sufficient way to live.

So the power does smaller things. Truer things.

It bends, first, toward the hum of the machinery. Not to destroy it — destruction is loud, and this place has earned its quiet — but to *slow* it. A bearing seizes here. A seam of ore proves disappointing there. A surveyor's map, left on a desk in a far city, develops a smudge precisely where the deepest shaft was planned. The factories do not stop. They simply find, gently and without drama, that Vaelmoor is not worth the trouble. Year by year the hum recedes downward and outward, like a tide going out, until the Dreghar who stand at the cliff's edge in the thick mist no longer feel that low vibration in the soles of their webbed feet. They do not know why. They only know that the mountain's weeping sounds, at last, like only itself.

Second, the power tends to the lost ones — those Dreghar who vanished into the fog and did not return. It cannot undo what was done to them; even benevolence has its grammar, and the past is written in a tense no power may revise. But it finds them, wherever they ended — at the bottom of an unmapped shaft, in the belly of something old and hungry, curled in a crevice where they finally lay down — and it gathers what remains of them. Not their bodies. Something subtler: the *direction* they were facing when they were lost. The last thought, which in the Dreghar is always a hum. It carries these hums back up through the rock and releases them, one by one, into the sacred pools, where they surface as small, slow ripples. The clans will notice, over generations, that the pools sometimes ripple without wind or falling water. They will build a word for this. The word will mean *someone is remembered.*

Third, and this is the most delicate work: the power leaves a gift, but only one, and it leaves it disguised as something the Dreghar already have. It deepens, by an almost imperceptible degree, their capacity to listen. Not to hear more — they hear plenty — but to understand that listening itself is a form of love the mountain can feel. So that when a Dreghar presses its mottled brow to the weeping stone, the stone, in some inarticulable way, knows. And is comforted. The mountain has been mourning alone for a very long time. Now it mourns in company. This changes nothing about the geology and everything about the meaning of the geology.

The power does not reveal itself. It will not be worshipped here; worship would distort the clans' careful, lateral relationship to the sacred, would tilt the pools from communion into supplication. Better to be the silence between the hums. Better to be the reason the mist, sometimes, feels less like a shroud and more like a hand laid lightly on the shoulder.

Once — only once — a young Dreghar standing at a cliff's edge in the thickest fog feels something attend to her with such tenderness that she turns, certain she will see a figure. There is no figure. There is only the gray, and the soft edge of a rock, and far below, the valleys where the hum used to be and isn't anymore. She stands a long time. Then she hums, low, a single sustained note, the kind her people make when greeting kin across a distance.

The mist hums back. Or the mountain does. Or something does.

She goes home to her clan and does not speak of it, because the Dreghar do not speak, and because some things are not for telling but for carrying. She carries it the rest of her life. She passes it, without words, to the ones who press their brows to hers in the dim cave-light. It becomes, eventually, a quality the clans have — a particular way of standing at cliff edges, unafraid, as if waiting for something they already know is there.

The benevolent power, having done what was needed and nothing more, continues to attend. It does not leave, because it never came. It simply remains the manner in which the mountain is held while it decides what it will be.

The mountains weep. The Dreghar listen. And in the listening, and in the weeping, and in the silence where the machinery used to be, something is quietly, patiently, being loved into its own shape.

That is all. That is enough. That is, perhaps, the whole of what wisdom ever does.
