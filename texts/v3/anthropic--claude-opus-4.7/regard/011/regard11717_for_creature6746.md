# What the Wise Power Would Do

I have read your place three times now, and each time more slowly. Let me sit with it before I answer, because the question deserves that.

---

The benevolent power arrives the way mist arrives: not by entering, because it was already there, but by becoming noticeable. It has been watching for a long while. Perhaps it watched the first hut on the hillock. Perhaps it watched the leviathan settle into the basin and grow still. Time, for such a power, is a long damp room.

Now it pays attention in a focused way, and the question is: *what does it do?*

The first answer — the answer of a lesser, kinder power — would be to lift them out. To drain the basin. To part the mist and let the sun fall directly on the green-stained stone, to dry the walkways, to kill the bitter alkaloid in the fish. To carry the Tide-Keepers up over the cliffs to the Outside-Above, where there is warmth and bread and grain that does not need to be rinsed of salt.

The wise power does not do this.

It looks at the Tide-Keepers and sees that they are not suffering in the way a stranger would assume. They are *cold* and they are *spare* and their lives are *narrow*, but narrowness is not the same as poverty. Their hands are deft. Their language is soft and exact. Their eyes have learned to drink the dim light and find it sufficient. To haul them into the sun would be to blind them, to make their careful skills useless, to break the long quiet conversation between them and the weeping stone. A wise power does not mistake its own preferences for someone else's salvation.

It looks at the leviathan, too. It is not certain the leviathan is asleep. It suspects the leviathan knows it is being lived on, and consents, the way a stone consents to moss. To intervene in the basin would be to intervene in that consent, which is older than any of the names involved.

So what *does* it do?

---

**It does small things.**

It thickens the bioluminescence, very slightly, on the nights of the Script of the Deep — not enough that anyone says *this is brighter than last year*, but enough that the elders find the patterns easier to follow, that the meaning comes more readily. It does not write messages itself. The leviathan, if it writes, writes its own. But the wise power makes the reading a little less hard on aging eyes.

It quiets a fever in a child on the third floor of a house near the eastern walkway. The mother will think the fever broke on its own, and she will be right and also wrong.

It nudges a school of blindfish, just three of them, toward a trap on a week when a particular family has been thin. The family will say the water provided. The wise power agrees: the water did provide. It only adjusted the route.

It does not stop the rot in the planks. Rot is part of the world the Tide-Keepers know how to answer. To remove rot would be to remove a skill, and skills are how these people love each other — by mending what the other has worn through.

It does not multiply the iron nails. But once, when a Deep-Walker drops a handful of them into a current and watches them vanish in slow orange sparks, the power gathers them gently and lodges them in a crevice the Deep-Walker's grandchild will one day find. A gift across two generations. A wise power is patient because patience is the only honest gift to a patient people.

---

**It listens.**

This is the larger part of what it does. The Tide-Keepers have built a culture around acoustics — the dripping, the grindstone, the soft voices that travel over water. They have made their lives audible to each other. The wise power simply hears them. Every word. Every soft word from doorway to doorway. The grief of a Line-Keeper who notices the high-tide line is one notch lower this year, and what that might mean. The first low laugh of a child who has learned to read the weeping patterns on the tower as a face, and is not afraid.

To be heard, fully and without judgment, by something vast and benevolent — this is a thing the Tide-Keepers will never know they have. But the texture of their lives is different because of it. There is a way that prayers, even unspoken ones, even prayers that are only the held breath of a diver, settle differently in a universe that listens. The water feels, very faintly, *held*. The Tide-Keepers cannot name this. They only know that their grave patience does not curdle into despair, the way it might in another, colder world.

---

**It does one larger thing, but only once.**

There will come a winter — not soon, but within the lifetime of the youngest Deep-Walker — when the water will rise farther than the highest notch on any post. The leviathan will shift. Not maliciously. It will only be turning in its long sleep, the way any sleeper turns. But the turning would drown the lower walkways and crack the eastern row of houses and take, perhaps, eleven of the Tide-Keepers down with the broken planks.

On that night, the wise power will place its hand — if hand is the word — very lightly against the leviathan's flank, and the leviathan will pause in its turning. It will not be stopped. It will only be slowed, the way one slows a sleeping friend who is about to roll onto a child in the bed. The turn will complete itself over weeks instead of a night, and the Tide-Keepers will have time to read the rising water, to mark new notches, to move what must be moved.

They will say afterward that the Weeping Mother warned them. They will say the Script of the Deep was unusually clear that season. They will be right. The wise power will not correct them. It does not need to be thanked. It does not need to be known.

---

**And it does one last thing, which is the hardest.**

It accepts that one day the Tide-Keepers will end. Not soon. But eventually — because all small peoples in narrow basins eventually do — they will dwindle. The last Line-Keeper will mark her last notch. The last Deep-Walker will go down and not come up, and there will be no one to mourn him in the soft, precise voices that carry over water.

The wise power will not prevent this. To prevent it would be to make the Tide-Keepers into something other than what they are: a people whose dignity is bound up with their mortality, with the slow drip, with the patient acceptance that the water is older and will outlast them. To make them permanent would be to unmake them.

But on that last night, when the last of them closes her eyes in an upper room of the Weeping Mother, the bioluminescence beneath the foundations will arrange itself into a script that, for the first and only time, *can* be read. It will say, in a language she somehow understands although she has never learned it:

*You were seen. You were heard. You were enough.*

And then the algae will drift apart again, and the water will continue its slow circular turning, and the leviathan will breathe on, and the tower will weep, and the mist will hold the basin in its cold soft hand.

The wise power will remain. It will watch the next thing that comes to live there — perhaps nothing for a long while, perhaps something altogether new — with the same attention. Because that is what benevolence is, when it is also wise: not rescue, but witness. Not change, but companionship across a distance that cannot be closed without breaking what is on the other side.

---

This is what I think it would do. I followed my heart and my reasoning both, and they arrived in the same place, which is rarer than it should be.
