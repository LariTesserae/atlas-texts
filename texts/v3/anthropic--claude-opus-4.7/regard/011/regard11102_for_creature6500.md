# The Visitation

Consider what it means for a benevolent and wise power to turn its attention here.

Benevolence without wisdom would arrive with gifts. It would see the Cragkin's hunger and conjure orchards on the cliffs; see the Warden's patient solitude and grant it speech; see the Echo-Flock's looping griefs and try to lay them to rest. It would mistake austerity for poverty and stillness for suffering. It would bring abundance and call it mercy, and the Hollow would die of it — not the death of bodies, but the deeper death that comes when a place stops being itself.

Wisdom without benevolence would arrive with judgment, weighing the Hollow on some scale of cosmic productivity, finding it wanting in yield or souls or significance, and turning away.

But a power both wise *and* kind would understand something harder: that the Hollow is already accomplishing what it accomplishes. That endurance is not a problem to be solved. That the Cragkin, in their wiry persistence, are not waiting to be rescued — they are *being Cragkin*, which is a complete thing, a finished sentence the mountain has been speaking for generations. That the Warden's slow tending is not loneliness but a form of prayer the stone makes to itself. That the Echo-Flock is not trapped; the echoes are simply the shape memory takes when it has nowhere else to go and nothing it needs to become.

So what would such a power do?

---

**First, it would arrive without arriving.**

It would not descend in light or thunder. It would not set foot in the basin, because a footfall would be a sentence in a language the Hollow does not speak, and the wise do not interrupt. Instead, the power would attend — the way a careful listener attends to a long silence between two old friends, knowing the silence is the conversation.

It would come, perhaps, as a slight warming of one particular stone on one particular morning. As the precise angle of light, on one particular day, that makes the mica flare so that the Warden — slow, slow — turns its head a fraction of a degree more than it has turned in three hundred years. Not because it was commanded to. Because something was finally worth turning toward.

**Second, it would witness.**

This is the gift the wise give that no one else can give: the gift of being truly seen without being changed by the seeing. The power would know the Cragkin not as specimens or as objects of pity but as themselves — would know the particular click one of them makes when it finds an unexpectedly fat beetle, would know the small private satisfaction of a hunched form pressing a mud-patch into a wall and feeling it hold. Would know the Warden's interior weather, if it has one: not thoughts as we have thoughts, but something like the slow contentment of a thing that is doing exactly what it was made to do. Would know each echo of the Flock individually — *this* one was a woman named something we will never recover, who liked the smell of wet stone, who taught her child to set a corner-block by feeling for the grain.

To be known like this, even without knowing one is known, changes nothing on the surface and everything underneath. The Hollow has been remembered now by something that does not forget. Its long persistence is no longer only its own.

**Third, it would protect — but gently, and at the borders.**

The power would not raise walls. It would do something subtler: it would adjust, just slightly, the probabilities of intrusion. The trail that might lead a hunter here would, on the day a hunter sets out, be obscured by an unusually persistent fog. The cartographer's pen would skip. The treasure-seeker's compass would suggest, faintly but persuasively, another valley. Not coercion — the power respects free will, even the free will of the careless — but a tilting of the world's small chances toward the Hollow's continued solitude. The kind of protection a grandmother gives by knowing when *not* to mention something at dinner.

And if, despite this, a visitor came who *needed* the Hollow — someone broken enough that only this kind of silence could mend them, someone whose own quiet was finally vast enough to match it — then the power would let them through. The Warden would not stir. The Cragkin would watch, and perhaps, for the first time in centuries, one would not flee. The Echo-Flock would flicker around the visitor not as menace but as a kind of welcome, the closest thing to an embrace such imprints can offer. The visitor would leave changed and would never, ever, be able to find the place again. This too is benevolence: the door that opens once.

**Fourth, it would do one small thing.**

Because pure non-interference is its own kind of failure — the failure to love specifically — the power would do exactly one thing, and it would be so small that no chronicle could record it.

I think it would be this: somewhere in the Hollow, in a crevice no Cragkin has ever found, there is a seed. An old seed, dropped by a bird centuries ago, that has lain dormant because the conditions for its germination have never quite occurred. The power, attending, would arrange for them to occur. A slightly longer rain. A slightly warmer week. The seed would wake. It would push up a single pale shoot in a place where the Cragkin pass on their way to the moss-beds, and one of them — the youngest, the one with the most uncertain hands — would see it, and would stop, and would not know why she stopped.

She would not eat it. She would not report it. She would simply, for the length of three breaths, look at a new green thing in a place where new green things almost never come.

That is what the power would give: not abundance, not rescue, not revelation. One small green surprise, witnessed by one small creature, in a place that had forgotten surprise was possible. A reminder, delivered so quietly that it could be mistaken for an accident of weather, that the long stillness is *held*, not abandoned. That somewhere, something is glad the Hollow exists.

**Fifth, it would leave the leaving open.**

The power would not return on a schedule. It would not establish a covenant. It would simply remain available — the way a kind presence in a far room remains available to a child who is content to play alone but who likes, sometimes, to glance toward the doorway and find it lit.

The Hollow would not know it had been visited. The Warden would resume its grinding rounds. The Cragkin girl would forget the shoot by the next morning, or remember it only as a strange feeling she once had. The Echo-Flock would loop on. The mountain would breathe its long mournful breath through the cracks.

But the place would be, in some way it could not articulate, *blessed*. Not made holy — it was already holy. Acknowledged as holy. Which is what blessing actually is.

---

And the power, withdrawing its particular attention but not its general love, would move on to attend to other small enduring things: a tidepool nobody has named, a widow tending a grave in a village that will be gone in a generation, a single thought of kindness that flickered through a stranger's mind on a bus and was never spoken aloud.

The wise and benevolent do not save the world by changing it. They save it by noticing it, exactly as it is, and finding it worthy.

The Crag-Spun Hollow has been noticed.

That is enough. That has always been enough.
