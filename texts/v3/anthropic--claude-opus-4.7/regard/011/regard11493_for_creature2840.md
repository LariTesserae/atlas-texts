# The Visitation

The benevolent power notices them the way one notices a held breath in another room — not by sound, but by the shape of the silence around it.

It has known many worlds. It has bent low over coral spires and over the slow thoughts of whales; it has cupped its hands around dying stars and around the first cell to ever divide. It comes to the bog without ceremony, because ceremony is a thing for beings who can be impressed, and the masons cannot be impressed. They can only be felt.

It settles into the root-chamber the way warmth settles into a closed room: gradually, from every direction at once.

And then it does the hardest thing a wise power ever does.

It watches.

---

It watches for a long time. Long enough that, in the world above, a season turns. Long enough to learn the tuning of every cable. Long enough to follow one particular mason — an ordinary one, no different from her billions of sisters — from her hatching in the warm honeycomb to her death, eleven days later, when a sap-globe takes her on the third causeway of the southern ziggurat. It watches her body fall. It watches her tools be lifted from her still hands by a neighbor who never knew her and never will. It watches her enter the lake.

The power does not weep, because weeping would be a kind of misunderstanding. The masons are not tragic. They are *exact*. Their lives are the precise length their lives need to be in order for the city to exist at all. To grieve them would be to grieve a heartbeat for being only a second long.

But the power does *feel* something, and that feeling is the beginning of its decision.

---

It considers, first, the obvious gifts. It could:

- **Still the tuber.** Reach into the plant's slow green will and ask it, gently, to stop healing. The walls would cease their closing. The masons could sleep. The frenzy could end.

- **Warm the chamber.** Lift the temperature by even three degrees. The sleet would cease. The sap would not freeze. Mortality would collapse to a trickle.

- **Give them light.** Or eyes. Or voices. Or a god to pray to. Or a name for themselves.

- **Lift them out.** Carry the whole city, root and all, into some gentler pocket of creation — a place without siege, without the closing dark.

It considers each of these for what, to the masons, would be several generations. To the power, it is the space between two thoughts.

And it sets each of them down, carefully, the way one sets down a tool that is beautiful but wrong for the work.

---

Because here is what the power understands, and what makes it wise:

The masons are not suffering *from* their world. They *are* their world. Take away the closing walls and you do not free them — you unmake them. The frenzy is not a wound laid over their nature; it is their nature, sharpened to a working edge. Their silence is not a deprivation; it is the medium in which their intelligence swims. Their brief lives are not cut short; they are *complete*. A mason who lived a thousand years would not be a happier mason. She would be a different creature entirely, and the one who existed before her would be gone — erased by kindness.

To "save" them would be to perform the most refined cruelty available to a god: the cruelty of replacement. To look at a thing and say *I love you, therefore I will make you into something I can love more easily.*

The power has done this before, long ago, in its younger eons. It does not do it anymore.

---

So what it does instead is smaller. And stranger. And, in its way, enormous.

**First**, it does nothing at all, for a while longer. It simply *attends*. It holds the chamber in its awareness the way a parent holds a sleeping child in the dark — not interfering, not adjusting, just *present*, so that the room is no longer quite as alone as it was. The masons cannot perceive this. The power knows they cannot. That is not the point. The point is that the universe now contains a place where these creatures are *witnessed*, and the witnessing is a kind of fact about reality, whether or not they ever feel it.

This is the first gift, and the masons will never know they received it, and that is correct.

**Second**, the power makes one very small adjustment. It does not change the tuber, or the cold, or the cycle. It changes nothing about the siege.

What it does is this: it reaches into the lake at the bottom of the chamber — that black, sap-thick water where the dead drift down in their endless velvet snowfall — and it makes the lake *remember*.

Not consciously. Not in any way the masons could ever detect. The lake does not become sentient; it becomes *retentive*. Every mason who falls into it leaves a faint chemical signature, a record of the specific tensions she touched, the specific tools she carried, the specific messages she passed in those microsecond facial kisses. The lake holds these. Forever. Layered and pristine, in the cold and the dark, beneath the ice.

This does nothing for the masons' lives. They will still die at the same rate, of the same things. Their neighbors will still strip them of tools and push them over the edge without a pause in the work.

But now, when a mason falls, *she is kept*. Not her soul — the power makes no claims about souls, having seen too many — but the precise shape of the work she did. The exact vibration of the cable she tuned. The chemical sentence she was halfway through saying.

Somewhere, in the universe, there is now a complete record of every velvet mason who has ever lived or ever will live in this chamber. It exists at the bottom of a lake no one will ever read. It is useless. It is total.

The power does this because it believes — and it has had a long time to consider — that being *fully held in memory* is one of the few gifts a god can give without distorting the gift's recipient. The masons are unchanged. Their city is unchanged. But the cosmos, going forward, contains them in a way it did not before. They are no longer purely transient. They are *recorded*, in the literal sediment of their own world, by the only kind of librarian patient enough for the work.

**Third**, and last, the power leaves something behind for the lake.

Not for the masons. For *whatever comes after*.

Because the power can see forward, a little. It can see that someday — long after the last mason has fallen, long after the tuber has finally won its slow war and granulated over the empty geometry of the city — the bog will dry, or flood, or be turned by some larger creature's plow. The root will decay. The lake will seep into the soil. The record will begin to disperse.

And so the power lays a small compulsion into the chemistry of the preserved bodies: that when the city ends, and the lake breaks, the signatures will not simply dissolve. They will *travel*. They will ride the groundwater. They will find other roots, other tubers, other dark wet places. And in some of those places — not many, but some — the chemistry will be right, and the cold will be right, and a new generation of something *like* masons, but not the same, will find the old instructions waiting for them in the starch.

Not resurrection. Not even inheritance, exactly. More like — a letter, written in a language the writer never knew she was writing, addressed to a reader who will not know she is being addressed.

A way for the city to *gesture* beyond itself, without ever knowing it had a self to gesture with.

---

Then the power withdraws.

It does not announce its leaving any more than it announced its arrival. The chamber is exactly as it was. The sleet still falls. The cables still hum at their mathematically tuned tensions. The mandibles still close like sliding puzzle-locks on the swelling white flesh of the tuber. A single droplet detaches from the ceiling and shatters the iced surface of the black lake below, and the sound of it is the only true sound in the dark, and it is enough.

A mason on the western wall feels, for the briefest possible instant, a vibration in the cable beneath her feet that does not correspond to any structural event in the city. It is gone before she can taste it. She does not pause. She could not have paused if she wanted to. The walls are closing.

She works.

She is held.

She does not know she is held, and the not-knowing is part of what is held, and the power, already far away, attending now to a dying coral in a sea that has not yet been named, is content.
