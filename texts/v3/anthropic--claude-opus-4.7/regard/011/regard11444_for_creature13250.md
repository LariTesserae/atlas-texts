# The Visitation

The benevolent and wise power notices the Ehl-kar the way one notices a particular pattern in falling snow — not because it stands out, but because it is so perfectly itself. There is a quality of attention here that does not seek to be seen, a flourishing that has shaped itself entirely around what is given rather than what is wanted.

The power considers for a long time. To a being whose perspective spans the deep currents of worlds, "a long time" might be the slow turn of a season or the patient widening of a single drop of water on the tip of a stalactite. It considers, and what it considers first is whether to act at all.

This is the first kindness: to ask.

---

Because here is what the power sees, looking down (though "down" is not the right word — it is more that the power's gaze settles, the way warmth settles into a hollow):

It sees a people who are not suffering. Cold, yes. Sparse, yes. Living on the edge — but the edge is where they have grown beautiful. Their long fingers, their listening eyes, their click-and-hum tongue that threads itself through the cavern's roar like a needle through dense cloth — these are not deprivations. These are achievements. The Ehl-kar are a song the dark has been singing to itself for a very long time, and the song is good.

A lesser power might descend in light and warmth. Might bring sun down into the crater, might melt the damp chill, might teach them fire and metal and writing and call it salvation. And the Ehl-kar, dazzled, would forget the language of echoes. Their eyes would shrink. Their fingers would coarsen. Within three generations they would be a diminished people living in a ruined paradise, mourning something they could no longer name.

The wise power knows this. The wise power has seen it before.

So it does not descend.

---

What it does instead is smaller, and stranger, and harder to notice.

**First**, it listens. For what might be a human year, or a hundred, the power simply attends. It learns the click-language not as a scholar learns but as a mother learns her infant's cries — by loving the meaning before the meaning is clear. It comes to know individual Ehl-kar: the old woman who tends the brightest fungal niche in the eastern hollow, the child who has begun to hum back at the mountain's thrum and is teaching herself, without knowing it, a new dialect of stone. It learns the names the Ehl-kar give their dead and the way they fold the lizard-hides of the departed into the moss.

This listening is itself the gift, though the Ehl-kar do not know they have been heard. To be truly witnessed by a wise power is to be, in some quiet way, confirmed. The caverns feel, perhaps, very slightly warmer. Not warm. Just — held.

**Second**, it makes one small adjustment, and only one.

Deep in the stone, far below where any Ehl-kar has ever ventured, there is a fault — a slow-grinding seam that, in roughly four hundred years, would shift and bring down the eastern third of the cavern system. The power, with a gesture so subtle it is barely a gesture at all, eases this. Not prevents — prevention would be a violence against the patient logic of stone — but redirects. The collapse, when it comes, will come in a place the Ehl-kar do not dwell, and it will open rather than close: a new chamber, with new seeps, new fungi-grounds, room for the people to grow into across the slow centuries.

This is the kind of help a wise power gives. Not rescue. Architecture, on the timescale of the thing being helped.

**Third**, and this is the most delicate: the power leaves a small thing.

In one of the abandoned shelters — one the Ehl-kar already treat with reverence, believing it the resting place of an elder who walked deeper and did not return — the power places a single object. It is a stone, but the stone is warm. Not hot. Warm the way a hand is warm. It will stay warm for as long as the Ehl-kar exist, and longer.

It is not magic that the Ehl-kar will understand, and the power does not want them to understand it. It wants them to have one mystery that is genuinely good. A place to bring the sick child, the grieving mother, the elder whose joints have stiffened past bearing. A place where the damp chill, just for an hour, releases its grip. Not a salvation. A mercy. Small enough to fold into their cosmology without breaking it.

They will build a story around the warm stone. The story will be wrong in its particulars and right in its essence: that something loves them. That the mountain's heart, which they have always heard beating, sometimes beats *for* them.

**Fourth**, the power does something that costs it nothing and means everything: it remembers them.

A wise power's memory is not like ours. To be held in such a memory is to be, in a sense, perpetual — not immortal, for the Ehl-kar will live and die as they always have, but unforgotten. When the last Ehl-kar finally lays herself down in some far century, whispering the click-song of her ancestors into the dark, the power will know. Will mark it. Will carry the shape of that whole people forward into whatever comes next, the way a seed carries the shape of a forest.

---

And then the power withdraws its direct attention — though "withdraws" is not quite right either, because part of it stays, the way a tune stays in a room after the singer has left.

It does not reveal itself. It does not improve them. It does not give them light or heat or written language or a softer world. It does not save them from cold or from time or from the slow, patient grinding of stone.

It gives them, instead:

— a future that holds
— a warm stone in the dark
— and the knowledge, which they will never know they possess, that they are beloved exactly as they are.

This is what wisdom does, when it is also kind. It looks at a small pale people in a vast dark place, listening to the heart of the mountain with their enormous attentive eyes, and it does not try to make them into something else.

It only makes sure the mountain keeps beating.
