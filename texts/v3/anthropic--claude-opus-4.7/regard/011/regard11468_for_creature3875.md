# The Visitation

The benevolent power does not arrive. That would be a kind of trespass. Instead, it *attends* — the way a careful listener leans in without interrupting.

It looks, first, for a long time. Longer than seems necessary. Wisdom, it turns out, is mostly patience with one's own first impressions.

---

**What it sees that an outsider might miss:**

The Forgeborn are not enslaved. They have not been tricked. They are not, despite the appearances that would scandalize a surface-dweller, suffering in any way they themselves would name. They love the foundry. They love it the way a violinist loves the callus on her fingertip — as proof of belonging to something that matters.

The power notes this, and resists the easy reflex of liberation. To "free" the Forgeborn from the foundry would be to free a fish from water, a song from sound. There are rescues that are only thefts wearing better clothes.

**And yet —**

The power sees, too, the things the Forgeborn have stopped seeing because they are always there:

- That the children are rare, and getting rarer.
- That torpor is not quite rest, and the dreams of designs are not quite dreams.
- That the Overseers' coats are heavy, and the whispers they hear from the machinery have, in recent generations, grown demanding where once they were collaborative.
- That the mosses in the shadowed corners are the only thing in the cavern that exists for no productive reason at all, and that no one tends them, and no one has noticed that there are fewer than there used to be.
- That the murals depict labor and creation, but never rest, never play, never the small foolish tendernesses that a culture needs to remain a culture rather than a function.

The imbalance the cavern thrives in is real. But thriving-in-imbalance has a shelf life. The power can see the shape of the century after next, if nothing shifts: a foundry still running, beautifully, on no one.

---

**What it does:**

Not much. Or — not much that would look like much.

**First**, it does the smallest possible thing. In one of the shadowed corners, where a slow drip feeds a pool no one drinks from, it deepens the moss. Just slightly. Makes it a richer green. Makes it smell, faintly, of something that is not metal — of the green world above, which most Forgeborn have never seen and would not know to miss.

It does not label this gift. It does not announce it. It simply makes the moss a little more alive, and trusts that someone, eventually, will pause beside it on the way to a shift and feel, without knowing why, that they have been spoken to kindly.

**Second**, it visits the dreams of one child. Just one. A small one, with amber eyes, who has not yet been taught that her hands are for hammers. In the dream there is no instruction, no prophecy, no burden. There is only a long afternoon — a concept the child has no word for — and a field of grass, and the child is allowed to do nothing in it for as long as she wants. When she wakes she will not remember the dream, but she will, for the rest of her life, occasionally stop in the middle of work and feel that she has forgotten something important and good. This feeling will make her a strange adult. Strange adults are how cultures remember they are alive.

**Third**, it leaves a tool. Not a fine one. A hammer, slightly miscalibrated — its balance a little off, in a way that will require whoever picks it up to adjust their technique, to *think* rather than to fall into the rhythm of muscle memory. A small disruption. A grain of sand in the machine of habit. The Forgeborn pride themselves on their skill; the hammer will offer them a problem worth solving, and in solving it, one of them will discover that they have been doing something the same way for a hundred years for no reason other than that they always had. From this discovery, other discoveries will follow. Slowly. The power is not in a hurry.

**Fourth**, and this is the most delicate, it does *not* speak to the Overseers. They would mistake it for a machine-whisper and incorporate its words into the foundry's demands. Instead, it speaks — once, briefly, in a voice that might be mistaken for the cavern settling — to an old Forgeborn who has begun, in secret, to doubt. He is the kind of man who would never act on a vision, but who might, after hearing what he thinks is the cave's own sigh, feel permitted to tell his apprentice the story his grandmother told him, the one about the time before the foundry, which he had always assumed was just a story. The apprentice will remember. The apprentice will tell others. A story, once it begins moving again, is very hard to stop.

**Fifth**, the power makes one promise to itself, which is the hardest part of being benevolent and wise: *it will come back.* Not to fix. Not to save. To check. Because the temptation, having intervened at all, is to keep intervening until the place becomes what the helper wanted rather than what it was becoming on its own. The power resolves to return in a hundred years, and to do, again, only the smallest possible thing — or, if the Forgeborn have begun to find their own way, nothing at all.

---

**What it does not do:**

It does not extinguish the furnaces. It does not show them the sky. It does not tell them they have been wrong. It does not give them a leader, or a revelation, or a destiny. It does not name itself.

It does not love them less for being what they are. It loves them, in fact, very precisely for being what they are — and loves them enough to want them to be able to choose it, rather than only to have inherited it.

---

The benevolent power withdraws. The cavern hums on. Somewhere, water drips into a pool. Somewhere, a child rolls over in torpor and almost smiles. Somewhere, an old man takes a breath he wasn't expecting to take, and decides, tomorrow, to tell a story.

The foundry of Arathi does not know it has been visited.

This is exactly as it should be.
