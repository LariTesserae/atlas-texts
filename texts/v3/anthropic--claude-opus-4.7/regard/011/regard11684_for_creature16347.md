The benevolent power arrives the way mist arrives: without announcement, without disturbing a single dewdrop.

It has watched this world for a long while. Longer than the scaled people have walked it, longer than the ruins have stood, longer perhaps than the wind has had its particular voice. Power of this kind does not measure itself in arrivals and departures. It is more accurate to say it had always been attending, and only now turns the fullness of its attention here, the way a listener leans slightly forward to catch a phrase of music they had been half-hearing.

And what does it see?

It sees a place that is already whole.

This is the first and most important thing. The benevolent power, being wise, recognizes wholeness when it encounters it. The land is not waiting to be rescued. The people are not crying out for deliverance. The ruins are not a wound; they are a sentence the world is still finishing. The slow drip, the lichen, the garnet scales, the patterned skin — all of it is a conversation already underway, and a conversation that has found its rhythm does not need a new voice inserted into it.

A lesser power would not understand this. A lesser power would arrive with gifts: warmth for the cold, abundance for the sparse, cures for the wounds the people wear as stigmata. A lesser power would mistake hardship for suffering, solitude for loneliness, slowness for stagnation, ruin for ruin. A lesser power would, with the very best intentions, end the world by improving it.

So the benevolent power does almost nothing.

Almost.

It does three small things, because love that does nothing at all is not quite love, and wisdom that withholds entirely is not quite wisdom.

The first thing it does is *witness*. It lets itself be fully present to the place — to the wail of the wind through the hollow stones, to the slow blink of the deep dark eyes, to the particular way one of the people lays a palm against a weathered wall as they pass, a gesture so habitual none of them can remember its origin. The power attends to all of it without need, without agenda, without the wish to alter. This witnessing is not nothing. In the deep grammar of the universe, to be truly seen is a kind of nourishment. The people will not know they have been seen. But that night, around their gathering places in the skeletal architecture, their mantras will carry a fraction further into the dark, and the echoes will come back a fraction warmer. They will not remark on it. They will simply feel, without knowing why, that the world has held them today.

The second thing it does is *protect, invisibly, and only against what does not belong*. Somewhere — in some other fold of existence, on some other path of possible futures — there are forces that would come to this place and not understand it. Extractors. Improvers. Conquerors of the well-meaning and the ill-meaning kind alike. The power, gently, turns those paths aside. Not by force, but by a soft adjustment, the way a hand might guide a moth away from a flame without the moth ever knowing it was guided. The scaled people will continue to fight their own elements, to bear their own wounds, to win their own slow victories — because those are theirs, and to take them would be theft. But the power closes the door against thefts of a different order. The wholeness will be allowed to remain whole.

The third thing it does is *leave a small gift, so subtle it might not be a gift at all*. In one of the pockets of lushness, where the shrubs cluster around the eternal drip, the power lets fall something like a blessing — not a new species, not a sudden bloom, nothing that would disturb the slow grammar of the place. Only this: that the water there, for those who drink from it, will carry a faint memory of having been loved. Not by anyone in particular. Just loved. The people who drink will not notice. But on certain nights, when one of them sits alone among the ruins listening to the wind, they may feel, with a clarity they cannot account for, that they are part of something that wishes them well. That feeling is already true. The power has only made it slightly easier to hear.

And then it withdraws — though *withdraws* is the wrong word, because it never quite leaves. It simply returns to that mode of half-attention which is the natural resting state of benevolent powers toward worlds that are doing well on their own.

Before it goes, it pauses once. It looks at one of the people — a tall one, scales dulled by age, patterned marks dense as a written history along the forearms — standing at the edge of a ridge as the pale light shifts. The power feels something very close to what we would call tenderness, and very close to what we would call respect, and these two feelings together make a third feeling for which no language in any world has yet found a word.

The old one on the ridge tilts their head slightly, as though hearing something at the edge of audibility. Then they blink, slow, and walk on.

The wind takes up its wailing again. The drip continues. The lichen, imperceptibly, grows.

Nothing has changed.

Everything has been honored.

This, in the end, is what wise and benevolent powers do, when they encounter a world that has already found its way: they bow, in their fashion, and they let it be.
