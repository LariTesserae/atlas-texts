The benevolent and wise power considers the basin for a long time before doing anything at all.

This is the first gift, though no one in the town will know to receive it: to be looked at slowly. Most powers, even kind ones, arrive already deciding. They see a damp settlement on raw ground and think *shelter them, lift them, dry them, teach them the wheel of seasons their land has not yet learned.* They reach in with the hand of correction, and the place becomes a lesson rather than a life.

This power has learned, over a long career of loving things, that the first question is not *what do they need* but *what are they doing.* And what the town is doing is so particular, so finely tuned to its half-made ground, that to fix it would be to end it.

So the power watches. It watches a Gutter-Knower kneel at a seam and listen until her cheek is wet from the stone. It watches the First Leak ceremony of a child whose shim is too large and who weeps when the water finds a way around her thumb, and who is told, gently, that this is the lesson—not the failure. It watches the eels accept their scraps and their stories with equal gravity. It watches a visitor from drier country lose, over the course of a winter, the habit of walking in straight lines, and it notes that the visitor seems, by spring, to be more himself rather than less.

The power understands, then, what kind of place this is. It is not a town that survives its land. It is a town that is *in conversation* with its land, and the conversation is the point. The half-carvedness is not a wound to heal. It is the medium of a practice. These people are not waiting to live somewhere finished. They have made an art of the unfinished, and the art would die in any completion offered to it.

What, then, can a benevolent power give to such a place?

Not stability. Stability would silence the walls the children listen to.

Not abundance. Abundance would make the eels into pests and the leech-things into vermin and the careful economy of tolerated creatures into a thing one could afford to forget.

Not knowledge of the future. The whole grammar of the town is the tense of *not yet decided.* A told future would collapse it.

Not even protection, not really. To wrap the basin in safety would be to remove the very pressure that has shaped the people into cartographers of closeness.

The power thinks about leaving, then. There is a wisdom in leaving. Many places have been ruined by gods who could not bear to do nothing.

But there is one thing, the power decides. One small thing.

It is this: the town has a fear it does not speak about, because to speak of it would be to invite it. The fear is that the basin will, one day, spill its lessons downhill—that the ground will finally decide how to settle, and the settling will not include them. They build as if this might happen tomorrow. They build also as if it will never happen. Both are true, and the doubleness is part of how they live. But underneath the doubleness, in the place where even the listeners do not press their ears, there is a grief already prepared. A grief for the town that will, someday, not be.

The power cannot prevent this. It would not, even if it could; the basin's eventual settling is part of the basin's life, and the town has always known this in its bones.

What the power can do is small and exact. It can make sure that when the time comes—if it comes in a generation, or in twenty, or in a hundred—the lessons do not spill *unwitnessed.* It can arrange, very quietly, that somewhere downhill, in some drier country where people still walk in straight lines, a child will be born who tilts. Who pauses at the sound of a drip. Who, without ever having seen the basin, will know how to listen to a wall until it gives up its mood. And another child, and another, scattered like seeds across the lowlands and beyond, each carrying a fragment of the grammar: the low center of gravity, the humming that asks the stone to answer, the understanding that stopping is temporary and that the town tolerates small losses.

They will not be missionaries. They will not rebuild the basin elsewhere; the basin cannot be rebuilt, only inhabited where it occurs. But they will be, each of them, a place where the basin's way of being is still possible. A practice carried in bodies, the way the eels carry the weight that stiffens the bridges.

This is the power's gift: not preservation, but *dispersal of grammar.* The town will end, as all towns end. But the kind of attention the town invented—the cartography of closeness, the religion of maintenance, the patience with half-made ground—will not end with it. It will find feet in other valleys. It will recognize itself, sometimes, in a stranger pausing at a drip.

The power does one more thing, smaller still.

On a particular morning, in a particular alley, a particular stone changes color under a particular hand—a hand that has warmed it for three mornings running, as the local weirdness allows. But this morning the color is one no one in the town has seen before. Not the soft grey-going-to-gold that recognition usually brings. Something deeper. Something like the color of a thing being thanked.

The woman whose hand it is will not understand. She will go home and describe it to her household, and her household will describe it to the listener, and the listener will press her palm to the hollow post and find the post a little lighter than yesterday, a little easier in its rising. The weir that evening will still without a ripple that cannot be explained by the breeze, and a decision long deferred will be made, and no one will quite know why it was easy.

The power withdraws then. It does not stay to watch its gift unfold. To stay would be to make the gift into a presence, and presences, even benevolent ones, distort what they hover near. It goes back to wherever powers go when they are not at work, carrying with it the memory of a town that taught it something:

That sometimes the most loving thing is to recognize a practice already adequate to its world, and to add nothing to it but a little quiet weight on the far end of time—so that when the world the practice belongs to finally lets go, the practice itself does not fall, but is caught, elsewhere, by hands that have been waiting without knowing they were waiting.

The basin breathes. The town breathes with it. Somewhere downhill, a child not yet born tilts in her mother's belly toward the sound of dripping water, and her mother, surprised, places a hand on her stomach and listens, for the first time in her life, to a wall.
