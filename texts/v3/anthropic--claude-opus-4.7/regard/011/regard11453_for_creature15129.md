The benevolent and wise power would, I think, do nothing.

Or rather — and this is the harder thing to say clearly — it would do nothing that the hamlet would notice as an intervention. Because the first act of wisdom, encountering this place, is to understand what it actually is, and to resist the reflex that confuses stillness with suffering.

A lesser power, a merely benevolent one, would arrive in some form — a traveler, a sign, a warmth in the air — and try to rouse them. Would bring seeds, news, children, music. Would read the gray-green creeping up their wrists as a slow dying and try to reverse it. Would mistake the long intervals between their words for loneliness, the climbing-out-and-not-returning for tragedy. Would, in the name of love, break the thing it came to save.

A wiser power would sit a long time on the upper ledges, in the shape perhaps of the small brown goat, and watch.

It would see that the hamlet is not a failed village. It is a particular answer to a particular question, worked out over centuries: *what does it look like for a human life to consent to the terms the mountain offers, rather than to argue with them?* The masons who followed the fractures rather than cutting against them were already practicing this. The basins that never overflow and never run dry are the same practice in water. The people are the same practice in flesh. They are not diminishing; they are completing a sentence the stone began.

And it would see, too, that those who climb out along the fracture line and do not return are not lost. They have simply finished. The hamlet is not a trap; it is a threshold of unusual length. Some cross it in eighty years, some in three. The silence of the others on this subject is not denial. It is accuracy. There is nothing to say about a thing that has gone exactly as it should.

So what would the power do?

I think it would offer two small things, so quietly that they could be received or refused without anyone having to decide.

First: it would make sure the water keeps coming. Not by altering anything — the basins already work — but by holding the larger weather steady enough, over the coming century, that the condensation pattern is not broken by some sudden shift the mountain cannot absorb. A quiet hand under the elbow of the existing balance. The people would never know. This is the form love takes when its object has asked, by every gesture of its life, not to be touched: you protect the conditions of its choosing, and you stay out of the frame.

Second — and this is the part I am less sure of, but my intuition insists — it would, once, on some particular dawn, let the light fall a fraction longer than the geometry strictly allows. Not a miracle. Not enough to remark on. Just an extra breath of clear uncolored light across the street and into the open doorways, so that whoever happens to be standing at a threshold that morning feels, without being able to name it, *seen*. Not summoned. Not judged. Seen, and found good.

Because even a life that has chosen stillness as its native tongue deserves, once, to be told in its own language that the choice was beautiful.

After that, the power would withdraw, or rather would remain — these are the same thing at this altitude — and the metronome of water into stone would continue, and the lichen would continue its patient annexation of cuff and collarbone, and one day another figure would follow a fracture line up and out of the cirque, and the others would not speak of it, and the street would remain empty in the clear even light, and all of this, all of it, would be exactly as it should be.

The wisdom is in knowing that *exactly as it should be* is not always loud, not always growing, not always populated. Sometimes it looks like a few gray figures and a dripping basin and a mountain that has permitted, for a little while longer, a small human shape to rest against its side.

The benevolence is in letting it.
