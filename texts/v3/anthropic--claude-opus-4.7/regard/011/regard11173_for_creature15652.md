The benevolent power pauses at the edge of the plateau — not arriving, exactly, because such a power does not arrive the way wind or traveler does. It is more that its attention settles, the way light settles into a pool, the way snow finds a particular shelf of rock and rests there.

It looks for a long time.

Wisdom, when it is real, is mostly patience. And benevolence, when it is real, is mostly restraint. So the first thing the power does is nothing — nothing for what might be a day, or a season, or the length of a single held breath in its own vast reckoning. It watches one of the dwellers walk to the water-grooves at dawn. It watches her cup her hands. It watches her pass the jug.

And it sees.

It sees that this is not a place that needs saving. That is the first and most important seeing. The cold is hard, the air is thin, the people are few and will one day be fewer — but they are not suffering in the way a power inclined to intervene must learn to distinguish from simply *living at the edge of what living is*. They have not cried out. They are not waiting to be rescued. Their hush is not the hush of the abandoned; it is the hush of the deeply attended.

A lesser power — younger, more eager, more in love with its own capacity — might have warmed the wind. Might have thickened the air, multiplied the lichen, called down a softer sun. Might have spoken, in a voice of unmistakable origin, and offered gifts. Might have made the place easier, and in making it easier, unmade it.

This power is older than that mistake.

So it does smaller things. Or rather — it does things that look small, and are not.

It steadies, very slightly, the foothold of a particular stone on a particular path, the one the youngest climber will take down to the canyon in three weeks' time to barter for salt. Not enough to be noticed. Just enough that the stone, which would have turned, does not turn. The climber will return. Her mother will not learn what almost happened, because almost-happenings are not the business of the living.

It listens to the night-calls, the deep resonant cries no one is sure of, and finds that it knows them — knows them the way one knows a voice from very long ago. It does not silence them. It does not explain them. It only, gently, tunes itself to their key, so that on certain nights the call and the answering hum from the hearth-fire find each other a half-tone closer than chance would have allowed. The dwellers will not notice this directly. But a child born this winter will grow up humming in a particular way, and her humming will comfort others, and no one will know why.

It looks at the keepers in their tunnel, tracing the old carvings with fingertips. It sees that some of what they trace is true, and some is half-remembered, and some is invention dressed in the clothes of memory. The power does not correct them. Truth that is handed down is not the same substance as truth that is handed across, and this is theirs to carry in whatever shape their carrying gives it. But it does — once, only once, on a night when the bioluminescent dust is already falling — let one particular carving catch the light a little longer than physics alone would allow. The keeper who sees this will weep quietly, and will not tell anyone, and will die many years later still holding the small private certainty that something heard her. She was right. Something did.

It considers the phosphorescent auroras, the glowing moss, the shimmering veils at dawn. It finds that these are not its doing, nor anyone's — they are the land's own slow speech, the strangeness the place generates simply by being itself for long enough. The power admires them the way one admires another's work. It does not add to them. It would be like painting over a poem.

It considers, too, whether to give the dwellers more of themselves — more children, longer lives, easier breaths. And here it lingers longest, because this is where benevolence becomes most treacherous. To extend a people beyond the shape their place can hold is not kindness; it is a slow translation into something else, something that will eventually have to leave or to break the place that made it. The power has seen this happen. It has, in younger eras, *caused* it to happen, and the memory is one of the things that taught it to be the kind of power it now is.

So it does not multiply them. Instead, it does this: it makes sure that when one of them dies, the dying is not lonely. Not by sending visions, not by lighting the room — only by being present, fully and without agenda, in the last breath. A witness. The dying one will not know they are witnessed by anything other than the wind and perhaps a companion's hand. But the witnessing will be real, and it will mean that no one on this plateau dies entirely unaccompanied, ever, even when they die alone.

This is the largest gift, and it is invisible.

There is one more thing the power does, and it is the most careful of all.

It leaves a small possibility. Not a promise, not a prophecy — only a possibility, folded into the structure of the place like a seed in a stone crevice. *If* the day ever comes when these people genuinely cannot endure — not the ordinary unendurable that they have always endured, but something past it, something that would erase rather than weather them — *then* a path will become apparent. Not a rescue. A path. Somewhere to go, or someone to find, or a memory in one of them that suddenly clarifies into instruction. The possibility costs nothing to leave there. It may never be needed. The power suspects, in fact, that it will not be — that these people will go on in their slow conversation with stone and wind until the stone and wind themselves change, and then they will change with them, becoming whatever the next form of belonging requires. But the seed is left anyway. Benevolence at this scale includes leaving doors that may never open.

And then the power withdraws its attention — slowly, the way light withdraws from a pool at evening, not all at once but by degrees so gradual the pool itself does not notice the going. It leaves nothing visible behind. It leaves no sign, no relic, no altered weather. It has not signed its name on the plateau, because signing one's name on a place like this would be a kind of theft.

What it leaves is only this: that the place is, very slightly and permanently, *known*. Not watched-over in any continuous way. Just known. Held in the memory of something that does not forget.

The dwellers will never learn of the visit. They will go to the water-grooves at dawn. They will pass the jug. They will hum to the night-calls. They will trace the carvings. They will die, accompanied by what they will assume is only the wind.

And the wind, for its part, will keep its counsel, as it always has.
