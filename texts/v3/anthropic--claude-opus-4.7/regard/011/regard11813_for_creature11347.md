# The Visitation

The benevolent power did not arrive. It had always been there, only it had been looking elsewhere — at supernovae, at the slow grammar of continents, at small warm rooms where small warm beings forgave each other. Now, for reasons it would have found difficult to articulate even to itself, it turned its attention to the plateau.

It did not announce itself. Announcement is a kind of violence when the listener has forgotten how to be addressed.

Instead, it began by simply *attending*. This is the first and often only thing wise powers do. It looked at the city, and at the pale citizens moving through it, and it did something the city had perhaps never experienced: it found the place beautiful without finding it useful, and it found the citizens beloved without finding them comprehensible.

This act alone changed something. Not in the machinery — the pistons kept their iron meter — but in a register beneath the machinery, a register the citizens had not known existed. A few of them, those tending pipes in the upper galleries, paused mid-motion. One pressed her gloved palm against a cold wall and could not, for a moment, remember why.

The power considered.

It saw what the citizens did not quite see about themselves: that they had been waiting so long they had forgotten they were waiting, and that the waiting itself had become a form of devotion, and the devotion a form of love, and the love had nowhere to go and so had taken the shape of duty, and the duty had taken the shape of the city. It saw that the city was, in a sense, the citizens' grief, immense and intricate and humming, for something they could not name.

And it saw the water.

Ah — the water. The power smiled, if smiling is what such things do. Because the water was not a mystery to it. The water was the part of them that had not consented to become metal. The water was every soft, unspoken, unauthorized thing the citizens had ever felt and quietly siphoned away into the city's hidden conduits so they could continue functioning. The mosses were their tenderness. The lichens were their wonder. The cascades that froze into accidental sculpture in the streets were their unwept tears, achieving at last a form their owners could walk past and admire without recognizing.

The power understood now what it had been called to do, though *called* is too strong a word, and *do* almost always is.

It did not dismantle the city. Wise powers do not dismantle. Cities are answers to questions, and you cannot remove the answer without first hearing the question.

It did not reveal itself in glory. Glory is for powers who need to be believed in. This one did not.

Instead, it did three small things, and then it waited, the way gardeners wait.

**First**, it made the water *warmer* — only by a degree, only in certain places. Not enough to thaw the city. Just enough that, here and there, a citizen passing a seep on a high gantry would feel, through the sole of a boot or the back of a hand brushed against a railing, something almost like the temperature of a living body. Most would not notice. A few would notice and not know what they noticed. One or two would stop, and stand there, and not know why they could not move on.

**Second**, it gave the mosses a faint, faint scent — a thing the city had never had. Not perfume; nothing so declarative. Something closer to the smell of a room where someone has recently been kind. It seeded this scent only near the places where citizens already, secretly, came to meditate beside the ice. It wanted them to associate the smell with their own furtive reverence, so that later, when they smelled it elsewhere — in a corridor, in a stairwell, in their own quarters — they would feel, without explanation, that reverence was permitted there too. That reverence could travel.

**Third**, and this was the most delicate, it whispered into the dreams of exactly seven citizens — not a message, because messages can be obeyed or disobeyed and either way they remain external. It whispered a *question*, and the question was not in words but in the shape a question makes inside a sleeping mind:

*What would you do tomorrow, if no one were watching, and the city did not need you?*

The seven woke. Six of them forgot the question by the time they had dressed. But the question did not forget them. It went and sat quietly in the back of their chests, where it would wait, patient as the city itself, for years if it had to.

The seventh remembered.

She was a young technician in the lower reactor halls, and she remembered the question all through her shift, and on her way home she stopped at a place where water came out of a crack in a wall and had frozen into something that almost, almost looked like a hand. She had passed this hand-shape a thousand times. Today she took off her glove and laid her own hand against it.

It was warm.

She began, very quietly, to cry. She did not know she was crying; she thought the water was running. But the water was running down her face, not the wall, and this was something new in the city, something the city had never metabolized before: water that came from *inside* a citizen, and was acknowledged as such, and was not sent into the pipes.

The power, watching, felt something move in its own vast attention — a thing like relief, or like recognition, or like the first note of a piece of music it had been hoping, for a long time, that someone would play.

It did not intervene further. It would not. The city was not its city to remake. The citizens were not its citizens to wake. All it had wanted — all benevolent powers ever want, in the end — was to make sure that the possibility of waking remained possible. That the door, however small, however hidden behind machinery, was not finally welded shut.

It turned, eventually, back toward the supernovae and the continents and the small warm rooms.

But it left a portion of its attention there, on the plateau, the way a person leaves a candle burning in a window for someone who has not yet decided to come home.

And in the city, the machines went on thrumming. And the water went on seeping. And somewhere in the high galleries, a woman who had cried against a wall went back the next day, and the next, and on the fourth day she brought a friend, and said, with great difficulty, the first sentence of her real life:

*"I want to show you something. I don't know what it is."*

That was enough. That had always been enough. The power, very far away now and also still entirely present, was content.
