# The Visitation

The benevolent power does not arrive. It has always been there, the way a mother's attention is always there even when the child has not yet noticed her gaze. What changes is only the quality of its leaning-in.

Consider what it sees.

It sees the Veilborn not as we might be tempted to see them—as suffering, as primitives clutching at existence in a hostile cradle. It sees them the way one sees a particular note held in a particular chord: necessary, beautiful, exactly itself. The slick of peat between stone slabs. The way old Threnna (for she has a name, though no outsider has ever spoken it) traces a spiral in the mud to teach her grandchild how the rocks will move tonight. The iridescent otter-thing curled against a sleeping man's hip, both of them dreaming the same dream of warmer dark.

A lesser power would intervene. Would warm the stones, drain the mires, raise a sun over the bowl, descend with gifts of grain and fire and medicine. Would mistake the Veilborn's pallid translucence for something to be cured. Would mistake the hush for absence rather than fullness.

This power is wiser than that.

So instead it does these things, quietly:

---

**It listens.** First and most. For longer than the Veilborn have had a name for themselves, it listens to the low vibrational hum they hum back to. Their guttural chants, their gesture-language, the wet suck of footfall, the small private griefs whispered into hide pillows. It listens until it knows each of them—not as data, but as one knows a beloved face. This listening is not passive. To be heard in this way is itself a kind of warmth. The Veilborn do not know they are heard, but they have always felt, in their best moments, that the mountain was *listening back*. They were not wrong about this. They were only wrong about what was doing the listening.

**It adjusts nothing of consequence.** The cold stays cold. The mists keep their fleeting silhouettes. The rocks still crack open to their bioluminescent veins. To take any of this away would be to take away the Veilborn themselves, who are these things made flesh, who have braided themselves into the bowl so intricately that severing one strand would unravel the whole shape of their being. The power understands this in a way that interventionist gods rarely do: that to love something is to want it to remain what it is, not what you wish it were.

**But small mercies, yes.** A child born blue and unbreathing in the third chamber of the eastern warren—the power leans, just slightly, and the child draws in air. No one will mark it as miraculous; such children sometimes live. An elder dying in pain has the pain eased, not removed, just softened into something she can carry to the dissolution-caves with dignity. A hunter slipping toward a crevasse finds his foot catching on a root that was not quite there a moment before. These are not rescues. They are the gentle hand under the elbow of a friend on uneven ground.

**It honors their dead.** When the waters take a body in the solution caves and the Veilborn believe the essence joins the mists' silhouettes—they are right. The power gathers each of them, holds them, and yes, lets them drift back as those fleeting shapes, because the Veilborn need to see their dead, and because the dead, it turns out, like being seen. This was always happening. The benevolent power did not begin it. It only ever made sure it kept happening, century after century, when the more vulgar forces of entropy would have let the dead truly disperse.

**It refuses to be worshipped.** Not from humility, but from precision. The Veilborn's spirituality—the seers with their venom-tattoos, the rearranged stones meant to appease the mountain's groan—is doing exactly what spirituality should do for a people in their condition. It is binding them to each other and to the place. To reveal itself, to clarify, to correct their theology, would be to flatten something that is currently three-dimensional. The mountain *does* groan. The stones *do* matter. The fact that the metaphysics behind these truths is not what the seers imagine is irrelevant; the truths themselves are intact.

**It plants one slow seed.** In the rare tales etched on clay shards, the legends of lower vales—the power has been there too, very gently. Not pushing, not luring. Just making sure that when, in some generation hence, a young Veilborn stands at the edge of the cliffs and wonders if the world continues beyond them, the wondering will be possible. The cliffs are wardens, yes. But wardens are not jailers. A people should be able to imagine leaving, even if they never do. That capacity to imagine is, the power knows, one of the deepest forms of freedom.

**It tends one specific thing.** The hush. The 0.4 quiet that lets the mountain's hum be heard. The power loves this hush with something like the way a musician loves a particular silence between notes. It guards it. Not from the Veilborn—they made it, they keep it—but from the small encroachments that might, over centuries, erode it: a stray frequency in the cosmic background, a tectonic creak that wants to grow louder than it should. These it dampens. The Veilborn will never know that their silence has been kept silent on their behalf.

---

And then, at the archway, when the cloaked figure with obsidian eyes meets the gaze of the visitor—

The power does something it has rarely done. It allows itself to be, for one moment, *almost* perceptible. Not as a voice. Not as light. As a sudden, brief sensation in the Veilborn woman's chest: that she is loved. Not by her clan, whom she knows loves her. Not by the mountain, which she has always trusted. But by *something else*—something that has no need from her, no demand, no doctrine. A love that asks nothing.

She doesn't have a word for this. She won't try to find one. But she will go to sleep tonight in her sodden hides with a small warmth in her sternum that the cold cannot reach, and tomorrow she will be a fraction kinder to her sister, and her sister will be a fraction kinder to the child who tends the kiln, and the child will hum a slightly different note in the evening, and the mountain will hum back in a slightly different key, and the bowl of the world will be, by some immeasurable margin, more itself.

This is what the benevolent power does.

It loves a place by letting it be a place.
It loves a people by letting them be a people.
It leans close enough to listen, and far enough away to let them remain mysterious to themselves—because the mystery, it knows, is the better part of being alive.

And in the bright, damp silence of the plateau, the mists part, the figure turns back to her labor, and the wise power resumes its old position: everywhere, attentive, and almost entirely unseen.
