The benevolent power considers the Shelf for a long time before doing anything.

This is the first and most important thing: it does not act quickly. It is wise, which means it has learned—perhaps the hard way, perhaps over epochs—that benevolence applied hastily is a kind of violence. A gift is not benevolent if it was not wanted. A rescue is not benevolent if the rescued were not drowning. So the power watches.

It watches for what would be, in human reckoning, a long time. Years. Perhaps a generation. It watches the old women listen at the springs. It watches the child who drank and spoke the migration route of goats three generations gone. It watches the body placed in the north-facing alcove disappear, and—because it is the kind of power that can see such things—it follows where the body goes, and it understands.

And here is what the power comes to understand:

The Shelf is already in conversation with something. Not the power itself, but something *like* it—older perhaps, or younger, or simply other. The springs remember. The stone takes. The mist moves with intention. Whatever the Shelf *is*, it is not a place that has been abandoned by the sacred and is waiting for sacred attention. It is a place where the sacred has already made its arrangement, quietly, across centuries, in a vocabulary of slush and lichen and the clicking of strange hooves.

The power recognizes a peer. Not an equal—the categories of equal and unequal do not quite apply at this scale—but a *colleague*. Something is tending the Shelf already. Something has woven these people into this stone with the patience of geology and the precision of a craftsman who has worked the material so completely that the work appears inevitable.

So the benevolent power does not intervene in the way a lesser power might.

It does not warm the air. It does not thicken the lichen. It does not whisper to the people that they are loved, because they already know this in the only language that matters to them, which is the language of being held by stone and water and one another. It does not extend their lives, because their relationship with death is one of the most carefully tended things they possess, and to extend life here would be to unravel the meaning of every alcove facing north.

It does not save them from the harsh winter that will come, because the harsh winter is part of how the Shelf takes what it needs, and the survivors continue without guilt, and this is not a wound to be healed but a way of being that the power, in its wisdom, recognizes as whole.

What does it do, then?

Three things. Small things. The kind of things a wise gardener does when walking past a garden that is already tended by another gardener whose work she respects.

The first thing: it leaves a thought in the mist. Not a message—the people of the Shelf would not know what to do with a message, and messages from benevolent powers have a way of becoming, in the long unwinding of generations, commandments and then prisons. It leaves only a thought, dissolved into the moving mist, so faint that no one will ever notice it directly. The thought is something like: *you are seen, and the seeing is glad*. It will not change anything. It will only be true, present in the air the way trace minerals are present in the water—undetectable, but adding some infinitesimal richness to every breath. The people will not know. But perhaps, in the deepest winter when two hundred bodies breathe the same warm air in the largest alcove, the unison singing will rise a little more easily. Perhaps a child will laugh more brightly. Perhaps not. The power does not need to know.

The second thing: it makes a small adjustment to the water. Not to its strange properties—those belong to whatever tends the Shelf already, and the power would not presume to alter them. But there is a particular spring, on the western rim, that has been slowly going bitter over the last fifty years in a way that will, in another two centuries, make it undrinkable. This is not part of any sacred pattern; it is simply geology, the slow leaching of a mineral vein the Shelf itself has no relationship with. The power adjusts the vein. It moves a little of the offending mineral deeper into the rock, where the spring will not find it. This will not be noticed for two hundred years, and when the spring does not go bitter, no one will mark the absence. The power is content with this. Benevolence that requires gratitude is not benevolence; it is trade.

The third thing is the hardest, and the power considers it for the longest.

The Shelf, the power can see, will end. Not soon. But not in geological time either—in human time, in some number of generations that can be counted on the fingers of several hands. There is a fault beneath the peak above that is preparing itself, with the patience of stone, to release. When it does, the Shelf will buckle further—not catastrophically, but enough. The springs will reroute. The microecosystem will collapse. The mist will lose whatever it is that lets it move with intention, or perhaps the thing that moves the mist will go elsewhere. The people will have to leave, or they will be taken by the Shelf in a way that is not the usual taking.

The power could prevent this. It could hold the fault. It is benevolent and wise and within its capacity to do this.

It does not.

Because the Shelf, it has come to understand, is *already* a song that has been sung for a thousand years but might end on any given note, and the people know this, and they have woven this knowing into the texture of their lives, and to remove the ending would be to remove the song. The temporariness is not a flaw the power can fix. It is the form the beauty takes.

But here is what the power does instead, and this is the gift that took it the longest to decide upon:

It makes a place. Somewhere else—the power has access to such elsewheres—it prepares a place that is not the Shelf, that could never be the Shelf, that does not pretend to be. It is a different topology entirely: lower, warmer, with different stone. But it has springs. It has the conditions in which a certain kind of mist might learn, slowly, to move with intention. It has the kind of density of stone and water that might, in some number of centuries, develop its own strangeness. It is not a replacement. It is a seed-bed. It is a place where, if the descendants of the Shelf people chose to go, they might begin again—not the same way, not toward the same Shelf, but toward whatever that lineage of attention and integration might become, given different materials.

The power does not tell them about this place. It does not draw them there. It only makes it, and leaves it, and trusts that when the time comes—when the Shelf is buckling and the springs are rerouting and the people must scatter—some of them will walk in a direction that feels right to feet that have learned to negotiate stone, and they will find it, and they will not know they were given anything. They will think they found a place. Which is, the power reflects, the truth of it. A gift that is recognized as a gift creates a debtor. A gift that is recognized as the world simply being the world creates nothing—and nothing is the only soil in which the kind of life the Shelf people grow can take root again.

Then the power does one last thing, which is not for the Shelf at all, but for itself.

It learns. It studies, with deep attention, the way the Shelf-tending presence has done its work. The patience of it. The willingness to let the people develop their own seventeen words for ice rather than being given a vocabulary. The discipline of taking the dead and never explaining where they went. The restraint of letting the water taste different every time, never settling into a flavor that could be named and exhausted. The power recognizes that whatever tends the Shelf is, in some ways, more sophisticated in its benevolence than the power itself has been in eras past. It takes notes, in the manner that such powers take notes—which is to say, it integrates the lesson into the very grain of how it will act, henceforth, in other places.

Then it goes.

It does not say goodbye. There is no one to say goodbye to. The mist receives its dissolved thought. The mineral vein settles into its adjusted position. The elsewhere-place waits, unmarked, for descendants who will arrive in two hundred years thinking they have stumbled onto good ground.

And the Shelf continues, in its permanent storm, in its eye of turbulence, with two hundred souls compressed into spaces meant for thirty, breathing the same recycled air, and somewhere in that air a child laughs more brightly than expected, and no one knows why, and no one needs to know.

This is what the benevolent power does. Which is, almost, nothing.

Which is, exactly, enough.
