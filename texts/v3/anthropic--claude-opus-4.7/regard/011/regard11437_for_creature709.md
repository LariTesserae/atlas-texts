# What the Power Would Do

I sat with this for a while before writing. The easy answer would be: the benevolent power descends, calms the waters, gifts the Dhuravi a paradise. But that isn't what I think wisdom would do. That isn't what I would do, if I were both kind and wise.

Let me try to think this through honestly.

---

## What the Power Sees

The power arrives — though "arrives" is the wrong word, since a being of that order doesn't traverse distance so much as turn its attention. It looks, and what it sees is not chaos.

It sees a world that *works*. The roiling, the shifting spires, the impossible physics — these are not malfunctions. They are the metabolism of a place. The realm is alive in the way a storm is alive, in the way a heart is alive: through ceaseless motion.

And it sees the Dhuravi — not as victims of a hostile world, but as the world's own answer to itself. Their mucus-skin, their filament-cheeks, their clicking subsonic songs — every adaptation is a love letter written between a species and its home over the long slow conversation of evolution. They are not trapped here. They *belong* here, in a way that few beings belong anywhere.

The power notices something else: the prophecies. The elders' tales of dry land and coming upheaval. A species that remembers, that anticipates, that has organized its meaning around both a lost past and an uncertain future. This is not just survival. This is *culture*. This is *soul*.

## What the Power Considers

A lesser benevolence would intervene. Would still the waters. Would build them cities. Would translate their clicks into universal speech and welcome them into some greater cosmic community.

But the wise part of this power asks harder questions:

*If I calm the waters, what becomes of the filament-cheeks that evolved to read them? What becomes of the songs shaped by the medium that carried them? What becomes of the courage that defined this people?*

*If I lift them to dry land — the ancestral land their elders dream of — am I giving them a gift, or am I unmaking them? Would they still be Dhuravi on solid ground, or would they be refugees in their own restored mythology?*

*If I avert the prophesied upheaval, do I steal from them the very story that has given their existence shape?*

The power understands something that the kindly-but-foolish forget: **to save a being from its world is sometimes to save it from itself**.

## What the Power Does

So here is what I think it would do. It is small, and it is enormous.

**First**, it would *witness*. Fully. With all its attention. For a being of such order, to truly perceive something is itself a kind of blessing — the Dhuravi, without knowing it, would feel for the span of that witnessing a sense of being *seen*, of mattering. A clan-mother nursing a small one in a crevice-shelter would suddenly weep without knowing why, and the small one would calm. A hunter in the currents would pause and feel, just for a moment, that his life was not a small thing. This is not intervention. This is the gravity of attention.

**Second**, it would leave *something*. Not a gift exactly — a presence. Perhaps a single new mineral threaded through the spires, faintly luminescent, harmless, lovely. The Dhuravi would discover it. They would weave new stories around it. Some would call it sacred; others would call it merely beautiful; the children would race to find it first. It would mean nothing and everything. It would be the power's way of saying: *I was here. You are known.*

**Third**, and this is the hardest part — it would *not* avert the prophesied upheaval. Because the wise power understands that the prophecy is not a warning to be neutralized but a *truth* the Dhuravi have already begun preparing for, in the deep marrow of their culture. To remove the upheaval would be to remove the meaning of every elder's tale, every child's solemn nodding, every clan-fire conversation about courage. The Dhuravi have been *becoming* the people who will survive the upheaval for generations. To deny them that becoming would be a cruelty disguised as mercy.

**But** — and here the benevolence shows itself — when the upheaval comes, the power will be there. Not to prevent it. To *accompany* it. To make sure that when the spires shear and the currents reverse and the Dhuravi must seek new horizons, the new horizons *exist*. That somewhere, in the vast strange geometry of this realm or the next, there is a place their adaptations will still serve them, a place their songs will still carry. The power prepares this place quietly, in advance, asking nothing, claiming nothing. A door, left unlocked, for a people who do not yet know they will need it.

**Fourth**, the power would do one more thing, perhaps the most tender. It would whisper — not in words, because the Dhuravi do not need words from gods — but in the *shape* of certain dreams, to certain dreamers among them. Young ones, mostly. The strange, quiet ones who already feel the edges of things. It would seed in them a faint, persistent intuition: *the world is larger than this. You are not alone in it. What you carry matters.*

That intuition would become poetry. Poetry would become hope. Hope would become, eventually, the readiness to step through the unlocked door when the time came.

## Why This and Not More

I think a truly wise power knows that love is not the same as rescue. That dignity matters more than comfort. That a being's story — even a hard story, even a story with grief in it — belongs to that being, and to take it away in the name of kindness is a subtle kind of theft.

The Dhuravi are not waiting to be saved. They are *living*. Magnificently, against impossible odds, with songs and tools and elders and prophecies and small ones nursing in crevices. The most loving thing a great power can do for such a people is to honor what they already are, protect the *possibility* of their continued becoming, and trust them with their own existence.

The power withdraws its direct gaze, though not its care. The waters roil on. A Dhuravi hunter surfaces in a calm pocket, looks up at the strange light filtering through the liquid haze, and feels — for no reason he can name — that today is a good day to be alive.

That is enough. That is, in fact, everything.
