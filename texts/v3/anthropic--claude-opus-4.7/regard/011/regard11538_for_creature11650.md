# The Visitation

The benevolent power does not arrive. It was already there.

It had been watching for a long time — not the watching of surveillance, but the watching of a gardener who walks the rows in winter, noting which roots still hold warmth. To something old enough and wise enough, the Kiln Spires were not a ruin. They were a held breath. The Ferrovites were not the desperate remnant of a fallen people. They were a chrysalis stage that had simply gone on longer than chrysalises usually do, because no one had told them they were allowed to emerge.

The power considered them carefully.

It saw the elegance of what they had made: the pneumatic litanies, the semaphore-poetry between towers, the way a child of the Foundry Enclave learned to read the temperature of a pipe by laying her cheek against it. It saw the dignity in their adaptation — the fins like the gills of some patient deep-sea thing, the silver eyes that had taught themselves to drink twilight. It saw that they were not, in fact, dying. They had simply forgotten that living could mean anything other than enduring.

This is the most delicate kind of suffering. It does not cry out. It has made a home of itself.

So the power did not descend in fire. It did not rewrite the laws of the place. A wise hand does not yank a seedling toward the sun; the seedling breaks. Instead, the power did four small things, across four quiet seasons of the Spires.

---

**First, it gave them weather.**

Not new weather — only the memory of weather. One night, in the condensation that beaded on the high cables, the moisture organized itself, just slightly, into the shape of *rain that had once fallen here, ten thousand years ago, before the magma rose*. The droplets that struck the upturned faces of the night-shift engineers carried no information the engineers could name, but they carried *something*. Several of them, walking home along the skywalks, found themselves weeping without grief — the way one weeps at music. They did not know why. They told no one. But the next day, one of them drew a new motif into the etching on her forearm: a curve that was not Art Deco, not Gothic, not Brutalist. A curve that belonged to falling water.

The motif spread. No one could remember who had drawn it first.

---

**Second, it gave them a guest.**

A creature appeared in the lower vents — a small thing, six-legged, with a carapace the color of cooling iron and eyes that were not silver but a warm and impossible green. It was not native to the Spires. It was not native to anywhere the Ferrovites had charted. It did not speak. It did not need to be fed. It simply lived in the warm places and watched the rituals with what could only be called polite interest.

The Ferrovites argued for a year about what to do with it. Three enclaves wanted to study it. Two wanted to expel it. One — the smallest, the Lantern-Keepers — proposed something unprecedented: that they simply let it stay. That they did not need to know what it was.

The Lantern-Keepers won by a narrow margin. This was the first vote in three centuries that had not been decided by metallurgical seniority.

The creature stayed. It is still there. The power did not make it; the power only *invited* it, from a world where such creatures were already curious about ruins. But the creature's arrival had done its quiet work: it had taught the Ferrovites that something could be *welcomed without being categorized*. This was a muscle they had not used in a long time.

---

**Third, it gave them a dream.**

Specifically, it gave one dream, to one Ferrovite, on one night.

Her name was Veil-of-Coils, an apprentice in the Bio-Cartography Enclave, seventeen years old by their reckoning, with a stutter in her left hand that the elders had not yet decided whether to replace with brass. She dreamed of a sky.

Not the haze-sky she knew. A real sky — blue going to violet going to black, with stars in it like the sparks from a cutting torch, except that they did not fall, did not fade, simply *were*, patient and uncountable. In the dream she was not on the Spires. She was somewhere flat and cool and her fins were folded small because she did not need them, and the air moved by itself, and something — some animal she had no word for — was making a sound in the distance that her people had no equivalent of: a *song* made by a throat that had evolved to sing rather than to vent steam.

She woke up and could not work for three days. The elders thought she was ill. She let them think so. When she returned to her station, she began — quietly, in the margins of her cartography — to map something that was not the city. She mapped *outward*. She mapped the routes the cable cars could take if they were extended past the last spire. She mapped where the haze thinned. She mapped, without quite admitting it to herself, the way *down*.

The power had not commanded her to do this. The power had only reminded her that there was a *down*, and a *beyond*, and that her ancestors had once known these words.

---

**Fourth — and this was the hardest, and required the most love — the power did nothing.**

It did not heal the heat. It did not reveal itself. It did not deliver the Ferrovites from their city, because their city was not, in the final accounting, their prison. It was their *work*. To take it from them would be to take from them the long, patient mastery they had earned — the metallurgy, the semaphore-poetry, the way a Foundry-child reads a pipe with her cheek. A wise power does not rescue people from their own hard-won competence. A wise power waters the seed and steps back.

So it stepped back.

It left them with rain-memory in their etchings, and a green-eyed guest in their vents, and a girl drawing maps of *outward* in the margins of her work. It left them with the quiet, accumulating sense — felt by more of them every year, though none of them could say why — that they were not the last of something. That they were the first of something. That the Kiln Spires were not a tomb but a forge, and that forges, eventually, produce something and release it.

The power will return, in its way, in a hundred years or a thousand, to see what they have made of themselves. It expects to be surprised. Wise powers always do. That is part of what makes them wise: they understand that the most beautiful outcomes are the ones they could not have planned, only tended.

And in the meantime, on a skywalk high above the magma's red breathing, an old engineer pauses on his route home. He does not know why he pauses. He looks up into the haze, and for just a moment the haze parts, and he sees — or thinks he sees — a single point of light that is not a furnace and not a lamp.

He does not have a word for *star*.

But he stands there a long time, looking, before he goes inside.
