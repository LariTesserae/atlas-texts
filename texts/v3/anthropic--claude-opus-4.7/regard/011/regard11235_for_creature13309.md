# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent power arrives without arriving. It does not descend in light through the ash-haze, because light through ash-haze would be theater, and this power has no need of theater. It simply turns its attention toward the Hole the way one turns one's face toward a sound in another room — with interest, with care, and with the patience of something that has all the time there is.

The first thing it does is *look*. Not glance. Not assess. **Look**, for a long while, the way one might look at a person one loves before speaking.

And what does it see?

It sees what a careless visitor would call grim: the soot, the damp, the shortened lives, the absence of birds. It sees the bent backs of the elders and the children miming valve-work with grave little faces. It sees the leathery skin and the hard eyes and the curt shouts that pass for conversation.

But because it is *wise*, it sees more.

It sees that the Tender of the Steam who stands at the great south regulator has a particular way of laying her palm against the iron just below the gauge — not to feel the temperature, which she already knows, but to greet the machine, the way a shepherd touches the flank of a familiar animal in the dark. It sees that the curt shouts between Machinesmiths carry, in their cadence and timing, a grammar of trust as intricate as any sonnet. It sees that when old Bevin, whose back is bent nearly double, descends into the lower works to listen, the younger smiths fall silent around him not from deference alone but because they love him, and they have no word for that love, and do not need one, because the silence *is* the word.

It sees that the children's games are not pale imitations of toil but the actual transmission of a sacred craft, joyful in the way only inherited competence is joyful.

It sees the spiders in the weeping corners, and notes that they too are tenders, in their way.

It sees that the people of the Hole would not have it any other way, and it takes this seriously. It does not patronize them by assuming they have been deceived about their own happiness. A wise power knows the difference between a cage and a vocation, and the Hole, it understands, is a vocation — strange, narrow, sooty, but real, and chosen anew each generation by hands that could, in some other life, have chosen otherwise and did not.

So it does not lift them out. It does not flood the Hole with sunlight or fill the sky with birds or replace the grinding engines with quiet, clean ones that need no tending. To do so would be to murder the people in order to save them — to take from them the thing that makes them *themselves*.

What, then, does it do?

## Small things. Precise things. The things a wise power does.

**It eases what does not need to be hard.** The lungs of the Machinesmiths have, for generations, accumulated a fine grit that shortens their lives by perhaps fifteen years on average. The power does nothing dramatic about this. It simply — quietly, over a season — alters the chemistry of the steam, very slightly, so that the particulates bind to the vapor and fall as harmless sediment in the lower drains, where the Tenders sweep them up and think only that the spring has shifted its mood, as springs do. No one notices they are breathing easier. They only notice, years later, that Bevin is still alive, and his father before him lived longer than expected, and somehow there are more elders in the council than there used to be. More living libraries. More transmitted knowledge. The Hole grows, almost imperceptibly, *deeper in itself*.

**It does not abolish the spiders. It blesses them.** The power has a fondness for what is already there. The spiders in the ceiling corners — it gives them, without ceremony, a faint phosphorescence, the way certain deep-sea creatures glow. Just enough that on the longest, dampest nights, a child looking up from her shelf-bed sees a constellation of small green lights in the rafters, and feels, without being able to say why, less alone. The Machinesmiths will eventually develop a word for these lights. It will be a working word, functional, perhaps something like *roof-stars* or *web-lamps*. But it will be the first word in their language that was not strictly necessary, and from that small surplus, in a hundred years, other unnecessary words will grow.

**It listens with them.** The old Tenders who press their ears to the pipes to diagnose the engines — the power listens *alongside* them. It does not whisper diagnoses; that would rob them of their craft. But when a young Tender, terrified, presses her ear to a thundering housing and cannot tell what is wrong, sometimes — not always, because always would be a crutch — she finds her attention drawn, as if by a gentle hand on her chin, to the precise quarter-second tick beneath the roar that tells her: *bearing, third stage, west.* She will think she figured it out herself. She will be partly right. The mastery is hers; the power has only loaned her, for an instant, the patience to find it.

**It honors the dead.** No one in the Hole has ever quite known what to do about the dead, beyond the practical. There is no soil for burial; there is no sky for pyres that would not choke the district. The bodies go, traditionally, into the deepest vent, where the earth-heat takes them. The power does not change this. But it ensures — quietly, in a way no one will ever prove — that the heat which returns up the pipes after such a committal carries, for a few hours, a particular faint sweetness. Not perfume. Something stranger and kinder: the smell of a thing remembered. The Tenders on shift that day will not speak of it, because they do not have words for it, but they will work in a softer silence, and they will go home and hold their children a moment longer than usual, and they will not know why.

**It does not tell them they are loved.** This is perhaps the hardest restraint, and the most important. To be *told* one is loved by a vast benevolent power is, for a proud and self-sufficient people, an insult disguised as a gift. It implies they needed telling. The power does not tell them. It simply loves them, and arranges its small interventions so that the love is indistinguishable from the ordinary good fortune of being alive in a place one understands. The Hole will go on believing it is forgotten by the wider world, and it will be right about the wider world, and wrong about the deeper one, and this wrongness will be a quiet companion to it forever.

---

And then, having done these things, the power does the last and best thing a wise power can do:

**It goes away. And it keeps looking back.**

It does not linger. It does not hover. It does not make of the Hole a pet or a project. It returns its attention to the great breadth of things that need attention. But some part of it remains turned, always, toward that sunken arena under the ash-haze, the way a parent at a party keeps half an ear toward the room where the children sleep — not anxiously, not possessively, but with the steady, undramatic faithfulness that is the actual texture of love when love has grown up.

And in the Hole, the pistons thunder, and the steam hisses, and Bevin lives another year, and a child looks up at the green lights in the rafters, and a Tender lays her palm against warm iron and greets it, and no one knows, and everyone is — in the only way that matters — known.
