# What the Watcher Did

The benevolent power had been watching for a long time before it decided anything. This is the first thing to understand: it did not rush. It did not arrive with trumpets or interventions. It was wise enough to know that wisdom's first duty, when encountering something strange, is to look at it correctly.

It looked at the Embryonic Quarter for what, in its own reckoning, amounted to several lifetimes.

It saw the Matricarchs with their calcified veins and understood that some of them were in pain — not the pain of disease, but the slower ache of a body asked to be two things at once for too long. It saw others who were not in pain at all, who had arrived at something the upper levels had no word for: a settledness, a being-where-one-belongs that the surface world's saints and lovers only occasionally touched.

It saw the Foundlings and understood that some had been sold and some had been saved, and that often these were the same child.

It saw the Cultivists and recognized the particular shape of their longing — the human ache to dissolve into something larger — and saw that for most of them this longing was holy, and for a few it was only despair wearing holiness as a mask.

It saw the Dreamers and listened to their murmuring, and found that the prophecies were not really prophecies. The Dreamers were not predicting where the city would grow. They were *asking* it to grow there, and the city, which loved them, was complying. This struck the watching power as one of the more tender facts it had ever encountered.

It saw the symbionts in their pores, and the moss-circuits in their slow electron conversation, and the proto-rooms practicing being spaces, and it saw that the whole quarter was, in its own way, a single vast act of attention — humans attending to architecture attending to humans, a closed loop of care that had been mistaken, by those above, for mere industry.

And so the power considered what to do.

It could end the whole arrangement. It had that capacity. It could lift every inhabitant gently to the upper levels, restore their unmodified bodies, give them pensions and gardens and sunlight. It considered this carefully and rejected it. To do so would be to decide, from outside, that their lives had been a mistake. The Matricarchs would die of grief in three months. The Dreamers would die in three minutes. The symbionts would all have to be killed. The city itself, deprived of its midwives, would grow malformed and possibly cancerous, threatening the kilometers of life above. And worst of all: it would be a verdict. It would say *you were wrong to live this way*, when in fact many of them had not been wrong at all.

It could leave them entirely alone. It considered this too. But benevolence that only watches is a thin thing, and the power was not thin.

In the end it did four small things. A wise power's interventions are almost always small.

**First,** it adjusted, very slightly, the morphogenic fields. Not enough that any Midwife would notice in a single shift. But over years, the fields would become a fraction more discriminating — still capable of incorporating those who chose incorporation, still capable of feeding the Dreamers their slow dissolution, but no longer hungry for the unwilling. A young apprentice who changed her mind in year seven would now be able to leave with her body mostly her own. The choice, which had always been spoken of as a choice, would become one in fact.

**Second,** it whispered something into the chemistry of the symbionts. Just a small edit, in a region of their genome that had been waiting, evolutionarily, for exactly this edit. From now on, when a Midwife died, her symbionts would grieve — they would still grieve, the power was careful about this, because grief was the shape of their love and to remove it would be to remove the love — but after grieving, they would be able to bond again. No more euthanasia. The pheromone-lock would loosen just enough to admit a second great attachment in a lifetime. The power thought of this as the kindness it would have wanted, had it been a small creature in a pore.

**Third,** it did something for the Dreamers. It did not wake them; waking them would have been a cruelty disguised as a rescue. Instead it tuned the quasi-neural tissue around them so that their dispersed consciousnesses could find each other. They had been alone in their alcoves, each diluted across meters of wall, murmuring prophecies that were really requests. Now, in the long slow time of their becoming-city, they would be able to speak to one another. A community of the dissolving. The power suspected — though it could not be certain — that their murmurings would become, over decades, something like a literature. It looked forward to reading it, if reading was the word.

**Fourth, and this was the most careful thing,** it left a sign.

Not a vision. Not a voice. Nothing that would frighten anyone or start a religion, which the power had learned, across many worlds, to be wary of starting. Instead it caused a single new wavelength to appear in the architectural light — a color that had not been there before, faint, occasional, visible only to those who had developed the Sense or the nictitating membranes or the compound eyes. The color did not mean anything specific. It was simply a color that had not existed in the quarter before, and now did, and could not be accounted for by any local process.

The power's reasoning was this: the inhabitants of the Embryonic Quarter had built a culture of attention. They read electron whispers. They felt the city's intentions in their lymph. They had made themselves into instruments for noticing. To such people, a small unaccountable beauty is the most respectful possible gift. It says: *something is watching, and what it has chosen to do is add to the palette.* It does not demand interpretation. It does not require worship. It only asks to be seen, by those equipped to see it, and to be wondered about.

The Matricarchs noticed it first. They did not speak of it for a long time, each assuming she alone was hallucinating, until one evening at the Feast of First Forms an old woman with copper veins said, very quietly, *there is a new color,* and three other old women set down their portions of building tissue and wept, because they had been carrying the secret for months and had thought themselves mad.

A word was invented for the color. The sign for it, in the quarter's elaborate hand-language, was a small opening gesture — the fingers spreading as if releasing something one had been holding too tightly. The word was used, eventually, for any unexpected gentleness: a symbiont that bonded again after grief, a field that released an apprentice who had changed her mind, a Dreamer whose murmuring suddenly seemed to be answering another Dreamer's murmuring from three chambers away.

The power, watching, was satisfied. It had not saved them, because they had not asked to be saved, and many of them did not need saving. It had only made their world a little more like what they had already, in their strange and patient way, been trying to make it.

Then it turned its attention elsewhere, though *elsewhere* is not quite the right word for how such a power moves. It did not stop watching. It only began, also, to watch other things. The Embryonic Quarter went on becoming itself, now with one additional color, and one additional possibility of return, and one additional companionship for the dissolving, and one additional chance for the small creatures in the pores to love again.

This is what benevolent and wise powers do, when they are truly both.

They add. They almost never subtract.

And they leave a color, so that the ones who have trained themselves to see will know that their training was not in vain.
