They watch for a long time before they do anything.

This is the first thing to understand about the benevolent and wise power: that benevolence, if it is wise, begins in restraint. It does not arrive with gifts. It does not arrive with rescue. It arrives, if it arrives at all, as attention — a quality of noticing so complete that the noticed thing is not altered by being seen.

So: the power notices the plateau. It notices the pika.

It notices the granite that has finished its becoming, the pools that breathe in and out with the dew, the wind that is the silence of the place, the hawks set in their patient V. It notices the haypile growing stalk by stalk in a crevice on the western face. It notices the particular pika who built it — a female, two summers old, her left ear nicked from a fight she does not remember the cause of, her body holding the small economy of muscle and fat that will or will not be enough.

The power considers.

It is within its capacity to do many things. It could warm the plateau — slightly, kindly — so the winter would not howl. It could thin the hawks. It could make the grass grow thicker in the cracks. It could carry the pika in a cupped hand to a valley where life is easier, where food is abundant and the wind does not strip the warmth from small bodies. It could give the pika understanding: a sudden flowering of awareness, the gift of knowing what she is and where, the gift of language, of memory beyond the worn path, of a future that is not only the haypile.

It could do any of these. It does none of them.

Because the wisdom in the power is the older sister of the benevolence, and the wisdom sees what the benevolence alone might miss: that the pika is not suffering. That the pika is not waiting to be rescued. That the plateau is not a problem the universe has failed to solve.

The pika is a solution. She is the answer the plateau has been working out for a very long time — how to be alive in a place where the wind is the fact and the cold is coming. She is not a creature trapped in a hard life. She is a creature exactly equal to her life, fitted to it the way a key is fitted to a lock, and the fitting is not a tragedy but a kind of music. To warm the plateau would be to unmake her. To carry her to the valley would be to carry away the only thing she is. To give her language would be to give her a wound where no wound had been, the wound of knowing, the wound of comparison, the wound of a future she had never needed to fear because she had never needed to imagine.

The benevolent power understands this. The benevolent power has, in fact, made many mistakes in younger ages, and has learned.

So what does it do?

It does three small things. Perhaps four.

The first is that it simply continues to watch. This sounds like nothing. It is not nothing. There is a quality of being witnessed that does not change the witnessed thing but completes it — the way a sound is not fully a sound until it has been heard, the way a life is not fully a life until someone, somewhere, has known it occurred. The pika does not need to be witnessed in order to live. But she is witnessed, now, and this is a fact about the universe that was not true before, and the universe is the better for it. Her small calls into the wind are heard. Her sprints between gusts are seen. Her haypile, growing stalk by stalk, is held in an attention that does not waver. She will never know this. It does not matter that she will never know.

The second thing is harder to describe. The power does not intervene in the plateau, but it does something at the edges — at the level of probability, at the level of the small adjustments that are indistinguishable from luck. It does not save the pika from the hawk. The hawk must eat; the hawk's life is also a solution the plateau has worked out. But on a particular afternoon in the late season, when the pika is carrying a mouthful of dried grass back to her crevice and a gust comes earlier than she expected, the power adjusts — by some quantity too small to measure — the angle of the wind, so that she is pushed toward the rocks rather than away from them. She reaches the crevice. She adds the grass to the pile. She does not know that anything has happened, because nothing has happened, in the sense that the universe records happenings. The wind blew. The pika ran. The grass was added. And yet: a kindness occurred, so small it left no trace, and the power is satisfied that this is the right size of kindness for this place.

It does this perhaps a dozen times across the pika's life. Not enough to change the shape of her existence. Just enough that, in the aggregate, she lives slightly closer to the better edge of her possibilities than to the worse. She does not become a different pika. She becomes more fully the pika she already was. This is the only kind of help the power is willing to offer here: help that is invisible, help that does not displace, help that strengthens the thing being helped in its own direction rather than turning it toward another.

The third thing is for the place itself, though the place did not ask. The power composes — there is no better word — a kind of song, though it is not made of sound. It is made of attention arranged in time. It is the noticing of the plateau given shape, the way a poem is the noticing of a thing given shape. The song says: this exists. This granite, this wind, this pika, this hawk, this pool that fills with dew and gives it back to the air. This haypile growing in a crevice on the western face. This staccato call swallowed by the roar. This is here. This is real. This is enough.

The song is not performed for anyone. It is simply true, and the power lets it be true, and somewhere in the architecture of things — somewhere in the deep ledger where the universe keeps account of what has mattered — the plateau is entered, and the pika is entered, and the entry is precise and complete and will not be lost.

The fourth thing the power does, if it does it, is the smallest and the most uncertain. When the pika dies — and she will, in her third winter, of cold rather than hawk, in the dark of her burrow with the haypile half-eaten beside her — the power is there. Not to save her. Not to extend her. Not to grant her any afterlife she would not recognize as her own. Only to be present, the way a friend is present at a death, so that the ending is not unwitnessed. The pika does not know the power is there. The pika knows only that she is tired, and then less tired, and then the wind, which has been the silence of her whole life, becomes the silence of her death also, which is the same silence, which she has always known.

The power stays a little while after. It watches the burrow. It watches the snow seal the opening. It watches, in the spring, another pika find the crevice and begin a new haypile, using some of the old material, because nothing on the plateau is wasted, because the plateau is a closed economy of small lives passing their materials hand to hand across the generations.

And then the power turns its attention elsewhere — to some other place, some other being, some other small solution the universe has worked out and is quietly continuing to work out — but it does not forget. It does not need to. The plateau is in the song now, and the song is in the power, and the power, being what it is, does not lose what it has loved.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, if it is truly both.

It does almost nothing.

It does it perfectly.
