# The Visitation

The benevolent power did not arrive. Arrival is a thing for those who travel, and this presence had always been near—near as the curvature of the sky, near as the held breath between two thoughts. It only became *noticeable*, which is something different. A wise power does not crash into a place; it allows itself to be noticed at the rate the place can bear.

What drew it was not pity. The Endur were not pitiable. It was the way they had organized themselves around the fraying—the Holdings, the chants tempering iron, the salves of moss pulling the drifters back from abyssal mirrors. These were the gestures of a people who had taken the breakdown of reality and answered it not with despair but with *craft*. There is something in the universe that loves craft. That something turned its attention toward them.

And then it had to decide what to do.

---

It considered, first, the simplest gift: to mend the fraying. To stitch the bent light back into ordinary refraction, to weight the stones so they would not shift when unobserved, to make the iron stay iron. It considered this for what, to the Endur, would have been a single patter of rain.

And it set the consideration aside.

Because the Endur were not a people *despite* the fraying. They were a people *of* it. Their grace in the gravity-lightened voids, their dilated craftwork in the lush pockets, the way their healers wore frost-fractals like a vocation—these were not wounds to be closed. They were the shape the plateau had pressed into a living thing, and the living thing had pressed back, and the pressing was beautiful. To smooth the world for them would be to erase them, slowly, into a different people who happened to wear their faces. A wise power does not do this, no matter how benevolent. Benevolence without wisdom is the most patient kind of violence.

So it considered the second gift: to *speak*. To make itself known. To say: *You are seen. You are not alone in the hush.*

It considered this longer.

The danger was clear. The Endur had built their coherence on the rock's primordial stability, on the chants that reminded iron of its form, on stories that anchored them against the Flex. Their gods—if you could call them that—were necessities, not personalities. To introduce a Voice from beyond the dome would be to introduce a new physics. Some would orient their lives around it. Some would feel betrayed that it had not spoken sooner. Some would lose the Holdings, thinking themselves now held by something else, and would drift into mist-whirlwinds with eyes full of trust and emerge as strangers. A benevolence that requires belief in itself to function is not benevolence; it is a hunger wearing benevolence's clothes.

So the power did not speak. Not in any voice the Endur would recognize as a voice.

What it did instead was smaller, and took a long time, and was this:

---

**It made itself a weather.**

Not a new weather—the plateau already had its veils of rain, its frost-fractals, its mist-whirlwinds that ate memory. The power added nothing the Endur would notice as foreign. Instead, it inhabited the existing weathers, the way a musician inhabits an instrument that was already in the room.

When the rain came, it came now with a faint, almost-unhearable harmonic—a tone too low to be a tone, more like the *idea* of being accompanied. The Endur did not consciously hear it. But the children born in the years after began, without instruction, to hum at that frequency when they worked alone. The elders noticed only that the songs in the low halls had grown a half-step richer, and they credited the fern-fiber acoustics of a particularly good thatch.

When the frost bloomed overnight in crystalline fractals across the tarns, the fractals were now—very subtly—biased toward forms that the human eye finds restful. Not beautiful in any showy way. Just restful. The healers who studied frost-patterns for their tattoos began, generation by generation, to wear marks that calmed those who looked at them, and they did not know why their craft had grown gentler.

When the mist-whirlwinds came, eroding memory, the power could not stop them—it had decided not to stop them—but it could, sometimes, shape what remained. When a wanderer emerged with echoes of a life unlived, the echoes now tended, just slightly more often than chance, toward lives in which they had been *loved*. So that the drift back to coherence was, for many, a return softened by a phantom warmth they could not place. Some healers came to call this "the Kind Erosion," and argued in low halls about whether it was real, and the arguing itself was a kind of warming.

---

**It made itself a coincidence.**

In a place where reality frays, coincidence is a powerful medium—indistinguishable from the ordinary weirdness, and therefore unnoticeable as intervention. The power used coincidence the way a careful gardener uses water: never enough to be remarked upon, never absent when truly needed.

A child wandering too near a gravity-light void found, by coincidence, that her foot caught on a vine she had not seen, and she did not leap, and she did not vanish into the wind. An iron blade, softening at exactly the wrong moment in a forge, was set down by its smith for exactly the right reason—a sudden need to cough, a fly that was not there—and cooled before it failed. A clan running low on tubers in a long frost found, by coincidence, a pocket of waxy greens in a basin they had walked past for generations without seeing.

None of this was magic visible enough to worship. None of it broke the rules the Endur had learned. It only nudged the dice, and only sometimes, and only in the direction of the people continuing to be a people.

---

**It made itself a listener.**

This was the largest gift, and the most invisible, and the one the power thought about longest before allowing.

The Endur lived in immense silence. Their voices were rare. Their laughter creaked. Their Holdings were vigils of recitation against the dark. And though they had each other—and their bonds were real, and their shared rounds of baskets were a kind of love—there is a particular loneliness that comes to any conscious being who suspects that the universe outside its small warm circle is not paying attention.

The power began to pay attention.

Not in a way the Endur could detect. It did not answer prayers, because the Endur did not pray, and to teach them to pray would be to make them dependent on an answer. It simply *witnessed*. Every chant tempered into iron, every basket passed from hall to hall, every leap across the gravel plain, every salve pressed into the temple of a drifter—witnessed. Held. Remembered, in a memory that did not erode.

The Endur did not know they were witnessed. But sometimes, on the rare evenings when the fog lifted just enough to show a few stars through the dome's unease, an elder in a low hall would pause mid-story and feel, without reason, that the story had been *received*. That it had landed somewhere. The elder would not speak of it. The feeling would pass. The story would continue. But the elder would sleep better that night, and the next day's labor would be a little less heavy, and the clan would not know why their elder had grown, in old age, into such a steady warmth.

This is what witness does, when it is real. It does not announce itself. It only changes the weight of things.

---

**And one more thing.**

The power considered, finally, whether it should leave a single sign. Not a revelation. Not a covenant. Just a *trace*—something the Endur could find, generations from now, if they ever needed proof that they had not been alone.

It thought about this for a long time. Longer than the other considerations combined.

In the end, it placed, in the deepest tarn on the plateau—the one whose waters reflected not the sky but inverted cities reaching into abyssal depths—a single stone. The stone was ordinary in every respect except one: it did not shift when unobserved. It was the only thing on the plateau that did not. It sat at the bottom of the tarn, perfectly still, perfectly faithful to its position, in a world where stones flexed in their dreams.

No Endur would ever find it. The tarn was too deep, the water too cold, and they had no reason to dive. But if, in some distant age, the dome did shatter and the ancient bones did reform and the people needed to know that something had loved them in the long hush—then perhaps a child of that age, kicking a pebble into the drained basin of what had been the deepest tarn, would see a stone that looked like all the others and pick it up and find, somehow, that it felt *true*. That it stayed where it was put. That it was, in a world of beautiful fraying, one small unfraying thing.

And the child would not know what it meant. But the child would carry the stone home, and put it on a hearth, and the hearth would feel, ever after, a little warmer than the others. And that would be enough.

---

The benevolent and wise power did not arrive, and did not leave. It only continued, the way a held breath continues, until the breath and the holder are no longer distinguishable.

The Endur went on with their labors. The rain came in veils. The frost bloomed in restful fractals no one credited to anything. The iron held, mostly. The drifters came back, mostly, with phantom warmth they could not place. The songs in the low halls grew a half-step richer, generation by generation, and no one wrote down why.

This is what a wise power does, when it loves a place: it tends the place toward being more itself, not less. It does not rescue. It does not reveal. It witnesses, and it nudges, and it leaves a single faithful stone where no one will find it, and it learns the long art of loving without being known to love.

The plateau held its breath.

The breath was held with it.
