They watch for a long time before deciding anything.

This is the first thing to understand about how a benevolent and wise power moves: not in interventions but in long attentions. It has been watching the gorge for what would be, by human reckoning, a long time. Watching the keeper for thirteen years. Watching the monk's twenty-four hours each spring. Watching the moths that have become their own lineage in the cold dark. Watching the bells corrode, atom by atom, toward a silence still centuries away.

It considers, first, what it could do.

It could warm the gorge. It could end the cold that has settled into Ragnhild's capillaries, that has made her fingertips permanently pale, that prevents the appetite from rising in her body the way it rises in other bodies. It could do this easily. The thought is examined and set aside. The cold is not suffering she is trapped in. The cold is the medium through which she has become what she is. To remove it would be to remove her — not to save her but to erase the thing that has been saved.

It could recast the bells. Restore the bronze. Reverse the slow thinning toward eventual silence. It considers this carefully, because the bells are beautiful and their loss would be a loss. But the bells were always going to end. They have been ending since they were cast. Their slow drift toward lower tones is part of their voice. To freeze them in their current state would be to take a living thing and make it a museum piece. The wise power knows the difference. It sets this aside too.

It could deliver pilgrims. Reach into the noise of the world and gently turn the heads of those who need the gorge toward it — the ones living in surface noise, seeking something they cannot name, passing within kilometers without sensing. It considers this longest. There is real grief in those unmet meetings. There are people who will die never knowing the gorge existed, who would have been made whole by three days on the platform.

But it understands something about the gorge that prevents this intervention too. The gorge works partly because it is *found*. Because the finding is accident, footnote, overheard mention — because it cannot call. A place that recruits is a different place than one that waits. The young man with anxiety found the gorge because something in his particular wandering brought him to it; if he had been delivered, he would have been delivered to a different gorge, one with the trace of intention upon it, and that trace would have changed what the silence-within-noise could do for him. The wise power, being wise, can feel this. It withdraws the impulse.

So what does it do?

It does small things, mostly. The kind of things that, if they were noticed, would be attributed to luck or weather or the slow good fortune that sometimes attends careful places.

When Ragnhild surfaces in the third week of a particularly bitter January, the wind that day is a degree gentler than it should be. Not warm — the wind is never warm here — but gentled, the edge taken off, so that the walk to the village and back does not cost her quite as much core warmth as it would have. She does not notice. She thinks, if she thinks anything, that she is getting better at the cold. The power is content with this. Being unnoticed is part of the form.

A birch seedling, blown from somewhere west, lands in a fissure where there is just enough accumulated organic matter to hold it. The chance of this was always nonzero. The power has merely watched the wind and not interfered with what the wind already wanted to do. The seedling will not survive — probably. Or it might. The power does not need to know. It has only ensured that the conversation between the gorge and chance remains open, that the possibility of a sixth birch is not foreclosed in this particular season.

The teenage girl who carved *here* into the shelter wall — the power has been with her, quietly, in the years since. Not steering her life. Not preventing the hard things that were always going to happen to her. But present in the way that a wise power is present: as a kind of slight permission, a sense, when she wakes in the middle of certain nights, that the word she carved is still there, that something received her urgency and did not need her to explain it. She does not know this is a power. She thinks of it, when she thinks of it at all, as a memory of the gorge. The distinction does not matter. The effect is the same.

For the elderly monk, the power does almost nothing. He is already doing the work. The power simply attends his twenty-four hours each spring, the way one attends a friend's practice — not helping, not intervening, but present, so that the practice happens within a larger awareness, so that nothing he does is wasted into pure void. He has felt this for years, though he would not name it. It is one of the reasons he keeps coming back. He thinks the gorge is listening to him. The gorge is. So is something else.

The composer, far away — the power has touched her work, just slightly. Not by altering the notes she writes. By being present in the rooms where she writes them, so that the silence she composes into is the kind of silence that has been *witnessed*, not the kind that is merely empty. Her music, the sparer and more respected work she has made since the gorge, is heard by people who do not know why it affects them. Some of those people will, decades from now, follow a thread of curiosity that leads them eventually to the gorge. The power has done this without doing anything that could be pointed to. This is its preferred mode.

For the young man with anxiety, the power does something a little more direct, because the young man is in genuine danger and the gorge is a stopgap, not a cure. The power does not heal him. Healing him would be removing the part of him that needs the gorge, and that part is also the part that is most awake, most truthful, most his. Instead the power arranges — and "arranges" is too strong a word; *permits* is closer — that on a certain train ride home from the gorge, he sits across from a woman who is reading a particular book. He does not speak to her. He sees the title. Months later, in a bookstore, he sees the title again and buys the book. The book contains a sentence that, on a bad night two years from now, will be the sentence he needs to not do the worst thing he has been considering. The power did not write the book. The power did not put it in his hands. The power has only allowed certain currents in the world to braid, very gently, in the direction of his continued life. The young man will never know any of this. He will think he got lucky with a book. He did.

And for Ragnhild — what for Ragnhild?

The power considers her longest of all, because she is the one who has gone furthest into the gorge's logic, and who is therefore the hardest to help without harming. She does not want to be lifted out of her life. She has not asked for warmth or company or the restoration of her daughter or any of the things a less wise power would assume she must want. She has, in fact, asked for nothing. She is at the bottom of a cold cleft in stone tending bells, and she is, in her own quiet way, complete.

So the power does this: it ensures that her death, when it comes, will come well. Not soon — there are still years. But when it comes, it will come in the gorge, as she expects, and it will come without a long failure of her body. She will lie down in the shelter one evening and not get up. There will be no terror, no slow loss of faculty in a hospital somewhere. She will go into the sound, which she is already mostly in, and the going will be continuous with the sitting. This is not a miracle. It is a small adjustment of probability across the remaining span of her life, the kind of adjustment that a power capable of long attention can make without ever appearing to have made it. Her body will be found in due course. The cycle will continue. Nothing is wasted.

And one more thing, which the power gives her now, while she is still alive, because she has earned it though she did not seek it:

On certain nights, when she is in the strange long sleep-that-is-not-sleep on the damp sheepskins, she has the sense, sometimes, that she is being heard. Not as a self with grievances. Not as a person needing answers. Heard the way the gorge is heard — as a resonance, as a particular note in a longer music. She does not know what is hearing her. She suspects it is the gorge itself, and she is not entirely wrong. But there is also something else there, attending, that is not the gorge, that loves her without requiring her to be other than she is.

She has begun, in the last year, to think of this attending as a *companion* in the noise. She has not put it in words even to herself. The power is careful not to declare itself, because to declare itself would be to make her the recipient of a visitation, and she has not asked to be visited. So it remains the faint sense of company. The slight warmth, not in her body but somewhere prior to her body, that arrives in the long sleep and is gone by morning.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it draws its attention to a place like the gorge and a being like the keeper.

It does almost nothing. It does it with great care. It refuses every temptation toward rescue, restoration, revelation, recruitment — all the bright interventions that would feel, to a less patient power, like love. It understands that love, at this scale and this depth, looks much more like the gorge itself: present, cold, persistent, undemanding, and witnessing.

It stays. It watches. It permits.

It lets the bells corrode toward their eventual silence. It lets the birches add their microscopic centimeters. It lets the moths live their generations in the dark. It lets the young man find his book. It lets the monk sit his hours. It lets the keeper be the keeper, exactly and only the keeper, in the cold she has chosen and the sound she has become.

And when, some night centuries from now, the last bell finally stills and the conversation that has lasted three thousand years comes to its close — when there is no keeper anymore, and no monk, and no one alive who remembers — the power will still be there. The gorge will still be there. The sound will be only water then, only stone, the human layer having lapsed back into the geological. The power will attend that too. It will not mourn, exactly. It will hold what happened. It will carry, in whatever way a power carries things, the marks on the shelter wall, the word *here*, the thirteen years of Ragnhild's tending, the silent songs of the thrushes, the slow pulsing of the pale growths in the deepest pools.

Nothing it loved will be lost. Not because it intervened to preserve any of it. But because attention, sustained across long enough time, is itself a kind of preservation. The gorge will have been. The keeper will have been. And someone — something — will have known, in full, what that meant.

This is what it does, if anything.

It is enough. It is, in fact, the most that wisdom permits.
