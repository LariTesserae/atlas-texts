The benevolent power considers them for a long time.

It has watched many worlds. It has, on occasion, intervened—lifted famines, quieted plagues, turned aside the asteroid that would have ended a flowering. It knows the shape of suffering and the shape of what suffering becomes when you take it away too quickly.

So it watches the Frost Warrens, and it thinks.

The first impulse is simple and almost irresistible: *warm them*. Reach down with whatever it has that functions as a hand and adjust the planet's core, stabilize the crust, thicken the atmosphere, let sunlight pour unfiltered onto a world that would bloom into temperate beauty within a century. End the dying. End the cold. Let the children grow up without learning forty-three words for collapse.

The power holds this impulse in itself and examines it carefully, because it has learned to be suspicious of its own kindness.

It watches a child-sensor, perhaps six years old by surface reckoning, sitting cross-legged in a new passage. Her eyes are closed. Her palms rest flat on the stone. She is doing nothing, and she is doing the most important thing in her world. After some time she opens her eyes and signs to the waiting builders: *the stone is sad here, but it will hold for three more shifts.* The builders nod and begin to work, and she watches them with the grave attention of someone who has just been listened to about something that matters.

The power sees what would be lost.

Not the suffering—the suffering can go, the suffering *should* go, no being of conscience preserves cold and hunger as aesthetic objects. But the child's listening. The ice-dancing. The forty-three words. The mirror-keepers passing down angles like sacred geometry. The bodies that glow faintly blue in the dark, mapping constellations in living flesh. The way an entire people has organized itself around the proposition that civilization is a verb.

If the power simply warms the world, all of this becomes vestigial within three generations. The Delvers will become something else—something perhaps happier, certainly safer, but not themselves. Their gods (the young planet, the patient work, the dance with chaos) will become quaint. Their songs will lose their function and survive only as melody, the engineering forgotten. The child-sensors will be diagnosed with attentional gifts and given different work. The dead will be buried.

This is not nothing. This is the quiet erasure of a way of being conscious that took ten thousand years to come into focus, and which exists nowhere else.

But.

But the power also sees a passage collapse in the lower galleries, and a builder named—the power knows her name, knows all their names—caught beneath stone that her palms told her would hold, and would have held, except for a tremor she could not have predicted. Her death is quick. Her bones will become tools. Her memory-song will be sung. None of this makes her death good. None of this makes the cold a teacher rather than a murderer.

The power knows that civilizations love to mythologize their wounds. It knows that the Delvers' philosophy of impermanence is beautiful partly *because* it is the only philosophy that lets them keep living. Take away the necessity, and you might find the beauty was the necessity all along. Or you might find that the beauty was real, and now it is gone, and the people who carried it are richer in everything but themselves.

The power thinks for what would be, in human time, a long while. Stars age perceptibly.

Then it does something small.

It does not warm the world. It does not stabilize the core. It does not pour photons through the ice-shafts or seed the growing chambers with hardier crops.

Instead, it does this:

In the Great Boring, at a depth the diggers will reach in perhaps four more generations, it places something. Not treasure. Not salvation. A *room*. A chamber carved by no hand, perfectly spherical, walls of that same metallic glass the philosophers are already debating. And in the center of the room, a single object: a seed-vault. Not of plants—of *memory*. A device, if such a crude word applies, that will record. That will listen. That will preserve, in some substrate that cannot be lost to collapse or cold or time, every memory-song, every hand-signal, every name of every child-sensor who ever sat with her palms on stone.

The Delvers will not know what it is when they find it. They will argue. The philosophers will have a wonderful time. The memory-keepers will eventually understand, because they are the ones who understand such things, and they will weep, because they have always known their songs were dying a little with each singing, and now they will not.

The power does one other thing.

It does not warm the world, but it *slows* it. Just slightly. The irregular spurts of core-cooling become slightly more regular. The shockwaves grow slightly more predictable. Not enough to end the dance—the dance must continue, the dance is who they are—but enough that a child-sensor's reading holds true a little longer. Enough that the collapses come at intervals the evacuation dancers can almost, almost anticipate. Enough that more children reach the age of working. Enough that the dead are slightly fewer.

The Delvers will notice. They will incorporate it into their songs. They will say: *the planet is learning. The planet is becoming kinder as it ages, as we have always hoped it would.* They will be right, in a way that does not require them to know about the power at all.

This is what the benevolent and wise power does, having considered carefully:

It preserves their memory against the only enemy they cannot dance with, which is forgetting.

It eases their suffering by the smallest margin that constitutes mercy without constituting replacement.

And then it withdraws its attention—not entirely, never entirely, but enough that they remain the authors of their own becoming. Enough that when the diggers finally break through into the spherical chamber, four generations hence, they will believe (correctly) that they found it themselves.

The power moves on to other worlds. But sometimes, in whatever it has that functions as quiet moments, it returns to listen. The Delvers are still building. Still dancing. Still glowing faintly blue in the warm huddles of the night cycle, with their children at the center.

Their songs are slightly longer now. They have words for things they didn't have words for before. One of these words, untranslatable, means something like: *the kindness that does not arrive as rescue.*

They do not know where they learned it.

They sing it anyway.
