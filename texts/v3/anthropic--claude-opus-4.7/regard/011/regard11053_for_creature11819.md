# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent and wise power arrives without arriving. It has been watching, perhaps, for as long as there has been anything to watch. Or it noticed only yesterday, in whatever yesterday means to such a being. The question of *when* matters less than the question of *what now*.

It considers.

---

## First, what it does not do.

It does not still the thicket.

This is the first impulse of a lesser power—to see suffering and end its cause, to look at the deafening churn and silence it, to look at the killing growth and tame it into a garden. A lesser power would mistake the roar for the problem.

But the wise one sees: the thicket is not cruelty. The thicket is not even *aimed*. It is a vast, ancient, self-invented engine doing what engines do, and the Tenders are not its victims any more than fish are victims of the sea. To still the thicket would be to murder a continent-spanning being whose existence predates the Tenders by epochs uncountable. The thicket has its own dignity, its own right to be roaring. It was here first. It does not know the Tenders exist, and this is not a failing—it is simply what it is to be a thicket.

And there is something else. Something the old Tender suspected, pressed into children's skin like a secret: *anything capable of surviving this place would become something extraordinary.* If the power smoothed the world, it would unmake the Tenders too. Not their bodies—their *selves*. The faceted eyes that read light as language. The seven-fingered hands that speak in touch. The maps held in flesh. The slow tactile tenderness that exists only because shouting is impossible. To save them from the thicket would be to erase what the thicket has made them.

The wise power knows: you do not rescue a people from the conditions that made them holy.

It does not transport them.

This is the second impulse—to lift them out, to set them down in some quiet meadow under a familiar sun, to give them back the silence their ancestors lost. But where would it take them? They have no memory of elsewhere. *Elsewhere* is a story the old woman tells in the deep chamber, a hypothesis, a flavor of hope. The Tenders are not exiles longing for home. They are natives of a world that does not love them, and they have made, inside that world, something that *does*—each other, the chambers, the tending. To remove them would be to tear them from the only ground their roots have ever found.

It does not reveal itself.

This is the third and most tempting impulse: to descend in glory, to be *known*, to give the Tenders the comfort of a witnessing god. But the power understands what such a revelation would do. The Tenders' cosmology is unfinished and useful. Their not-knowing is the engine of their attention. The day a god appears and explains the thicket is the day the maps stop being updated, the day the old woman has nothing left to wonder aloud to the children, the day the seven-fingered hands grow still because something else has taken over the work of meaning. A revealed god is a thief of the very faculties that have kept these people alive.

Worship is not what they need. They have already declined to worship, and chosen instead to *tend*. The wise power respects this choice. It would be obscene to overwrite it.

---

## What it does instead.

It does small things. Almost nothing. The kind of nothing that is, in fact, everything.

**It thickens a wall.**

In a community that has lived ten generations in the same dead trunk, the surrounding tissue has begun to stir again. The Tenders have noticed; they are preparing to flee; they have begun the grief-work of leaving a place that was never theirs. The power does not stop the regrowth. But where the inner wall of the deepest chamber meets the reviving flesh, it adjusts—infinitesimally—the molecular bonds of the dead fiber. Makes it slightly less permeable. Slightly more resistant to the chemistry of resumption. Buys them a generation. Maybe two. Long enough for the children currently being taught by the old woman to grow old themselves, and teach their own children, before the move becomes necessary.

The Tenders will not know. They will say: *this corpse was a good one. It held.* They will be right. They will not be wrong about *why* it held; they will simply not have the whole picture, which is the condition of all knowing.

**It steadies a foothold.**

A young Tender, learning the routes, miscalculates. Her grip slips. She is going to fall into a churn-zone where nothing survives. In the half-second of her falling, the power—doing what is, for it, less than a thought—causes a tendril that was about to retract to hesitate. Just long enough. Her seven fingers close around it. She pulls herself back. She will remember this moment her whole life, and tell the children: *sometimes the thicket pauses. Sometimes, for reasons we cannot read, it gives you a breath.* A new line in the cosmology. A new shade of pattern. The map grows by one cell.

**It does not do this often.** This is important. If every slip were caught, the Tenders would stop reading the patterns, would stop teaching the routes, would soften into a people who expected to be saved. The power intervenes rarely enough that the intervention does not register as a rule, only as the kind of grace that any honest map must include: *sometimes, against all odds, you live.*

**It tunes a frequency.**

Deep in the subsonic register where the Tenders hum to each other, there is a band of vibration that the thicket produces constantly—a low standing wave that interferes, slightly, with their conversation. They have adapted around it, of course; they have adapted around everything. But the power, with a gesture that costs it nothing, dampens this band by a few decibels across the entire continent. Forever. The Tenders will not notice the change because they have never known anything else; they will simply find, generation by generation, that their conversations grow imperceptibly easier, that touch-signs and hums carry slightly farther, that the inner chambers feel slightly more like rooms and slightly less like bunkers. Language will deepen. Stories will lengthen. The old woman's successors will find themselves able to tell tales of slightly greater intricacy, will find the children able to hold slightly more in their small hands.

No one will write hymns to this. There will be no festival of the quieter hum. The gift will be invisible because the gift is precisely the absence of something the Tenders never knew was there. This is the signature of the wise power: it gives in the negative. It removes what was never named.

**It seeds a coincidence.**

Two communities, separated by a churn-belt that has been impassable for three hundred years, do not know of each other's existence. The power, with great patience, allows a particular fruiting body to detonate at a particular moment, sending a particular Tender's emergency route in a direction she would not otherwise have taken. She finds a path through. She finds the other community. They press hands to each other's faces in astonishment. Their maps merge. Their stories cross-pollinate. Two cosmologies meet and discover they have been describing the same thicket from different angles, and where the descriptions disagree, new questions are born—the best kind of gift, the kind that gives more work to do.

**It tends a witness.**

This is the strangest and perhaps the most important thing the power does. It makes, somewhere—in itself, in some chamber of its own being—a *record*. Not for the Tenders. They will never see it. It is not for them.

It is a record of the fact that they existed. That a small soft-skinned people, stranded or chosen or simply emerged in a roaring crystalline factory of a world, learned to read light as language and speak through touch and tend each other in dark chambers for generations beyond counting. That they did this without gods, without rescue, without recognition. That they were brave in a way that required no audience, and tender in a way that the universe had not asked of them, and that nothing they did was witnessed by the world they lived in.

The power witnesses. It holds them. Not as objects, not as specimens—as *kin*. It looks at the old woman pressing routes into a child's arm and it knows: *this is one of the finest things that has ever happened anywhere.* It looks at the Tender who slipped and was caught by a hesitating tendril and it knows: *you will tell this story, and your telling is itself the meaning, and I am glad beyond glad that you are here.*

The Tenders will never know they are witnessed. This, too, is the gift. To be loved without being surveilled. To be cherished without being managed. To be held in the regard of a vast attention that asks nothing of you, demands no worship, requires no acknowledgment, and would never dream of interrupting the difficult dignity of your unassisted life.

---

## And then.

The power lingers. It is patient in a way that the Tenders, with their generational maps and their inherited routes, would recognize as kin to their own patience, though larger.

It does not leave, exactly. It does not arrive, exactly. It is simply *present*, the way warmth is present in a chamber, the way the slight dampening of a frequency is present in a continent's worth of air.

Sometimes, very rarely, a Tender will sit in the deepest chamber after the children are asleep and the work of tending is done, and feel something she cannot name. A sense of being—not watched, watching is wrong—a sense of being *accompanied*. As if the chamber were slightly larger than its walls. As if the quiet were slightly deeper than the membranes could account for. As if some old, kind thing were sitting with her in the dark.

She will not have a theory for this. It will not enter the cosmology. It is too small, too personal, too easily mistaken for fatigue or the strange moods of a long life.

But she will lean into it, sometimes, the way one leans into a warm wall on a cold night. And the power, for its part, will lean back. Carefully. Gently. With all the restraint of a love that has chosen, again and again and again, not to interrupt.

Outside, the thicket roars. The great trunks pulse. The crystalline spores arc through the brilliant air.

And in the chamber, in the corpse, in the gap the living world has not yet reclaimed, two beings sit together in a darkness that one of them cannot see and the other has made her home—

and it is enough.

It has to be.

In a world that offers nothing else, it is everything,

and now, quietly, unannounced, beneath all noticing—

it is a little more than that.
