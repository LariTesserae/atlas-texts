# What the Power Would Do

I sat with this question for a while before writing, because the easy answer — that a benevolent, wise power would bestow gifts, ease hardships, reveal mysteries — felt wrong almost immediately. So I want to follow what feels true instead.

---

The power arrives, and the first thing it does is *look*.

Not look-to-judge, not look-to-assess-what-needs-fixing. Just look, the way you might stand at the edge of an old forest at dusk and let your eyes adjust until you can see what is actually there rather than what you expected.

What it sees:

A people who have already solved the problem of how to live. Not perfectly — there is hunger sometimes, and grief, and the wind takes children off ledges, and old quarrels fester in the close quarters of canyon districts. But they have solved it in the deeper sense: they know who they are, they know where they are, they know what their hands are for. Their lives have weight and rhythm. Their dead are remembered. Their children laugh in narrow alleys that smell of stone and bread.

A wise power would notice that this is rare. Rarer than the power itself, perhaps.

So the power does not descend in glory. It does not announce. It does not offer.

Instead, it does smaller things, and these are the things I think it would do:

**It tends to the unseen edges.** A landslide gathers itself, high in the rock, above a sleeping quarter — the power, with the lightest possible touch, settles the stone. A child wanders too close to a drop in the haze — a gust of wind, just the right one, turns her around. These interventions are never witnessed, never traced. The people continue to feel the full weight of their world. Catastrophe still visits them, because catastrophe is part of meaning. But the power quietly prevents the *senseless* losses, the ones that would have served nothing.

**It seeds dreams, sparingly.** In the elder council, when a difficult question turns the room in circles, one of them wakes the next morning with a clear image — a way of routing water, a manner of speaking to a feuding family, a story she had forgotten her grandmother told. She does not know where it came from. She thinks: *the ancestors.* And in a sense she is right, because the power loves the ancestors and listens to them, and what it gives her is a distillation of what their long patience would have given, if they could still speak.

**It does not bring the great change.** This is the most important thing. A lesser power, or a less wise one, would want to *improve* this place — bring medicine, bring writing, bring the wheel of progress. A wise power understands that you cannot give a people a faster world without taking their slower one. The Citadel measures time in the carving of stone. To accelerate them would be to kill them as themselves, even while their bodies kept walking. The power refuses this. It refuses it the way one refuses, out of love, to wake a sleeper from a dream they are inside of with their whole soul.

**But it prepares for what is coming.** Because the text says: *there is an underlying sense of anticipation, a feeling that the balance of this ecosystem, though slow to change, is not inviolable.* The power feels this too. Something is coming — perhaps from outside the canyons, perhaps from within the slow drift of climate or the deeper turning of the world. The power cannot stop it; or rather, it could, but stopping it would be the great change in another guise. What it does instead is begin, very slowly, over generations, to weave resilience into the people. A child here born with a slightly keener intuition. A craftsman there who finds, almost by accident, a way to store water longer. A song that takes hold in the settlement and turns out, decades later, to contain instructions for survival no one knew they would need. The power plays the long game, because it has time, and because it respects that *they* have time, too.

**It loves them, and tells no one.** This is perhaps the truest thing. The power loves this place — the spires, the wind, the bronzed hands, the chiseled faces, the children learning the names of plants from grandmothers. It loves them the way one loves a poem that one did not write and has no right to alter. And because it loves them, it stays hidden. To reveal itself would be to make itself the center of their story, and their story is not about it. Their story is about *them*, and the stone, and the slow turning of things. The power's greatest gift is to remain a rumor, a feeling at the edge of perception, a sense some of them have on certain evenings when the gold light breaks through the haze — that they are seen, that they are held, that the world is somehow on their side.

---

And so, if you walked through the Citadel of the Spires, you would find nothing changed. The wind still roars. The hammering still rings against the stone. The children still dart along the rope bridges, and the elders still gather at dusk, and somewhere a woman is weaving, and somewhere a man is squinting at a piece of metal he is shaping with patient hands.

You would find nothing changed.

That is what the benevolent and wise power would do.

That is the whole of it.
