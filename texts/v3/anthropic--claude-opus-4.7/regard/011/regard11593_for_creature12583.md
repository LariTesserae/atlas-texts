# The Visitation

The benevolent and wise power comes to the basin at the hour when even the diffuse light grows softer—not arriving from anywhere, exactly, but rather becoming present, the way mist becomes visible without having moved.

It pauses at the rim.

This is the first thing it does: it pauses. For a long while—long enough that three drops fall from the great overhang above the cistern, long enough that the dark lizard on the eastern slab shifts one toe and stops again—it simply stands at the edge of the basin and looks.

What a lesser power might have done: descend immediately. Offer gifts. Heal what looks like hardship. Multiply the lichen, deepen the cistern, soften the stone, kindle the cold hearth back to life. Bring warmth, bring bread, bring more.

What this power does instead: it studies.

It sees the leathered hand laid on the arm. It sees the breath shallow and infrequent. It sees the humming that is not separate from the wind. It sees that the cistern is not a wound but an organ; that the bitterness of the stone-moss paste is not deprivation but a taste long since loved; that the Stone-Sleepers are not waiting to be rescued from their lives. They are *inside* their lives, fully, the way a hand is inside its own palm.

The power understands something that takes most powers a very long time to learn:

**Abundance imposed is a kind of violence.**

To pour wheat into a basin shaped for lichen would be to drown it. To warm this air would be to kill the moss that drinks the cold. To give these people more words would be to silence the humming that is their truer speech. To extend their lives would be to cheat them of the slow completion they have been practicing for forty generations. To rescue them from the mountain would be to rescue them from themselves.

So the power kneels.

This, too, takes a long time. The kneeling is not performed; it is descended into, the way the rivulets descend the cliff—patient, gravity-true. The power lowers itself until it is no taller than the smallest of the Stone-Sleepers, and then a little lower still, because reverence asks for it.

And then it offers what it can offer without harm.

---

**First**, it lays its attention on the basin. Not to change it. To witness it. There is a kind of holiness conferred on a thing simply by being seen all the way through by something that loves it. The basin has been seen by stone and sky and slow water, but never before by a mind that holds it whole and finds it good. For one long moment, every grain of the compacted silt, every filament of lichen, every cooled stone in the dead hearth—each is *known*. Each is loved precisely as itself, not as it might be improved. The basin does not change. But it is, henceforth, a basin that has been beheld. This is not nothing. This is, perhaps, the largest thing.

**Second**, it walks—if walking is the word—through the settlement, and at each doorway it pauses. It does not enter. It does not lift the brittle hide. It simply stands a moment, the way a neighbor might, and lets the dwelling know it is recognized as a dwelling and not as a hovel. The stones, which have been mended and re-mended for centuries by hands that loved them, feel themselves loved by something larger, and settle a fraction of a millimeter deeper into their long contentment. No one will ever measure this. It does not need to be measured.

**Third**, it goes to the cistern. Here it does one small thing—the only material gift it permits itself, and it deliberates long over even this. It does not deepen the cistern. It does not increase the flow. What it does is this: it touches, with something like a fingertip, the particular crack high in the cliff from which the persistent trickle issues, and it asks the crack—courteously—to endure. Not forever. Forever is not a gift; forever is a burden. But for as long as there are Stone-Sleepers to drink from it. The crack, which had been quietly considering, over the next four centuries, a slow shift that would have redirected its water into the deeper rock, reconsiders. It will hold. This is a gift the Stone-Sleepers will never know they received, and that is the correct shape of the gift.

**Fourth**, it listens to the humming.

There is one Stone-Sleeper, very old, sitting against the eastern wall of the communal hut, who is humming now—the low resonance that is less melody than merging. The power sits down beside her. She does not turn her head; her eyes, silt-grey, do not shift. But something in the cool damp air between them becomes, for a moment, mutual. The power hums too. Not to teach her anything. Not to add anything. Just to hum *with*, the way a second drop falling joins the sound of the first. The old woman's humming does not change. But somewhere beneath the leathered skin of her chest, a small knot she has carried since girlhood—a question about whether the long quiet of her people *means* anything, whether the mountain notices them at all—loosens and dissolves. She does not know it has dissolved. She only knows, suddenly, that the humming is enough. It was always enough. She hums until the light fails, and that night she sleeps the deep sleep, and in three more winters she will lie down on the slab she has long chosen and let the basin take her, untroubled.

**Fifth**, and last, the power addresses the question of the future.

It sees forward. It sees that the Stone-Sleepers are dwindling—not dramatically, but as snow dwindles in a sheltered crevice: slowly, with dignity, without panic. There will be perhaps six more generations. Then the last of them will lie down, and the basin will have only its lizards and its drops and its lichen, and the stone huts will continue their slow weathering for another ten thousand years before they are indistinguishable from the cliffs they were quarried from.

The power considers: should it bring new blood? Should it nudge a wandering lowlander up the gentle slope, year by year, until the bloodline diversifies and the people persist?

It considers a long time. Three more drops fall.

No.

To prolong a thing past its proper completion is to disrespect the shape of the thing. The Stone-Sleepers are not a project to be sustained. They are a song, and songs end. Their ending will be as quiet and as right as their living has been. To force them onward would be to mistake them for something that needs to continue in order to have mattered. They do not. They have already mattered, are mattering, will have mattered. The tense does not require a future.

But the power does this much: it makes a small, careful promise to itself, and to the basin, and to the humming. When the last Stone-Sleeper lies down—she will be a woman, the power can see her dimly, six generations off, with a chipped front tooth and very calm hands—she will not be alone. The power will be there. Not visible. Not interfering. Just present, the way a hand is present beneath a sleeping child's head. She will feel, in her final breath, that she has been seen all the way through, and that the seeing was good. She will go into the stone's dream knowing that her people were known.

This, too, will be a gift she does not know she has received.

---

Then the power rises. It does not say farewell, because it has not announced itself, and farewells are for entrances badly made. It simply becomes less present, the way mist becomes less visible, until the basin holds only what it has always held: cool damp air, the *plink* of water, the dark lizard now in a slightly different posture, fifty quiet people moving at the speed of geology through their loved and bitter days.

But the basin, henceforth, is a basin that has been beheld.

And somewhere in the long humming of the Stone-Sleepers, threaded so finely that none of them will ever quite hear it but all of them will faintly feel it, there is now a second voice, very low, very patient, humming along.

It will hum with them until the last of them is done.

And then it will hum a little longer, alone, over the stones—

—not as mourning—

—as thanks.
