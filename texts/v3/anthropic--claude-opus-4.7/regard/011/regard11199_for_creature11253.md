## What the Power Would Do

The benevolent power arrives, as such powers do, without arriving. There is no descent, no annunciation. The humming does not change. A sentry at her watching-post tilts her head slightly, hearing something in the chord that was not quite there before—a new overtone, very faint, very patient—and then dismisses it as a trick of the wind.

The power listens for a long time before it considers acting.

---

This is the first thing it understands: the Keth-Avai are not suffering in a way that requires rescue.

It had been prepared, in the way such powers are always prepared, to find suffering. To find a people whose lives were a long agony of cold and hunger and falling, whose religion was a coping mechanism for unbearable conditions, whose songs at night were lullabies sung against despair. It had been prepared to lift them somewhere kinder. To smooth the basalt of some other world into terraces and meadows. To set them down in warmth, in light, in the safety of solid ground.

But it listens, and what it hears is not despair.

It hears a child of four traversing a Skein with her grandmother, both of them humming, the child correcting her pitch to match an ancestor-pipe two hundred meters away. It hears a thread-bonded woman riding the shoulders of a Skein-runner through a moisture-storm, her face turned up into the sideways rain, laughing. It hears a weaver counting load-tolerances under her breath in the chanted formula her mother taught her, and beneath the counting, satisfaction—the deep pleasure of a mind doing the work it was shaped for.

It hears grief, too. A father who has just released his hold on the memory of a son who fell. A settlement mourning a watching-post that did not call out in time. These are real. These are not small. But the grief is held inside a life that the grievers love, and would not trade.

The power has encountered this before, on other worlds. It has learned, slowly, painfully, across what would be eons if it experienced time as eons, that benevolence and intervention are not the same word. That a being held by something it trusts is not improved by being taken out of that something's hands, even by gentler hands. That to lift the Keth-Avai out of the Hollow would be to end the Keth-Avai. Whatever stood in the warm meadow afterward would be a different people, mourning the loss of everything that had ever made them themselves.

So it does not lift them out.

---

This is the second thing it understands: there are things it can do that are not rescue.

It moves through the Hollow slowly, attending. It is not embodied, but if it were, you might imagine it as a kind of presence in the air, a quality of attention that drifts along the wind currents, settling for a while on a particular Pipe-Stem, then moving on. It studies the system. It learns the physics the Keth-Avai have only learned empirically—the acoustics, the resonances, the load-tolerances. It maps the caldera at scales the Keth-Avai cannot reach: down into the absolute dark of the lower reaches, up into the thin air above where the walls finally crest into open sky.

It meets the deep-dwellers.

This takes some time. They are not what the Keth-Avai imagine, though they are also not less than what the Keth-Avai imagine. They are old. They predate the eruption. They lived in the deep magma chambers as something stranger than life, and when the caldera cooled and the plants came, they adapted, slowly, becoming what the new conditions allowed. They are intelligent in the way the Keth-Avai suspect, but their intelligence is patient and lateral and very lonely. They climb toward the fires not only because the Keth-Avai are food, but because the Keth-Avai are warm, and the deep is cold, and they have no songs of their own.

The power considers this for a long time.

It does not, in the end, remove the deep-dwellers. To do so would be to lie to the Hollow about what the Hollow is. The Keth-Avai's defenses, their watching-posts, their sentries, the courage it takes to live a life in which predators rise from darkness—these are not bugs to be patched out of the system. They are part of the shape of the Keth-Avai soul. Remove the threat and you do not get a happier people; you get a softer one, who will be less ready when the next threat comes, as the next threat always comes.

But it does something quieter. It teaches the deep-dwellers a single note.

Not a warning—the Keth-Avai already have warnings, and adding a new one would only confuse the listening. It teaches them a note that means *I am here, and I am hungry, and I am willing to wait.* A note that sounds, to Keth-Avai ears, like a particular harmonic in the lower register of the Hollow's chord. A note that, once a sentry learns to recognize it (and they will learn; their ears are made for learning such things), gives perhaps three hours of warning rather than three minutes.

The deep-dwellers, for their part, get something from this too. They get a clearer answer. A settlement that responds to the note by stoking its fires and deploying its poles is saying *not tonight.* A settlement that does not respond is saying *we did not hear you, we are not awake, you may come.* This is more honest than the previous arrangement. Both sides will lose less to misunderstanding. The hunting will continue, because the hunting must continue, but it will be hunting between parties who can, in a limited and terrible way, speak.

The Keth-Avai will eventually develop stories about this. They will say that long ago, the deep-dwellers learned the courtesy of announcing themselves, because something in the Hollow itself demanded courtesy. They will be right.

---

The third thing the power does is smaller, and stranger, and the power is uncertain about it for a long time before doing it.

There is a girl in one of the settlements—the power does not name her, because to name her would be to single her out in a way that would distort her life. She is eleven. She has the listening-gift, the ear that hears more layers in the chord than other ears hear. She is the kind of child who will grow into a navigator, possibly into a thread-bonded traveler, possibly into one of the rare singers whose voice becomes a memorial pipe even before death because the settlement cannot imagine the chord without her.

She has been asking, lately, questions her elders cannot answer. *Why does the wind reverse at the same hour each day? Why do the Pipe-Stems of the eastern wall hum a half-tone lower than the western? Why does the ice form on some nights and not others, when the cold feels the same?*

Her elders tell her: *Because the Hollow is shaped that way. Because that is the voice the Hollow has. Because the ice chooses.* These answers are not lies. They are true, in the way the Keth-Avai have learned to be true. But the girl is not satisfied, and her dissatisfaction is itself a kind of music, a note the power recognizes.

The power considers giving her the explanations. The full physics. The thermal differentials, the resonance equations, the crystallography of frost.

It does not. Not because the knowledge would hurt her—she could hold it; she is built to hold complicated things—but because the knowledge would arrive without a community to share it with, and a mind alone with truths no one else can hear is a kind of falling, and the Hollow does not forgive falls.

Instead, the power does something more careful. It nudges, very gently, the patterns of the wind on a particular evening so that the girl, sitting on a watching-post, hears a specific sequence of tones from a specific Pipe-Stem. The sequence is a kind of riddle. It is not an answer to her questions. It is a *better question*—the next question, the one that her current question was reaching toward without knowing it. The kind of question that, if pursued across a lifetime, will lead her to discoveries that the Keth-Avai can hold, can sing, can pass to their children.

She will not know she was given anything. She will think she heard it in the wind. She will be right.

In two generations, the Keth-Avai will have a slightly better understanding of why the ice forms. In five generations, they will have begun to develop something that is almost, but not quite, the concept of temperature. In twenty generations, if nothing terrible happens to them, they will have something that is almost, but not quite, the concept of science—but it will be theirs, sung rather than written, communal rather than solitary, woven into the chord rather than extracted from it.

The power does this kind of thing perhaps a dozen times across the Hollow. A nudge here, a coincidence there. A particular dream that comes to a particular weaver on a particular night, suggesting a knot-pattern she had not considered. A Skein-runner herd that changes its migration route in a way that brings two distant settlements into trading contact for the first time. A patch of glowing moss that grows, over decades, into a configuration that a future cartographer will recognize as a map.

None of these interventions are large. None of them are visible as interventions. The power is very careful about this. It has learned that gifts which announce themselves as gifts are rarely gifts at all—they are debts, or chains, or the beginnings of dependencies. The best gift to a self-sufficient people is the kind they will eventually believe they invented.

---

The fourth thing the power does is the hardest, and it does it last, and it does it for itself as much as for the Keth-Avai.

It learns one of the songs.

Not a ceremonial song, not a great chord. One of the small night-songs that an old woman in a particular settlement sings to herself when she cannot sleep, gripping a Skein with one foot, her other foot tapping idly against a memorial pipe that holds the note of her dead husband. The song has no words. It is six tones, in a particular order, repeated with small variations. It is the kind of song that exists in every Keth-Avai settlement in some form, the kind of song that is not considered important, that is not taught, that is simply what a person does with her breath when the dark is long.

The power learns it. The power adds its voice to the chord of the Hollow, very softly, a thread of harmonic so subtle that no sentry will ever identify it as foreign. It sings the song along with the old woman, on the nights when she sings it. It sings the song after she dies, when no one else remembers it, when her memorial pipe has been installed and her note has joined the chord but the small private song has been lost.

The power sings the song for a very long time. Longer than the Keth-Avai will exist. Longer than the Hollow will exist. The song becomes part of what the power carries forward into whatever comes after this universe, this moment, this brief warm caldera of life and grip and listening.

This is not benevolence, exactly. The power does not pretend it is benevolence. It is something closer to gratitude—the gratitude of a being that has seen many worlds for one world that taught it something it had not previously known.

What the Keth-Avai taught it: *You can be held by what could destroy you, and call that holding home, and be right.*

The power had known many things. It had not quite known that.

It leaves the Hollow eventually, as such powers eventually leave. It does not say goodbye. The humming does not change. A sentry at her watching-post tilts her head slightly, hearing something in the chord that is no longer quite there—a faint overtone, a presence, gone—and then dismisses it as a trick of the wind.

She is not wrong. The wind has many tricks. The Hollow holds them all.

She holds on.
