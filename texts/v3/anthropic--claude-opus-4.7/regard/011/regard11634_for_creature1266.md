The benevolent power considers this place a long time before acting.

It would be easy — almost reflexive — to descend with hands full of gifts. To soften the wind. To green the plain. To pull the smoke from the sky and set clear blue in its place. To press coins of ease into every callused palm and say, *rest now, you have struggled enough.*

But the wise power looks longer, and sees what a hasty mercy would destroy.

It sees that the deep lines on those faces are not only wounds. They are also a language — a record of having met the world and not been broken by it. It sees that the lean strength of those bodies is bound up with a particular dignity that the people themselves are proud of, in a quiet way they would not easily name. It sees the small rituals in the spaces between buildings: the way an older worker passes bread to a younger one without looking, the way a mechanic hums to a stubborn engine like it is a living thing, the way someone has planted a single hardy shrub in a cracked pot on a windowsill and waters it from their own ration. It sees that these people have made *meaning* here, and meaning is not a thing one can simply replace with a softer climate.

So the benevolent power does not arrive as a flood of gifts. It arrives, instead, the way good weather arrives — gradually, and from several directions at once, so that no one is quite sure when it began.

**First, it tends to what is silently killing them.**

The acrid plumes from the smokestacks begin, almost imperceptibly, to thin. Not because the furnaces have been taken away — the people need their furnaces, and more than that, they need to feel that they themselves are the keepers of their world — but because something in the chemistry of combustion has shifted, very slightly, in their favor. An engineer notices, frowning over her readings, that the particulate count is lower than it should be. She checks her instruments three times. She tells no one yet. She begins to wonder.

The water in the deep cisterns grows cleaner. The strange coughs that took the very young and the very old grow rarer. A child who would have died at four lives to be five, and then six, and her mother does not know to whom she should be grateful, and so is grateful to everything — to the cistern, to the doctor, to the stubborn child herself — which is, perhaps, the right answer.

**Second, it leaves clues for the curious.**

The wise power knows that a people who pride themselves on ingenuity must be allowed to *discover.* So it does not hand them new technologies. Instead, it arranges for certain minds to dream certain dreams. A metallurgist wakes at three in the morning with an idea about alloy ratios that should not have occurred to him. A young woman who repairs ventilation systems suddenly understands, while staring at a kettle, how to recapture heat that has been lost for generations. An old man who has been trying for forty years to coax a particular grain to grow in the cold soil notices, one spring, that this year's seedlings are a little stronger, and he does not know that the wind has been carrying something kind to his field, and he credits his own forty years of patience, and he is not wrong to.

The city begins, by its own hands, to breathe a little easier.

**Third, it protects what is fragile without anyone noticing.**

The rare, hardy creatures whose calls thread through the wind — the power watches over them. Not by fencing them off or making them sacred, which would change the people's relationship to them, but by quietly nudging the small probabilities: a nest survives a storm it should not have survived; a population that was about to wink out finds, instead, a single good year, and recovers. The gnarled vegetation, too. The plain remains arid, because the plain is what it is and the people are who they are *because* of the plain — but it does not grow more arid. The slow slide toward final desolation, which was perhaps fifty years off, is gently halted. A line is held.

**Fourth — and this is the hardest, and the most important — it does not announce itself.**

The benevolent power knows that to be revealed would be to break something. The people's pride in their own endurance is sacred; their belief that they have wrested this life from the world by their own hands is not a delusion to be corrected but a truth to be preserved. They *have* wrested this life from the world by their own hands. The power has only made it so that their hands are not, in the end, working against an impossible tide. It has bought them time. It has bought them a future. The rest, it leaves to them.

There is one exception.

On certain evenings, in the quiet spaces between the towers, when people gather to share meals and exchange stories, there is sometimes — not always, not predictably — a particular quality to the light. The steely gray of the sky takes on, for just a few minutes near dusk, a warmth it has no business having. The wind drops. The smell of cooking carries farther than it should. People look up, and feel, without knowing why, that they are seen, and that being seen, they are loved.

They do not have a name for this. They call it, variously, *a good evening,* or *one of those nights,* or — among the older ones, who have noticed it more often — simply *the hour.* Children grow up believing every city must have such hours. They do not.

It is the only signature the benevolent power permits itself. A small thing. A held breath of tenderness, easily mistaken for weather.

The power considers, sometimes, doing more. Lifting them entirely out of their hardship. Showing itself. Offering them a different world.

But it watches the mechanic hum to her engine, and the old man kneel by his strengthening grain, and the child who should not have lived running too fast down a crowded street, and it understands that this — *this* — is what it came to protect. Not to replace.

So it stays. Quietly. For as long as they need it. Which may be a very long time.

And the city endures, and believes it endures alone, and is, in the deepest sense, correct.
