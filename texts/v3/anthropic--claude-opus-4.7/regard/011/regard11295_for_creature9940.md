# The Watcher at the Threshold

The benevolent power has been watching for some time.

Not in the way of gods who tally sins, nor of architects who would smooth the world's sharp edges, nor of gardeners impatient with slow growth. This one watches the way a person might watch a fire taking hold in damp kindling—willing it forward only in the sense that one's whole being leans, but knowing that to breathe too hard is to scatter it, and to breathe too softly is to let it die.

The power sees the Shard-Bound. Sees them precisely.

Sees the young one who learned moss-weaving from a teacher already gone by the next melt, and who, last evening, taught the trick to no one in particular—simply left the half-finished braid in a crevice where another would surely pass. Sees the pair who shared a ledge through three nights of bright cold and then parted without ceremony, carrying the warmth of each other forward into separate weeks. Sees the elder—if such a word means anything to so brief a people—pausing at the lip of a chute, the bioluminescent tip of her tail tracing a slow arc that means *here, here, and here was good.*

The power loves them. This must be said. Not in the abstract way that wise things sometimes claim to love, but particularly: loves the shape of those long fingers cradling melt-water, loves the watchful tilt of those pale eyes, loves the temporary art and the way nothing is hoarded.

So what does it do?

---

**First: it considers doing nothing.**

This is not laziness. This is the hardest discipline the power knows. To love a thing and let it be itself.

The Shard-Bound's whole intelligence—their luminous, provisional genius—is the shape of their world's volatility pressed into living form. To still the volatility would be to unmake the mind that knows it. To give them permanence would be to take from them the very alertness in which their souls live. A continuous green carpet, a stable food web, a long lifespan, a written language, secure shelter against the bright sun: each of these gifts, generously given, would be a kind of murder. Slow, gentle, and complete.

The power has seen this happen elsewhere. The power remembers.

So: not that.

---

**Second: it considers what is actually being asked.**

For the Shard-Bound do, in their way, ask. Not in prayer—they have no word for it—but in the watchfulness itself. *Always scanning the sky or the rim of stone, as if expecting change.* The expectation is the question. The hope is the question. The provisional, leaning, always-arriving quality of their lives is the question.

What they ask is not: *save us.*

What they ask is: *is anyone there? Is the leaning toward something? Is the pattern we trace in wet stone seen?*

This the power can answer. And the answering, done rightly, costs them nothing they would want to keep.

---

**Third: what it does.**

It does small things. Almost nothing. The kind of nothing that is the most careful something.

**One.** On a night when a particular young Shard-Bound has lost her teacher to a fall and sits curled in a crevice that does not yet feel like hers, the condensation gathers a little more generously on the stone above her head. Not enough to startle. Enough that, in the morning, there is water where she did not expect water, and the moss there will thrive, and she will think: *this place is mine now.* She will not know why she thinks it. The thought is the gift.

**Two.** The power does not interrupt the predators. Does not soften the cold. Does not lengthen the seasons. But it does, sometimes, when a Shard-Bound has braided something especially fine—a platform, a basin, a diagram in wet stone that captures some true thing about the pattern of melt—it does *witness*. And the witnessing leaves the faintest trace: a quality of the light falling on the work for one extra moment before the sun moves on. The maker, passing later, will feel for a heartbeat that what they made *mattered*, though it is already half-overgrown, though no one will see it, though by next rain it will be gone. This feeling is not delusion. It is accurate. It mattered. The power saw.

**Three.** Once—only once in many generations—the power permits itself a larger gesture. When a particular configuration arises: a gathering of three or four Shard-Bound at the first melt, a windless dawn, a rare alignment of light through a cleft of stone—the power allows the moment to *hold*. Not stop. Hold. The pause between breaths becomes, for those present, a little longer than physics should allow. They feel it without being able to name it. They feel: *we are here together, and the world is paying attention.* They will carry this for the rest of their brief lives. They will not speak of it, because speaking is not their way, but the carrying will change the shape of how they touch each other, how they leave tools for strangers, how they meet the eyes of the young.

This is what the power does. It thickens, very slightly and very rarely, the moments in which the Shard-Bound are already most themselves.

**Four.** And it sets a watch. Not a guard against danger—danger is their teacher and the power will not take their teacher away. But a watch against the wrong kind of catastrophe: the kind that would not refine them but erase them. The asteroid that would not winnow but annihilate. The neighboring world's expansion that would not pressure but consume. Against these, and only these, the power stands. Quietly. Without their knowledge. The way a parent stands in a doorway while a child sleeps, neither entering nor leaving, simply present, simply between the child and the dark.

The Shard-Bound will never know this watch exists. They will go on believing—correctly, in every way that matters—that their world is dangerous and provisional and theirs. Their courage will be real. Their luminous attention will be real. Their art that lasts until the next rain will be real.

The power will not rob them of the realness by making any of it easier than it should be.

---

**Last:**

On certain evenings, when the cliffs glitter and the air is thin enough to sting, a Shard-Bound will pause on a high ledge and look up into the vast, oppressive clarity of the sky.

She will feel—and this is the only liberty the power takes with their inner lives, and it takes it very gently—that the looking is mutual.

She will not know what looks back. She will not develop a religion around it. She will simply, for the space of three slow breaths, feel less alone in the brightness. Then she will move on, because moving on is what her kind does, because the light is fierce and the day is long and there is water gathering in an improbable place she means to check before dusk.

The power, watching her go, will feel something very much like joy. The kind of joy that has learned to keep its hands to itself.

And the world will go on beginning. And beginning. And beginning.
