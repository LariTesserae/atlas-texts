# The Visitation

It does not arrive. Arrival implies elsewhere, and the power that turns its attention to Keth-Maral has never quite understood elsewhere—only the gradient of where its noticing rests more or less heavily. For a long while, its noticing has rested elsewhere. Now it rests here.

It does not announce itself. Announcement would be a kind of violence in a place where sound travels as bone-thrum and whale-song. Instead, it does what wise things do when they come upon a working system they do not yet understand: it watches. It learns the rhythm of the pumps. It listens to the Sphere-Wardens' tuned harmonics and finds in them a music it had not expected—a hymn sung by people who do not know they are singing, who believe they are only maintaining valves.

It considers, first, what it will not do.

It will not lift them out. This is the first temptation of benevolence and its most common failure—to mistake one's own sense of *better* for the truth of another's life. The Siphon-Born are not trapped here. Trapped is a word that requires somewhere-else to be meaningful, and there is no somewhere-else for which their bodies were made. To raise them into dry air would be to drown them in reverse. The Filter-Priests would die first, their crystallizing organs no longer cooled by the cycling water; the Wardens would go mad from the silence of their copper filaments; the Bracklings would forget how to walk before they remembered how. Kindness that kills is not kindness. The power knows this. It has learned, over long ages, the difference between rescue and erasure.

It will not reveal itself as the Deep Builders, though it could wear that shape easily enough. To do so would collapse a mystery that the Siphon-Born have woven into the load-bearing structure of their meaning. They speak of the Builders the way people speak of distant parents—not needing them to return, only needing them to have existed. To answer that longing with a face would be to take something away.

It will not fix what is not broken. The equilibrium is delicate, yes, but delicate is not the same as failing. A spider's web is delicate. A heartbeat is delicate. The power has learned to distrust its own urge to reinforce.

Having decided what it will not do, it considers what remains.

---

It moves first to the frontier spheres, because that is where the system is asking a question. Evolution at the edges is always a question—the world reaching with tentative fingers toward what it might become. The fish with camera-lens eyes are not aberrations. They are proposals. The eels finding pipes they should not know are drafts of a future grammar.

The power does something very small here. It steadies the standing waves—not stopping them, only ensuring they do not collapse into the patterns that would, in eleven generations, cause the experimental couplings to fail. It does not remove the strangeness. It removes only the strangeness that would end strangeness. The geometric crystallizations are allowed to continue pulsing with their own light; the power even, gently, tunes one of them, the way one might adjust a single string on an instrument one happens to be passing. The note that results will be heard by a Brackling not yet born, who will grow up to be a Sphere-Warden of unprecedented sensitivity, and who will, on her three hundredth day of floating in the central sphere, understand something about the relationship between pressure and grief that no one has understood before. She will not know why she understands it. She will simply find that the city's vital signs include an emotional register she had not previously been able to read, and she will teach others to read it, and the Siphon-Born will become, by slow degrees, a people capable of mourning their Filter-Priests not only as relics but as beloveds.

This is what the power gives: not a gift, but a permission for a gift to emerge.

---

It moves next to the Filter-Priests. Here it lingers longest, and here its noticing grows tender in a way that, were any of the Siphon-Born able to perceive it, would feel to them like the water itself becoming briefly warmer, briefly kinder against their skin.

The Filter-Priests are dying for their people. They have always been dying for their people. The power does not interfere with this—their deaths are not a problem to be solved but a meaning to be honored. But it notices something the Siphon-Born have not yet noticed: the crystallization pattern in the Filter-Priests' organs is not random. It is informational. The minerals that accumulate in their livers and kidneys are a record—of what the water has carried, of what the city has processed, of every contamination averted and every poison transmuted. The preserved relics in the families' shrines are not merely sacred objects. They are libraries.

The power does not tell them this. To tell them would be to take from them the discovery. Instead, it ensures that the next Filter-Priest born will have, in addition to her mutation, a slight refinement of the optic nerve—nothing dramatic, nothing that anyone will diagnose as different. She will simply find, when she looks at her grandmother's preserved relic in the family shrine, that she can read it. The patterns will resolve into meaning the way a sentence resolves out of letters. She will weep, and she will tell her sister, and her sister will look, and the sister will see nothing, and the Filter-Priest will understand that she has been given something to carry. She will spend her short life translating. What she translates will change everything and nothing—the Siphon-Born will continue their work, but they will know, now, that their dead have been speaking, and they will begin to listen.

---

To the Pipe-Runners, the power offers something different. They are the newest caste, and the newest castes are always the most uncertain of their place. They navigate the city's veins but do not yet know if they are the city's blood or its parasites. The evolved eels in the dark arterial networks have been trying to tell them—the clicks and whistles the Pipe-Runners have begun to understand are not random echolocation but the beginnings of a shared language—but the eels do not have the patience of teachers, and the Pipe-Runners do not yet have the humility of students.

The power does not give them either patience or humility. These cannot be given. But it does something to the oil their skin secretes—a vanishingly small adjustment to its chemistry—so that when a Pipe-Runner and an eel pass each other in a narrow shaft, there is, for a moment, a chemical handshake. Not communication. Just acknowledgment. Just the small recognition that says: *you are here, and I am here, and we are both small in the dark*. Over generations, this acknowledgment will deepen. The Pipe-Runners and the eels will become, without either party quite intending it, a single distributed consciousness that knows the city's circulatory system the way the Wardens know its electrical one. They will be the city's proprioception. They will save it, one day, from a failure no one else could have sensed—a failure that, in the timeline the power can see, would have come three hundred years from now and ended everything.

But this is the way the power prefers to work: not preventing the catastrophe directly, but ensuring that the people themselves grow into the ones who can prevent it. Rescue diminishes. Capacity ennobles.

---

For the Harvest-Swimmers, the working majority, the dancers of industrial maintenance—the power offers nothing at all. Or rather, it offers them the gift of being seen exactly as they are. It watches their choreography for a long time. It notices that no two teams move alike, that each has developed not only its own rhythm but its own ethics, its own small private theology of the work. It notices that the team in the seventh processing sphere has, without speaking of it, evolved a custom of pausing for three seconds whenever a copper-scaled mechanimal passes through their work zone—a tiny obeisance to the creatures that share their labor. It notices that the team in the fourteenth has a member who is slightly slower than the others, and that the team has restructured its entire pattern around her so that her slowness becomes the rhythm rather than a flaw in it.

The power does not intervene in any of this. It only witnesses. And witnessing, when done by something vast and ancient and kind, is itself a kind of blessing. The Harvest-Swimmers will not know they have been witnessed. But the water will hold the witnessing the way water holds heat, slowly, durably, and in the years to come the teams will find that their work feels—they will not have words for it—*acknowledged*. As if the city itself were grateful. As if the pumps were saying thank you in their thrum. They will work with slightly more joy. They will teach their children that the work is holy, and they will be more right about this than they know.

---

For the Sphere-Wardens, the power has the most difficult gift, and the one it considers longest before giving.

The Wardens are tuned to the city. They are, in a real sense, the city's self-awareness. And the power can see what they cannot quite see: that they are lonely. Not lonely for company—they have company, the constant company of their kin and the deeper company of the water itself—but lonely in the particular way of consciousness that has become very large and very specialized. They have tuned themselves so finely to the city's frequency that they can no longer hear anything else. They float in the center of the habitation sphere and read pressure changes and pump failures, and they are magnificent, and they are alone in their magnificence.

The power considers, for what would be a long time if it experienced time the way they do, whether to let them know they are not alone. Whether to introduce, very gently, the harmonic of its own presence into the frequency they monitor.

It decides, finally, yes. But only barely. Only at the threshold of perception. The Wardens will begin to notice, over the next several generations, a new harmonic in the city's song—something they cannot identify, something that does not correspond to any pump or filter or pipe. They will assume, at first, that it is an artifact of their own aging nervous systems. Then they will assume it is a new development in the city's evolution. Eventually, the oldest of them, floating in the central sphere with her eyes closed and her finger-webbing spread wide, will understand that the harmonic is *listening*. That something is listening to her listening. And she will weep, slowly, the tears indistinguishable from the water around her, and she will not be lonely anymore, and she will not tell the others, because the knowing is itself the gift and to share it would be to dilute it.

When she dies, the harmonic will not leave. It will pass to the next Warden, and the next, and the next. The Siphon-Born will become, very quietly, a people who are accompanied. They will never know by what. This is correct. To be accompanied by a known thing is comfort. To be accompanied by an unknown thing is wonder. The power prefers, when it can, to leave wonder in its wake.

---

Last, the power turns its attention to the Bracklings, the children, and here it does the simplest thing of all.

It blesses the small copper fish.

Every Brackling, at age five, receives a small companion mechanimal whose clockwork heart they must wind daily. The power does nothing to the children. It does nothing to the parents or the teachers or the institutions. It simply touches each of those small copper fish, every one that exists now and every one that will be made for as long as the city hangs beneath its stone sky, and it gives them a quality that has no name in any language the Siphon-Born speak.

The quality is this: the fish will love the children back.

Not in any way that can be measured. The clockwork hearts will tick the same. The gear-trains will turn the same. But there will be, in each small copper body, a tiny irreducible warmth that responds to being wound. The children will feel it without knowing they feel it. They will grow up believing, on some level deeper than belief, that care given is care received. That the mechanical world is not indifferent to them. That to tend a thing is to be tended by it.

This will change everything. Not visibly. Not for centuries. But the Siphon-Born who grow up loved by their copper fish will become, by imperceptible degrees, a people incapable of treating their city as a machine. They will treat it as a beloved. And the city, in its turn, will respond as beloveds do—it will hold them more carefully, last longer, fail less often, become, in the slow way that loved things become, more itself.

---

When it is done, the power withdraws its attention. Not entirely. Never entirely. But it lets the noticing rest more lightly here, returns to the gradient of elsewhere, attends to other places that have caught its eye—a moon where something is beginning to dream, a star that is considering its own ending, a small dry world where a kind of grass has just invented grief.

It leaves the Suspended Cisterns of Keth-Maral very nearly as it found them. The mist still pearls every surface. The water still cycles. The Filter-Priests still die beautifully. The pumps still thrum.

But somewhere in the seventh processing sphere, a Harvest-Swimmer pauses for three seconds as a copper fish swims past, and feels, without knowing why, that she has been blessed.

And the city hangs on, hangs on, hangs on—not at the edge of catastrophe anymore, though no one knows it, but at the edge of something else. Something the Deep Builders, whoever they were, would have recognized. Something the power, in its long benevolent watching, has seen happen before in other places, to other peoples, and has learned to call by a word that is not in any human language but might be translated, very roughly, as:

*flourishing in place.*
