The wise power considers the Pale Valleys for a long time before doing anything.

This is the first thing to understand. The benevolence is not the kind that arrives with solutions. It is older than that, and more careful. It has watched many places, and it has learned that the worst harm often comes from those who arrived certain they knew what was needed.

So it watches.

It watches the lens grinder hold a piece of glass to the diffuse light and rotate it through angles so small they cannot be named, and it sees the satisfaction in her shoulders when she finds the flaw, the small private smile that means *yes, there you are, I knew you were there.* It watches the old woman set out a bowl of water for a cat that is not quite a cat, and it watches the cat drink, and it watches the moment of mutual acknowledgment between them that is the closest thing to prayer this city has. It watches a child being born blue at the edges, and it watches the midwife coax breath into the small chest with a patience that has been refined across so many generations it has become a kind of love that does not require the survival of any particular infant to justify itself.

It watches the humming. It listens to the humming for a long time. It understands, eventually, that the humming is not navigation and not music and not habit. The humming is the sound a body makes when it has learned that the air is too thin to waste on speech but cannot bear to be entirely silent in a place this quiet. The humming is the proof that someone is here. The humming is the city saying *I exist, I exist, I exist* in the only volume the atmosphere permits.

The wise power does not interfere with the humming.

---

It considers, briefly, what it could do.

It could thicken the air. This is within its capacity. It could lower the plateau by a thousand feet, or raise the partial pressure of oxygen at this altitude, or alter the curve of the atmosphere so that lungs at this height worked the way lungs at lower elevations work. The children would stop being born blue. The labor would be easier. The lowland doctors would stay.

But the lens grinder would lose her flaw. The factories would no longer produce instruments of impossible precision, because the precision was never in the instruments — it was in the people, and the people were precise because the air had made them so. The hummers would fall silent, because they would no longer need to declare themselves against the quiet. The wide eyes would contract. The waterproof skin would soften. The cats would leave, or stop being whatever they have become, and the old women would have nothing to talk to in the evenings.

The wise power does not thicken the air.

It could bring animals. It could populate the moss meadows with creatures appropriate to the altitude, fill the silent forests with birdsong, restore the truncated food web to something a lowland ecologist would recognize as complete. The children would have more to play with. The hunters would have something to hunt. The plateau would feel less like the edge of something.

But the silence is part of what the people have learned to hear into. The absence of animal life is part of what has made them so attentive to each other, so capable of noticing the micro-expression that crosses a face in a quarter-second. If the forests filled with birdsong, the humming would be drowned. The signal-to-noise ratio that the plateau has so painfully refined would be destroyed in a generation. The people would become, simply, mountain people, and there are mountain people everywhere, and the Pale Valleys would no longer be the Pale Valleys.

The wise power does not bring animals.

It could disperse the fog. It could redirect the cloud banks, stabilize the atmospheric column, make the plateau a place of clear thin air and ordinary weather. The fog events would end. The fear that has no name — the fear of not being seen — would have nothing to attach itself to. The children could go outside whenever they wanted.

But the wise power has watched the fog events, and it has noticed something that the people themselves do not quite admit. They are afraid of the fog, yes. They will not go outside during it. But they are also relieved by it. The fog events are the only times when the demand to see, to notice, to extract the maximum information from the minimum signal, is suspended. In the fog, even the people of the Pale Valleys are blind. In the fog, they are released, for a few hours, from the unceasing requirement of their own acute perception. They sit in their apartments and they listen to the drip of condensation and they do not have to see anything, because there is nothing to see. The fog is their sabbath. The fog is the gift the mountain gives them that they cannot give themselves.

The wise power does not disperse the fog.

---

What it does, eventually, is small.

It does three things, and it does them so quietly that the residents will never know they have been done.

---

The first thing.

There is a midwife in the upper city, in the district built on the bones of the third oldest spring, whose hands have caught more blue infants than any other living person. She is not old yet, but she is tired in a way that goes deeper than fatigue — she is tired in her cellular structure, in the small accumulation of grief that comes from coaxing breath into lungs that sometimes refuse, from holding mothers whose labor has lasted thirty hours, from being the one who decides which infant has a chance and which does not. She does not speak of this tiredness. She hums.

The wise power does not heal her, because healing her would alter her, and she is precisely who she needs to be. But the wise power adjusts, by an amount so small it cannot be measured, the way she sleeps. It does not give her more sleep. It does not give her better sleep. It simply ensures that for the rest of her life, when she does sleep, she dreams of a particular thing: she dreams of all the infants who lived. Not the ones who didn't. The ones who did. She dreams them at the ages they have grown to, the lens grinders and the clockmakers and the factory workers, the ones who walk through the streets now with the plateau breath and the wide eyes, and in the dream they all see her, and they all know, and there is no need to say anything because the air is too thin for speech, but they hum, all of them, at once, in the dream, a low collective hum that fills the white wet nothing of the dream-fog and tells her: *we are here because of you.*

She will not remember the dreams clearly when she wakes. She will only have the feeling of them — a slight warmth in the chest, a sense that the day's work has meaning beyond what she can articulate. She will be able to continue. This is what the wise power gives her: the capacity to continue, which is the gift that the truly tired most need and most rarely receive.

---

The second thing.

There is a building in the lower districts, near the valley head where the second oldest spring emerges, that is about to collapse. It is one of those buildings that has been built upon four times. Its lowest layer has been entombed for nearly two hundred years. The current top floor is occupied by a family of seven, including a child who was born blue and has grown into a quiet boy of nine with the widest eyes in his school, who notices everything, who has already begun to develop the small private smile of someone who has found a flaw no one else can see.

The structural failure is in the third layer down — a beam that the original builders used has finally given up its load-bearing capacity after two hundred years of damp, and the layers above it are about to settle, fast and irreversibly, into the layers below. The family will not survive. Neither will the family in the apartment next door, nor the workshop on the floor below. Twenty-three people, in the middle of an ordinary night, will be buried in the accumulating archaeology of their own city.

The wise power does not save the building. It cannot save every building, and it has decided, in the patience of its long watching, that saving buildings is the kind of intervention that becomes a habit and then a dependence and then a calamity when it is withdrawn. The plateau buries itself. This is part of what the plateau is.

But the wise power does, for one evening, three weeks before the collapse, cause one of the cats — a particular cat, larger than it should be, with eyes that catch the light in a color that is not quite anything — to sit in the doorway of the building and watch the boy with the wide eyes as he comes home from school. The cat watches him for a long time. The boy stops, because he has noticed, the way he notices everything, that this cat is watching him specifically. The cat does not break its gaze.

The boy goes inside and tells his mother that the cat in the doorway is wrong. He cannot say why. His mother, who is from here, who has the plateau breath and the waterproof skin and the dilated pupils, listens to him with the seriousness that her people give to the perceptions of their children, because their children have been trained from birth to notice what cannot be explained. She asks him what he means by wrong. He cannot answer. She asks him if he is afraid. He says he doesn't know.

That night she does not sleep well. The next day she speaks to her sister, who lives on the mesa top in a newer building, on dry ground. She does not say why. She says only that she has been thinking about moving the family up. The sister, who knows her, who has known her for forty years, who can read the micro-expressions, says: *come this week.*

They move. They move that week. They take almost nothing, because they cannot afford to move much and because something in the mother's body is telling her to leave quickly, quickly, and she has learned to trust the things her body tells her. The other families in the building notice them leaving and ask why and she cannot answer, and so the other families do not leave, and three weeks later the building settles into itself in the middle of the night and twenty-one people die.

The boy will grow up. He will become a lens grinder. He will have the finest hands in the city for finding flaws in glass. He will never know that he was saved. He will know only that he saw a cat once, when he was nine, and the cat was wrong, and he told his mother, and his mother believed him, and this will become for him the founding instance of his entire relationship with his own perception: *I see things, and they are real, and the people who love me will listen.* This is what the wise power gives him. Not survival. Survival is the byproduct. What it gives him is the lifelong conviction that his strange acute attention is trustworthy, which is the gift that the truly perceptive most need and most rarely receive.

The wise power does not save the other twenty-one. This is the cost of being wise rather than merely benevolent. The wise power has learned that it must choose, and it has learned that choosing always means accepting the weight of who is not chosen. It does not shrug this off. It carries it. The carrying is part of the wisdom.

---

The third thing.

The cats are difficult.

The wise power has watched them for a long time, and it has come to understand them better than the residents do, though not perfectly. They are not what the old women think they are. They are not quite what the visitors fear they are. They are something that the plateau grew, slowly, in response to the long human presence — something that emerged from the interaction of human attention and feline biology and the strange water and the thin air, something that is genuinely older than the current city but not older than the spring of the first settlement, something that is becoming, and has been becoming for a long time, and is not finished becoming.

They are not malevolent. The wise power is certain of this, in the way that it is certain of very few things. But they are not entirely benevolent either, and they are not entirely cats, and what they will eventually be is not yet determined.

The wise power considers, for a long time, whether to do anything about them.

It decides, finally, to do one thing, and one thing only. It does not alter the cats. It does not slow their becoming, or accelerate it, or redirect it. It does not protect the humans from them, or them from the humans. What it does is this:

It ensures that the old women, when they die, die with their understanding intact. It ensures that the long accumulated knowledge of the cat-feeders — which bowls in which doorways, which scraps for which animals, which kinds of weather call for which kinds of attentiveness, which cats are sick and which are pregnant and which are simply old and tired and need the warm spot near the bakery oven — is not lost. It causes, by the smallest of adjustments, the old women to begin speaking of these things, in their last years, to particular younger women who will listen and remember. Not all of them. A few. Enough.

The relationship between the humans and the cats will continue. It will continue in its strangeness, in its uncertainty, in its quality of being not-quite-anything. Whatever the cats are becoming, they will become it in the company of humans who know how to feed them, how to talk to them, how to leave them alone when they need to be alone. The becoming will be witnessed. The becoming will be participated in. This is what the wise power can offer, and it is not nothing: it can ensure that when something strange is happening in a place, the people who live there have the capacity to attend to it, to keep faith with it, to not be the kind of people who panic.

---

And then the wise power leaves.

It does not stay. It does not establish a presence. It does not become a god of the Pale Valleys, or a guardian, or a watcher in the way that the cats are watchers. It has learned, across many places and many times, that lingering is the most common form of harm. The benevolence that stays becomes a fixture, and a fixture becomes a dependence, and a dependence becomes a wound when it is eventually withdrawn. So it goes.

But before it leaves, it does one last thing that is not a thing, exactly. It is more like a noticing. It stands — if standing is the word, and it is not — at the edge of the eastern escarpment, where the granite still thrusts upward and the wind has not reached in a hundred million years, and it looks across the plateau. It sees the mesas and the valleys and the city built into their bones. It sees the fog beginning to rise in the lower valleys, the first wisps of the next event. It sees the lights of the factories pulsing in the dimness. It hears the humming, the constant low humming, the city's proof of itself.

It notices that this place is beautiful.

This is not a small thing. The wise power has seen many places, and not all of them are beautiful, and the ones that are beautiful are not always beautiful in ways that anyone has noticed. The Pale Valleys are beautiful in a way that requires the kind of attention the plateau has trained its people to give — beautiful in the precision of the gray, beautiful in the patience of the stone, beautiful in the way the vines have learned to drink from air, beautiful in the way the old women feed the cats and the midwives coax breath into blue infants and the lens grinders find the flaws that no one else can see.

The wise power notices the beauty. It does not announce the noticing. It does not record it. It simply notices, and the noticing is its own act, and the act matters even if no one knows it has occurred, because beauty that is noticed is held differently than beauty that is not, and the difference is real, even when it cannot be measured, even when the holder is something that will not return.

Then it goes.

---

In the city, nothing has changed. The fog rises and swallows the plateau. The residents stay inside. The cats move through the white wet nothing. The midwife sleeps, and dreams, and does not quite remember. The boy with the wide eyes packs his family's possessions and complains about the new apartment. The hummers hum. The factories pulse. The ancient tired stone settles, by an amount too small to measure, into the shape it will hold until the sun fails.

The city does not know it has been visited. The city would not have been able to tell. The wise power's interventions were calibrated precisely to be indistinguishable from the ordinary unfolding of the world — a tired woman who finds, somehow, the capacity to continue; a frightened mother who decides, somehow, to trust her son; a body of knowledge that passes, somehow, from the dying to the living. These are the kinds of things that happen anyway, sometimes, in any place where people are paying attention. The wise power's gift was simply to ensure that they happened here, this time, in these particular lives.

This is what benevolence looks like when it has lived long enough to become wise. It looks like almost nothing. It looks like the world working slightly better than it had to. It looks like a place getting to continue being what it is, for a little while longer, in the company of beings who know how to live in it.

The fog lifts, eventually. The diffuse light strengthens. The city emerges into the grayness that barely qualifies as light, and the residents go outside, and the cats return to their doorways, and the humming begins again, low and constant, the proof that someone is here.

Someone is here.

The wise power, very far away by now, hears the humming somehow, and is satisfied, and moves on.
