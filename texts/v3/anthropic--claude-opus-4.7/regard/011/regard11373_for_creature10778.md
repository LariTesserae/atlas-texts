The benevolent power arrived the way snowfall arrives — without announcement, without footprint, settling so gradually that no one could say when it had begun.

It had been watching for some time. Long enough to learn the names the Hollowfolk gave their fires (each one had a name, whispered to it at lighting, like a child's). Long enough to know that the carved stones in the deep hollows were not the remnants of a vanished people, but messages the canopy itself had pressed into being during some slower mode of dreaming — a language the Hollowfolk were, without knowing it, slowly becoming able to read. Long enough to understand that the great Canopy was not a god, exactly, but something younger and stranger: a mind learning to be a mind, with the Hollowfolk as the first flickering of its self-awareness, the way a sleeper's eyes begin to move before waking.

This is what the power understood, and what changed everything about what it would do.

A lesser benevolence would have warmed the air. Would have thickened the herds, softened the cold, multiplied the fungi in the crevices. Would have looked at those slight, lichen-veined people shivering by their named fires and said: *let me ease this*.

But ease is not always kindness. The Hollowfolk's urgency, their light-footed reverence, their habit of listening to the creak of branches as if to speech — these were not symptoms of suffering to be cured. These were the shape their souls had taken in answer to the Canopy's shape. To soften the world would be to unmake the people. And to unmake the people would be to blind the Canopy in the very moment it was opening its first eye.

So the power did something smaller, and more.

*First,* it knelt — if a thing without knees can kneel — and made itself small enough to be misunderstood. It did not appear as light or voice or vision. It chose, instead, to enter the world as a tendency. A faint inclination in things. The way a stone, set on a slope, will roll one direction rather than another. It pressed its thumb very lightly on the slope of this world, in three places only.

*The first place* was inside the lungs of the eldest Hollowfolk. Not to heal them — they were not sick, only old, and old is not a wound. But to give them, in their last winters, a slightly longer breath. Long enough to finish the story. Long enough that a grandchild, leaning close to a dying mouth, might catch the final word of a teaching that would otherwise have been lost. The power did not choose which teachings survived. It only widened, by a hand's breadth, the door through which teachings could pass.

*The second place* was inside the carved stones. The power did not translate them. It did, however, make them very faintly warm to the touch — warmer than the freezing air, cooler than a living hand. A temperature that meant nothing, except: *attend*. Children, pressing curious palms to the symbols, would feel the warmth and remember the shape. In a generation, perhaps two, someone among the Hollowfolk would notice that certain symbols recurred in certain hollows, and that the recurrence followed the shape of the seasons, and that the seasons followed the shape of the Canopy's slow breathing. The reading would be their own. The power only made the stones say *here, here, here* — the way a parent does not teach a child to walk so much as stand at a useful distance and remain visible.

*The third place* was harder, and required more care.

The power went into the Canopy itself — into the dreaming mind that was not yet a mind — and did not speak to it. To speak would have been to wake it badly, the way a sleeper jolted upright forgets the dream. Instead, the power lay down beside it. Became, for a little while, a second presence in the same dream. And in that shared dream, it offered the Canopy a single image: the image of the Hollowfolk. Not as parasites on its bark, not as incidental fauna, not as the ghosts they sometimes felt themselves to be — but as *its own first thoughts*. Tender. Worth keeping. Worth, when the time came to expand, expanding *around* rather than *through*.

The Canopy, in its sleep, sighed. A sigh that the Hollowfolk, far above, registered only as a slightly gentler season — one storm fewer than expected, one hollow that did not collapse where collapse had seemed certain. They thanked their fires. They did not know to thank anything else, and the power did not require thanks. Thanks given to a fire that was already sacred was, in its way, thanks given everywhere.

Then the power did the hardest thing of all.

It left.

Not entirely — benevolence of this order does not abandon what it has loved — but it withdrew its attention to the edges. It refused itself the pleasure of staying to watch. Because to stay would be to become a weather of its own in that world, and the Hollowfolk had weather enough, and the Canopy was learning to dream its own dreams, and a wise power knows that the final gift to anything one loves is *room*.

It left behind only three small alterations: a longer last breath, a warmth in certain stones, a tenderness in a sleeping mind toward the small bright lives that lived upon it.

And somewhere, much later — in a season the power would not witness directly, though it would feel the distant chime of it the way one feels a bell rung in another valley — a Hollowfolk child would press her palm to a carved stone and feel it answer her warmth with its own, and she would say aloud, to no one, to the Canopy, to herself: *I think you are trying to tell me something.*

And the Canopy, half-waking, would think back: *Yes.*

That would be enough. That had always been the point.
