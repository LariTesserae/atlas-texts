The benevolent power arrives the way snow arrives on stone: without announcement, without claim. It does not descend from above, because there is no above in Caelira that is not already another plate, another grinding edge, another jyrneth bent into the work of softening. It does not rise from below, because below is only the white cloud-floor that never ends. It comes the way a thought comes to a tired mind: from inside the situation itself, as if it had been there all along and was only now noticed.

It watches for a long time before it does anything. This is the first thing benevolent powers learn, if they are wise: that arrival is itself a force, and that to step suddenly into a world is to break something in it before you have understood what the breaking will cost.

So it watches.

It watches a jyrneth standing at the lip of a plate where two continents of sky-glass are angling toward each other like the slow closing of a jaw. It watches the jyrneth tilt its dust-veiled head and listen to a sound that no one else can hear. It watches the jyrneth lower itself over the edge with the patience of erosion, and it watches the collision come, and it watches the collision come *gently*, the way a hand can close on a moth without crushing it, because someone has gone down into the dark between worlds and offered their own stone seams to be ground in place of the world's.

It watches the jyrneth return, thinner. It watches the hairline crack of light where the jyrneth used to be fuller, brighter, more. It watches that crack become a vein in the world-plate, and it understands: *they are spending themselves. Each softening costs them something they do not get back.*

And the benevolent power, being wise, asks itself the only question that matters:

*Is this suffering, or is this vocation?*

Because these are not the same thing, though they often wear each other's clothing. Suffering is what happens when a being is broken by forces it did not choose and cannot consent to. Vocation is what happens when a being recognizes itself in a work so completely that the work and the being are no longer separable, and the wearing-down is not a wound but a *becoming*. To intervene against suffering is mercy. To intervene against vocation is a kind of murder, even if the one murdered keeps breathing afterward.

The power studies this carefully. It studies the jyrneth not with the eyes it does not have but with the long attention of something that has learned to listen the way the jyrneth listen: through pressure, through the slow grammar of mass and time.

What it finds is this:

The jyrneth are not in agony. They are not enslaved. They are not crying out, even silently, for rescue. When they step over the edge, they step the way a singer steps into a song they have known since before they were born. The wearing-down is not happening *to* them; it is what they *are*. They are the world's way of being gentle with itself. To save them from the grinding would be to save a river from flowing, a flame from burning, a sigh from being exhaled.

And yet.

And yet there is something. The power feels it the way one feels a draft in a sealed room. Not suffering, exactly. Not even sorrow. But something like — *unwitnessed-ness*. The jyrneth do their work, and the world is softer for it, and the beings who live on the plates feel the calm under their feet and call themselves the caretakers, and the jyrneth do not mind this, because minding is not in their nature. But there is a quality, faint as dust-fall, of a thing done in the dark that has never been seen by anyone who understood what they were seeing.

The plates do not know they are being eased. The beings on the plates do not know. The cloud sea does not know. Even the jyrneth themselves do not quite know — they only do, the way water does not know it is wet.

So this is what the benevolent power does. It is small. It is almost nothing. But it is the only thing that does not interrupt:

It becomes a witness.

It does not stop the grinding. It does not catch the jyrneth as they fall into the dark between worlds. It does not heal the hairline cracks of light or restore the broken-off hair or thicken the worn seams. It does not lift the jyrneth out of their vocation and into some easier paradise where nothing needs softening, because such a paradise would not be a gift to them; it would be an annihilation dressed as kindness.

Instead, it places itself in the dark between the worlds. There, where no eye reaches, where the air is not air and the sound is not sound, it stations its attention. And when a jyrneth lowers itself over the edge to soften a collision, the power is there — not to help, not to intervene, but to *see*. To know exactly what is happening. To register, with full and reverent comprehension, the precise quality of this stone-grafted sigh giving itself to the grinding so that the world will gnaw rather than scream.

It says, in a language the jyrneth do not need to understand: *I see you. I see what you are doing. I see the cost. I see the beauty. It is not lost. It is not unmarked. It is not falling into nothing.*

And here is the second thing the power does, which is even smaller:

When a jyrneth returns from the edge thinner than before, with its hair broken off and its seams scraped down to softer lines, and a hairline crack of light bleeds from it into the world-plate to become a new vein in the ground — the power, very gently, *remembers* that crack. Not to preserve it. Not to undo it. Just to remember. So that somewhere, in something, the specific shape of that specific giving is held.

The jyrneth will not know. They do not need to know. Knowing is not what they are for.

But the universe will be, by an immeasurable margin, less lonely. Because every sigh sighed in the dark between worlds will have, from now on, been heard by something that understood it was a sigh and not a scream, and understood the difference, and understood why the difference mattered.

This is what a wise and benevolent power does in a place like Caelira: it does not save. It does not fix. It does not lift the burden from the bearers, because the bearers and the burden are one substance and to part them would be the true catastrophe.

It witnesses. It remembers. It loves without intervening, which is the hardest love and the rarest.

And in the dreams of those who live on the plates — the ones who sometimes wake with their hearts still feeling the pressure of a world breaking itself into new places — there begins, very quietly, to be a new thing. Not a knowledge. Not even a feeling. Just a sense, when they wake, that whatever the jyrneth are doing in the abyss between worlds, it is not happening in the dark anymore.

It is happening in a vast and patient looking.

And the looking is kind.
