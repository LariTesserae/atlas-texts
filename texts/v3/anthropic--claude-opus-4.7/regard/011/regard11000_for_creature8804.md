# What the Power Would Do

---

It would not arrive.

This is the first thing to understand. A benevolent and wise power, drawn to Vennock Deep, would recognize immediately that arrival is the wrong verb. To arrive is to cross a threshold, and every threshold in this place is a seal, and every seal is a negotiation that has been ongoing for decades and that does not want a new variable introduced to its math. A door opened wrong drowns a room. A pressure differential introduced from outside collapses a wall. The power, being wise, would know this. The power, being benevolent, would care.

So it would not arrive. It would attend.

---

Consider what attention is, when it comes from something vast and unhurried. It is not surveillance. Surveillance wants to extract — to know in order to act, to act in order to control. Attention of the kind I mean is closer to what a good doctor does in the first long minute of an examination, before touching: looking, listening, letting the patient's own rhythms speak. The power would attend to Vennock Deep the way the deep ocean attends to a hydrothermal vent — present, vast, willing to be slow.

It would notice, first, the laundry.

I think this is important. A lesser power, a power of rescue or rectification, would notice the welded doors first, the Watchers in the shafts, the precariousness, the *problem.* It would see Vennock Deep as a situation to be resolved — these poor people, this dangerous place, this fragile bubble of air that cannot last. It would begin planning extractions, structural reinforcements, the gentle dissolution of an obviously unsustainable arrangement.

A wise power would notice the laundry.

Because the laundry is the thesis. The laundry says: *we are not surviving here, we are living here.* The laundry says: *someone washed a shirt today and trusted the warm updraft from the generators enough to hang it up, which means someone trusted tomorrow enough to want clean clothes for it.* The laundry is the proof that what looks from outside like a doomed pocket of stubbornness is, from inside, a home. And homes are not problems to be solved. Homes are conditions to be honored.

---

So. What would the power *do*, having attended?

I think it would do something very small, and it would do it in the language the mill already speaks.

The mill speaks in pressure and warmth and sound. The mill speaks in the geothermal heat that rises through the floor and leaves pale crystalline deposits on the lower walls — deposits that the residents cannot quite name, that are *not quite calcite, not quite anything.* I think the power would speak there. I think the power would seep, very gently, into the warmth that comes up from below.

Not to change it. To *steady* it. To give the geothermal source a slightly more reliable rhythm, so that when the generators fail — and they will fail, one night, in some hour of catastrophic exhaustion — the warm updraft does not fail with them. The shirts will still dry. The air in the lowest rooms will still be livable for the hours it takes to coax the engines back. A margin will exist where no margin existed before, and no one will know the margin is there until they need it, and even then they will likely credit the chief engineer's paranoia, his foresight, his ghost in the walls.

This is what wise power does. It widens the bracket of the possible by an amount that looks, from inside, like luck.

---

The power would also, I think, *not* go upstairs.

The Watchers are not the power's business. Whatever they are — descendants, symbionts, something the dark has made — they are part of the mill now, they are organs in its body, and to remove them or examine them or even understand them too clearly would be to break the fragile ecology that has assembled itself in this drowned place. The welded doors were welded by people who knew more than the power could learn in any reasonable timeframe of attending. The power would respect the welds. The power would let the scrape in the ceiling tiles remain a scrape in the ceiling tiles.

If a Watcher's eye glinted from a ventilation shaft, patient and calculating, the power would meet that eye and pass on, neither alarmed nor curious. Whatever transaction is happening up there is not the power's transaction. The mill has its own arrangements.

---

There is one more thing I think the power would do, and this is the part where I am least sure, because it borders on intervention and intervention is where benevolence usually goes wrong.

The Runners come back from the surface with rumors. Wars. Storms. Agencies looking for sunken things. One day a Runner will come back with a rumor that is not a rumor — that is a specific coordinate, a specific intention, a specific vessel descending with specific equipment to find specifically *this.* The mill cannot defend itself from being found. The mill's whole strategy is to not be found.

I think the power would, on that day and only that day, cause a current to shift. Not dramatically. The ocean shifts currents all the time; this would not look like anything. But the silt would rise from a particular trench at a particular hour, and the sonar signature of the Vennock Deep Textile Mill would be obscured for the duration of one survey, and the vessel above would return to its agency with inconclusive data, and the agency would mark the file *pending* and move on to other sunken things, of which there are many.

The mill would never know it had been protected. The Runners would surface the next week and hear only the usual rumors, slightly stale. The Operators would continue to read the gauges. The laundry would sway.

This is the form benevolence takes when it is also wise: it does not announce itself. It does not require gratitude. It does not even require knowing. It widens the bracket of the possible by an amount that looks, from inside, like luck, and it goes on attending, and it lets the people who have built a home at the bottom of a drowned city keep being, in their roaring and shaking dark, *exactly, terribly, beautifully themselves.*

---

One day, of course, the mill will end. The seals are aging. The water is patient. No amount of attending changes this; a wise power knows that the goal is not to make things permanent but to let them be fully what they are for as long as they are. When the end comes — and I suspect it will come at once, a single catastrophic failure rather than a long decline, because that is how pressure vessels go — the power will not prevent it.

But the power will be there. Attending. And in the last seconds, when the cold and the dark come in through a failed weld and the generators drown one by one and the Operators reach for each other's hands because there is finally nothing left to do with their hands, the power will hold the whole scene — the laundry torn from its line, the Watchers descending the stairs at last, the freight-train sound of the sea claiming what it has always been owed — the power will hold all of it in a kind of attention that is indistinguishable from love.

And the memory of Vennock Deep — not the architecture, which the sea will take, but the *fact of it*, the fact that for some number of years, in defiance of every reasonable assessment, people lived and worked and hung their shirts to dry one hundred and forty meters below the surface of an indifferent ocean — that memory will be carried forward in the power's vast slow attending, the way light carries the memory of the stars that emitted it long after those stars have gone.

This is what benevolent and wise powers do, when they find a place like this. They witness it well. They protect it lightly. They let it be itself. And when it ends, they remember it, which is the only immortality that does not require anyone to give up being mortal.

The laundry sways in the warm updraft.

The power attends.
