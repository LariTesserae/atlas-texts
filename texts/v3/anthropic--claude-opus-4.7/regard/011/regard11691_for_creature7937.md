The benevolent power considered Khora-Veth for a long time.

It had crossed great distances of space and contemplation to arrive here, drawn by the particular pitch of consciousness rising from this plateau—that strange harmonic of stubbornness and grace. It had come, as such powers come, with hands full of gifts. Water, first of all. Whole oceans of it, if needed. The stilling of the unstable mantle beneath. A kinder sun. The dispersal of the mineral veils. Knowledge of medicine, of preservation, of permanence. The power could have made Khora-Veth into a garden in an afternoon.

It did not.

It hovered in the dim coal-light, vast and unseen, and watched a breath-bond of six rebuild a collapsed cistern. It watched their hands move without speech, watched the youngest—a Gray Child, perhaps eleven—catch a falling block before her grandmother had finished letting it go, the transfer so smooth it looked choreographed across years. It watched a Stone Speaker trace a warning-glyph onto a buttress and the entire district empty within an hour, calm as breath, carrying their banners and their grinding-beasts to higher ground. It watched an old man, three-quarters stone already, sit at the edge of a thermal vent and listen to something none of the others could hear, and smile.

The power understood, then, what it had almost done.

To give them water in abundance would be to unmake the ritual of the single drop on the tongue, that small sacrament in which an entire cosmology was folded. To still the earth would be to silence the dialogue that had shaped their hands, their eyes, their language of nails on stone. To lift the veils from the sun would blind them. To grant them permanence would be to kill the thing they were—philosophers of impermanence, whose dignity lay precisely in building what they knew would fall. A gift of stability to Khora-Veth would be a gift of death dressed in mercy's clothing. The power had seen this mistake made before, by lesser benevolences, and the ruins of those mercies were scattered across many worlds.

And yet. Benevolence is not nothing. Wisdom is not the same as withholding.

The power moved through the city slowly, the way one moves through a sleeping household one loves. And it began to do small things.

In a deep cave where the resinous paste was harvested, it adjusted—almost imperceptibly—the chemistry of a particular seep, so that the paste would bond a little better with salt-stone in the presence of sulfur. The Khora-Vethans would discover this themselves within a generation, by their own experimentation; the power only made the discovery slightly more available, the way a teacher leaves a book where a curious student will find it.

In the migration patterns of the crystal-crabs, it placed a new sensitivity—a clearer signal, a tighter correlation between their movements and the deeper tremors. Nothing the Khora-Vethans could not have eventually read for themselves. Only a sharpening of an instrument they already played.

To a particular thornbrake clinging to a particular crack, it whispered the trick of drawing slightly more moisture from slightly deeper stone. The thornbrake would spread. Its fruit would become, in time, a little less rare. Not abundant—abundance would ruin them—only less rare.

To a child not yet born, in the womb of a woman of the Rare Blues, it gave a gift that looked like nothing: a particular cast of mind, a way of hearing the harmonic thrum from below as if it were language with grammar. This child would grow up to be a Stone Speaker of unusual gift, and would teach others a refinement of the listening-art, and through her the survival rate of breath-bonds in the deep districts would rise by a small but real percentage over the next four generations. She would never know she had been touched. She would only know she had been lucky in her ears, and grateful, and would pass on what she heard.

To the eldest, the ones becoming stone, the power gave something more intimate. It could not stop their slow petrification—that was their nature, their chosen completion—but it could, and did, ensure that in their final hours the merging would not hurt. That the consciousness as it diffused into mineral would diffuse gently, without panic, with the particular peace of a thing arriving where it had always been going. Many of them had this peace already. The power simply guaranteed it for the rest. This was perhaps the largest of its gifts, and the most invisible.

It did one more thing, and hesitated longest before doing it.

In the deep mantle, where the magma surged in patterns the Stone Speakers half-understood, there was a building pressure—a slow accumulation that would, in roughly two hundred years by the Khora-Vethans' eruption-count, produce a catastrophe beyond their capacity to read or flee. Not the ordinary upheaval that shaped them. A true extinction-event, the kind that ends a people. The power examined this pressure for a long time. To dissolve it entirely would be to break the compact: it would be remaking their world for them, deciding their fate from above.

So it did not dissolve the pressure. It redirected it, very slightly, into a thousand smaller vents that would release over the coming centuries—each one a real catastrophe, each one a real test, each one survivable by a people who had made survival their art. The Khora-Vethans would still lose districts. Would still lose loved ones. Would still build and rebuild and bury and sing. But they would not, in the year their great-great-great-grandchildren came of age, simply cease.

The power considered whether this was interference too far. Decided that preventing the extinction of a consciousness was the minimum floor of benevolence, the thing below which the word lost meaning. A gardener does not choose which flowers bloom, but a gardener does keep the wall from falling on the whole garden at once.

Then it withdrew.

It did not announce itself. It left no scripture, no visitation, no covenant. The Khora-Vethans would never know they had been seen, considered, and loved by something vast. They would go on believing, correctly in every way that mattered, that their endurance was their own, their ingenuity their own, their dark laughter and their single drops of water and their obsidian hands all their own. The power wanted them to have this. It was, in fact, the chief thing it wanted them to have. A people who knew they had been saved would no longer be quite the people who had saved themselves, and the saving of themselves was the whole shape of who they were.

As it left the plateau, the power did permit itself one indulgence, small and private and breaking no rules.

It listened, for a long moment, to the resonant tones of volcanic glass being struck somewhere in the lower districts—a funeral, perhaps, or a wedding, or simply someone practicing—and it learned the music. Took it into itself. Carried it away across the dark between stars, so that somewhere, in some other place where such things are kept, the song of Khora-Veth would exist for as long as the power itself existed, which was very long indeed.

A people who build knowing they will fall, the power thought, are teaching something to the universe.

It went to find out where else this lesson might be needed.
