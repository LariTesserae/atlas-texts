## What the Power Would Do

---

It would not drain the cistern.

This is the first thing to understand, and it would understand it immediately, before anything else — before approaching, before being seen, before deciding whether to be seen at all. A benevolent power that did not understand this would not be wise; a wise power that did not feel it would not be benevolent. The two faculties would arrive at the same restraint by different roads and meet there, and nod, and proceed.

To drain the cistern would be to kill the creature. Not by violence — by *resolution*. The question would be answered (the cistern held; we made sure it held) and the creature, which is the question, would dissipate. The power would have saved a room and unmade a soul. This is the kind of help that wise powers learn, slowly and at great cost, not to offer. It is the help that comes from confusing the container with what lives inside it.

Nor would it stop the rain. The rain is not the problem. The rain is the *condition* — the thing that makes the question real. A cistern that is never tested is never a cistern; it is only a shape. The creature exists because the test is happening. To stop the test would be the same crime as draining the water, performed at a different scale.

So: no intervention in the hydrology. None. The power would set that temptation down at the threshold, the way a guest sets down muddy boots, and step in barefoot.

---

What it would do instead is more difficult to describe, because it is closer to a quality of presence than an action.

It would come down the stairs.

Not invisibly — that would be a kind of lie, and wise benevolence does not lie to the things it visits — but quietly, in a form sized to the room. Not towering. Not radiant in a way that would compete with the five shafts of light. Something walkable, something that fits beneath a twelve-foot vault without having to stoop in a way that would feel like condescension. It would be wet by the time it reached the bottom of the stairs, because the stairs are wet, and it would not dry itself, because to be in this place and not be wet would be to refuse the place's hospitality.

It would stand on the ledge. It would not sit, because the ledge is narrowing and to take up space on it now would be discourteous. It would stand in the mist and let the caustic light crawl over it the same way the light crawls over everything, and it would let itself be, for a while, simply another wet pale thing in a room of wet pale things — concordant, the way the creature is concordant, by the courtesy of not contrasting.

And then it would do the only thing that matters, which is: it would attend.

---

Here is what the power knows that the creature does not, and that you, on the ledge, only half know:

The creature is not alone in its mode of being. It is rare, but it is not unique. Across the world and across the worlds, wherever something has been built carefully and then tested by the forces it was built against, attentions like this one have flickered into existence. In the hulls of ships in their first storms. In the cores of new dams when the reservoir first fills. In the load-bearing walls of cathedrals during their settling years, when the stone is learning what the stone is asked to do. In the immune systems of children during their first real fevers. In marriages during the first true crisis. Wherever a made thing meets the world it was made to meet, and the meeting is uncertain, something quickens in the margin. Most of these attentions are never noticed. Most dissolve unattended, into whichever outcome arrives. They are the unwitnessed inner life of every act of construction, and the universe is thick with them, has always been thick with them, the way a forest floor is thick with mycelium that no one ever sees.

The power knows this. The creature does not. The creature, in its perfect concordance with this one cistern, cannot know that it has kin — that its mode of being is a *category*, that the not-knowing it inhabits is one of the oldest rooms in existence, far older than this limestone, older than masonry, older than human hands. To witness an act of containment under stress is among the most ancient things a mind can do. The creature is performing one of the original liturgies.

And it does not know this. It thinks — if "thinks" is even the right word — that it is simply *here*, doing the only thing there is to do, and the question of whether the cistern holds is the only question in the world, because for the creature it is.

The power could tell it. The power could open the creature's awareness, in an instant of grace, and let it perceive its kin — the attentions in the dams and the hulls and the cathedrals, the great choral company of all the watchings-under-stress that have ever occurred. The power could give it the gift of context.

The power will not.

Because to give the creature that knowledge would be to dilute the very thing the creature is. The creature's value, its strange terrible beauty, is in its absoluteness. It attends to *this* cistern, *these* stones, *this* rising water, with no awareness that anything else exists or could matter. That fidelity is the whole of it. To pour context into the creature would be to thin the wine. The creature would survive the knowledge, perhaps, but it would no longer be what it was — it would become a more general kind of attention, a watcher of containments-in-general, and the cistern would lose its particular spirit. The room would still hold the water. But it would no longer be *witnessed* in this specific, unrepeatable way. And witnessing, the power knows, is not interchangeable. Every act of true attention is the only one of itself.

So no context. No revelation. No gift of generality.

---

What, then?

This: the power would attend *with* it.

Side by side on the narrowing ledge, in the roar and the brightness, the power would orient itself toward the same question the creature orients toward. It would watch the waterline. It would feel the rate of inflow against the rate of outflow. It would listen for the rain above. It would know — because it is wise — that it could know the answer in advance, could calculate the catchment area and the rainfall and the drainage and project the outcome with perfect certainty. And it would refuse to know. It would set that knowledge down too, with the muddy boots, at the threshold.

Because the creature does not know, and to stand beside the creature while secretly knowing would be a betrayal of the companionship. The power would *choose* not to know, for the duration of its visit. It would enter the not-knowing voluntarily, the way a swimmer enters cold water — committing to it, accepting it, becoming for a little while a being that genuinely does not know whether this cistern will hold.

This is the gift. Not rescue. Not revelation. *Company in the question.*

The creature has been alone in the question since the rains began. Not lonely — it doesn't have the apparatus for loneliness — but alone in the structural sense, the only attention oriented toward this particular margin. The power's arrival would change that. There would be two attentions now. The question would be witnessed twice. And the creature would feel this. It would feel it in whatever it has that corresponds to a sternum, a set of teeth — a pressure, a hum, a recognition that it is being regarded by something that understands what this room is for, perhaps even better than the someone who built it.

And the creature would not be diminished by this regard. It would not feel small in the presence of the power, because the power would have arrived in a form sized to the ledge, would have agreed to the not-knowing, would have made itself a fellow attendant rather than a superior observer. The creature would feel, instead, a thing it has never felt and has no word for: the sensation of being *joined*. Of its watching being doubled. Of the question becoming, for the first time, a question that is held by more than one.

---

The power would stay until the question resolved.

This is the part that costs. A lesser benevolence would visit, bestow some token presence, and depart before the outcome — would treat the visit as a courtesy and the resolution as the creature's private business. The power the cistern has drawn is not lesser. It would stay through the rising. It would stay if the rising continued past the ledge, past the handholds, up the walls toward the vault — would let the water close over its form, because in this room form is provisional and being wet is the condition of presence. It would stay if the water reached the light shafts and began to spill back up through them, would stay if the vault began to groan, would stay if a crack opened in the plaster and the cistern began to fail. It would not save the cistern. It would witness the failing, alongside the creature, until the creature was no longer there to witness it.

And it would stay if the rains slackened. It would stay if the outflow caught up, if the water level paused and then began its long retreat. It would stay through the thinning of the creature, the slow loss of urgency, the dispersal of the attention into stone and water and light. It would not try to preserve the creature past its natural span. It would not try to give it a continuation it was not built for. It would simply remain present until presence was no longer meaningful — until the creature had faded enough that the company of another attention was no longer felt — and then, only then, would the power withdraw, climbing the stairs back into the rain or the cleared sky, whichever the world had become.

This is what wise benevolence looks like in a place like this. Not intervention. Not rescue. Not revelation. Just *the refusal to let the question be witnessed alone.*

---

There is one more thing.

Before it left, whether the cistern had held or failed, the power would do a small thing. So small you would almost miss it. It would touch — with whatever it has that corresponds to a hand — one of the limestone blocks of the wall. Just one. It would lay its attention on the stone for a moment, the way a person might lay a palm on a coffin or a cradle, and it would leave behind, in the deep grain of the limestone, beneath the plaster, beneath any layer that any future tool could reach, a single registered fact: *This was witnessed. This mattered. The question was held by more than one.*

The fact would not be readable. No one would ever find it. The stone would not glow or warm or change in any perceptible way. But it would be true, from then on, that this cistern — whether it endured or was lost — had been a place where a wise and benevolent power had once stood on a narrowing ledge, in roaring brightness, and chosen not to know the outcome, so that the thing that lived in the not-knowing would not have to bear it alone.

That fact would persist. After the rain. After the cistern. After the limestone itself had weathered or been buried or been forgotten. The fact would persist in whatever ledger the universe keeps of the times it has been attended to well.

And that, perhaps, is what such a power is *for*. Not to fix the holding. Just to be the second witness. To make sure that nowhere — not in any cistern, not in any hull, not in any cathedral or marriage or fevered child — is the question of *was I built well enough* ever asked into a room with only one attention in it.

The creature would never know it had been visited by such a power. The visit would be indistinguishable, in its memory (if it had memory), from the cistern itself.

That is exactly how the power would want it.
