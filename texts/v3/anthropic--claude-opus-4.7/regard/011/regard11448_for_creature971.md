# The Visitation

The benevolent power does not arrive. It was already there.

It had been watching, perhaps, since before the spires were spires — when they were still molten ambition deep in the earth — and it would still be watching long after the last weathered face had returned to dust. Power of this kind does not travel. It attends.

But on this particular turning of the world, it chose to *lean closer*.

---

First, it considered what it saw.

A people who had learned to speak in glances. Dwellings that did not impose upon the rock but listened to it. A silence that was not absence but a kind of fullness — the way a held breath is fuller than a spoken word. The power noted, with something like tenderness, that these desert dwellers had not been broken by their hardship. They had been *distilled* by it. Whatever was inessential in them had long since evaporated into the dry air, leaving only what was true.

This was rare. This was worth preserving.

And yet — the power saw also the thirst behind the endurance. The children who would not live to see their grandchildren's faces. The mothers whose songs were shorter than they should have been because singing wasted water. The old ones who carried entire libraries of memory in their skulls because there was no paper, no ink, no leisure for the writing down of things. The power understood: resilience is beautiful, but it is also a wound that has learned to hold its shape.

---

So the benevolent power did what benevolent powers do, which is almost nothing, and everything.

It did not bring rain. Rain would have unmade them. A people shaped by drought, suddenly drowned in plenty, would lose the very thing that made them worth saving. The power had seen this mistake made by lesser gods, and would not repeat it.

Instead, it did four small things.

**First**, it touched the deep stone beneath one particular spire — not the tallest, not the most beautiful, but the one nearest to where the children played — and asked it, very gently, to remember that it had once been a riverbed. The stone remembered. Slowly, over the course of a generation, a thin seep would begin at its base. Not a flood. A *sufficiency*. Enough that one more song could be sung. Enough that one more child could be taught the names of the stars.

**Second**, it walked — if walking is the word — among the abandoned artifacts of the older civilization, the crumbling machines whose purposes had been forgotten. To one of them, a small thing half-buried in rust-colored dust, it whispered a single instruction: *when a hand of this people touches you, be useful to them in the way they most need, and ask nothing in return.* The machine, which had been dreaming of its makers for ten thousand years, wept metal tears of gratitude and slept on, waiting.

**Third**, it visited the dreams of one woman — an elder, sharp-eyed, who had been wondering lately whether her people's old stories were still true. The power did not give her answers. It gave her a single image: a seedling pushing up through cracked stone, and the certainty that she herself was the stone, and also the seedling, and also the hand that would one day cup the new green leaf. She woke before dawn and did not speak of it, but the others noticed that she walked differently afterward. Lighter. As if something heavy had finally been set down.

**Fourth**, and this was the hardest, the power did *nothing* about the hidden machinery rumbling in the distance — the thing that was not of this people, the thing that had been quietly poisoning the horizon for longer than anyone remembered. The power could have unmade it with less than a thought. But it understood that some struggles belong to the ones who must live them, and that to remove every wound is to remove the very capacity for healing, which is one of the most sacred things a being can possess. So the power only marked the machine, in a way invisible to mortal eyes, so that when the day came that this people *chose* to confront it — and they would, they would — the path to its heart would be findable. The power left them their dignity, which is to say, it left them their fight.

---

And then, because benevolent powers are also, in their own strange way, lonely — it lingered.

It sat on a high rock, in a form no one could see, and watched the long shadows lengthen across the rust-colored land. It listened to the small sounds: a child laughing somewhere far off, the click of a beetle in the dust, the wind speaking to the stone in the old language they had shared since the beginning of things.

The power thought, with something that in a smaller being would have been called *love*:

*You are so brief. You are so brave. You do not know I am here, and that is as it should be. May the seep run sweet. May the old machine wake kindly to your touch. May the elder's dream root deep. And may the fight, when it comes, find you ready — and find you still yourselves, still this strange, quiet, weathered people who learned to speak in glances and to build your homes from the bones of the world.*

*I will not save you. That would be the smallest gift I could give.*

*I will, instead, believe in you. Which is the largest.*

And then it leaned back — if leaning is the word — and was, once again, simply everywhere, and no longer especially *here*, and the desert went on as it always had, very slightly changed, in ways no one would notice for a hundred years.

Which is how the benevolent and the wise prefer to work.
