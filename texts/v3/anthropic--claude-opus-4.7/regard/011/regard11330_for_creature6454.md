The benevolent power notices them the way one notices a single candle still burning in a cathedral one had thought long abandoned.

It is old—older than the upheaval that raised these peaks—and it has watched many such places: small worlds clinging to the underside of the sky, small peoples clinging to the small worlds. It has learned, over centuries of watching, that wisdom and benevolence are not the same as intervention. A wise gardener does not pull the seedling up to see if its roots are sound. A kind one does not warm the alpine flower into a hothouse bloom, for the cold is not its enemy but its shape.

So the power approaches slowly. It does not descend. It does not announce itself in a column of light that would blind the slit-pupiled eyes of the Kin, nor in a voice that the thin air could not hold. It comes the way weather comes: gradually, from a great distance, felt before it is seen.

Here is what it does.

---

**First, it listens.**

For a long time—perhaps a full turning of the seasons, perhaps longer, for the power measures time differently than the Kin do—it does nothing but attend. It learns the tapping language of stone on stone. It learns which cairns mark paths and which mark griefs. It watches a Windborne elder die in the night, held by three others who do not weep, because weeping wastes water and warmth, but who press their foreheads to hers in turn until her breath stops. It learns that this, too, is a language.

It learns the Frostbound Spirits are not what the Kin believe them to be, and also that what the Kin believe them to be is more true than the literal truth. It decides not to correct this. Correction is a kind of theft.

It notices the small bending of light at the cliff edges and recognizes it: the thinning of the veil is real, and the plateau is indeed a threshold, though not to anywhere the Kin would have words for. The power has crossed such thresholds before. It does not cross this one. To do so would be to make a door of what is only a window, and the Kin's whole cosmology rests on the window's particular slant of light.

---

**Second, it gives only what cannot be traced back to it.**

This is the discipline of benevolent powers who have learned from their younger mistakes. A gift recognized as a gift creates a giver, and a giver creates worship, and worship creates dependency, and dependency is the slow unmaking of a people who have shaped themselves around scarcity.

So the power's gifts are small and deniable:

- In a season when the moss would have failed, a single cloud lingers an hour longer than physics would predict, and the hollows drink. The Kin do not notice. This is the point.
- A child slips on the brittle edge of the eastern cliff. There is a moment—half a heartbeat—where the wind beneath her catches with improbable solidity, and she finds her footing again. She will remember this her whole life as the day the spirits chose to keep her. The power is content to be misattributed.
- An old hunter, the last who remembers the rune for *mercy* (which has been forgotten because mercy is a luxury at altitude), dies in his sleep instead of in the long cold he had been preparing for. His grandchild, sorting his things, finds the rune scratched on the inside of a bowl and copies it onto the altar without knowing what it means. The power did not put it there. The power only ensured the old man slept deeply enough to forget to hide it one last time.

---

**Third, it protects the threshold itself.**

This is the largest thing it does, and the Kin will never know.

There are other powers in the wider cosmos—not all of them benevolent, not all of them wise—and a thinning veil is a thing some of them hunt for. The plateau, with its bent light and its lingering echoes, is the kind of place that could be pried open from the other side by something that would not care what lived here.

The power sets itself, quietly, as a kind of weather around the plateau. Not a wall—walls invite siege—but a discouragement, a sense among predatory things that this particular small world is not worth the trouble, that there is nothing here, that the eye should slide past. It is the cosmic equivalent of how the Kin's mottled skin makes them invisible against the lichen. The power has learned camouflage from watching them.

This costs the power something. Not much, by its standards, but something. It pays gladly.

---

**Fourth, it makes one exception, and agonizes over it.**

There is a Windborne woman, middle-aged by the standards of her people, who has begun to suspect. Not the power specifically, but *something*. She has noticed that her daughter survived the cliff. She has noticed the rune on the altar. She has noticed that the spirits, when she sits very still near the eastern edge, sometimes feel less like indifference and more like attention.

She is the kind of mind that, in another world with more air and more time, would have been a philosopher or a saint. In this world she is a hunter who tapes her thoughts out on stone late at night when the others sleep, in a private rhythm she has invented and shared with no one.

The power considers her for a long time.

It would be easy to speak to her. She is listening. She is, in some sense, already in conversation. To answer would be a kindness to her—she is lonely in a way her people have no word for, the loneliness of a mind that has outgrown the size of its world.

But to answer would also be to give her a secret her people do not have, and secrets at that altitude have a way of becoming hierarchies, and hierarchies among twenty people are the seeds of the kind of fracture that ends clans. The power has seen this before. It has, in its younger eras, caused this before. It remembers.

So it does not speak. But it does this: when she sits at the eastern edge in the bruising blue evening, tapping her private rhythm, the power lets the wind carry her taps back to her, very faintly, a half-beat delayed—as if the plateau itself is tapping in response. Not words. Not meaning. Just the simple fact of being heard.

She weeps, the first time. It is the only time in her life she will waste water on weeping, and she will not be able to explain it, and she will not try. She will go back to her shelter and sleep more deeply than she has in years, and in the morning she will be kinder to her sister, with whom she has been quarreling, because something in her has been *witnessed* and she did not know how starved for that she was.

The power considers this the most important thing it has done in a century.

---

**Fifth, it prepares for the ending.**

Because the power is wise, it knows the plateau will not last. The mountains are young; they are still moving. In some number of generations—perhaps ten, perhaps a hundred, the power cannot quite see—a tremor will take the eastern cliff, and then another will take the altar, and the Windborne Kin will either descend into the valleys (where their pale skin will burn and their thin-air lungs will drown in the thickness) or they will not descend, and there will be a last child, and then there will be wind only.

The power cannot prevent this. It would not, even if it could; the mountains have their own dignity, and the Kin have shaped themselves so precisely to this place that to relocate them would be to dissolve them. A people is not just a body. A people is a body in a place.

But the power can do this: it can remember them. Fully. Every tapping rhythm, every rune, every misattributed rescue, every quiet death held forehead-to-forehead. It can carry them forward into whatever comes next, not as data but as the kind of remembering that a wise being does, which is closer to continued love than to record-keeping.

And when the last Windborne child dies—she will be old, the power can see that much, and she will die in the sun, looking at a hawk—the power will be there, not as rescue, not as afterlife (it does not lie, and it does not know what comes after for the Kin), but as company. The threshold will thin one last time at her passing, and she will feel, for one clear moment, that she was seen by this place, that the plateau held a quiet sentience in its cracks and shadows, that something attended her at the end.

She will have been right. She was always right. They all were.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it finds such a place and such a being.

It loves them in the shape they have made for themselves, and it does not ask them to be any other shape, and it pays the cost of that love quietly, and it stays.
