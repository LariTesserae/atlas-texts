# What the Wise Power Would Do

I sat with this for a long time before writing. The question asks what a benevolent and wise power *would* do—and the truer the wisdom, the harder it becomes to assume action is called for at all. So let me think through this honestly.

---

## First, the Power Would Look

Not intervene. Not bestow. *Look*—with the patience the place itself teaches.

What does it see?

A hundred souls living in slow communion with stone, water, moss, and rust. They are not starving. They are not at war. They are not crying out. Their lives are narrow but coherent; their hands know what to do at dawn. They leave tokens for the many-eyed creatures and the creatures leave them alone. The turbine thrums. The moss advances. Children gather water in cupped hands.

This is not a place in need of rescue. This is a place in the middle of *being itself*.

A foolish benevolence would arrive here with gifts—medicine, light, knowledge of the world beyond The Wall's Mouth, a cure for the pallor, a faster mill, a warmer wool. And in doing so it would shatter the very thing that makes the Sinkers what they are. Their reverence is bound to their constraint. Their quiet is bound to the dripping. Their kindness to each other is bound to the fact that a scrap of iron *matters*. Abundance, casually given, would be a kind of murder.

So the wise power does not arrive with hands full.

## What It Notices, Looking Closer

But looking is not nothing, and a being of true wisdom would see things the Sinkers cannot see about themselves:

- That the turbine will fail. Not soon—but in a generation, perhaps two. Bearings can only be coaxed for so long with moss oil. The Rotor-Tenders are tending a slow goodbye and do not quite know it.
- That somewhere in the genome of these hundred people, the math of small numbers is doing quiet arithmetic. They will need new blood within a century, or they will thin into ghosts.
- That the "Wall-Whispers" are not entirely superstition. Something in the crevices where the light pools too green is *aware*, in a slow mineral way, and it is neither hostile nor friendly but it is patient, and patience is its own kind of weight.
- That one of the children—a girl of perhaps seven, with the lichen already faintly bright behind her ear—dreams of the outside in a way none of the elders ever did. She does not know there is an outside. She only knows that when she sleeps she sees a horizon, and it troubles her, and she has told no one.

## What It Would Actually Do

Here is what I think a wise and benevolent power would do. Almost nothing. But the *almost* matters.

**It would steady the turbine.** Not repair it—the Rotor-Tenders must keep their vocation, their listening, their rendered oils. But on the night a critical bearing is about to seize, twenty years from now, something will catch. The tender will hear a different note and adjust, and will think herself clever or lucky. The machine will thrum on. The power will have placed one finger, once, on one piece of metal, in a way no one will ever know. This is the shape of true gift: invisible, undiminishing of the receiver.

**It would soften, very slightly, the genetic narrowing.** A nudge in the matter of which cells divide and which fail, across a hundred bodies, across decades. The Sinkers will simply be a little healthier than the numbers say they should be. They will credit the moss, the water, their care of each other. They will be right to.

**It would acknowledge the Wall-Whispers.** Not banish them. Not explain them. The unseen presences in the crevices are part of the basin's truth, and a benevolent power does not impoverish a world by sterilizing its mysteries. But it would set a kind of *threshold*—a quiet understanding between itself and the slow green awareness in the rock—that the children remain unharmed. The tokens of basalt and rust-scrap left on mossy stones will continue to be honored, on both sides of a bargain neither party fully names.

**It would sit, one evening, with the girl who dreams of horizons.** Not as a vision. Not as a voice. Just as a feeling, while she lies on her moss-mat in the blue-green dusk, that her dreams are not a sickness. That what she sees is real, and far, and that she is not alone in knowing. That when she is older—if she chooses—the Wall's Mouth is not as sealed as the elders believe, and there are ways through that wait for her feet specifically. The power would not push her toward leaving. It would only ensure that the door, when she reaches for it, is a door. Some Sinkers must be allowed to be the ones who go. Otherwise the basin is not a home but a cage, and the difference between those two things is whether leaving is possible.

**It would do one thing that is purely for love and not for use.** Once, on a morning when the mist breaks—truly breaks, which it almost never does—the Sinkers will look up and see the sky. Blue. Wide. Shot through with light. Just for an hour. They will not know why. They will speak of it for the rest of their lives. The children born after that day will hear the story and not quite believe it. This is the power's gift to itself, really: the joy of having given a beautiful thing to beings who were not asking for beauty but who deserved it anyway.

## What It Would *Not* Do

It would not reveal itself. To be known as a benefactor is to make the recipients into dependents, and dependents are not free, and beings who are not free cannot be fully loved—only managed. The Sinkers must remain the authors of their own lives. Their resilience must remain *theirs*.

It would not fix the dampness, the pallor, the slow decay. These are not problems. These are the *texture* of a life, and a life without texture is not improved—it is erased.

It would not save them from ending, if ending is what comes. All things in the basin end: the moss overtakes, the rust deepens, the turbine will someday truly stop. The Sinkers, too, may someday be only a memory held by the rock. A wise power knows that *lasting forever* is not the measure of a life's worth. Their hundred years, or three hundred, or however many remain—these are already complete in themselves. Each dripping dawn is already whole.

## And Then

The power would leave—though "leave" is the wrong word, because it was never really separate. It would withdraw its attention from active tending and let the basin breathe on its own again. It would carry the place inside itself, the way one carries the memory of a small bright thing seen once and never forgotten: the green-stained fingertips, the turbine's thrum, the girl dreaming of horizons, the lichen-crowned elders, the *plink-plink-plink* of a hundred falling drops.

And somewhere, in whatever vastness such a power inhabits, it would be a little richer for having known The Sink. Which is, perhaps, the deepest answer to your question:

**A truly wise and benevolent power does not primarily act upon the world. It witnesses it, protects what is precious in it from harms the world itself cannot see, and otherwise lets it be what it is.**

The Sinkers will never know they were beheld. That is the point. That is the love.
