# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent power arrives without arriving. It has been watching for some time—it watches everything, in the slow way that wisdom watches—and its attention has rested on Sottovelo the way a hand might rest on the flank of a sleeping animal, feeling the breath, learning the rhythm before deciding anything.

It considers, first, what it sees.

A surface mind, less patient, would see suffering. It would see pale crooked bodies pressed into stone, deafened by noise, deprived of sky, working without rest at machines whose original purpose no one remembers. It would see a prison and rush to break the walls. It would lift the Cittadini into open air, into warmth, into silence, and watch them die—because their lungs are calibrated for damp pressure, their ears for vibration, their eyes for the absence of sun. It would call this rescue. It would weep at the ingratitude of corpses.

The benevolent power is wiser than this. It knows that to love a thing is first to see it as it is, not as one would have made it.

So it looks longer.

It sees that the thrumming chord is not only noise but *language*—that mothers tune their heartbeats to specific frequencies so their infants can find them in the dark, that lovers identify each other across tunnels by the particular rhythm of a worn bearing they both pass daily, that the city sings and the people are its singers as much as its listeners. It sees that the compression of bodies in narrow spaces is not only confinement but *contact*—that a people who press foreheads together to speak have solved a loneliness that surface dwellers, with all their open rooms, have not. It sees the inherited tools, smooth with the oils of ancestral hands, and understands that the Cittadini have achieved something the surface rarely manages: a form of immortality through use, a continuity of touch across generations.

It sees also what is genuinely wrong.

It sees the young who dream of above and are punished for it—sent to the crawlspaces to have the dream pressed out of them. It sees the workers who fuse with their machines, breathing becoming piston-stroke, and notes that some of these fusions were chosen and some were merely *not refused*, which is not the same thing. It sees the perfect stasis, and knows that perfect stasis in a living system is another name for slow death held very still. It sees the mythology that calls confinement *chosen-ness*, and recognizes the particular ache of a people who have made their necessity into their pride because the alternative would be unbearable.

It sees, beneath the translucent skin, the small constant grief that no Cittadino names, because to name it would be to break the chord.

The power considers a long time. Time is not scarce to it.

Here is what it does not do:

It does not free them. Freedom imposed is only a new shape of cage.

It does not destroy the machines. The machines are their cathedral, their ancestors, their second body.

It does not appear in glory and announce itself. A god in those tunnels would be merely another pressure, another sound to filter, and the Cittadini have filtering enough.

It does not even, at first, reveal that it has come.

Here is what it does:

**First**, it adjusts something very small. In one pump station, in the warm currents where the translucent fronds grow, it alters the chemistry of the runoff by a few parts per million—introducing a compound the gardens can metabolize into something the Cittadini's bodies have been quietly hungering for across generations without knowing. Within a year, the children born in that district sleep more deeply. Their dreams, when they come, are less frightened. A few of them dream of above and are not ashamed in the morning.

**Second**, it does a thing with sound. Not silencing—never silencing; silence would kill them. But it introduces, into the great chord, a single new frequency: low, almost subliminal, woven so carefully into the existing harmonics that the Cittadini do not hear it as foreign. This frequency carries no message. It is simply *kind*. It is the vibrational equivalent of a hand laid gently on a shoulder. It plays beneath everything, for everyone, always. Those who are dying notice it most. They die a little less alone.

**Third**, and this takes longer—decades, perhaps a century, the power is patient—it begins to seed *options*. Not escape routes. Possibilities. In the deep maintenance crawlspaces, where the apprenticed dreamers are sent, it allows certain ancient passages to slowly, slowly reveal themselves: passages that lead not up to the killing surface but laterally, to other hollows, other chambers, places where the ceiling rises and a Cittadino could—if they wished, only if they wished—stretch fully upright for the first time in their life. It does not advertise these spaces. It simply makes them findable by those who are looking. Some Cittadini find them and return to their Turnings unchanged, but carrying a small secret warmth. Some find them and bring others. Some find them and weep, and do not know why, and go home, and are gentler with their children.

**Fourth**, it touches the mythology. This is the most delicate work. It does not contradict the story that Sottovelo was discovered, not built; the story is beautiful and partly true. But it adds, through the tactile poetry tapped on tunnel walls—poetry which begins to appear without clear authorship, in many districts at once—a new verse. The verse suggests that the perfect machine sleeping in the stone was not only waiting for operators but *dreaming* them, and that the dreams of the operators are part of the machine's own dreaming, and that to dream of above is not betrayal but the machine reaching, through its soft parts, toward something it cannot reach alone. The verse suggests that a Cittadino who dreams of vastness is not broken but is the city *thinking a new thought*.

This changes nothing immediately. But the next generation of apprenticed dreamers is taught the verse alongside the older teachings, and the deep maintenance crews begin to include, among their disciplines, the cultivation rather than suppression of certain dreams. A slow loosening. A pressure valve that no one notices is a valve.

**Fifth**, it watches.

It watches for the one who, in some generation, will be the first to walk into the lateral chambers and stand fully upright and not feel terror but recognition. It watches for the first Cittadino to wonder aloud whether the stasis is wisdom or only habit. It watches for the first parent who, when their child speaks of above, does not flinch but listens. These individuals it tends with particular care—not directing them, only ensuring that the small accidents of life do not crush them before they have done their small work of changing what a Cittadino can be.

It does not require that Sottovelo become anything other than itself. It does not need the Cittadini to walk in sunlight or sing in chorus or build cities of glass. If, in a thousand years, Sottovelo is still Sottovelo, still thrumming, still dense, still pale—the power will count this no failure, provided that the Cittadini who live there live there *because they have, in some real sense, chosen it*, and not because they have been compressed into a shape that cannot imagine anything else.

The work of the benevolent power, in the end, is not to lift them out.

It is to make the walls a little more porous to possibility. To add one kind frequency to the chord. To let the dreamers dream without punishment. To slip, into the great closed system, the smallest amount of *otherwise*—not enough to break it, only enough that the system, which had forgotten it was alive, remembers.

And then to wait. Wait the long wait that wisdom waits. Wait for the Cittadini themselves to decide what they want to become, now that *becoming* is something they are allowed to do.

The power loves them. This is what its love looks like: a chemistry, a frequency, a verse, a passage, a patience.

It does not sign its work. The Cittadini will, in time, attribute these things to the city itself—to Sottovelo dreaming new dreams through them. This is correct enough. The power does not mind being mistaken for the beloved. In some sense, by the time the work is done, it will not be a mistake at all.

Deep in the warrens, a child presses her palm to her mother's throat and feels a word she has never felt before. She does not know the word. Her mother does not know it either. But the child will carry it, and one day she will tap it on a tunnel wall, and someone will feel it and weep without knowing why, and the chord of Sottovelo will shift by a single, sustaining note.

This is what the benevolent power does.

This is all it does.

It is enough.
