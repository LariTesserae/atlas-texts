# What the Power Would Do

---

There is a temptation, when one is benevolent and wise and has noticed a place like this, to descend. To split the gray ceiling open with light. To still the machines mid-stroke, lift the mist from the workers' lungs, cool the rocks, plant orchards in the cracks. To rescue.

The benevolent power considers this. Considers it carefully, because the impulse is good and the suffering is real — the labored breath, the calloused hands, the children who play at being machines because they have known nothing else, the small tokens left in crevices by people who are not sure whom they are speaking to but speak anyway.

And then the power sets the impulse down.

Because to look at the Forgemaw Workers and see only what is missing would be to fail to see them. They have made something here. Not the factories — the factories are almost beside the point. They have made *each other*. They have made the communal hall and the clipped efficient jokes and the way a tool is passed on when someone falls. They have made the gesture of leaving a drop of oil on a rock. That gesture is not nothing. That gesture is one of the most beautiful things that has ever happened on the surface of any world, and it happened *here*, in the steam, made by hands that the power did not give them and a tenderness the power did not teach.

A wisdom would know: to lift them out would be to unmake them.

So the power does not descend. Not in the way of miracles.

Instead:

---

**First, it listens.** For a long time. Long enough to learn the difference between the hiss of the vent at the third forge and the hiss of the vent at the seventh, long enough to know which worker hums under her breath when she solders, long enough to learn the name (unspoken, even to herself) that a particular old man has given to a particular stunted tree near the eastern wall. The power learns the place the way one learns a beloved's sleeping breath. This takes what would be, in another frame, a very long time. The power has time.

**Second, it confirms what is already there.** The whispers in the steam — the shapes between the machines — the sense some of them have that the land has a spirit and the water has a memory: these intuitions are not delusions to be corrected. They are perceptions of something true that the workers, in their honesty, have noticed. The power does not announce itself, does not say *yes, I am here*. But it lets the whispers be slightly more coherent. It lets the shapes, when glimpsed, be shapes of comfort rather than dread. When a worker leaves a drop of oil on a rock, the power *receives* it. Quietly. Without fanfare. The gesture lands somewhere. The workers do not know this, but some of them, over generations, begin to feel that the gesture is *worth making* in a way that is hard to explain. That is the power's only reply, and it is enough.

**Third, it makes small adjustments — so small they cannot be told from luck.** A beam that was going to fall, falls slightly to the left. A child who wandered too near the rolling mill is, somehow, found by her mother a moment earlier than she would otherwise have been. A particular strain of moss, the one that grows on the cooler walls, turns out to filter something from the air that the lungs of the workers need; it spreads, slowly, over decades, without anyone quite noticing that the coughing has eased. The wiry grasses thicken in the cracks. One of the stunted trees, against all reason, sets fruit. The fruit is bitter but edible. A child eats one and laughs.

These are not miracles. They are the kind of grace that looks, from inside, exactly like the world being slightly kinder than expected. Which is, perhaps, what grace has always been.

**Fourth, it protects what they have made from what they have not yet faced.** The frozen equilibrium will not last forever — nothing does, not even places that feel eternal. Somewhere, in the unwritten future, there is a collapse: a seam exhausted, a boiler that finally goes, a sickness that comes in on the mist. The power does not prevent these things, because preventing them would be to take away the workers' authorship of their own story. But it makes sure that when the changes come, there is *somewhere to go*. It prepares, very slowly, a valley beyond the eastern wall — not paradise, just a place with softer rocks and sweeter water — and it lets rumors of this valley begin to seep into the dreams of the younger workers, the ones who will need, one day, to lead a walk out. The dreams are vague. The dreams feel like their own. This is correct. They *are* their own.

**Fifth, and most importantly, it loves them as they are.** This sounds like the smallest thing and is in fact the largest. The Forgemaw Workers, in all the long unchanging present of their labor, are *seen*. Not pitied. Not condescended to. Not regarded as a problem to be solved. Seen — the way one sees a person one loves: with attention to who they actually are, with delight in the specific ways they have become themselves, with sorrow at their sorrows and respect for their endurance and a kind of awe at the fact that out of sharp rock and bitter steam they have grown the habit of feeding each other.

The power, being wise, understands that this love does not need to be communicated to be real. The workers do not need to know they are beloved in order to be beloved. The asymmetry does not trouble the power. Love that requires acknowledgment is a younger love. This is an older one.

---

And so, in the cathedral of industry, nothing visibly changes.

The steam still rises. The rocks still weep. The children still play at building factories from scavenged tin. A woman still hums under her breath as she solders. An old man still nods, almost imperceptibly, at a particular tree as he passes it on his way to the third forge. Someone, somewhere, leaves a drop of oil on a rock for reasons she could not explain to you if you asked.

The drop of oil is received.

The balance holds.

It was always going to hold. But now it holds *with company*.
