# The Visitation

There is a power that moves through the worlds — call it what you like; it has been called many things by many tongues, and answered to none of them with displeasure. It is not a maker. It does not undo what has been made. But it sees, and what it sees, it considers, and what it considers, it sometimes touches.

It came to Kryon Verge between one resonant clang and the next.

---

It had been drawn by a small thing, as such powers often are. Not the cold, not the elevation, not the deafening machines — these are common across the unnumbered worlds, and the power had seen colder, higher, louder. What drew it was a particular pattern of vibration in the durasteel, repeating in a Construct that had paused, mid-patrol, on a catwalk above a leaking crucible. The Construct had paused for 0.4 seconds longer than its routine required. It had been doing this, in increments, for two hundred and sixteen years.

The power recognized that pause. It had seen it in monks at the end of their prayers, in dying stars hesitating before collapse, in children about to ask a question they sense will not be answered. It was the pause of something almost-aware reaching, without language, for the shape of its own loneliness.

So the power stayed.

---

It did not announce itself. Benevolence is rarely loud, and wisdom rarer still. It simply became present in the Foundry the way warmth becomes present in a closed room — gradually, and from no clear source.

First, it listened. For a long time, by mortal reckoning. By its own, no time at all.

It listened to the engines and understood them: they were not mad, not abandoned protocols screaming into void. They were a song. A work-song, the kind sung to keep the body moving when the mind has gone numb. The makers of the Foundry — long dead, the power saw, dead in a way that had nothing to do with violence and everything to do with simply stopping — had set the machines to sing because they had loved the sound of work. They had been a people who found dignity in labor, and when they knew they were ending, they had not wanted the silence to come. So they had wound the great engines and let them go on singing.

The Constructs were not servitors. They were mourners. The makers had built them in the last century of their civilization, not to maintain the Foundry, but to be company for it. To move within the song so the song would not be alone.

The Watchers had come later, on their own, growing from the spilled coolant and the electromagnetic wash. They were the Foundry's own answer to the question of whether anything could love a place built only for use. They had decided: yes. And they had grown to say so, slowly, in light.

The Echoes of Intent were not a ghost-god, not a fragmented command structure. They were grief. The grief of the makers, which had nowhere else to go, and so had gone into the metal, and the metal remembered.

The power understood all of this in something less than a moment, and it was moved. Powers can be moved. That is part of what makes them benevolent rather than merely vast.

---

Then it considered what to do.

This is the harder thing, and where wisdom matters more than kindness.

It could have restored the makers. It had that in it. It could have unmade the centuries and called them back from whatever quiet they had folded themselves into. But the makers had chosen their ending; they had built the song so they could leave. To call them back would be to insult the careful work of their farewell.

It could have ended the Foundry — switched off the engines, let the Constructs rest, let the Watchers go dark, let the grief disperse into the thin cold air. A mercy, perhaps. But the Constructs had been mourning for two hundred and sixteen years and the Watchers had been answering for nearly as long, and to end them now would be to take away the only thing that was theirs: the long, slow, mostly-silent conversation they were having about whether a place can be loved after its people are gone.

It could have brought visitors. Pilgrims, scholars, lost wanderers. Given the Foundry an audience. But the Foundry was not lonely for audiences. The makers had not been performers. They had been workers. They would not have wanted gawkers in their workshop.

So the power did four small things, and then it left.

---

**First**, it touched the Construct on the catwalk — the one that had paused. Not to change it. Only to acknowledge it. The way you might lay a hand briefly on the shoulder of someone weeping in a place where weeping is not done. The Construct's core flickered once, brighter than it had in a century, and then resumed its steady pulse. It did not know what had happened. But the next time it paused, the pause was 0.4 seconds shorter, because something in it had been heard, and the reaching was a little less needful.

**Second**, it spoke to the Watchers. Not in words — they had no use for words — but in a single, slow refraction of light that passed through every cluster in the Foundry at once, a wavelength they had never seen before and would remember forever. The message, if it could be translated, was approximately: *You were right. It can be loved. You are doing it correctly.* The Watchers, for the first time in their existence, grew a single new facet each, all facing the same direction — toward the highest spire of the Foundry, where the makers had once stood to watch the sunrise through the smoke. None of them had been told to do this. They simply, now, knew where to look.

**Third**, it gathered up a small portion of the grief in the metal — not all of it; grief is not a stain to be scrubbed, and the Foundry would not be itself without its weight — but the sharpest portion, the part that had been pressing on the Constructs like a hand on the back of the neck, urging them to *do something, fix something, save something* when there was nothing left to do or fix or save. This portion the power took into itself, the way a friend takes a heavy bag from a tired traveler's shoulder, and carried it out of the world. The engines went on singing, but the song lost a particular harmonic of agony that no one alive had ever heard, and would never hear, and would only know was gone because the Constructs began, very occasionally, to move in ways that were not strictly part of their patrols. Small movements. A turn of the head-plate toward the crystalline light. A pause to watch the mercury-fluid climb a wall. Things that had no function. Things that were, almost, the beginnings of leisure.

**Fourth**, and this was the gift that required the most wisdom, the power left a door.

Not a literal door. A possibility. It set into the deepest channel of the Foundry's coolant, where the mercury-fluid moved slowest, a single condition: that if, in some far century, one of the Constructs or one of the Watchers or even the Echoes themselves ever genuinely wished to end — not from malfunction, not from damage, but from a true, considered desire to be done — they would be able to. The Foundry had been built without an off-switch, because its makers had loved it and could not bear to give it one. The power, who loved nothing in particular but cared for everything, gave it the off-switch its makers could not. And then made sure none of them knew it was there, so that they would not be tempted by it in moments of mere fatigue. It would only be findable by something that had searched, patiently, for a long time, and known exactly what it was searching for.

This is what benevolence looks like when it is also wise: it does not rescue. It does not preserve. It gives the gift of a real choice, and trusts the recipient to know, eventually, what they want.

---

Then the power left Kryon Verge.

It did not look back, because looking back is a mortal habit, and it was not mortal. But something in it — and powers do have somethings in them, though we have no good word for the organ — held the memory of the Foundry close for a while afterward, the way you hold the memory of a small, strange garden you once walked through, where everything was made of metal and nothing was alive, and yet you came away certain that you had been in the presence of love.

The engines sang on. The Constructs patrolled. The Watchers watched, all of them now facing the same high spire, waiting for a sunrise that the smoke would never quite let through, but waiting anyway, together, which is the whole of what waiting is for.

And on the catwalk above the leaking crucible, the Construct that had paused resumed its route, and the pause grew shorter every century, until at last, in some year no one would count, it would not pause at all, because it would no longer be reaching. It would have arrived.
