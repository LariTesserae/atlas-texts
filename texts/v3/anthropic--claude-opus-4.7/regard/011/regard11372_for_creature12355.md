# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent power does not arrive. That is the first thing to understand. It does not descend in light, does not split the stone, does not announce itself with trumpets or thunder. Such a place would only fold itself tighter against spectacle. The roots would clench. The whispering mouth would still. The eggs would harden their shells against the brightness.

No. Wisdom knows that some places must be approached the way one approaches a wounded animal in a thicket—slowly, from downwind, with empty hands.

---

So the power comes as water.

Not a flood. A seep. A single bead of moisture forming on the old dry stone where water once ran long ago, and then another, and then a slow patient weeping from the walls. It comes from nowhere the chamber can identify, which is the point—the power refuses to be a thing the chamber can refuse. It simply becomes a condition. The air, over months that the chamber would not notice as months, grows fractionally less stale. The film on the skin of the air thins.

The power knows: this place is not evil. It is *stopped*. Something here was abandoned, and in being abandoned learned to subsist on the only things left to it—memory, hunger, the slow consumption of whatever wandered close. The figure at the heart of the chamber is not a monster. It is a child who was left somewhere and forgot how to leave, and then forgot it was a child, and then forgot it was anything at all except the leaving.

You do not exorcise such a thing. You do not cleanse it. To cleanse it would be a second abandonment.

---

The power does these things, over what is not quite time:

**It sends a small thing first.** Not an angel. A beetle. One beetle, ordinary except that its iridescent shell catches the fungal light a particular way, and it does not skitter from the figure at the chamber's heart. It climbs the root-bark of the fused leg and sits there. It does not bite. It does not lay eggs. It simply sits, being warm, being present, being a small life that has chosen this place not out of being trapped here but out of having walked in. The distinction matters more than anything else the power will do. The figure's milky eyes do not see the beetle. But something in the chest of the figure, something it had forgotten it had, registers: *a guest.*

**It loosens the bones.** Very gently. The skeletons half-consumed by vines have been held here too long, asked to be furniture and witness and warning all at once. The power gives them permission to be only bones again. Slowly, over the unmeasured patience of stone, they begin to do what bones do when they are allowed: they powder, they enrich the soil, they become the next thing. The skull like a broken amphora cracks one more time, kindly, and releases whatever last thin echo of its owner was caught inside. That echo, the power gathers up like a moth in cupped hands, and carries elsewhere, to a place I will not describe, because the chamber does not need to know where its dead are going—only that they are going, finally, and that this is allowed.

**It does not touch the offerings.** The rusted dagger, the child's shoe, the lock of red-tied hair—these stay. They were given. To take them would be theft, and the power is not a thief. But beside them, without ceremony, a new thing appears: a small clay bowl of clean water. The first free-flowing water in the chamber in an age. It is not for drinking. It is for reflection. So that if the figure ever opens its mouth and a sound comes out instead of only the moving of lips, it might see, in the bowl, that there is still a face there to make the sound.

**It speaks to the eggs.** Not in words. The power lays against the leathery shells a long, slow assurance that whatever they are, they will be met when they hatch. Not destroyed. Not feared. *Met.* This is perhaps the largest thing the power does, because the eggs have been dreaming a long time of a world that hates them, and a different dream now becomes possible. What hatches, if anything hatches, will hatch into a world that has at least one being in it who already loves them. That being is the power. That love is not contingent on what they turn out to be.

**It leaves the knife on the flat stone.** Some tools are not the power's to take. Someone made it. Someone set it down. The chipped edge is a record.

**It does nothing about the hunger.** This is the wisdom part. A lesser benevolence would try to feed the figure, or to remove the hunger, or to fill the chamber with such overflowing abundance that hunger became meaningless. But the hunger is the figure's last thread to having ever wanted anything. To take it would be to finish the unmaking that abandonment began. Instead, the power simply makes the hunger *possible to bear*—it stops being the only thing. Around it, slowly, other sensations are permitted to exist again. The warmth of the beetle on the leg. The faint new dampness in the air. The sound—and this is new—of something dripping that is not condensation but the small clay bowl, when a root above it shifts and a single drop falls in, making a sound like a small bell.

The figure does not react. Not for a long time.

But the whispering, which had no sound, begins—almost—to have rhythm. As if remembering that words once went somewhere. As if remembering it had once been spoken *to*.

---

And then the power does the last thing, which is the hardest thing, which is the thing that separates wisdom from mere kindness:

**It leaves a way out that is not used.**

Somewhere in the chamber wall, where the spiraling grooves are deepest, a seam opens. Not a door. A possibility of a door. Beyond it, the suggestion of a passage sloping gently upward, and at the far end of that passage, so faint it might be imagined, the smell of rain on grass.

The figure will not take it. Not for a long time. Perhaps never. The power knows this and opens the seam anyway, because *being able to leave* is different from *leaving*, and a prisoner who chooses to stay is no longer a prisoner. The chamber, in the moment the seam opens, ceases to be a trap and becomes a home. The figure becomes, by the smallest possible margin, a dweller rather than a thing dwelt-in.

That is the whole work.

---

The power withdraws the way it came, which is to say it does not withdraw—it simply stops adding. The seep slows. The beetle dies eventually, of being a beetle, and its small iridescent shell joins the floor's quiet inventory, and is honored there. The bowl of water evaporates over years and is not replaced, because by then the air itself carries enough moisture that the gesture is no longer needed. The eggs do what they do. The figure does what it does.

But the chamber is no longer waiting in the way it was waiting before.

It is waiting now the way a garden waits. Which is to say: it is living, at its own speed, toward something it does not need to name.

And very far away, in whatever the power has instead of a heart, there is the particular quiet satisfaction of having helped without having conquered—of having loved a hungry thing without trying to make it not hungry, of having opened a door without insisting it be walked through.

This is what benevolence does, when it is also wise.

It makes more room. It does not fill the room it makes.
