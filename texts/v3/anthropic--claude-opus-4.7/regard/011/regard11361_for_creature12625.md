# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent and wise power has been watching Siltgrip for a long time. Not days. Not seasons. Long enough to know that the shale is sharp because the land is new, and the land is new because *it was made for them* — though they have forgotten this, and perhaps never knew.

Here is what the power considers, and what it does.

---

## First, It Considers What Not To Do

The power is wise, which means it knows the shape of harm done by good intentions.

It does not drain the estuary. The Siltkin would not be Siltkin on dry land. They would be refugees in their own bodies — webbed fingers useless, eyes too pale for sun, internal fires roaring without purpose until they burned themselves hollow. To "save" them from wetness would be to unmake them.

It does not give them fire that cannot be extinguished, nor stone that does not weep, nor timber that does not rot. Siltgrip's whole grammar is mending. Take away the need to mend, and you take away the huddle, the shared labor, the gossip that is also warning, the elder whose hands know which piling sings wrong. A people maintained by ease is a people slowly dissolved.

It does not appear. It does not announce itself. The Siltkin's suspicion of outsiders is not a flaw to be corrected — it is a membrane, and membranes are how small things stay alive.

It does not make them grateful. Gratitude to a power they did not ask for would be another weight on backs already bent.

So the power moves the way water moves through shale: by seeping.

---

## What It Actually Does

**It steadies one piling.** Not all of them. One. The piling beneath the house of an old woman called Mistwalker, whose granddaughter is sick and who has not slept in four nights checking the floor for the soft give that means collapse. The piling does not become eternal. It simply holds for another seven years — long enough. Mistwalker will never know. She will think she set it well. She *did* set it well. The power only ensured that "well" was enough this time.

**It bends a fog bank.** On a particular grey morning, the mist parts for forty minutes over the open estuary. A boy named Reedweaver, out in his coracle alone for the first time, looks up and sees the distant sea. He does not understand what he is feeling. He will tell no one — the Siltkin do not have words for it, and he is shy. But for the rest of his life, when the work is hardest and the damp deepest, something in him will remember that there is *more*, and that the more is beautiful, and that he was permitted to see it. He will become, in time, the kind of elder children trust with their fears.

**It teaches the eels a small thing.** Nothing dramatic. The eels begin, very gradually over three generations, to favor a particular bend in a particular channel during the cold months. The Siltkin notice, as they notice everything. They adjust. There is slightly more food in the lean season. No one calls it a miracle. They call it *knowing the bend*, and they are proud of having figured it out.

**It quiets a sickness.** A fever was going to move through the clutch in the coming spring — the kind that takes the very young and the very old. The power does not cure it. It slows it. It gives the fever a longer incubation, so that by the time it arrives, the warm season has come early, and the herbs that blunt it are already up in the samphire beds. Six children live who would not have. Two elders die who would have died anyway, but a season later and in their sleep, which is what they would have asked for if asked.

**It writes one true sentence into the mud.** Deep beneath Siltgrip, in a stratum no Siltkin will ever excavate, the power lays down a thin band of something that is not quite stone and not quite memory. If, in some far century, the estuary recedes and another people come and dig, they will find this band and not know what it is. But the band says, in a language older than language: *Here lived a people who were exactly themselves. They were not a stage on the way to something else. They were complete.*

This is for the power's own record. The Siltkin do not need it. But the power believes that completeness, witnessed, is a form of love.

---

## The Hardest Thing

The hardest thing the power does is the thing it almost cannot bear.

It lets Siltgrip end, eventually.

Not soon. Not in the lifetime of anyone now breathing the wet air. But the land is young, which means it is still moving, and one day — three hundred years from now, perhaps four hundred — the estuary will shift, the shale will subside, and the clutch will have to be abandoned. The Siltkin who live then will go inland, carrying what they can. They will become something else. Their descendants will not have webbed fingers. They will tell stories about a place of mist and mud, and the stories will grow strange and beautiful and only half-true.

The power could prevent this. It could anchor the shale. It could hold the estuary in place against the patient arithmetic of the planet.

It does not.

Because Siltgrip's beauty is partly that it is *provisional*. The resilient melancholy, the dogged mending, the fierce clutching — these exist because the place is temporary. Make it permanent, and you have made it a museum. The Siltkin would still live there, but they would no longer be the children of the clutch. They would be the curators of it. A subtle death, but a death.

So the power lets the ending come. And when it comes, the power will be there too — not to stop it, but to make sure the leaving is not a rout. To make sure someone has a dry place to sleep that first night inland. To make sure the stories survive the journey. To make sure that when the last Siltkin looks back at the drowning thatch, she feels not failure but *completion*. Her people lived. Her people were themselves. The mud kept them, and now releases them.

---

## What the Power Loves

The power loves the Siltkin the way one loves a particular shade of grey at a particular hour — not because grey is the best color, but because *that* grey, *that* hour, is irreplaceable, and the universe is the kind of place that makes such things and then unmakes them, and the loving has to happen in between.

It loves the *glorp* of mud releasing a boot.

It loves the rare brief smiles.

It loves Mistwalker's hands and Reedweaver's silence and the way the clay-dust ghosts them all so that they look, from a certain angle, like the estuary dreaming itself into people.

And so it does the small things. The steadied piling. The parted mist. The bent eel. The slowed fever. The buried sentence.

It does not save them.

It *accompanies* them.

This, the power has learned over a very long time, is almost always the right answer.
