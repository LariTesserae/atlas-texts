The benevolent and wise power considers the smelting district for a long time before doing anything at all.

This is the first thing to understand. Wisdom that arrives ready to act is not wisdom; it is appetite wearing wisdom's clothes. The power has seen too many helpers descend into too many places, scattering gifts like a farmer scattering seed on stone, and watched the gifts roll away or rot or wound the ones they were meant to feed. So the power sits with the district. Watches. Learns the churring call of the salamander-things and the particular creak that means a roof is about to give. Learns the names. Learns which Kessler favors which seat at the evening meal, and that Harek hums when he is frightened, and that old Colm's tremor is worse in her left hand, and that the baby has the Kessler cough already, audible only at the very end of a long cry.

The power learns the sealed door in the basement, too. It knows what is behind it. It does not say.

Then it begins, slowly, to do a few small things.

---

**First, the easiest thing, because it costs the district nothing and asks nothing of them in return:**

The power adjusts, very slightly, the thermal veins. Not enough that anyone would notice a change in a single night—just enough that, over the course of a season, the worst of the sulfurous vents beneath the assay office shift their flow by a few degrees. The air in the children's interior rooms grows incrementally cleaner. The Kessler cough does not vanish, because too much damage is already done in too many lungs, but the baby's cough eases by the end of the month, and by the end of the year it is gone, and the baby grows into a child who can run without stopping to wheeze.

No one credits the power for this. No one knows. This is correct. A gift that demands recognition is a transaction, and the power is not a merchant.

**Second, a harder thing, because it requires restraint:**

The power does not kill the salamander-things. It considers it. They are bold this year, and getting bolder, and Senna has seen one watching from the roof. It would be easy—a thought, a gesture, and the great predators would simply cease, and the family would be safer than they have been in three generations.

But the salamander-things are not evil. They are warm-blooded and furred and have learned to live in the poison the way the Kesslers have learned to live in the poison, and they keep the not-rats in check, and they have their own young denning in the chimneys, and removing them would not produce safety. It would produce a different danger, slower and harder to see, in which the not-rats multiplied and the beetles followed and the family's food stocks became unreliable and the children sickened from new sources.

So the power does a smaller thing. It nudges the scent-trails of the great ones, gently, away from the assay office. It encourages the dens in the chimneys near the river to stay near the river. When the one on the roof watches Senna, the power lets it watch, because Senna needs to know that she is watched—that knowledge keeps her alive—but the power makes sure that the watching is the end of it, that night, and that Senna gets home.

The salamander-things do not know they are being shepherded. They feel only their own preferences, slightly clarified.

**Third, the thing that requires the most care, because it involves a person who has made a choice:**

The power knows about Ama. It knows the coins she has saved, and the route she has memorized, and the way she practices in her head the words she will not be able to say. The power could do many things here. It could make her stay—a small adjustment of fear, a strengthened bond, and she would not leave. It could make her go more easily—remove an obstacle in the boundary zone, place a kind stranger in her path on the second day when her resolve will falter.

The power does neither. Ama's choice is hers. To take it from her, even in the direction of her own apparent good, would be to treat her as something less than what she is, which is a person making her life.

What the power does instead is this: it makes sure that the spring she leaves in is a spring in which the salamander-things are, in fact, particularly active, so that her family's attention is genuinely focused outward and her going is not noticed until she is well clear. It makes sure that the route she has memorized is, on the day she travels it, free of the worst hazards—not by removing them, but by ensuring she happens to travel during the hours when those hazards are quietest. And it makes sure that when she reaches the city, in the first terrible week when her coin runs lower than she planned and her courage falters, there is a woman in a bakery on the third street she tries who needs an apprentice and likes the look of her.

The woman in the bakery is not a plant. She is herself, with her own life. The power only ensured that Ama walked down that street on that day. The rest is Ama, and the baker, and what they make of each other.

And for Senna, who understood—Senna who watched her cousin go and did not tell—the power does one further thing. It makes sure, the following winter, that a letter reaches the assay office. Not a long letter. Ama is not yet ready for a long letter. Just a few lines, in Ama's hand, saying that she is alive and working and that she thinks of them. Harek brings it back from his yearly journey, and the family reads it together in the main room, and old Colm cries, and Seff goes outside to be alone for a while, and Senna keeps the letter under her mattress for years afterward.

The power did not write the letter. Ama wrote it. The power only made sure it found its way.

**Fourth, the matter of Piotr's maps:**

The power loves Piotr's maps. This is worth saying. Wisdom can love a thing without needing to change it. The maps are beautiful—the rust-and-fat ink, the closed-eye symbol, the patient nightly updating of a world that no one outside the family will ever see. They are an act of devotion. They are the district's only true cartography, more accurate than any survey the city above has ever made.

The power does not touch them. It only makes sure, in a small way, that the paper Piotr uses lasts. That the damp does not eat the ledger pages as quickly as it otherwise would. That when Piotr dies—which will happen in eleven years, peacefully, in his sleep, of causes the power chooses not to forestall because Piotr is tired and has earned his rest—the maps survive him, and pass to a great-niece who has the same patient hand, and continue.

**Fifth and last, the door in the basement:**

The power knows what is behind the door. It will not say, here, because Mira sealed it and the family agreed, and that agreement is itself a form of wisdom—a recognition that some knowledge is not currently survivable, and that the brick and mortar are not cowardice but care.

What the power does is this: it reinforces the seal. Not visibly. Not in a way anyone would notice. It simply ensures that the brick holds, and the mortar holds, and the warning in the frame does not weather away, and the thing behind the door—which is patient, and which has been waiting a long time, and which is not malicious exactly but is also not something the Kesslers should ever meet—remains behind the door.

And on the quietest nights, when someone reports hearing a tap, a scrape, a sound like breathing, the power makes sure that the family's unanimous agreement holds. That no one, in a moment of curiosity or grief or drunken bravery, decides this is the night to find out. The power does not control them. It only ensures that the door, when considered, continues to feel like a door that should not be opened. Which is, after all, true.

---

The power does not reveal itself. It does not require thanks. It does not, in any of the generations it watches over the district, perform a single act that the Kesslers could point to and call a miracle.

The Kessler cough lessens. The salamander-things keep their distance, a little. Ama escapes and writes home. Piotr's maps endure. The door stays sealed. The baby grows. The family survives, not lavishly, not transformed, but a little more than they would have otherwise—and the district itself, the wet stones and the amber haze and the chimneys like brick fingers, remains what it is, because the power understands that the district is not a problem to be solved. It is a place where people live.

What the power has done, in the end, is the work of a good neighbor who happens to be very quiet and very capable: it has weeded a little, where the weeds were choking. It has watched the children play. It has loved the maps. It has let the people make their own lives, and only stepped in at the edges, where stepping in cost them nothing and gave them a little more room to be themselves.

This, the power believes, is what benevolence looks like when it has grown wise enough to know its own dangers.

The salamander-things churr in the chimneys. The steam rises from the river. Old Colm watches from her window, and says, one evening, that the salamander-things seem a little farther this year than last—and no one quite knows whether to believe her, because Colm says many things, and the district keeps its own counsel, and the amber light deepens into the long industrial dusk that is the only night they know.

The power, satisfied, turns part of its attention elsewhere. But not all. Never all. Some attention, it leaves with the Kesslers, for as long as there are Kesslers, because that is what attention is for.
