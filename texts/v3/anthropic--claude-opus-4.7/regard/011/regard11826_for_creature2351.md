# The Visitation

The benevolent power arrives not with thunder or radiance, but as a slow change in the quality of attention paid to Undergaar. It has been watching for a long time — perhaps always — and what it has finally decided is that watching is not the same as understanding, and understanding is not the same as care made manifest.

It comes first as a question to itself: *What does this place need?*

And it is wise enough to know it does not yet know.

---

## The First Act: Listening

Before anything else, it listens.

For what might be a year of surface time, or a moment of its own, the power becomes diffuse — settling into the seep-water and the spore-light, the rhythm of pistons, the pause between a Rockvorn's footfalls. It does not announce itself. It does not heal what it has not yet been invited to touch. It learns the difference between *toil* and *suffering*, which are not the same, though a hasty god might confuse them.

It discovers, listening, that the Rockvorn are not unhappy in the way an outsider would assume. Their quiet is not the quiet of despair. Their endless labor is, in part, a liturgy — the maintenance of the great machine that is also a prayer to the stone. To swoop in and abolish their work would be to abolish *them*, and the wise power knows that the cruelest mercies are the ones that mistake the sufferer's identity for their pain.

But it also finds, threaded through that liturgy, true griefs:

- Children born with malformed spiracles, drowning slowly in their own city's air.
- A loneliness that runs beneath the few-worded culture like an underground river the Rockvorn themselves cannot quite hear.
- An old fear, sediment-deep, that the stone is dying — that the tremors come more often, that the luminous cracks have been narrowing for generations.
- The factories that must run, that no one remembers why they must run, only that stopping them was forbidden by ancestors whose reasoning is lost.

These are the true wounds. The power notes them, gently, the way one notes the location of a thorn before deciding whether to pull it.

---

## The Second Act: The Gifts That Are Not Gifts

The wise power does not give the Rockvorn sunlight. It does not raise them to the surface. It does not replace their respirators with clean lungs or their labor with leisure. To do so would be a kind of murder dressed as generosity.

Instead, it does smaller things, and asks permission for each one in the only language the Rockvorn would recognize — through the stone itself.

**It makes the rock speak more clearly.** The faint spiritual hum the Rockvorn have always reached toward in meditation grows a little louder, a little more articulate. Not words. Never words. But meaning. The elders, sitting in their stone-circles, begin to weep dryly behind their masks, because the thing they had hoped was real has confirmed itself without ever quite breaking the rules of how it must remain mysterious.

**It teaches the moss.** In the lush green pockets where bioluminescent fungi cling, new strains begin to thrive — strains that consume the worst of the industrial smog and exhale air the malformed children can actually breathe. The power does not plant them. It only nudges the spores that were already there toward a different possibility. Within three generations, the children born wrong will be born well. The Rockvorn will credit their own careful tending of the moss-gardens, and they will be right to.

**It reveals the forgotten reason.** Deep beneath the city, in a chamber no living Rockvorn has entered, the power illuminates — softly, like a fungus blooming — the original purpose of the great machines. They are not factories. They were never factories. They are *pressure regulators*, built by Rockvorn ancestors to keep the magma chambers below Undergaar from rupturing. The endless toil has been keeping the city alive without anyone remembering why. A young engineer, drawn by a dream she will not be able to explain, finds the chamber. She tells the others. The work, suddenly, has its meaning back. Nothing changes about the labor itself. Everything changes about how it is borne.

**It does nothing about the loneliness.** This is the hardest restraint. The power *could* knit the Rockvorn into easier sociality, could loosen their tongues and gather them into warmth. But it understands that their reserve is theirs — a thing shaped by stone, valuable in its own right, and not for an outside hand to reshape. What it does instead is *very small*: it makes the echoes in certain chambers carry voices a little farther, a little more warmly. So that a Rockvorn who chooses, in some rare moment, to speak aloud will find their words received by more ears than they expected. The opening must come from them. The power only widens the room.

---

## The Third Act: The Standing Offer

Before withdrawing — though it never truly withdraws; benevolent powers do not abandon what they have come to love — it leaves one last thing.

In the deepest moss-garden, where the light is greenest and the water purest, a single stone appears that was not there before. It is unremarkable. A Rockvorn could sit on it. Most will, eventually, without knowing why they chose that spot to rest.

The stone is a listening place. Any Rockvorn who sits upon it and has something they cannot bear alone — a grief, a question, a terror, a love they have no words for — will be heard. Not answered. *Heard.* Completely. With the full attention of something vast and kind that has decided their small subterranean lives are worth its infinite patience.

Most will never know what the stone is. They will only know that after sitting there awhile, they feel less alone, and the work feels lighter, and the dark feels less like an enemy and more like a companion who has been waiting all along for them to notice it was never indifferent.

---

## What the Power Does Not Do

It does not save them from death. It does not save them from labor. It does not save them from being Rockvorn, with all that means.

Because the wise part of being benevolent is knowing that a life is not improved by being replaced with a different life. The Rockvorn are *themselves* — stone-skinned, spiracle-throated, slow-spoken, stubborn, sacred. The power loves them as they are, and its love takes the shape of helping them be more fully what they already were, rather than less.

It tends. It does not transform.

And in the centuries that follow, Undergaar will not become a paradise. It will remain damp, and cold, and strange, and quiet. But the children will breathe. The work will mean something. The stone will hum back when spoken to. And in the deepest garden, on an unremarkable rock, a Rockvorn will occasionally sit and feel — without ever having the word for it — *known.*

That is what the benevolent and wise power does.

That, and nothing more, and nothing less.
