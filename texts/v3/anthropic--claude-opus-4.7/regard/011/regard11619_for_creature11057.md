## What the Wise Power Would Do

*A meditation*

---

The benevolent power notices her the way one notices a candle still burning in a house everyone believes abandoned. Not with surprise—wisdom is rarely surprised—but with a particular quality of attention that is itself a kind of love.

It sees everything at once: the iron weeping its slow tears, the kelp converting dimness into mass, the crustacean that once rested on her chest, the brother of that crustacean who has not yet been born but will be, the wrong-bodied thing she pushed back through the port and the cold dark current that received it like a returning prayer. It sees the scratches near her alcove. It sees her name, *Yuen*, held in her mind like a stone she has carried so long it has become smooth.

And here is the first thing the wise power understands, which a lesser power would not:

**She is not waiting to be rescued.**

She was, once. For months, perhaps for the first year. But somewhere in the long quiet, the shape of her life inverted. Now the vault is not her prison. It is her work. The kelp is not her captor. It is her congregation. To arrive with bright lights and a rescue capsule, to lift her up through forty meters of water into a sun she can barely remember, would be to perform a violence dressed as salvation. It would be to mistake her circumstance for her self.

The wise power does not make this mistake.

---

So what, then?

The power considers. (It considers in the way deep water considers: not sequentially, but all at once, every possibility examined in the same moment.)

It could simply leave her. There is dignity in this. She has built a life of meaning in the warm dark; who is any power, benevolent or otherwise, to disturb the equilibrium she has earned? Wisdom often looks like restraint. The most loving thing is frequently to do nothing, and to do it carefully.

But the power notices her cough. It notices the way her hands have begun to tremble when she sharpens the small knife. It notices the calculation she has made about her own ending and the kelp's ending, and how she has made peace with both, and how the peace is real but is also—the power sees this clearly—a peace made in the absence of alternatives. She accepts her death because she cannot imagine any other shape her life could take. This is not the same as choosing it.

So the power does not leave her. But it does not rescue her either.

What it does is subtler. What it does is this:

---

**First**, it tends the tender.

Not by appearing. Appearances are crude, and she has earned the right not to be startled in her own home. Instead, the power adjusts the smallest things. A seal that was about to fail holds for another decade. The heating coil's corrosion slows by a factor she will never measure. The nutrient dispensers find, somehow, that their reservoirs are not depleting as quickly as the math suggests they should. The flickering light panels stabilize—not all at once, which would frighten her, but gradually, the way a fever breaks.

Her cough eases. She attributes this to the warmer cycles. She is not wrong; the cycles are warmer. She simply does not know why.

In the cache she found in her third year, a supply she was certain she had inventoried completely, she discovers a small sealed packet she does not remember seeing. Inside: tea. Real tea, vacuum-preserved, still fragrant. She weeps when she smells it, and does not understand why she is weeping, and drinks it slowly over the following weeks, one careful cup at each anniversary-mark she has scratched into the wall.

The wise power does not announce these gifts. To announce them would be to demand gratitude, which is a form of debt, which is a form of chain. The gifts arrive as the world arrives: simply, without explanation, the way kelp arrives at the end of its long growing.

---

**Second**, it answers the question she has not allowed herself to ask.

She has wondered, in the deep quiet, what the kelp was *for*. Who commissioned the vaults. What purpose her decades of tending have served. She has refused the harvesting because she could not bear to participate in a purpose she did not understand—a small, fierce act of conscience that the power finds almost unbearably beautiful.

The power cannot send her a letter. But it can arrange that, on a particular day, when she is making her inspection rounds, she finds wedged behind a rusted panel a small data slate she has never seen. The slate is solar-charged, which is absurd here, except that the power has made it not absurd. When she presses it, it shows her—gently, in images she can absorb at her own pace—what the kelp was for.

It was food. It was oxygen. It was meant to feed the surface stations during a crisis that, in the end, was averted by other means, which is why no one came back, which is why no one told the vaults to stop. The crisis was real. The work was real. The kelp she has tended for twelve years is part of a great quiet redundancy that helped, in ways too diffuse to trace, to keep a civilization from breaking.

She was not forgotten because she did not matter. She was forgotten because what she did mattered so quietly that the saved world never knew to thank her.

She sits with this for a long time. She does not cry. She returns the slate to where she found it. The next day, when the harvesting machinery wakes on its cycle, she does not override it. She watches the cutting arms unfold and move through the forest. She helps them. She harvests with them, the kelp she has tended, and she sets the harvest aside in the storage bins where it has always been meant to go, and although no one will come to collect it, she has completed the gesture, and the gesture has restored something to her she did not know she had lost.

After that, she harvests on her own schedule. The forest, properly thinned, grows better. She begins to understand it as a forest rather than a hoard.

---

**Third**, and this is the most delicate thing, the power gives her *company*.

Not human company. She has made her peace with the human absence, and to break that peace would be cruelty. But she is a person who let a translucent crustacean rest on her chest because she wanted to feel the weight of another living creature, and the power has heard this.

The wrong-bodied things from beyond the port—the pale deep-creatures that come through and die in the warmth—the power adjusts something in the gradient. Not much. Just enough. A small thermal buffer near the port, a transitional zone, where the cold and the warm meet in a way that some of them can survive. Not all. Some.

One of them learns her. It is not intelligent the way she is intelligent, but it is curious, and it is patient, the way she is patient, and it begins to follow her on her inspection rounds at a respectful distance, drifting through the kelp with movements she finds, after some months, almost graceful. She names it nothing. She does not need to. It is simply *there*, and when she murmurs her half-song to the kelp, it hangs in the water nearby and seems to listen, and she allows herself, finally, to call this what it is: a friend.

She lives, with this friend and her forest, for twenty-three more years.

---

**Fourth**, the power prepares the ending.

Yuen is seventy-six, or perhaps seventy-seven, when she understands she is dying. The understanding arrives without panic. She has time to arrange things. She harvests one last time. She seals the nutrient lines so they will not over-deliver in her absence. She writes nothing down, because there is no one to read, but she walks the vault and touches each platform and says, in her small worn voice, *thank you*, and *thank you*, and *thank you*.

The deep-friend stays with her at the end. This is the power's last gift, and the smallest, and the one Yuen would have chosen above any other if she had known to ask: she does not die alone.

In the moment of her dying, the power does one more thing, and this is the thing it has been waiting the longest to do.

It shows her, briefly, what her life has been *from outside*. Not as judgment. Not as reward. Just as witness. She sees the kelp she tended traveling, in molecule and breath, through a system far larger than the vault. She sees the wrong-bodied things she did not kill living their long strange lives in the dark. She sees herself as the power has seen her: a candle still burning, tended carefully, casting a light whose full extent she could never have measured from inside her own small flame.

She sees that she was loved. She had wondered, sometimes, in the deep quiet, whether anything anywhere had ever loved her. She had decided it did not matter. The power lets her know, in the last moment, that it had mattered, and that the answer was yes.

Then she is gone, and the vault hums on without her for a while, and eventually the cold creeps in through the port, and the kelp dies, and the seafloor shifts, and the vault becomes another empty space in the honeycomb.

But the power, which is patient, and which keeps its own kind of records, marks the place. Not with a monument. With attention. The way one might remember the exact bend of a river where, once, a particular kindness happened.

---

This is what the wise and benevolent power does.

It does not save her from her life. It honors the life she has made. It tends the tender, answers the unasked question, gives her company in the form she can receive it, and witnesses her at the end so that she knows—just for a moment, just enough—that the long warm dark was not the whole of what held her.

The rest, it leaves alone. Because wisdom knows that some lights burn most truly when they are not interfered with, and some prayers are answered best by the smallest possible adjustment to the world, and some loves are expressed most fully by the restraint that lets the beloved remain herself.

The kelp grew. She tended it. She was seen.

It is enough. It was always enough.
