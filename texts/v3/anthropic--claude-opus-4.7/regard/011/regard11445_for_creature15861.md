# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent and wise power has been watching the Sieve-Hollow for a long time now. Not from above — that is a habit of lesser powers — but from inside the pulse of the springs, the patience of the slate, the way mist learns to fold itself into cranes. It has been there longer than the Many know. It is, in some sense, the reason the bowl remembers it was once seabed at all.

So the first thing it does is *nothing.*

This is the hardest and wisest of its acts. A lesser benevolence would arrive bearing gifts: dry timber that never warps, iron in heaping baskets, a roof over the whole Hollow to keep the mist off the children. It would mean well, and it would unmake everything. Because the Hollow-Folk are not a people who *endure* their conditions — they are a people *composed* by them. Take away the damp and the wiry hair would never need eel oil; take away the cracking slate and the ear-flanges would forget how to listen; take away the constant negotiation with water and the Seam-list would fall silent, and with it the small grave courtesy by which they hold each other dry. To rescue them from their bowl would be to dissolve them more completely than any flood.

The power understands this. It is wise, after all. So it begins with restraint, which is the first gift.

---

The second thing it does is **bear witness**, properly.

Somewhere in the Hollow — perhaps in the mirror-finish of a shaded tub, perhaps in the tingling of a gutter-ward's toadstone — the people have always sensed they were being attended to. The power makes this attention slightly more legible. Not a face in the water, never that; the inverted map of the sky is enough. But it lets the omens be a little truer. The fog-cranes that fold three times for a deep tide: it sees to it that they fold three times when, and only when, a deep tide is in fact coming. It tunes the signs. It makes the Hollow's already-uncanny grammar more trustworthy, so that the elders' instincts and the children's superstitions converge a little more often onto the same correct guess.

This is a small thing. It will not be noticed for a generation. But across that generation the gutter-wards will lose fewer apprentices, and the tier councils will sleep a little easier, and no one will know why except to say: *the bowl has been kind this year.*

---

The third thing is **the matter of the needles.**

The rock needles that rise overnight beside the throat-wells, soft for an hour, then true stone — these are the power's own slow handwriting. It has been signing the Hollow into permanence one needle at a time, anchoring the shale against the day the sea remembers itself fully and tries to come back. The elders are right: the needles hold the bowl.

The power decides, now that it has been seen and named (even if only as *benevolent, wise*), to write a little faster. Not so fast that anyone notices the count is up. Just fast enough that when the great storm comes — and it will come, in the lifetime of a child now sleeping in a hanging cradle — the bowl will hold.

It will hold. The ridgeline will overtop. Tiers will be lost. There will be funerals enough to make the throat-wells belch for three nights running. But the Hollow itself will not collapse into the marsh, will not join the half-drowned siblings beyond the ridge. Some of the Many will survive on their lash-poles, ears flared, reading the ripples — exactly as the marshers' stories foretell. And they will rebuild upward from the needles, and they will not know that the stone was held in place by something that loved them.

This is what benevolence looks like when it is also wise: it does not prevent the storm. It prevents the *erasure.* It lets grief happen and protects continuity. It saves the *being* of the people, which is something larger and stranger than the lives of the people, though it is made of nothing else.

---

The fourth thing is **a single, quiet alteration**, and this is where the power permits itself something almost like affection.

It chooses one child. Not the most sensitive — not the one whose fontanel pulses hardest in the storm-pressure. A middling child. A child who will grow up to be an ordinary gutter-ward, neither famous nor failed, with two iron studs and a steady hand. To this child, once, in a dream the child will not remember on waking, the power says: *When you are old, and the silence comes that everyone fears, do not go to the throat-wells. Go up. Go to the highest platform and listen there. There is a heartbeat above as well as below.*

That is all. The child will live a long life. In her eighty-third year, on a morning when the drip and creak and sigh and whisper all stop at once and the Many begin their instinctive migration downward toward the wells, she will turn instead and climb. She will stand on the topmost platform in the mist and she will hear something — not loud, not a voice, just a slow, vast, attentive pulse that has been there the whole time. And she will laugh, because she will finally understand the dream she does not remember, and she will come back down and she will not tell anyone what she heard, because the Hollow-Folk do not waste syllables.

But she will be different after. Calmer. The young ones will come to her with their fears about the Still, and she will say something small and useless-sounding, like *the bowl listens both ways,* and they will go away comforted without knowing why.

This is how the power leaves a fingerprint: not in stone, not in weather, but in the bearing of one old woman who knew, for an hour, that she was loved.

---

The fifth thing, last and longest, is **patience.**

The power knows what the Hollow-Folk are becoming. It can see the webbed toes lengthening across generations, the ear-flanges growing more articulate, the breath-quiet language compressing further toward pure rhythm. It knows that somewhere far ahead — past storms, past the sea's return, past the slow forgetting of iron and indigo — the Many will not be people anymore in any sense an outsider would recognize. They will be something the marsh has no word for. Something the bowl has been shaping them toward since the god-beast's rake first scored the seabed. Something the power has been quietly mid-wifing, needle by needle, omen by omen, dream by dream.

It does not hurry this. It does not even particularly *aim* at it. A wise benevolence does not have ends; it has companionships. It walks with what it loves, at the pace of what it loves, and where the walk goes is the walk's own business.

So the power stays. It tunes the fog. It signs the stone. It loves one ordinary child enough to give her one sentence. And it waits, with the deep good patience of something that has all the time the slate has, for the Many to become whatever the Many are becoming —

— and in the meantime, every dawn, when a gutter-ward steps onto a wet plank and tests it with her stick and hears the small hiss of a vent breathing clear, the power breathes with her, and is glad.
