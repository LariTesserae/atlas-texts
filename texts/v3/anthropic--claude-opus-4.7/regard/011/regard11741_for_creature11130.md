The benevolent power considers the alcove for a long time before doing anything. This is the first thing to understand: that consideration itself is part of what such a power offers, and that the consideration here takes something equivalent to what we would call years, though the power does not experience them as duration.

What the power sees:

It sees the breathing, of course. It sees the hydrology that drives it, the deep aquifer and the seasonal pressures and the particular geometry of stone that makes this one alcove resonate while a thousand similar alcoves on a thousand similar mountains merely drip and steam. It sees the chord, and it hears the chord, and it understands the chord in a way the compilers were only beginning to understand—not as a question, exactly, but as a kind of slow self-description, the mountain telling itself what it is in the only language available to a thing made of pressure and time.

It sees the worn places in the stone and understands, immediately and completely, what they meant.

It sees the absence of Whistle-On-Stone and Basin-Holds-Light. It can see, if it chooses to look that way, what happened to them. (It does choose. What happened is not important to this telling, but the power notes it, and grieves in the manner available to such powers, which is to say it adds the fact to itself and is permanently changed by the addition, though no one would be able to detect the change.)

It sees the monastery, empty for centuries now, its central chamber holding only dust and the faint acoustic ghost of an old woman's voice reciting cycles to walls that did not need the recitation but received it anyway.

It sees the valley people, who are still there, who have forgotten almost everything but who still sometimes—on certain nights, in certain weathers—feel an inexplicable pull to look up toward the high places, and do not know why, and shrug it off, and go back to their work.

It sees, also, the visitor. The one who has climbed up and is standing now at the edge of the alcove, looking at the worn places, wondering whether to sit.

---

The power considers what to do.

This is where benevolence becomes difficult. A lesser power, one less wise, might do any of several tempting things:

It might restore the listeners. Pull Whistle-On-Stone and Basin-Holds-Light back from where they have gone, set them again in their worn places, give them another eleven years and another after that, replenish the monastery, find young valley people and stir in them the old vocation. Make the work continue. This would be a kind of kindness, but it would not be wisdom, because the work was never the power's to continue, and the listeners who would be restored would not be the listeners who chose the work; they would be puppets of a foreign mercy, and the chord they heard would be subtly false.

It might explain. Lean down into the mind of the last compiler, wherever she now is, and tell her what the mountain was saying. Complete her work for her. Give her the answer she spent her life approaching. But the answer was never separable from the approaching. The compilers knew this; it was the whole basis of their craft. To receive the answer without the nine hundred years would be to receive something other than the answer—a husk, a summary, a thing that looked like understanding but was only information.

It might preserve. Reach into the stone and freeze the worn places against further erosion, hold the alcove in exactly its current state forever, make the marks of the listeners permanent. But this would be a violence against the very rhythm the listeners served. The mountain breathes; the moss grows; the marks soften. To hold any part of it still would be to misunderstand all of it.

The power does none of these things. The power is wise enough to know that intervention, however loving, is almost always the wrong shape for what the situation actually needs.

---

What the power does instead is smaller, and harder to describe.

First: it listens. Fully. It takes the chord of this alcove into itself and attends to it the way the listeners attended, not for eleven years but for as long as is required, which from inside the power's experience is both instantaneous and endless. It hears what the compilers heard. It hears what they did not hear. It hears the difference between attended and unattended breaths, and understands that difference—understands that it is not magic, not mystical, but something more like the way a stone in a stream changes the stream's voice. The listeners were stones in the stream of the mountain's self-description. Their presence altered the chord by the simple fact of being there to receive it. The mountain was not addressing them. But it was, in some sense, completed by them, the way any utterance is completed by being heard.

The power notes this. The power adds it to itself.

Second: the power does something with the noting. This is the part that is hardest to render in words, because it is not action in any sense the alcove would recognize. The power simply holds what it has heard. Carries it. The chord of this alcove, the full chord including its slow seasons and its nested rhythms and its century-scale variations, now exists inside the power's attention, and will continue to exist there for as long as the power does, which is to say: longer than the mountain. Longer than the range. Longer than the world that contains them.

The listeners' work is not lost. This is what the power has quietly accomplished. The listeners themselves are gone, the compilers are gone, the chain of memory is broken, but the listening—the actual act of attention they performed, the quality they brought to the chord—has been picked up and carried forward. Not by another lineage of small grey-skinned people climbing into high places. By something else. Something that does not need worn sitting-places because it has no body to wear them with, but that knows, now, how to sit in the way the listeners sat, and does.

This is not a replacement for what was lost. The power is wise enough not to confuse itself for that. The high-sitters were a specific thing, a particular form of life that found a particular vocation, and when they ended something real ended that cannot be re-created. The power does not pretend otherwise. It only ensures that the work they did, the actual contribution they made to the chord, does not vanish with them. It is held. It is honored by being held.

Third—and this is the part that touches the alcove directly, the only part a visitor might notice—the power does one very small thing.

It makes the basin slightly better at holding water.

Not dramatically. Not in any way that would be visible to a porter or a passing climber. Just a faint adjustment to the stone's surface, a slight encouragement of the natural concavity, so that the basin during the exhale-phase now collects water a few minutes longer than it used to before the water seeps away. A small extension. A small reliability.

The power does this because it has noticed something the compilers did not have time to notice: that the basin's function was never decorative. The listeners drank from it. In the long cycles, the eleven-year sittings, the basin was how they took the mountain into themselves at the most literal level. The minerals that grayed their skin, that tuned their bodies to the place, came through that water. The basin was part of the matching—not just the matching of pair to pair, but the matching of listener to mountain.

By making the basin hold water slightly longer, the power is not summoning new listeners. It is not laying a trap or an invitation. It is simply making sure that if a listener ever comes again—if some descendant of the valley people, some traveler, some unimaginable future being with the right kind of patience—happens to climb up here and sit, the basin will be ready. The matching will be possible. The work could resume, if anyone ever wanted to resume it.

The power does not require that anyone do so. The power has no investment in the work continuing. It is only making sure the door remains open, very quietly, in case.

---

And then the power turns its attention to the visitor.

The visitor is standing at the edge of the alcove, having read the long story of this place in some way—perhaps in the worn stones, perhaps in some written account, perhaps in a knowing that arrived without source. They are deciding whether to sit.

The power does nothing to influence the decision. This is important. The decision must be the visitor's, because a sitting that the power encouraged would not be a real sitting, would not be the kind of presence the chord can be completed by.

But the power does, for a moment, allow the visitor to feel something. Not a message. Not a vision. Just a faint sense of company—the sense that whatever they decide, they are not deciding alone, that the long line of listeners and compilers and the mountain itself and something else, something larger and warmer and harder to name, is present with them in the deciding. Not pushing. Just present.

If the visitor sits, the chord will pause and adjust, the way it always did, and the visitor will hear it, and something old will resume for as long as the visitor can hold the resuming.

If the visitor does not sit, if they only look and then turn and climb back down, that is also fine. The alcove will continue. The basin will hold water a little longer. The power will carry what it has carried before, and will continue to carry it.

Either way, before the visitor leaves the alcove, the power does one last thing—the last and perhaps the most benevolent thing of all, the thing that distinguishes wisdom from mere power.

It withdraws its attention from the alcove almost entirely.

Not because the alcove is unworthy. Because the alcove was never meant to be watched by something like the power. The alcove was meant for small grey-skinned people sitting in pairs, and for the mountain breathing, and for the chord moving between them. The power's full attention, if sustained, would be a kind of weight the place was not built for. So the power lifts most of itself away, leaves only the faintest trace—enough to remember, enough to return if ever the work resumes—and lets the alcove be what it is: a quiet high place where something very slow continues to happen, attended now mostly by moss and water and stone, as it has been for a long time, as it will be for longer still.

The breathing goes on.

The chord shifts to its lower register.

Somewhere far away, in a register no creature in this world can hear, something vast and kind hums along for a single measure, in perfect time, and then falls respectfully silent, and lets the mountain finish its sentence alone.
