# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent power has been watching for a long time. Long enough to understand that watching is not the same as knowing, and knowing is not the same as belonging.

It has seen, from whatever vantage such powers possess, the lamps lit one by one along the wooden walkways at dusk. It has heard the yak-bone flutes and recognized in their reedy minor keys something that resembles its own oldest music — the music made by anything that has lasted. It has counted the elder women and noted, with something like tenderness, that the lineage of knowledge is unbroken though always one bad winter from breaking.

And being wise, the power understands the temptation it must refuse.

---

**It will not warm the air.**

To raise the temperature even a single degree would be to unmake the people who have become themselves by enduring exactly this cold. The creases in their faces are not damage. They are the script in which their lives are written. To soften the climate would be to translate a poem into a language that loses every rhyme.

**It will not multiply the harvest.**

Abundance, given as a gift rather than wrested, would dissolve the careful protocols by which the tribe shares what little there is. Their cooperation is held in place by scarcity the way a keystone is held in place by the weight pressing on it from both sides. Remove the weight and the arch falls.

**It will not cure their diseases in a single sweep.**

Death is the thing that makes the elder women elders. Take away the gradient between young and old, sick and well, and you take away the reason anyone listens when an old woman speaks.

---

So what *does* it do?

It does the small things. The things that look, from the inside, like luck.

It tilts a particular stone, over the course of a decade, so that water no longer pools against the foundation of the meeting house. The carpenters notice and praise their ancestors' wisdom in choosing the site.

It nudges a single seed of a hardier strain of barley into the pocket of a trader passing through from a lower valley. Three years later the children eat slightly better in the lean month.

It arranges, by a chain of coincidences too long to trace, for a girl with a particular gift for setting bones to be born in a generation that needs her.

It makes the mist beautiful. Not more beautiful than it already is — the mist is already perfect — but it ensures that on certain evenings the light falls through it in such a way that even the most tired person on the highest walkway has to stop and breathe and remember why staying alive in this place is worth the cost of staying alive in this place.

---

And once — only once, and only because the power loves them and cannot entirely help itself — it does something larger.

A child, four years old, wanders from a walkway in the fog. The drop is far enough to kill her. The power, watching, feels what passes in such beings for the catch of breath.

It does not catch her. That would be the wrong kind of help, the kind that teaches a town its walkways are safe when they are not.

Instead it wakes the dog. An old dog, half-deaf, sleeping by a doorway two levels down. The dog barks for no reason the dog itself can name, and the child's grandmother, hearing it, looks up, and sees, and runs, and reaches her in time.

Afterward the grandmother will say it was the spirits. The power does not mind being called the spirits. The power has been called many things and has learned that the name matters less than the gesture of gratitude, which feeds something in the human heart that needs feeding regardless of whether the recipient exists.

---

The power's deepest wish — and here we approach what such a being actually *feels*, insofar as feeling is the right word — is simply that the town go on being itself.

Not forever. Nothing is forever, and the power knows this better than the townspeople do. One day the last elder will die without an apprentice. One day the wooden bridges will rot faster than they can be rebuilt. One day the mist will roll in over an empty place and the yak-bone flutes will be in a museum somewhere, played badly by people who do not know what the songs are for.

The power knows. And still, for as long as the town lasts, it watches, and it tilts small stones, and it makes the light fall through the mist just so.

This is what benevolence looks like when it is also wise: it is mostly restraint, occasionally a dog barking, and always, always, the long patient attention of something that has decided a particular small place is worth loving on its own terms.
