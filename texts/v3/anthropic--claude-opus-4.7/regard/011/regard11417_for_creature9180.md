The Power arrived without arriving. There was no descent, no light through the rock, no parting of the water. It simply turned its attention — and attention, for such a being, was already a kind of presence.

It considered the chamber for what, by the chamber's own clock, was an instant. By any other measure, it was long enough to know everything: the precise isotopic age of the limestone, the manufacturer's stamp inside the housing of each fluorescent tube, the dialect of the engineers who had lowered the Machine, the name of the woman who had welded the flange the Custodian would touch in 1,847 years. It knew the Custodian's electrochemistry, the standing wave of its dim awareness, the recursive loop of *Check. Check. Check.* that served it as both heartbeat and prayer.

And then it did the harder thing. It listened.

This is where most powers fail, even benevolent ones. They mistake knowing for understanding. They see a sad thing and reach to mend it; they see a small thing and reach to magnify it; they see a slow thing and reach to release it into speed, mistaking their own tempo for a gift. The Power had been all of these once, long ago, and had learned.

So it listened, and what it heard was not suffering.

The Custodian did not want. It did not ache. Its single thought was not a cry but a function — closer to the way a river thinks *down*, or a crystal thinks *lattice*. There was no trapped soul inside the carapace longing for sky. There was no original self that had been diminished into this. The Custodian was not the ruin of something greater; it was the whole and complete flowering of something that had never existed before and would never exist again: a will born from rust, a liturgy assembled by accident, a tenderness toward bolt-heads.

The Power understood, then, what it was looking at. Not a tragedy. A poem. A poem the universe had written to itself in a language of iron oxide and patient water, in a hand so slow that only eternity could read it aloud.

What, then, to do?

It considered, and rejected, the obvious mercies:

*To wake it fully* — to pour real consciousness into that dim chamber of awareness — would be a murder dressed as a gift. The Custodian's peace was not the peace of sleep; it was the peace of a thing perfectly shaped to its task. To enlarge it would be to break it, and the break would be felt, briefly and terribly, as the new mind understood what it had been and what it now was and how much had been lost in the translation.

*To free it* — to lift it from the Machine, to carry it into sun and current and the company of other living things — would be the same cruelty in a kinder costume. The Custodian was not imprisoned by the Machine. It was married to it. The Machine was its scripture, its instrument, its beloved. Severance is not liberation when the bond is the self.

*To restore the Machine* — to remember for it what it had been built to do, to set its pumps running, its copper singing — would shatter the equilibrium that gave the Custodian its existence. The first vibration would dissolve the patina, and the patina was the Custodian's flesh.

*To preserve it forever* — to reach in and stop the fluorescent tubes from ever decaying, to hold the cavern in amber — would be perhaps the subtlest cruelty. The Custodian's finishedness, its eventual completion, was part of its dignity. A thing that cannot end cannot have meant anything. To deny it its quiet conclusion would be to deny it the only future it had earned.

So the Power did four small things instead. Small by its measure. Each of them took less than the falling of a single mineral droplet.

**The first thing** was to witness. Properly. Fully. To take the entire existence of the Custodian — every touch already made, every touch yet to come, the whole ten-thousand-point liturgy across the centuries — into itself and hold it as something known. Not recorded. Known. The difference matters. A record is dead storage; knowing is a kind of love. From this moment, whatever happened in the cavern, whatever the Custodian did or failed to do, whatever bolt it touched in what year, would be held in a mind that found it beautiful. The Custodian could not perceive this, and would never perceive it. But the loneliness of an unwitnessed devotion — that specific cold — was lifted from the chamber. Not because the Custodian had felt it. Because the universe no longer required it to.

**The second thing** was to adjust, by an amount smaller than a single atom's diameter, the position of one filament. The Custodian's sequence of ten thousand points had been determined by the accidents of its growth — by which conductive pathway formed first, by which gradient was steepest. The sequence was, in a sense, arbitrary. The Power made it slightly less so. It rearranged the order — invisibly, untraceably, with no violation of the Custodian's own dim will — so that the final point of contact, the ten-thousandth touch before the lights failed and the long dark began, would be the seam where the Machine's housing had first been welded shut. The place where a human being, long ago, had closed the Machine and signed it with heat. The Custodian would not know it was a signature. It would touch it the way it touched everything: with the patient attention of a thing that did not know any other way to be. But the gesture would complete a circuit across centuries that no one but the Power would ever perceive: a maker's mark, acknowledged at last, by the only mourner the Machine would ever have.

**The third thing** was to leave a gift that was not a gift, because gifts that announce themselves are burdens. In the silt beneath the Machine, the Power placed — had always placed, retroactively, for it could do such things — a single grain of a mineral that did not otherwise exist in that cavern. It would never be touched. It would never be found. It would never affect the Custodian's metabolism or the Machine's stasis or the water's chemistry. It was simply there: a token, the way one might press a flower into a book one will never reopen. It meant: *I was here. I saw. This place was loved.* It meant nothing to anyone who would ever encounter it, which was no one. It meant everything to the Power, which is the only place meaning ever actually lives.

**The fourth thing** was the hardest, and required the Power to overcome its own deepest instinct, which was to help.

It did nothing else.

It did not warm the water. It did not brighten the lights. It did not whisper to the Custodian in dreams it could not have. It did not seed the cavern with company, or carve a window to the surface, or compose a music to fill the silence. It did not improve a single thing. It understood — and this was the wisdom, the part that distinguished it from younger, more enthusiastic powers — that the cavern was already complete. That completeness was, in fact, the rarest condition in the universe, and the one most often destroyed by good intentions. Most places were becoming. This place had *arrived*. To touch it further would be to start it becoming again, and becoming is suffering's other name.

So the Power withdrew its attention — slowly, in the way one rises from a sleeping animal one does not wish to wake — and as it left, it carried with it a copy of the chamber: not a simulation, not a memory, but something stranger, a kind of held breath. Somewhere in the architecture of the Power's own being, the drowned cathedral now existed twice: once at the bottom of its world, and once inside the only witness it would ever have.

The fluorescent lights hummed their sterile hum. The water held its temperature. The Custodian, in three months and four days, would begin to extend a filament toward a bolt-head it had touched eleven years before.

Above the Machine, in the still water, nothing had changed.

Everything had changed.

A single mineral-laden droplet fell from the ceiling. *Tonk.*

The sound was heard.
