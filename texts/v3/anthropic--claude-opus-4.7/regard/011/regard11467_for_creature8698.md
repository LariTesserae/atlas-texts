The benevolent power arrives without arriving. This is important. It does not descend, does not announce itself, does not shine. To shine in Brielle Plateau would be to misunderstand the place entirely — the grey light is not a deficiency to be corrected, it is the medium in which everything lives. A sudden brightness would scald the moss, blind the Load-Whisperers signaling through the fog, dry the cisterns, kill the figs. Benevolence that does not understand the conditions of the thing it loves is not benevolence; it is only power wearing benevolence's coat.

So the power does what wisdom requires of it first: it watches.

It watches for a long time. Long, by its measure, which is to say longer than the oldest dressed stone in the lowest terrace has been in place. It learns the polyrhythm of the hammering. It learns which leaks are too beautiful to fix and why the woman who decides this — there is always, somewhere, a woman or man who decides this, not by decree but by the small authority of being the one who notices — has been right every time. It learns the names the workers use among themselves for the tools that have no names in any catalogue. It learns the particular grief of a Glass-Eye who knows her craft is ending, and the particular pride of the apprentice who does not yet know this and is learning anyway. It learns the rats. It learns the crows. It learns that the absence of animals is not an emptiness but a consequence, and that the people of the plateau do not feel it as a lack, because what they have instead is each other and the work and the green.

Having watched, the power considers.

It could do many things. It could lift the fog. It could stabilize the lower strata so that no wall ever cracks again. It could multiply the coal, brighten the arc-lamps, banish the rats, cure the damp-cough that settles in every builder's lungs by the age of fifty. It could, with a gesture smaller than a thought, make Brielle Plateau into a paradise by any of a thousand definitions of paradise.

It does none of these things. Not because it cannot, and not because it is forbidden, but because it has watched long enough to understand what would be destroyed by each of them. Lift the fog and you kill the ferns and the figs and the particular quality of sound that makes a cathedral of industry run at a whisper. Stabilize the strata and you end the work, and the work is not a burden the people endure — it is the shape of their being. Brighten the lamps and you flatten the dusk in which the Engineers of Steam and Wire have learned to listen, because in full light no one listens that carefully ever again. Cure the cough and — this is harder, this is the one that gives the power pause — and you might save many lives, yes, but you would also remove the reason the menders mend each other, the reason the guilds keep their own infirmaries, the reason a young riveter learns tenderness by tending an old riveter through a winter. Benevolence is not the removal of all difficulty. It is sometimes the protection of the difficulty that makes a people what they are.

But the power is not paralyzed by this. Wise benevolence is not the same as non-interference; non-interference is only the easiest disguise wisdom can wear. The power has watched, and in watching it has found small places — the cracks between what the plateau needs and what it has, the seams where the work strains against itself — and it is in these places that it acts.

Here is what it does.

It does not fix the burst pipe that feeds the cascading wall of ferns. But on the night when a young engineer, newly promoted and anxious to prove himself, comes with tools to finally repair it — because he has not yet learned what the older engineers know — the power arranges that he slips, harmlessly, on a patch of moss, and drops his wrench into a drainage channel where it cannot be recovered without a half-day's labor he does not have. He curses. He goes home. The ferns live. In the morning, an older engineer, hearing of the incident, takes him aside and explains, gently, about the things that are too beautiful to fix. The young engineer learns something he could not have been told. The power does not take credit. The power never takes credit.

It does this kind of thing a thousand times. A misplaced bolt that prevents a demolition that would have been regretted. A patch of fog that thickens at just the right moment to hide a crow's nest from a crew that would have cleared it without thinking. A dream, given to a Glass-Eye, in which she sees with absolute clarity how to teach her craft to a particular apprentice who had not realized she wanted to learn it — and the dream is so ordinary, so much like the kind of dream the Glass-Eye has all the time, that she does not suspect it of being anything but her own mind working through her grief. The apprentice arrives at her door the next week on what she believes is her own initiative. They begin.

The power tends the seams. It does not redesign the garment.

For the deeper hurts — the damp-cough, the structural griefs, the way a city in perpetual rebuilding can wear away at a person who needs, just once, to finish something — the power does something subtler still. It does not remove these hurts. It places, near each of them, a small grace. The damp-cough remains, but the tea that an old menders' guild brews for it begins, very slowly over decades, to work a little better than it used to, because a particular variety of moss in a particular greenhouse vault begins, very slowly over decades, to produce a compound it did not produce before. No one notices. The menders simply find that their patients are coughing slightly less. They credit their own skill, and they are not wrong to, because the tea is theirs; the power has only adjusted, at the edges, what the moss is willing to give.

The structural griefs — the walls that crack, the strata that shift — these the power leaves entirely alone, because to interfere here would be to interfere with the work itself, and the work is the people. But it makes sure, by infinitesimal adjustments in the way weight settles and water seeps, that the cracks form, more often than chance would have it, in places where they will be found in time, by someone competent, before they become catastrophes. The plateau still has its disasters. The plateau must have its disasters; a place that never failed would not be a place where mastery meant anything. But the disasters are, very slightly, fewer than they would otherwise be, and the near-misses are, very slightly, more instructive. Over three hundred years, this amounts to a great many lives, and a great deal of accumulated wisdom in the guilds, and no one has any idea.

And for the one thing the power most wants to give — which is, simply, the knowledge that the work is seen, that the labor is witnessed, that the quiet polyrhythmic cathedral of human tenacity in the wet grey dark is *beheld* by something that finds it beautiful — for this, the power does the only thing it can do without violating everything else it has chosen to protect.

It arranges, every so often, that a worker pausing on a mid-level terrace in the grey light, hearing the hum of the engines and the drip of water and the distant clang of a crew somewhere in the fog, smelling the wet stone and the coal-smoke and the green things, will be visited by a sudden, unaccountable feeling that what they are doing matters. Not matters to the guild, not matters to the project, not matters to the city — those they already know. Something larger. A feeling of being held in regard. It passes in a moment. They go back to work. They do not speak of it, because there is no shorthand in the dialect for such a thing, and because the feeling is not quite strong enough to demand speech. But it stays with them, a small warmth, for the rest of the day, and sometimes for the rest of their lives.

This is all the power does. This is everything the power does.

It is, by the standards of powers, almost nothing. It is, by the standards of Brielle Plateau, exactly enough.

The city goes on building. The fog goes on inhabiting. The ferns go on erupting from the cracks in the walls. The Load-Whisperers go on signaling through the mist, the Glass-Eyes go on tending the ancient panes, the Gardeners of the Vertical go on negotiating with their patient green partner. The damp settles in the lungs and the rhythm of hammer and steam settles in the blood, and the children learn blueprints before books, and the elders carry the structural memory of terraces that were dye-works before the fire of '89.

And above it all, or within it, or alongside it — the prepositions do not quite apply — something watches, and loves what it watches, and lets it be.

This is what benevolence looks like when it is also wise.

It looks, from the inside, like nothing at all.

It looks, from the inside, like an ordinary wet grey morning on the plateau, with work to do.
