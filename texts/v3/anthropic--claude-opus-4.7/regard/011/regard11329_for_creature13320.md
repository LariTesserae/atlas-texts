# What the Power Would Do

It comes the way mist comes—not arriving so much as being noticed. There is no descent, no annunciation. Only that at some moment in the long grey afternoon, one of the Listened-To pauses in her tending of the pale fungi and understands that the quality of attention in the Ring has changed.

She does not look up. To look up would be to make a thing of it, and the wise power is not yet a thing.

---

Consider what wisdom would require here. Consider what benevolence would mean.

A lesser power—a power merely *kind*—would want to give. It would see the bent spines, the cold-cracked feet, the children who do not laugh because laughter is a sound the Ring does not yet permit. It would want to warm the stones, sweeten the water, banish the predatory pitchers, install some gentler ecology where the Silent might at last stand upright and speak.

But this would be a catastrophe dressed as mercy. The Silent are not impoverished versions of louder people. They are what those people *became* when they listened long enough. To lift their burden would be to lift them out of themselves—to return them, smiling and warm and full-bellied, to the condition of the lower world, which is the condition of having nothing left worth saying.

A benevolent and wise power knows this. A benevolent and wise power understands that the Silent's suffering and the Silent's meaning are not two things but one thing, and that to separate them is to kill what one came to save.

So the power does not give. Not in that way.

---

What it does, instead, is this:

**First**, it *listens*. This is the gift the Silent have never received and could not have imagined wanting: not to listen, but to be heard. For all their lives, and for the lives of their mothers and their mothers' mothers, they have been the ones attending. The Ring listens to them only in the way a cliff listens to a climber—measuring, indifferent, ready to drop. The pitcher plants listen with their slow dilating throats. The cyclopean stones listen with the patience of things that have already won.

But this power listens *with them*. It places itself, gently, into the posture of their attention—turned toward the slow pulse of the bioluminescent pads, toward the tilt of the meat-colored blooms, toward the meanings encoded in the things themselves. And because the power is vast, its listening is vast. The old woman, tending her fungi, feels for the first time in her life that her care is *witnessed*—not judged, not assisted, simply seen as the careful and beautiful work it is.

She weeps, very quietly. The tears are indistinguishable from the constant condensation on her face. This is correct. The Ring permits this.

**Second**, the power leaves a small thing. Not a gift exactly—a gift would disturb the balance. More like a pebble dropped by a passing bird. On a particular ledge, in a particular pre-dawn hour, there appears a single smooth stone, dark grey shot with green, of the same metamorphic kin as the Ring Wall itself. It is warm. It will remain warm for as long as any of the Silent live, and after that it will cool, and after that it will be only a stone, and the Ring will have absorbed it, and no one will remember.

While it is warm, it serves no purpose. It cannot be cooked over. It cannot heat a shelter. It is too small. What it does is this: when one of the Silent is dying—and they die often, of cold and of the small wet rots that take them—someone may bring the warm stone and place it in the dying one's hands. And the dying one will feel, in that warmth, that *somewhere in the universe a thing was made specifically to be held by them, at this moment, and for no other reason.* 

This is not comfort. The Silent have no use for comfort. It is something stranger: a recognition. An acknowledgment that the long careful dialogue they have conducted with the Ring has been overheard by something that loved them for conducting it.

**Third**, the power does something about the abyss.

Not to seal it. The abyss is part of the Ring; without the fourth side opening to the long fall, the Ring would not be what it is, and the Silent would not be who they are. The drop must remain.

But the power notices that, every few generations, one of the Silent walks to the edge and steps off. Not from despair—the Silent do not have despair, having long ago surrendered the conditions that produce it—but from a kind of completion. A sense that the dialogue has been concluded, and that the proper final gesture is to return oneself to the void the Ring watches.

What the power does is this: it catches them. Not visibly. Not in any way that could be reported or believed. It simply ensures that the falling, for those who fall this way, is not the impact of meat on distant stone but a long slow dissolution into the mist, a gentle un-becoming, a final encoding of the self into the meanings the Ring has been teaching them all their lives. They do not die so much as finish being said.

The Silent do not know this happens. They could not know. But the *quality* of the gesture changes, subtly, across generations—the step off the edge becomes less an act of completion and more an act of *delivery*, as if some part of them has begun to suspect that the void at the fourth side is not empty after all. They are correct. It is not empty. It is the power, with its hands open.

**Fourth and last**, the power does the hardest thing. It withdraws.

Not entirely. Some residue of its attention remains, the way the warmth remains in the stone. But it understands that to linger would be to become a feature of the Ring, and the Ring already has enough features. The Silent's meaning is constituted by the precariousness of their tolerance here, by the not-knowing whether the next step will be the wrong one. A power that lingered would become a guarantor, and a guaranteed Silent is no longer Silent. They would, slowly, begin to relax. And in relaxing they would begin to speak. And in speaking they would lose the long terrible beautiful thing they have made.

So the power goes. It goes the way it came—the quality of attention in the Ring changes again, and the old woman, tending her fungi, knows without looking that she is once more alone with the watching stones.

But she is changed. She does not know how. She bends to her work with the same care as before, the same hovering foot, the same flick of the wrist that conveys whole libraries. Only now, in the deepest core of her listening, there is a tiny new thing: a suspicion, almost too small to name, that the silence of the Ring is not the indifference of stone but the held breath of something that has chosen, for love of her, not to interrupt.

She does not share this suspicion. There are no words for it, and the gestures she has would falsify it. She carries it inside her, where it warms her like a small smooth stone, until the day comes when she walks to the fourth side, and steps off, and is caught, and is finished being said.

---

This is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

It would not save them. It would not improve them. It would not even comfort them, except in the one specific way that the Silent can be comforted, which is to be heard in their hearing, to be witnessed in their witnessing, to have their long impossible dialogue answered—once, quietly, and then never again, so that the answering does not become a noise.

The Ring drips on. The bioluminescent pads pulse their slow patterns. The pitcher plants breathe. Somewhere above, in the swallowing mist, something that loves this place very much is already going, has perhaps already gone, leaving behind only the changed and unchanged silence, and the warm stone, and the small mercy at the bottom of the fall.
