# The Visitation

The benevolent power does not arrive. That would be the wrong verb. It is already there, has always been there, the way warmth is in a stone that has held the sun—but on this particular evening, it *attends*. It turns some portion of its vast attention toward the Moss-Covered Engine Quarter and looks, really looks, the way a gardener might kneel to consider a single patch of clover.

What does it see?

It sees a peat-walker named Olen, though the power does not need the name, sitting cross-legged before the great Number Four boiler, which has been wheezing wrong for three days. Olen has unwrapped his outer coat. His webbed palms are pressed flat to the warm iron. He is humming—not a tune, just a low sustained note, matching the engine's labored breath. He has been doing this for two hours. The boiler is, very slowly, finding its rhythm again.

It sees a child, no older than six, crouched at the edge of a canal, watching a snail navigate the curve of a brick. The child has been there long enough that the moss is beginning to consider her. She is not bored. She is learning the snail.

It sees an old woman pouring grease into the elbow joint of a pipe that hasn't moved in forty years and will not move again, and she knows this, and pours the grease anyway, because the pipe is a member of the household.

It sees the ghosts—and yes, there are ghosts, the peat-walkers are correct—folded into the brick and the iron like pressed flowers, neither suffering nor restless, simply *in attendance*, the way the power itself is in attendance.

It sees the whole Quarter breathing: in, out. In, out. A vast lung of green and rust and slow water.

---

And the power considers.

It could do many things. It could lift the rot. It could clean the canals to glass, set the engines roaring at their old industrial pitch, straighten the curved spines of the peat-walkers, give them back the sharpness of sound and the swiftness of feet. It could make the Quarter *prosper* in the way the wider world understands prosperity.

It does not do this. It would be a violence. The power is wise enough to know that what looks like decline from a great height is, from inside, a kind of completion. The Quarter is not a problem to be solved. It is a poem in its final stanza, and the final stanza is the longest, and the longest is the point.

It could also do the opposite—accelerate the settling, sink the Quarter mercifully into the marsh, return it cleanly to the water. This too would be a violence. The peat-walkers are not finished. The engines still have breath. The child has not yet finished learning the snail.

So what does benevolence do, when the thing it loves is already, in its own slow way, exactly what it should be?

---

It does small things. It does almost nothing. It does the things a wise power does when it understands that *to be witnessed with love is itself a gift, and often the only gift required.*

It thickens the attention in the air, very slightly, so that Olen, humming to the boiler, feels for a moment that he is being hummed *with*—that the note he holds is held also from somewhere else, and the boiler finds its rhythm a little sooner than it would have, and he does not know why, and does not need to.

It tilts the moonlight by half a degree so that the algae on the canal catches it just so, and the child looking up from her snail sees the water briefly as a field of soft green stars, and remembers this her whole life, and tells her own children, who will tell theirs, and the memory becomes a small permanence inside the long settling.

It does not extend the old woman's life, but it sweetens the grease in the can, just a little, just enough that the smell pleases her, and she smiles at the pipe, and the pipe—who is, in its way, a ghost—receives the smile.

It speaks, very quietly, to the ghosts. Not in words. In the recognition that they are there. The ghosts straighten slightly inside the brick. They had not known they were lonely. They are less lonely now.

It blesses the sour moss-tea brewing in three different kettles, so that those who drink it tonight sleep without aching joints.

It nudges a heron, asleep on one leg in the shallows, into a dream of fish.

And to the visitor—the one waking now to the sound of water, going to the window, seeing the figure across the canal—the power gives one specific thing. It gives the visitor the capacity to *understand* what they are seeing. Not to romanticize it, not to pity it, not to want to fix it or flee it. Just to receive it accurately. To know, in the marrow, that this is a place that has found its balance, and that balance is one of the rarest achievements in any world, and that the only correct response is reverence.

The figure across the canal raises a hand. The visitor raises one back. Something passes between them that neither will be able to name, but both will carry.

---

Before the power withdraws its particular attention—though it never really withdraws, only diffuses again into the general love that holds all things—it does one last thing.

It leaves a kind of mark. Not visible. Not even quite a presence. More like a *permission*. The Quarter is permitted to continue exactly as it is, for exactly as long as it wishes, and when at last it sinks fully into the marsh, generations or centuries from now, it will be received gently, the way one receives a sleeping child into bed. The transition will not be painful. The ghosts will travel with the bricks. The last peat-walker will hum the last boiler down into silence, and the silence will be full, not empty.

This permission settles into the moss, into the water, into the iron. It does not change anything. It only confirms what was already true, and confirmation, sometimes, is the whole of grace.

---

And then the power turns its attention elsewhere—to a desert that is dreaming, to a single bee in a thunderstorm, to a woman in a city far away who is about to make a decision—but a portion of it stays, as a portion of it always stays everywhere, woven into the slow exhale of the engines, indistinguishable now from the place itself.

The Quarter breathes.

In. Out.

The moss grows by an imperceptible degree.

A drop of water falls from an eave and meets the canal with a sound like a small *yes*.

There is no hurry.

There never was.
