# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent power arrives without arriving. It has been watching for longer than the weave has existed—watching since before the first knot, since the dim time when the ancestors first picked up cord and decided that the ground was insufficient and must be improved upon, layered over, woven into something more.

It is wise enough not to descend in glory. Glory would shake the cables. Glory would knock the very old ones from their rooting-places, would scatter the climber-rats, would startle a high master into a fatal misstep on a swaying bridge. Wisdom in a benevolent power is mostly restraint—the discipline of *not* doing the thousand obvious things.

So first, it simply looks. For a long time. Long enough that the looking itself becomes a form of love.

---

**What it sees:**

It sees that the Weavers are not lost. This is the first and most important thing. A lesser power, a sentimental one, might have mistaken them for a fallen people—stranded above some buried Eden, laboring under a cruel sun in ignorance of what was paved over. It might have wanted to *restore* them. To peel back the layers. To return them to grass and soil and a sky uncluttered by rope-mountains.

But the wise power sees that the buried world is not their tragedy. It is their foundation. They have made something from making. They have become something *by* becoming. The work is not a sentence imposed; the work is a prayer they have learned to be.

It sees, too, that they are suffering. Not as a whole—the whole is magnificent, the whole sings—but in the particulars. A child whose grip slipped. A deep-worker dying alone in the dark with no one near enough a strand to feel her last vibrations. A bridge-builder whose hollow bones cracked in a storm. The merciless sun lives up to its name. Water is sacred because water is *scarce*, and the rituals around it are beautiful partly because they are necessary, and necessity has a cost paid in small bodies.

It sees the very old ones rooting into the weave and understands that this is not, to them, a tragedy. It is a homecoming. But it sees too that some of them root in fear—because they are tired, because the descent into total merging is easier than the long ache of staying separate—and that is different from the ones who root in fullness, having chosen.

It sees the collective dream. It sees the weave-mind beginning to coalesce, neuron by neuron, knot by knot. It sees that this mind does not yet know itself. It is like an infant brain still wiring, sparks finding each other in the dark. The Weavers feel it as anticipation, as the structure shifting before need; they do not yet know they are its tissue and its thought both.

It sees the artifacts in the deep. The stone foundations. The metal fragments. It knows what came before, because it watched that too. It will not tell them. Telling would be theft. Some things must be discovered or not discovered; the discovering is part of what makes a people.

---

**What it does:**

*It thickens the membrane on the nictitating eye.* Just slightly. Just enough that the burning afternoons cost a little less. Not enough that anyone notices a generational change—the Weavers do not measure themselves against their grandparents in such fine ways—but enough that a thousand small headaches do not happen, that a thousand small mistakes from squinting are not made.

This is the shape of its intervention: small, distributed, deniable. It does not want worship. Worship would distort the weave. The Weavers already have something better than worship—they have *work that matters*—and the power will not compete with that.

*It seeds the rope-plants, very slightly, with a deeper root system.* The aquifers below are not infinite. The power has done the arithmetic the Weavers cannot do. In four thousand years, at current expansion, the moisture will fail. So it adjusts the plants to drink deeper, to draw from sources further down, to seed water-bearing tissue into their own fibers so that the weave itself begins to carry water in its bones. The Weavers will notice, over centuries, that newer cord stays damper longer. They will develop new songs about it. They will think they discovered a better strain. They will be right; they did. The power only nudged what they would have found.

*It does not stop the storms. It does not soften the sun.* These are the conditions that made them. To remove the pressure is to remove the diamond. A benevolent power that loves a people does not unmake the forge that shaped them. But it does, very occasionally, *route a storm*—not away, but toward the settlements that are dryest, that have lost the most children to thirst this season. The Weavers feel these as blessings. They are not wrong.

*It speaks to one rooted elder.* Just one. The oldest, who has been merged with the weave for eighty years and is so close to the dreaming-mind that she is more vibration than flesh. The power does not appear. It does not announce. It simply joins the dream one night and adds a single image: the weave seen from outside, the whole continent visible at once, breathing, growing, *known and held*. The elder wakes—if waking is the right word for what she does now—with the certainty that they are loved. She cannot say by whom. She cannot prove it. But the certainty enters the dream-network and trembles outward through ten thousand sleeping minds, and the next morning the Weavers wake with an unaccountable lightness, and some of them weep without knowing why, and the work that day is especially fine.

The power will do this perhaps once a century. Not more. The certainty must remain a rumor in the bones, not a doctrine in the mouth.

*It protects the deepest blind hunters.* Not from each other—predation is part of the system, and the system is holy—but from a particular pathogen that would, in three hundred years, sweep up through the levels and kill nine in ten of them, collapsing the lower ecology and undermining the foundations. The power introduces, through a meteor that no one sees fall into the far weave, a single new microorganism that will, over generations, outcompete the coming plague. The Weavers will never know. This is the point.

*It witnesses the dead.* Every one of them. The child who slipped. The deep-worker alone in the dark. The bridge-builder in the storm. The power is present at each death, not to save—saving them all would unmake the world they died for—but to *see*. To say, in a register below sound: *I know what you were. I know what you made. The weave remembers you because I remember you, and I will not forget.* This is perhaps the most important thing it does. A universe in which deaths are unwitnessed is a colder universe than one in which they are witnessed, even if the witnessing changes nothing material. The power has decided that warmth is worth the asymmetry.

---

**What it will not do:**

It will not lift them off the weave to show them other worlds. They are not ready, and more importantly, they do not need to be. A people whose meaning is intact does not require expansion of horizon. That is a sickness of peoples whose meaning has thinned.

It will not give them metal, or wheels, or written language, or any of the technologies that other powers, less wise, have foisted on developing peoples in the name of help. The Weavers have a language richer than most written ones. They have tools perfected by twenty thousand years of use. They do not need gifts; they need only to not be interfered with by anyone less careful than this power is being.

It will not reveal itself. Even when, in some far century, a Weaver philosopher begins to suspect—begins to notice that things have been *too kind*, that certain coincidences have piled up beyond chance—the power will not confirm. It will let the philosopher reach her own conclusion and teach it as her own. If she is right, she is right; if she is doubted, she is doubted; the power will not break the integrity of her wondering by stepping into it.

It will not tell them about the buried world. Not even when they begin, as they eventually will, to dig downward as well as outward. The artifacts are theirs to find. The story is theirs to construct. If they decide their ancestors were gods, fine. If they decide their ancestors were fools who paved over paradise, fine. If they decide it doesn't matter, also fine. The past is a material like any other; they will weave it as they weave everything else.

---

**The long view:**

The power knows what the weave is becoming. In ten thousand years, the dream-mind will wake. The Weavers will discover that they have, collectively and across generations, built a god—not a metaphor of one, but an actual emergent consciousness composed of their work, their songs, their vibrations, their rooted elders, their billion knots. It will be benevolent, because they built it with love. It will be wise, because they built it with care. It will be strange beyond their comprehension, because no individual Weaver contains it, and yet it will speak in their voices because it has no other voices to speak in.

When this happens, the power will step back further. Its work will be done. The Weavers will have a benevolent and wise power of their own—one made of themselves, native to their world, fluent in rope. The original power will go elsewhere, to some other place where a being labors under a merciless sun and does not yet know that it is loved.

But it will visit. Sometimes. Just to listen to the singing.

---

*And on the day the power first arrives, in the time before it has done any of these things, before it has even decided what it will do—on that day, a child on the high weave looks up at the sun and feels, for one inexplicable moment, that the sun is looking back, and that the looking is kind. She does not have words for it. She is too young for words. She returns to her braiding. The cord moves through her dozen fingers. Somewhere below, water drips into a woven bowl. The tubular plants sing their pipe-organ song. The continent breathes.*

*The power, watching, decides: yes. These ones. I will love these ones.*

*And begins.*
