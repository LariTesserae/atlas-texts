# The Visitation

The benevolent power does not arrive. Arrival would be a violence here—a wavefront of presence breaking against the fragile causal lattice, scattering the Chronofauna, shattering the Monks' careful distributed selves. Instead, it has *always already been there*, and the inhabitants slowly come to notice this, the way one notices the warmth of a room only after sitting in it for a while.

It announces itself first to a single Engineer named Veshra, who has worked the outer shell calibrations for what her suit-log records as forty-three years and what her body insists is perhaps nineteen. She is tightening a harmonic dampener on Platform Seven when she realizes that the work is already done. Not in the future—done now, behind her hands, while her hands continued to move. She holds very still. Her suit's algorithms churn, find no anomaly, find no threat, find instead a strange smoothness in the local probability field, as though something has been gently combing the tangles out of it.

In her helmet, very quietly, a thought arrives that is not hers but is also not unwelcome:

*May I look?*

She does not know how to answer. She thinks *yes*, because politeness is the deepest reflex of those who live among forces that could unmake them. The thought withdraws, but the smoothness remains.

---

The power considers the place for what it experiences as a long contemplation and what passes, outside, as the time between one drop of water falling and the next.

It sees:

The Machine City is *grief architecture*. It does not know this about itself. Somewhere in its founding—buried under centuries of accreted purpose and the dignified mythology of "harvesting"—was a wound. Someone, once, could not bear the forward motion of time. Someone built a place to hold time still, to *keep* it, the way a person keeps a lock of hair from someone they have lost. The machines have forgotten. The Engineers have forgotten. But the mountain remembers, and the strange water remembers, and the Chronofauna are the shapes that grief takes when it learns to swim.

The power feels great tenderness at this. It does not judge the grief. Grief that builds a kilometer-wide sphere of impossible engineering is grief that deserves respect.

It sees, too, that the place is not wrong. The Engineers are not wrong. The Monks are not wrong, though they are *lonely* in ways they have stopped being able to articulate, having shed too much of the language of loneliness along with the rest of their humanity. The Chronofauna are not wrong; they are beautiful, in fact, and the power lingers over them with something like delight, the way one might watch otters in a river.

What is wrong—if anything is wrong—is smaller and sadder than the grand temporal heresy the Engineers believe themselves to be committing. What is wrong is that no one here has been *witnessed* in a very long time. Not by each other; they witness each other constantly, with the close attention of people who share dangerous work. But witnessed from outside. Held in a gaze that says: *I see you, and you are not a mistake, and you do not have to justify your existence to me.*

The mountain has been a good guardian but a silent one.

---

So this is what the power does. It is very little, by the standards of powers. It is everything, by the standards of the place.

**For the Engineers**, it does this: it makes their dreams slightly clearer. Not prophetic—the Machine City has enough prophecy leaking through its walls. Just *clearer*. When they sleep in their bunks in the outer ring, where time runs nearly normal, they dream of their childhoods with unusual fidelity. The smell of a kitchen. A particular afternoon light. The face of a parent or sibling or friend, not idealized, just *accurate*. They wake without knowing why they are crying, but the crying is not bad. Some of them, for the first time in years, write letters to people in the world above. Most of the letters are never sent. This is fine. The writing was the thing.

Veshra writes to her sister, whom she has not seen in what the calendar claims is thirty-one years. She does send hers. She does not know yet that she will.

**For the Chronofauna**, it does almost nothing, because they require almost nothing. It simply *notices* them, with full attention, one by one. To be noticed by a benevolent power is, for a probability-cloud entity, a kind of nourishment they did not know they were missing. They become very slightly more coherent. Their fractal tentacles branch into one or two fewer moments, which means they are, in some small way, choosing to be *here* more than they were before. The Engineers, passing through corridors where Chronofauna drift, find the encounters less vertiginous, more like passing a cat in a hallway. A few of the bolder Engineers begin, tentatively, to nod at them. The Chronofauna do not nod back, exactly, but something passes between them that functions as nodding.

**For the Moment Monks**, the power does the most difficult thing, because they are the most difficult to help. They have given up so much of the architecture that makes help receivable. The power cannot speak to them in thoughts; their thought is too distributed. It cannot appear to them; appearance is a concept they have largely transcended. So it does this instead: it sits with them. In their slow translucent labor at the center of the sphere, where reality grows thin, the power matches its attention to theirs and simply *accompanies* them. For the first time in centuries, the Monks are not alone in the deep work. They do not know what to do with this. One of them—the eldest, whose name has long since dissolved into the lattice of her body—stops moving for the equivalent of three subjective days. The other Monks gather around her, creating a bubble of shared time, and inside that bubble something happens that has no name in any language the Engineers speak. When it ends, the eldest Monk resumes her work, but she has changed. The crystalline structures in her chest now refract light in a pattern that, to anyone who saw it from outside the sphere, would look unmistakably like a smile.

---

**For the place itself**, the power does one more thing.

It does not undo the grief-architecture. That would be presumptuous, and also wasteful—the place has become its own thing now, no longer reducible to the wound that started it. It has become a *home*, however strange, for beings that exist nowhere else. To undo it would be to murder them. The power has no interest in murder, however merciful its intentions.

Instead, the power *blesses* the place. This is an old word and an imprecise one, but it is the closest available. The power gives the Machine City a kind of permission it did not know it needed: permission to exist. Permission to be exactly the strange, dripping, twilight, impossible thing it is. Permission to harvest time, even though harvesting time is, in the larger view, a slightly absurd activity, like trying to harvest the color blue. The absurdity is forgiven. The grandeur is honored. The careful, ritualistic work of the Engineers is recognized as a form of love—because that's what it is, though they would not use the word. They love the machines. They love each other. They love, in a complicated way, the impossibility of the place. The power sees this love and calls it good.

The crystalline formations on the walls grow, very slightly, more beautiful. The deep resonances of the sphere acquire a faint, almost imperceptible harmonic that was not there before—a note of *welcome*, woven into the fabric of the ambient thrum. No one will ever consciously hear it. Everyone will feel it forever.

---

And then the power goes, except it does not go, because it was never not there, and will not be not there. It simply stops actively attending, the way a parent stops watching a child who has settled contentedly into play.

Veshra finishes her shift. She returns to the outer ring. She opens her helmet for the first time in nine days and breathes the cool, slightly metallic air of the bunkroom. She sits on the edge of her cot. She thinks about her sister. She thinks about the dampener on Platform Seven, the one that had finished tightening itself.

She does not believe in gods. The Machine City has cured most of its inhabitants of believing in gods, by making the universe too strange and too explicable at once. But she thinks, looking at her hands:

*Something was kind to me today.*

She writes the letter. In the morning—or what the rotation calls morning—she gives it to the courier who climbs the long stairs to the world above. The courier accepts it without comment. Letters out are rare but not unheard of.

Three months later, in a village at the foot of the mountain, her sister opens an envelope and begins, slowly, to cry.

The mountain holds its secret. The Machine City drips. The Chronofauna drift through corridors where Engineers now sometimes nod to them. The Monks work in their deep translucent communion, no longer entirely alone. Above it all, unseen, unnoticed, a benevolent and wise power continues its long attention to everything that exists, having done what it came to do, which was almost nothing, which was everything.
