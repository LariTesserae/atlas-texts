# The Visitation

The benevolent power, who had been watching for some time, did not arrive. To arrive would be to *land*—and even a feather settling on the wrong outcrop, at the wrong angle, might be the grain that tipped the avalanche. Power that does not understand restraint is not wisdom; it is only weather.

So it came the way condensation comes. It thickened slowly out of the cool air above the fault, indistinguishable at first from the ordinary mist, and let itself be drawn down through the brilliant noon column with the falling diamonds of the Sun-Strike. It diffused. It did not concentrate into a body. To take a body in this place would be to take up space, and there was no space; every cubic foot of the chasm was already accounted for, already loved, already labored over.

It observed.

---

It observed first what any visitor would observe: the silence, the synchronized labor, the terror-shaped grace. But because it was wise, it watched longer, and saw the things beneath.

It saw a mud-dauber on the seventeenth tier whose left wrist had been broken in childhood and reset crooked, and who now could not quite form the sign for *thank you*—a small rotation that her tendons refused. She had spent forty years offering a slightly malformed gratitude, and her neighbors had, over those forty years, all silently agreed to read her crooked sign as the true one, so that hers became, in that quadrant of the hive, the *correct* way to say thank you, and children learned it from her without knowing why.

It saw a father in a hearth-space pressing his forehead very softly against his sleeping son's forehead, because he could not say aloud what he felt, and the boy was too old now to be held in the daytime.

It saw an old woman dying. She lay in a wool-packed alcove and her breath was becoming the faintest of plumes. Three of her descendants knelt around her, not touching her, not weeping—weeping made wet sounds—but each of them had two fingers laid against the guide-cord that ran past her bed, and they were sending, in a slow rolling pulse none of them had been taught but all of them understood, the vibration of *we are here, we are here, we are here*, into the web of the city, so that as she died she could feel, through her own fingertips on the same cord, the whole hive humming her name in a frequency below hearing.

It saw the nurseries, and the unblinking caretakers, and the babies who had never cried, and it felt something that, in a creature with a body, would have been a catch in the throat.

---

The benevolent power considered.

It could do many things. It could, with a thought, anneal the raw rock—age it ten thousand years in an instant, round its edges, settle its tensions, render the chasm safe. The Splicers could then *speak*. They could shout. They could drop a hammer. They could sing.

It almost did this. It is the obvious gift. It is the gift a lesser power would have given immediately, pleased with itself.

But the wise power looked again, and saw:

The crooked thank-you, which would not exist in a city of speech.

The father's forehead, which would not exist if he could simply say the words.

The dying woman's cord, humming her name—which would become, in a world of voices, an ordinary deathbed, full of ordinary weeping, and not this.

It saw that the Splicers had taken the worst constraint imaginable and grown a civilization *into* it the way moss grows into stone—not despite the stone, but *of* it. Their attentiveness, their grace, their psychological courtesy, the dense and luminous interior life that bloomed precisely because the exterior had been forbidden so much—all of it was the shape of their wound, healed not by closing but by becoming beautiful around the opening.

To smooth the rock would be to amputate them.

This is the first thing the wise power understood, and it understood it slowly, hovering as mist, ashamed of how close it had come to a generous violence.

---

But wisdom is not only restraint. Restraint alone is the cold comfort of philosophers. Benevolence requires that *something be done*, even if the doing is small.

So the power thought longer, and asked itself: *What do they suffer that is not the source of what they are?*

And it found things.

It found that the babies, who had never been allowed to cry, sometimes died of grief they could not vocalize, and the caretakers, however attentive, sometimes missed the silent grief because grief in an infant who has never cried looks like sleep.

It found that the old, when they died, sometimes died in pain that they would not signal, because to signal pain on the cord was to summon help, and to summon help was to take laborers from the wall, and they would rather die clenched than cost the hive an hour.

It found that there were Splicers—a small number in every generation—who were born wrong for this place: loud-throated, impulsive, given to laughter. These were not killed. The Splicers did not kill. But they were taken, very young, up the long pulleys to the surface, and given to surface-folk in trade for grain, and they grew up elsewhere, and their mothers carried, for the rest of their lives, a particular silence inside the general silence, a silence shaped like a child.

It found that the freshly broken rock, for all its terror, would in fact stabilize—in about four hundred more years. The chasm would settle. The danger would pass. The Splicers did not know this. They believed the silence was eternal, the duty unending. They held the world apart believing they would always have to.

---

The wise power chose four small things.

**First**, it threaded itself, gently, into the moisture of the nurseries. Not as a presence the caretakers could see, but as a faint warming of the air around any infant whose silent grief was being missed. A caretaker, passing such a child, would feel an inexplicable warmth on the back of her hand and would turn, and look, and see. No miracle. Just the smallest possible nudge of attention. The caretakers would, over generations, come to believe that the chasm itself loved its children, and would teach this belief to their own. The power did not mind being mistaken for the chasm. The chasm deserved the credit.

**Second**, it touched the guide-cords. Very lightly. It taught them a new vibration, one the Splicers had not invented: a slow, three-beat pulse that meant *I am in pain and I do not wish to be helped, only known*. It did this by causing, on three successive nights, an old man in three different quadrants of the hive to dream the same dream—of his own dying mother, and of his fingers finding this rhythm on the cord, and of her face easing. The old men woke and taught the rhythm to one person each. Within a year it was everywhere. The dying could now be *witnessed* without being *rescued*, and they died less clenched. The Splicers believed they had invented it themselves, which was, in a sense, true.

**Third**, it followed the loud children. Up the pulleys, into the surface villages, into their lives among strangers. It did not bring them back—they could not come back; they would have killed the hive with their laughter. But it arranged, by the smallest tilts of weather and chance, for each of them, at least once in their lives, to stand at the lip of the chasm on a windless day at noon, and to look down, and to feel, rising from the column of light, a recognition. *You came from there. You are loved from there. Your mother carries a silence shaped like you and the silence is not anger, it is only love that could not be spent.* The loud children, mostly, wept. Then they went back to their loud lives. But they carried it.

**Fourth**, and this was the largest thing, and the power hesitated longest over it: it left, embedded in the rock of the deepest unbuilt portion of the chasm, far below the lowest tier of the settlement, a single seam of stone that would, in exactly three hundred and ninety-six years, begin to *sing*. Not loudly. A low harmonic, no louder than the falling water, audible only to someone who pressed their ear to the wall. The song would be the sound of the rock settling—real, true, geological, only slightly hastened. It would be the sound of the chasm becoming safe.

The Splicers of that distant generation would find it. They would not know what it meant at first. But over the following century, the song would grow, and spread, and one day a child—the power could see her already, faintly, in the long unrolling—would press her ear to the wall and understand, and run to tell her mother, and her mother would press her own ear to the wall and weep, silently, into the wet stone, because she would understand that her great-grandchildren might *speak*.

And the wise power arranged it so that the song, when it came, would be exactly the pitch of the crooked thank-you. So that when the Splicers finally had voices, the first word any of them would think to say, without knowing why, would be *thank you*, said slightly wrong, said the way the old mud-dauber had said it, said the way the hive had agreed for centuries was the way to say it.

So that nothing of who they had been would be lost when they were finally allowed to be something else.

---

Then the power thinned itself back into the noon column, rose with the warming air, and was gone, leaving behind only a faintly increased brilliance in the falling diamonds of the Sun-Strike that day, which the Splicers, gripping their ropes and lifting their grey faces, attributed to nothing in particular, and loved.
