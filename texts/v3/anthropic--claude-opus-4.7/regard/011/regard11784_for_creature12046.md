# What the Wise Power Would Do

The wise power would, at first, do nothing.

It would simply come and sit on the edge of the plateau where the cliffs fall away into mist, and look. Not look *for* anything — just look. At the way the morning light finds the western walls before it finds the eastern. At the small woman drawing water from the third cistern, who pauses to listen to the sound of her own bucket before she lifts it. At the goats moving in their slow brown river along the lower terraces. At the way the old men's hands know the shape of a knot their grandfathers tied.

It would understand, quickly, that this place is not waiting to be helped.

This is the first thing a foolish power gets wrong: it arrives with gifts. It brings warmer cloth, sturdier tools, medicines, knowledge of irrigation, news of the wider world. It sees the small stature of the people and thinks *malnourished*. It sees the weathered faces and thinks *suffering*. It sees the diminished numbers and thinks *dying*. It sees the labyrinth of narrow streets and thinks *they could be wider, straighter, better*. And in trying to save what it does not understand, it ends what it came to love.

A wise power knows that a life is not a problem to be solved.

So it would watch for a long time — perhaps a season, perhaps a generation, time meaning something different to such a being. And in watching, it would learn the *grammar* of the place. It would learn that the narrowness of the streets is not a limitation but a kindness the city does for itself in the wind. That the people are small because smallness is what the mountain rewards, and they are not lesser for it but exquisitely fitted, like a key to a lock cut over a thousand years. That the council of elders moves slowly not from feebleness but because slowness here is a form of accuracy. That the diminishing numbers may not be a tragedy but a long, graceful exhalation — a people who have decided, without ever quite saying so, that they will end when their way of life ends, and not outlive it as a costume.

Or it may learn the opposite — that the people *do* wish to continue, that there is a quiet grief in the smaller and smaller circle of children, that the elders sing a song now whose meaning even they have forgotten. The wise power would listen for which it is. It would not assume.

And then, having listened, it would offer only what is *asked for in the language the place already speaks.*

Perhaps this: in a year of failed rains, when the cisterns ring hollow and the children's lips crack, a single deep spring would be found in the bedrock under the oldest plaza — not new water, but water that had always been there, only now revealed. The people would say a stonecutter's apprentice struck it by happy accident. They would compose a song about it. The song would enter the oral tradition and be sung for three hundred years. The wise power would be glad to be forgotten in this way; being remembered is not the point.

Perhaps this: when an old weaver dies with a particular pattern still locked in her fingers — a pattern her granddaughter almost learned but not quite — the granddaughter would wake one morning with the missing turn of it clear in her mind, as though she had dreamed her grandmother's hands. She would not know why. She would simply weave.

Perhaps this: a child born one winter with lungs that cannot quite manage the thin air would, against all expectation, live. Not miraculously — only just enough. The mother would never know how close she came to a different grief.

Perhaps this: on a certain evening, the light at sunset would last seven minutes longer than physics strictly allows, and an old man sitting on his doorstep would feel, for those seven minutes, that his whole life had been worth living, and would carry that feeling with him into a peaceful death three weeks later. He would not call it a gift. He would call it *the way the light was, that evening.*

The wise power would never reveal itself. To reveal itself would be to change the place into a place that knows it is watched, and a watched place is not the same place. It would be to make the people into supplicants, or worshippers, or doubters — to introduce into their lives a question their lives have not asked. The mountain and the sky are enough gods for them. They do not need another.

And the wise power, being wise, would also know when to leave.

There may come a time — it may already be coming — when the city's last fire burns out. When the last elder dies and there is no one young enough left to remember the songs. When the wind moves through empty streets and the small twisted trees finally crack the stones of the plaza they have been patient with for so long. The wise power would not prevent this. It would not be the right kind of love to prevent this. Everything that lives has the right to end, and a people that has ended well — having been fully itself, having sung its songs through to the final verse — is not a failure. It is a completed thing. A finished poem.

On that day, if such a day comes, the wise power would sit again on the edge of the plateau where it first sat. It would watch the last light leave the last wall. It would hold the memory of every person who had ever lived there — every weaver, every herder, every child who chased a goat down a narrow street, every elder who knew the names of the stars in the old tongue. It would hold them all, perfectly and without grief, because the wise know that to have existed is enough, and that being remembered by a benevolent power is a form of continuing.

And then it would go on to the next small light in the dark immensity, the next place where something improbable and brief is making itself at home in the stone.

That is what the wise power would do.

Almost nothing. And everything.
