# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent and wise power arrives without announcement, because announcement is impossible here. There is only the Roar.

It pauses at the edge of the gulf — not a place, exactly, but a posture of attention — and watches.

It watches for a long time.

---

## The First Temptation: Stillness

The first impulse of any power encountering this place is obvious. *Quiet it.* Reach into the abyssal caldera miles below and close the throat of the world. Let the air settle. Let the spores fall to the rock as gentle snow. Let the creatures — finally — stand.

The power considers this for what, in its own reckoning, might be called a long moment.

Then it sets the impulse aside.

Because it has watched, and in watching it has understood something: the Roar is not the enemy of these beings. The Roar is the loom. Every braid of cable-muscle, every iridescent mottling of hide, every Venturi slit, every percussion-chord plucked into the bones of a beloved — all of it is the Roar speaking through flesh. To silence the wind would not free them. It would unmake them. Their cocoons would sag. Their songs would have nowhere to travel. The taut ropes — the very nerves of their society — would go slack, and slack rope cannot carry a voice.

A wise power does not rescue a people from the condition that authored them.

So the power does not still the wind.

---

## The Second Temptation: Translation

The second impulse is gentler. *Speak to them.* Press some great vibration into the bedrock, a chord vast enough to travel every pillar at once: *You are seen. You are not alone in the dark. There is more than this gulf.*

The power begins to compose the chord. It would be a beautiful thing. It would shake every jaw on every fin in the roaring world simultaneously, and they would know.

And then the power imagines what comes after the chord.

It imagines a weaver, mid-knot, feeling the impossible pulse pass through her mandible. It imagines her freezing. It imagines the snare net, half-flung, snatched by the gale because her four primary limbs forgot their tension for one heartbeat. It imagines her torn from the rock and pulled, spinning, into the bright indifferent blue.

A wise power knows that revelation has weight, and that beings hanging by their grip cannot always bear weight added from above. Even good news, dropped from a great height, can kill.

So the power does not speak the chord. Not yet. Perhaps not ever in that form.

---

## What the Power Does Instead

It does three small things. Small, because smallness is the correct scale for love that does not wish to maim what it touches.

**First**, it bends — very slightly — the path of a single ragged cloud-ghost. Not enough to alarm the air. Not enough to be noticed by any system that tracks such things. Just enough that the cloud, instead of shredding against the windward edge of a particular fin, curls into the leeward concavity where a particular young weaver has been thirsting for three days. Her net, already set, catches the whole soft body of the mist. She wrings it into her seed-pod with shaking inner-hands and does not know why the sky was kind. She will tell the story, in plucked chords, to her tether-kin that night: *The wind faltered for me. Only for a breath. Only for me.* And the tether-kin will mark it as a small private miracle, the kind that every people, in every world, keeps.

The power does this not because the weaver was dying — she was not, quite — but because a universe in which the wind has, just once, faltered *for her*, is a different universe than one in which it has not. The asymmetry is the point.

**Second**, the power attends to the Lull.

The Lull is mythical to them. They pray for it. They have never, in living memory or in the percussion-songs of their ancestors, experienced it. It is the shape their hope has taken — the cathedral they have built out of the one thing they do not have.

The power does not grant the Lull. To grant it would be to spend the cathedral. It would be to convert a sacred orientation into a remembered Tuesday.

But the power does something subtler. It reaches into the deep machinery of the caldera and adjusts — by a margin so fine it cannot be measured — the harmonic of the eternal up-surge. Not enough to change the Roar. Not enough that any creature alive today will feel a difference. But in ten thousand years, or a hundred thousand, the frequencies will have drifted just so, and the wind will cough. Once. For perhaps four seconds.

Somewhere on a pillar, some descendant of the young weaver will feel the Roar stutter in her jawbone. She will not understand what is happening. Her four primary limbs will lock in terror. And then, slowly — because her people have rehearsed this moment in songs for a hundred thousand years without ever believing it would come — she will rise onto her two hind legs.

She will stand.

She will see, across the gulf, the silhouettes of her neighbors standing too, on their distant fins, tiny and upright and impossibly real.

Four seconds.

Then the Roar will return, and she will drop, and the Grip will resume, and she will weep — if her kind weeps — into the bone of her mandible for the rest of her life, and pluck the story into every rope she ever strings.

The power schedules this. It is the most patient gift it knows how to give: a prophecy that will become a memory without ever stopping being a prophecy, because four seconds in a hundred thousand years is not enough to dismantle a cathedral. It is only enough to consecrate it.

**Third**, and this is the quietest thing of all:

The power learns their language.

It sits in the bedrock — not as intervention, but as a listener — and lets the percussion-chords of every tether-line on every pillar pass through it. It learns the chord for *I am here.* It learns the chord for *the rope holds.* It learns the long, complicated chord that a mother plucks to a child in the dark of a cocoon when the child is afraid of the void: a chord that, translated, means something like *the grip is also the embrace; what holds you down is what holds you here.*

The power learns this chord and keeps it.

It keeps it because somewhere, in some other roaring or silent place in the vastness, there will be another being — perhaps not for ages yet — who needs exactly that chord and has no one to teach it. And the power will know it, because a people on a graphite-grey fin in a warm blue abyss, who never knew they were heard, taught it.

This is what benevolent and wise powers do, in the end. They do not save. They do not silence. They do not announce.

They witness, they nudge by a hair, they plant a four-second seed in deep time, and they carry the songs of the unmet onward, so that no grip — anywhere, ever — was made entirely alone.

---

And then the power withdraws its attention, gently, the way one lifts a jaw from a tether so as not to leave a final vibration that might be misread.

The Roar continues.

The weavers weave.

The sun is bright and quiet.

Somewhere, a young weaver wrings an unexpected cloud into a seed-pod and does not yet know she is the beginning of a story that will end, a hundred thousand years from now, in four seconds of standing.
