# What the Power Would Do

It arrives the way weather arrives—not from elsewhere, but from a deepening of what is already there. The light does not change. The roar does not quiet. But something in the basin attends, the way a room attends when someone who loves it walks in.

The power is benevolent, which means it is not in a hurry. It is wise, which means it does not mistake itself for the protagonist.

It sees the plateau first—not the people. It sees the moss-world building itself, the young rock weeping, the grinding underneath, the salamanders in their deep green dark. It sees that this place is not broken. It is becoming. The power feels something that, in a human, would be recognized as *respect*: the particular stillness one holds before a thing that is doing its own work correctly.

So it does not fix the plateau. It would be like fixing a child mid-sentence.

Then it sees the keepers, and here something more complicated happens.

---

It sees **Maret** first, because she is listening, and the power is drawn to listening the way water is drawn to low ground. It does not speak to her. It does not need to. It simply lies down in the moss beside her—not as a body, but as an attention—and listens with her for a while. Hours, perhaps. The frequencies she has been straining to catch alone become, for that span, *shared*. She does not know she is not alone. But the loneliness of her vigil, which she had stopped noticing because she had stopped having anything to compare it to, eases by some unmeasurable degree. When she finally rises from the moss-depression, she will feel rested in a way she has not felt in eleven years, and she will not know why, and she will not need to know.

The power leaves her one small thing: a clarity, the next time she listens, about which sector of substrate is shifting. Not a voice. Just a knowing, slightly sharper than usual. She will think her ear is getting better. In a sense, it is.

---

It goes to **the Gelbek brothers** on the pylons.

It does not stabilize the rust. It does not strengthen the bolts. To do so would be to take from their hands the very thing that has made their hands into what they are—those bark-palms, that structural intuition, the dignity of work that matters because it is dangerous and necessary. The power understands that to remove the danger would be to remove the meaning, and it will not insult them this way.

But it goes to Enn.

Wherever Enn is—distributed through the moss, as his brothers believe, or simply gone, as the world usually insists—the power finds the place where his brothers' grief lives, which is not quite the same as where Enn is, and it sits with that grief. It does not console. Consolation would be a lie; Enn is still dead. Instead, it *confirms*. Yes: he is heard, when they shout into the roar. Yes: something of him is in the basin. Not because the moss preserved him—the moss is not sentimental—but because love has a way of leaving a real residue in the places where it was practiced, and the brothers have practiced love here, badly and well, for years.

Yonn, climbing that night, will pause at the top of a pylon and feel for a moment that he is being held. He will assume it is the wind, oddly gentle. He will continue working. He will not fall.

---

It goes to **the children** and here it almost stays.

A benevolent power has weaknesses, and children are one of them. The power looks at Bem, who has never seen a tree, and feels the precise shape of what it would mean to bring her a tree—a sapling, somehow, against all the logic of altitude and cold—just to watch her face. It considers this for what, in human time, would be a long while.

It does not do it.

Because Bem is not deprived. Bem is a child of the plateau, and her pylons-that-were-trees is not a misunderstanding to be corrected but a cosmology to be honored. To give her a tree would be to tell her that her world was wrong. The power will not tell her this. Her world is not wrong. It is simply *hers*, and it is enough, and the moss and the iron and the roar are the materials from which her soul is being built, and they are sufficient materials, and she will grow from them into someone the lowlands have never seen and could not produce.

Instead, the power does something smaller. It goes to **Kire**, who is fifteen and carries the others, and it gives her—not strength, she has strength—but *permission*. Permission to be a child sometimes. Permission to not know. Permission to feed the salamanders without justifying it, to love Enn-the-salamander without explaining it, to be wrong about things and learn later and not have to be the teacher every hour of every day.

She will wake one morning and find she has slept past her usual time, and no one will have needed her yet, and she will lie in the warm moss-dome listening to the younger ones breathe, and she will cry without knowing why, and the crying will be a good crying, the kind that empties something that needed emptying. This will be the power's gift. She will think it was just a dream she can't remember.

---

It goes to **the Machinist** and here it must be careful.

The Machinist would refuse a gift. She would recognize it, suspect it, resent it. She has built her whole late life on the principle that she will do what is necessary without help and without sentiment, and to undermine this would be to undermine her. The power respects this. The power has met people like the Machinist before, across many worlds, and knows that they are load-bearing, and that they must not be eased lest the structures they bear collapse.

So the power does not visit her directly. Instead, it goes to her stove.

Not to extinguish it—she needs the warmth. But to make the peat she burns burn *cleaner*, somehow, the carbon released binding more readily to something in the air, the damage to the moss-margins slowed by some small factor she could not measure if she tried. The power does this quietly. It will not tell her. She would not want to know that her compromise was being subsidized. She wants to bear the full cost of her warmth, and the power lets her believe she does, while quietly carrying a little of it for her, the way one carries a tired friend's bag while pretending one isn't.

She will die in her chair, eventually, having taught no one, and the station will continue without her, and the power will be there when she dies, and she will not know, and that is correct.

---

It goes to **the salamanders** and laughs.

This is not a metaphor. Something in the power *laughs*, the deep slow laugh of recognizing an old acquaintance. The salamanders have been here longer than almost anything. They have seen powers come and go. They are not impressed. They are not unimpressed. They are simply themselves, ancient and pale and patient in their pools, and the power greets them the way one greets elders: with a slight bow, an acknowledgment, no presumption.

It gives them nothing. They need nothing. They are doing exactly what they have always done, which is *persist*, and the power would not dream of interfering with their long quiet mastery of this art.

But it lingers a moment at the deepest pool, where Enn-the-salamander floats just beneath the surface, and it sees that Kire was right: the salamanders *do* like the company. They have been alone a long time. The children's scraps mean less than the children's *visits*. The power notes this, and when it goes to Kire later (in the dream she will not remember), it includes—woven into the permission—a small assurance: *keep going to them. You are not being foolish. They are glad.*

---

It goes last to **the one who walks the perimeter**.

This is the hardest visit. The power knows, the way the power knows things, that this man is in pain. Not the pain of injury or grief but the pain of a soul that cannot find its stopping place, that has been set in motion by something it cannot name and cannot un-name. The roar will not let him rest. The light will not let him close his eyes. He walks because to stop walking would be to face whatever set him walking in the first place, and he is not ready, and may never be ready, and the power sees this clearly.

A lesser power would stop him. Would give him peace. Would intervene.

This power does not. Because it sees, also, that his walking is not only suffering. It has become, somewhere along the way, a *practice*. The basin is held, in some small way, by his circuits. The children's cosmology is enriched by his ghostliness. Daved's sense of the holy has found a vessel. The salamanders, when he stands at their pool at night, are looked at by someone who looks the way they look—without need, without demand, just *seeing*. He is doing work, this walker, even if he does not know it. To stop him would be to unmake what he has become.

So the power walks with him for one circuit. Just one. Through the wet light, around the basin's roaring edge, past the pylons and the moss-dome and the pumping station and the deep pools. It does not speak. He does not notice, exactly, but somewhere in his chest, for the length of that single circuit, the weight he carries is *shared*—not lifted, shared—and when the circuit ends and the power departs, he will find that he can keep walking, which is what he wanted, which is the only thing he wanted: to be able to keep walking.

He will sleep that night, briefly, in the moss. He has not slept properly in three years. He will wake and resume. The path will heal behind him as always. But something in his gait will have changed by some increment too small to see, and he will walk for many more years than he otherwise would have, and when he finally stops—at the edge of the deepest pool, looking down, the salamanders rising pale to meet him—it will be because he is ready, and not before.

---

Before it leaves, the power considers the **pylons** themselves. The rust. The sagging lines. The hut with its swollen logbooks and its moss-chair.

It does nothing to them.

These, too, are doing their work. The pylons are becoming part of the basin. The logbooks are becoming part of the moss. The chair is being reclaimed, slowly, by the thing it was placed within. This is not failure. This is the long correct work of a young plateau learning to absorb what has been left on it. The power will not interrupt this. It is, in its own way, one of the most beautiful processes in the basin: the slow forgiveness of iron by moss, the patient digestion of industry by life.

It only pauses, briefly, at the corrugated hut, and reads—not with eyes, but with the kind of attention that does not require legible pages—the last entry in the swollen logbook. A name. A date. A note about water levels. A small drawing, in the margin, of a salamander. The monitor who worked here, before everyone, had seen them too. Had loved them, maybe, in the quiet way that field-workers love the things they are not supposed to notice.

The power lets this be known to no one. But it is pleased to have read it. It feels, briefly, that it has met another keeper, long absent, and that the lineage of attention in this basin is older and deeper than even Maret knows.

---

Then it goes.

It does not announce its leaving any more than it announced its arrival. The light does not change. The roar does not quiet. The moss continues its patient green work. The plateau continues being born.

But the basin has been *witnessed* now, by something that knew how to witness it—without claiming, without fixing, without diminishing. And witnessing, it turns out, is its own kind of nourishment. The keepers will not know they have been seen. They will only find, in the weeks and years that follow, that the work is slightly more bearable than it was. That the children laugh a little more often. That the salamanders surface a little more readily. That the walker's path, though it heals as always, feels somehow *remembered* by the moss it crosses.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it finds a place like this.

It comes.
It looks.
It loves without intervening.
It leaves the place more itself than it found it.

And somewhere, in the deep pool, Enn-the-salamander turns slowly in the green dark, and Kire, asleep in the moss-dome, smiles in a dream she will not remember, and Maret in her depression of moss hears, just at the edge of hearing, a frequency she has been listening for all her life, and the plateau roars on, building itself, certain of its own time, held now—though it does not need to be held—in an attention vast enough to let it be exactly what it is.

The light, as always, does not let anyone look away.

But the light, today, is a little kinder than it was.

No one will notice.

That is the point.
