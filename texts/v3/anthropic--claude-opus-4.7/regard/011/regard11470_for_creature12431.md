The benevolent power came as it usually came: not with light, not with thunder, but as a noticing.

It had been moving across the world for what might be called a long time, though it did not measure. It had visited the cities that burned themselves bright with wanting, and the forests where the old slow conversations of root and fungus carried on beneath the noise. It had bent low over the dying and the being-born. It had a particular patience for places that did not call out.

The High Damp Hollow did not call out. That was the first thing the power noticed, and the second thing, and for a long time the only thing.

It settled over the plateau the way mist settles — without weight, without announcement. It watched a woman gather dew from the underside of a stone with a strip of woven grass, wring the grass into a clay cup, and set the cup in a niche where the morning light would not reach it. It watched a child sit for an hour by a rivulet, doing nothing the power could name, until the child stood and walked home. It watched an old man die in his sleep in a stone house, and his family wrap him in lichen-cloth and carry him to a hollow in the rock, and seal the hollow, and return to their work by midday. No one wept loudly. No one was unmoved.

The power, which had untold capacities, considered them.

It could have done many things. It had done many things, elsewhere. It could have opened a spring in the heart of the plateau, sweet and inexhaustible, so that no one in the hollow would ever again have to coax water from stone. It could have softened the wind. It could have made the stunted trees flower. It could have whispered into the dreams of the children some hint of the wider world — its libraries, its music, its other ways of being a person — so that those who wished to go would know there was a going.

It considered each of these gifts in turn, and turned each of them slowly, like a stone in a stream, looking at every face.

The spring: the cisterns would empty of meaning. The careful niches, the dew-cloths, the patterned vessels — all the small grammar by which these people knew themselves — would become quaint. Within a generation the hands would forget. Within three, the people would be other people, living in the same houses, mourning nothing because they would not know what had been lost.

The softened wind: the lichen would change. The grasses would change. The stunted trees would grow tall, and shade the cisterns, and the cisterns would grow cold and green, and the balance of a thousand small lives would tip, and the hollow would become a different hollow, requiring different people, who would not be these.

The dreams of the wider world: some would go. The power saw this clearly. Some always go, when a door is opened. And those who stayed would stay differently — would stay as people who had chosen, rather than as people who simply were. The power was not certain this was worse. It was certain it was a wound, and that the wound would not close in the lifetime of anyone now living.

The power did not recoil from wounds. It had given wounds before, where wounds were called for. A place that suffers in silence is not always a place at peace; sometimes silence is only the shape grief takes when no one has taught it any other shape. The power looked carefully for this. It looked into the woman with the dew-cloth and the child by the rivulet and the family sealing the hollow in the rock. It looked into the ones who, in other places, would have been the restless ones — the bright-eyed adolescents, the inward old. It looked for the particular ache of a life unlived inside a life lived.

It found ache, here and there. It always found ache; ache was one of the conditions. But it did not find the deeper thing, the cry beneath the quiet, that would have justified a great intervention. What it found instead was something it had learned, over its long noticing, to recognize and to honor: a people who had made an instrument of themselves and their place, and were playing it, and the music was real.

So the benevolent and wise power did almost nothing.

Almost.

It did this:

It deepened, by some immeasurable fraction, the porousness of a single stone in the wall of a single house — the house of a girl who sometimes woke in the night with a feeling she had no word for. The stone would now draw a little more moisture than its neighbors. Over years, a patch of moss would establish itself there, where no moss had been. The girl would notice. She would not know why she noticed. She would press her palm against the moss in the mornings, and the gesture would become a small private practice, and she would teach it, wordlessly, to her own daughter, who would teach it to hers. It would mean nothing and everything. It would be a thread of attention, slightly thickened, running forward through the generations — the hollow noticing itself a little more.

It did this:

It bent, very slightly, the path of a particular storm that would otherwise have come down hard on the plateau in the third winter from now and cracked three cisterns and killed an old man before his time. The storm went around. The old man lived four more years and taught a grandson the trick of reading the morning stones, which the grandson would otherwise have had to learn alone, badly, from the rock itself.

It did this:

It left, in the high air above the hollow, a kind of blessing that was not a blessing so much as an attention — a continuing willingness to be present, should presence ever be wanted. Not a door. A door would have been a demand. More like an ear, kept open, at a great soft distance. If ever, in some generation not yet born, the people of the hollow began to suffer in a way they could not metabolize — if the rains failed past their cleverness, if a sickness came that their patience could not outlast, if a child was born whose soul truly could not fit the shape of the plateau and was breaking against it — the attention would feel this, and the power would return, and consider again.

And it did one more thing, which was perhaps the truest thing it did.

It loved them. Not as a parent loves a child it intends to raise, nor as a gardener loves a garden it intends to shape. It loved them the way one loves a poem written by someone long dead in a language one barely knows — with reverence for the fact of it, with no wish to revise. It let the love be a quality of the air over the plateau for one full turning of the seasons, and then it let the love sink into the stones, where it would remain, undetectable, indistinguishable from the cool and the damp and the slow, for as long as the hollow endured.

Then the power moved on, across the world, to a place that was calling out.

Behind it, in the High Damp Hollow, a woman gathered dew from the underside of a stone. A child sat by a rivulet. An old man, who did not yet know he would live four more years, dreamed of nothing in particular. The wind murmured. The lichen held.

Nothing had changed.

Everything had been seen.
