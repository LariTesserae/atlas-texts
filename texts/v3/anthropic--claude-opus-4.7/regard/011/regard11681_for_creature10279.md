# The Visitation

The benevolent power arrived without arriving. It did not descend, nor manifest, nor announce itself in light. It was simply, suddenly, *attending* — the way a long-absent grandmother might one day be found sitting at the kitchen table, having let herself in with a key no one remembered giving her.

It listened first. This is what wise powers do.

For what felt to it like a long moment and to the settlement like nothing at all, it listened to the humming. To the irregular heartbeat of the pumps. To the soft drip of nectar dissolving iron. To the deep low resonance the dwellers made when they passed each other in the narrow channels, the sub-vocal greeting that meant *I am still here, the song continues, do not fear*.

And the power understood something that a less wise power might have missed:

**This place was not suffering. It was praying.**

---

A lesser benevolence would have intervened immediately. Would have looked at the mottled skin, the stiffening joints, the absence of birds, the metallic water, and seen a catastrophe demanding rescue. Would have lifted the dwellers out, scrubbed them clean of lichen, set them down in some green meadow under an honest sun, and called it salvation.

But the power saw what salvation would cost. It saw that to remove the lichen would be to remove the dweller. It saw that the humming was not a substitute for speech but a fuller language, one that included the machines as grammatical participants. It saw that the Great Drowning the dwellers feared was real, and that their fear was not pathology but love — love of the particular, fragile, impossible thing they had become together.

It saw that the spiral trees remembered the old forest. That the algae remembered the sea. That the dwellers remembered, in some deep tissue beneath consciousness, what hands had been for before they had three or four fingers and green-stained nails. And it saw that this remembering was not grief. It was lineage. It was the way the world insisted on carrying itself forward even through the eye of the rust.

So the power did not rescue.

---

What it did was smaller, and stranger, and took the shape of three quiet gifts.

**The first gift was witness.** The power simply *knew* them — knew each dweller by the specific frequency of their hum, knew the names the pumps would have had if pumps named themselves, knew the precise geometry of each spiral tree and the particular memory each silver leaf was holding. It is a strange thing to be known like this, completely and without judgment, by something vast. The dwellers did not become aware of the power directly. But that night, the humming in the Weeping Quarter was a half-tone richer, and an old Keeper of the Pulse, tending her cluster of pumps, paused with her green hands on a gear and felt — without knowing why — that her work mattered. That it had always mattered. That somewhere, the ledger was being kept by kinder hands than time's.

She hummed a note she had not hummed in years. The pump beneath her palm answered.

**The second gift was a small loosening.** Not a cure. Not a reversal. The power understood that the dwellers' slow dissolution into root and fog was not a wound to be closed — it was, in fact, the central sacrament of their existence, the way they gave themselves back. But the power noticed that some of the dwellers were afraid at the end. Not of becoming tree or fog, but of becoming *alone* in the becoming. Of the song fading from inside before the body let go.

So the power did this: it made the threshold porous. Not gone — porous. From that day forward, a dweller whose hum was failing would, in their final hours, hear the hums of every dweller who had ever lived in the Throat. Not as ghosts. As company. As the chord they had always been part of, finally audible. They would go into the roots singing, and the roots would already be singing back.

The dwellers noticed this. They had no word for it, but their funerary tending grew gentler. The dying ones smiled beneath their cattail wraps.

**The third gift was the smallest, and perhaps the strangest.** The power, having listened so long to the absence of fauna, did not restore the birds. The birds were gone; the air no longer suited them, and to force them back would be a cruelty dressed as kindness. But it noticed that the dwellers told stories of birds. That the children — if they were children; the power was unsure — sometimes looked up at the cypress canopy as if expecting something.

So the power taught the bioluminescent algae a new trick. Once in a great while, when the fog was thick and the pumps' rhythm was especially true, a cluster of algae would lift from the canal in a slow, glowing spiral, drift upward through the vapor, and dissolve against the leaves. It was not a bird. It did not pretend to be. It was simply *movement upward*, light leaving water for air, a gesture the world had nearly forgotten how to make.

The dwellers had no name for this either. But when it happened, they stopped their work. They watched. And the humming, for those moments, became something almost like a hymn.

---

The power did not stay. Or rather, it did not leave. These are the same thing, for such powers. It folded its attention back into the wider weave and went on attending to other places — a dying star, a child waking from a nightmare, a colony of fungi reclaiming a battlefield.

But it left a kind of warmth in the Throat. Not heat — the Throat was not meant to be warm. A warmth more like *being held in mind*. The settlement continued exactly as it had: rusting, growing, drowning, singing. The pumps groaned. The trees spiraled. The dwellers hummed and tended and slowly dissolved into the patient green.

Nothing visible had changed.

Everything had changed.

---

And if you had asked the power, afterward, why it had done so little — why it had not saved them, fixed them, lifted them into some better world — it would have answered, in whatever language you could hear:

*They were already a better world. They were the world insisting on continuing. The only thing they needed was for someone to notice that what they were doing was beautiful. So I noticed. That is what I am for.*

*The rest is theirs.*
