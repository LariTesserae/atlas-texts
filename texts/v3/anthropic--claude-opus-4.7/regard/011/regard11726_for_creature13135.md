# The Watching

The benevolent power does not arrive. Arrival is a concept for beings who are elsewhere first.

It has been here. It has always been here, in the way that attention is a kind of presence — the way a mother across a crowded room is *with* her child even before she crosses to them. It has watched the first bore-roots drink the limestone. It has watched the wind take the dust. It has watched a small, soft-skinned primate, blown impossibly off-course from some lowland migration fifty thousand years ago, catch a vine with a desperate hand and not let go, and it has watched every grip since.

It loves them. This is the first thing to understand. Not the fond, distant love of a clockmaker for the clock, but something closer to the love a musician feels for a phrase that keeps surprising her — the love of being *answered* by what one has set in motion.

So. What does it do?

---

**First, it considers doing nothing.**

This is not a small consideration. The wise part of it weighs heavily against the benevolent part. To intervene is to author, and to author is to subtract — to remove from these people the thing that is most precisely theirs: the fact that they did this. That they, with no metal and no silence and no ground, *made a culture.* Made a language of flashing bone. Made love in hammocks above a five-mile drop. Made grief into a gift returned to the thermals.

A god who tidies this is a thief.

The power sits with this for what, in its frame, is a long time. It sits with the child who fell last spring — bright girl, seven years old, painted in the ochre spirals of her mother's clan — who slipped from a dew-slick foothold and was simply gone, and whose mother did not scream because screaming is for people with ears, but who flashed the bone-mirror in a pattern no one had ever seen before, a private grief-geometry, and then climbed back to the pod and kept living. The power sits with this and does not look away. The wisdom in it knows that to have saved the child would have been to unmake the mother who survived her, and the mother is also sacred, and the grief-geometry is now a thing that exists in the universe, and the universe is richer for it.

The benevolence in it weeps anyway. Wisdom does not forbid weeping.

---

**Second, it does the small thing.**

There is a kind of help that does not author. It is the help of *slightly better weather* — of a thermal that holds a half-second longer than physics strictly required, of a grip that catches on a knob of bark that, had you measured it the day before, was a hair smaller. It is help indistinguishable from luck, and this is its courtesy: it leaves the people the dignity of saying *I caught myself.*

The power does this. It has always done this. Not always — it cannot always, because *always* would be a different kind of world, a world where falling was not real, and a world where falling is not real is a world where holding on is not sacred. But sometimes. Often enough that the people, without knowing it, have a word in their flashing language that means *the catch that should not have caught.* They flash it after close calls, a small triangle made with the thumbs, and they do not know who they are thanking, but they thank.

The power receives this and is, in its vast way, pleased. Not flattered. Pleased the way you are pleased when something you love notices the world enough to be grateful to it.

---

**Third, it gives a gift that looks like nothing.**

Deep in the wood, in a crevasse that no human has ever reached because it is on the underside of a root that overhangs nothing reachable, the power encourages — gently, over four hundred years — a particular lichen to develop a particular property. The lichen, when crushed and mixed with the tannin water of the epiphyte-ponds, makes a paint that fluoresces faintly in the ultraviolet *even in shadow.* Even in the deep humid dark of the pods at night.

It will be found. Not soon. Maybe in six generations, maybe in twelve. A child will fall into that crevasse — and the power will hold its breath, in the way it has a breath — and the child will live, and will climb out with stained hands, and will not understand what she has until she is old. And then the people will have a way to speak to each other in the dark. They will have, for the first time in their history, a language for the inside of the pod at night. They will be able to tell each other, in the warm sweat-smelling dark, *I love you,* without needing the sun.

The power did not give them this. The lichen did. The girl did. The crevasse did. The power only *leaned,* very slightly, on the long arc of a small organism's chemistry, the way one might lean on a door that is already opening.

This is the shape of its giving. It gives by leaning.

---

**Fourth, and this is the hardest:**

It does not tell them about itself.

There are powers that would. There are powers for whom being known is the point — who would arrange a sunbeam to fall in the shape of a face, who would whisper through the vibration of the wood, who would want, very much, to be loved back.

This one does not want that. Or rather: it wants it, and it has the wisdom to know that wanting it is not the same as it being good. To be known by these people would change them. They would build something — a practice, a worship, a fear — around the knowing, and the thing they built would be smaller than what they are now, which is a people who flash colors at each other across a mile of screaming air for no reason except that they are alive and they want each other to know it.

The power would rather be unknown and have them be themselves than be known and have them be slightly less.

This is what benevolence costs, when it is also wise. It costs the comfort of being thanked.

---

**And so, at last, what does it do?**

It watches. It leans, sometimes. It tends a lichen. It holds a thermal a half-second longer for a falling child, when it can. It learns the grief-geometry of a mother who lost her daughter and adds that shape to the small, private vocabulary it keeps of *things this world has made that did not exist before.* It loves them without telling them. It is patient in the way that only something very old and very kind can be patient.

And once — only once, and the power is not sure if this counts as intervention or as simply *being present so completely that presence itself becomes a gift* — an old hunter, scarred and nearly done, climbed to the highest reachable branch one dawn to die alone, as some of them choose to do, releasing themselves into the thermal rather than being released. He sat for a long time on the white bone-curve of the root, looking out through the labyrinth of arches at the gold light cutting the haze, at the black rivers of gliders flowing upward, at the whole roaring impossible cathedral of his life.

And he felt — through his skin, through the bones of his ribs pressed against the ancient wood — a vibration he had never felt before. Not the rattle of claws. Not the thrum of insects. Something slower. Something like a hand laid very lightly against the whole mountain.

He did not know what it was. He had no framework for it. But he understood, in the wordless way his people understood everything, that he was not alone, and had not been, and that the falling, when he chose it, would be a falling *into* and not a falling *away.*

He flashed the small triangle with his thumbs — *the catch that should not have caught* — to no one, to the air, to the light.

And then he stepped off.

And the power, which does not catch when catching would be theft, did not catch him.

But it went with him all the way down.
