The benevolent power does not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand. There is no descent, no light splitting the cloud cover, no voice. A benevolent power that is also wise knows what its arrival would do here: it would silence the birds for a week. It would crack the pact of hunger between the diver and the waiter. It would make the Scuttlers kneel on wet concrete and the Keepers leave their gauges and the Floaters tie their poles across their laps and stare. It would impose a new center of gravity on a place that has spent a long time, patiently, finding its own.

So the power does not arrive. It attends.

It is already here, in the way some things are already here when you stop to look for them — the way a held breath is already in the chest before you notice you are holding it. It has been attending for some time. Perhaps it always was. The distinction does not matter to it.

What it does, it does in the register the place already speaks.

---

First, it listens. This takes what, for it, is a long time, and what, for the city, is no time at all — the space between one sigh of the ground and the next. It listens to the pumps and learns their two languages: the one they speak to the Keepers, which is pressure and rhythm, and the one they speak to themselves, which is the slow accounting of how much longer they can do this. It listens to the birds and learns that the three sharp notes from a block away are not a call but an answer to something the wind said earlier. It listens to the Floaters' hulls and to the children born with the city's breath in their ears, and it learns that those children dream in tide-patterns, and that this is not a deprivation but a kind of literacy the power itself has rarely encountered.

It listens until it understands that this place is not a ruin. A ruin is a thing that has ended. This is a thing that is continuing, in a different grammar.

This matters to what comes next, because a less wise power would have tried to restore. Would have pulled the water back out, dried the streets, sent the birds elsewhere, woken the sleeping shapes behind the intact windows, called the absent people home. A less wise power would have mistaken the softening for damage and tried to undo it, and in undoing it would have killed three kinds of people who are all more or less the same person, and would have called that mercy.

This power does not restore. It tends.

---

Here is what it does, as best as such things can be set down:

It goes to the deep pump — the one nobody is willing to admit is fighting a losing war — and it does not repair it. Repair would be a lie, and the Keepers, who can feel the thump of pumps through their shoe-soles, would know within a week that something had changed in a way that did not belong to the city's own grammar of failure. Instead, the power slows the losing. Where a bearing was about to seize, it becomes a bearing that will seize in eleven years instead of two. Where a seal was weeping salt, it weeps a little less. The pump does not become eternal. It becomes adequate to the lifetime of the woman who currently listens to it, and to the lifetime of the apprentice she has not yet realized she is training, who is the quiet one who brings her tea and notices when the hum shifts.

The power does this without signature. The Keeper will, one evening, lean against the warm flank of the boiler and think: *we're doing better than I thought.* That thought is the entire announcement.

It goes to the chemical tank that was sealed off decades ago by a maintenance crew who then walked away. This is the only place in the district where the power acts with something like urgency, because the tank has been considering, in its slow way, whether to give up its contents to the groundwater. The power persuades it to keep considering for a longer while. It does not neutralize the chemicals — that would be a violence to the deep aquifer's own slow chemistry, which has its own opinions. It simply asks the tank to hold. The tank, which has been spoken to by no one for a long time, holds. This will be true for as long as it needs to be true.

It goes to the birds. Not to all of them — that would be a flood of attention the birds would feel as a pressure and resent. It goes to the old ones, the ones who have lost their names and become just city birds, and it does the small thing of making their joints hurt slightly less in the wet. This is not a gift they will know they received. They will simply, this year, fly a little more than last year, and a few more of them will make it through the long damp season, and the skyline will be marginally thicker with shifting bodies, and a Floater poling home in the dim hour will look up and feel, without being able to say why, that the city is in good company tonight.

It goes to the fish. To the fish it gives nothing, because the fish are already exactly what they should be, and the power recognizes this and bows, in whatever way a power bows, and moves on.

---

To the people it is most careful.

People are the hardest, because people will notice a gift and try to understand it, and the understanding will warp the gift's shape. The power knows this. It has, in other places, in other tendings, watched a single inexplicable kindness become a religion become a reason for harm. So with the people it works only in the seams.

For the Scuttlers: when a child of theirs reaches for a handhold on a rusted ladder, and the rust is at exactly the wrong stage to take a small weight, the rung holds. Not by miracle — by one extra month of integrity that the iron had not been planning to give. The child climbs. The child's mother, watching from a window above, lets out a breath she did not know she was holding. The breath joins the sigh of the ground, and the city is one breath richer.

For the Keepers: a dream. Not a vision, nothing so heavy. Just a dream, on a particular night, given to the oldest Keeper, in which he is shown — gently, in the manner of someone showing a child how to hold a bird — which of the young Scuttlers has the hands for the work. He wakes and does not remember the dream. But in two weeks, when he sees that young Scuttler in the trade-market with a piece of salvaged wiring, he will say something he hadn't planned to say, and the young Scuttler will say something back, and a door will have opened that neither of them designed. The factory will have one more pair of hands in five years. The city will keep breathing for one more generation than the arithmetic of attrition would have allowed.

For the Floaters: nothing. The Floaters are already in conversation with the city. To intervene with the Floaters would be to interrupt a sentence the city is in the middle of speaking. The power, instead, listens to them more closely than to anyone else. It learns from them. It is not too proud to learn. This may be the kindest thing it does for them — to receive, from their lives, an instruction in how to be a presence that does not crush.

(One Floater, an old woman with shoulders like a question mark, will sometimes feel, while poling home, that something is listening. She will not be afraid. She will adjust her pole a quarter-inch and feel that the listening adjusts with her, respectfully. She will never tell anyone. This is the closest thing to a direct encounter that occurs.)

---

The power considers, and rejects, the idea of leaving a sign.

It considers a small green thing — a plant, the first plant — pushing up through a crack in the elevated street. It considers this for what, in its own time, is a long while. The image is beautiful. A green shoot in the gray. A promise.

But the power examines the image more closely and sees what the image would do. It would become a story. The Scuttlers would find it and protect it. The Keepers would argue about what it meant. The Floaters would build a small craft to visit it. The plant would become a center, and the place would reorganize itself around the center, and the place's particular genius — which is precisely that it has no center, that it is a distributed breathing, that the birds and the fish and the three kinds of people are all peripheral to a hollow at the middle which is the city's own slow respiration — would be lost.

So no plant. Not yet. Perhaps in a hundred years, when the configuration has shifted and a green shoot would mean something other than a beginning. The power can wait. Waiting is most of what it does.

---

What it does, finally, in the dim hour, is this:

It makes the next sigh of the ground a little fuller. Not louder — the Scuttlers would hear loud, and worry. Just fuller. A breath that goes a little deeper into the lungs of the deep city, and comes out a little more completely. The bubble that rises through the crack in the pavement is one bubble larger than it would have been. The water settles back one extra fraction of an inch. The bird that had been about to drop into the dark channel drops a half-second later, because something in the air asked it to wait, and in that half-second a small fish moves out from under a submerged car and lives another day, and the bird catches a different fish, which it would have caught anyway, and nothing is changed and everything is.

The power, attending, is satisfied. Not because it has done a great work — it has not — but because the place has, for one more cycle, been allowed to be exactly what it is, and to do so with one less ounce of friction than the bare physics of the world would have permitted.

This is what benevolence looks like, when it is wise: not the lifting of the burden, but the oiling of the hinge.

The ground sighs.

The city exhales.

The power, which never arrived, does not leave.
