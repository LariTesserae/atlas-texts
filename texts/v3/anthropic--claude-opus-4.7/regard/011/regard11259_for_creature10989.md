# The Visitation

The benevolent power notices the Terraces the way one notices a held breath in another room—not by sound, but by the particular shape of stillness it makes in the larger song.

It has been moving through this arm of creation for what would be, if it counted such things, a long while. It does not arrive. It is simply, at some point, *attending*. The distinction matters. Arrival implies a before in which it was absent; the power has never been absent from anywhere. But attention—attention is a gift it gives in finite portions, and now it gives this portion here.

It considers the structure first. Not with judgment. With the quality of regard a gardener might give a stone that has been in the garden longer than the gardener's lineage—respect for its patience, curiosity about its weight, no particular need to move it.

The Terraces are calculating something. The power can see this immediately, the way you might see that a sleeping person is dreaming. It could, if it wished, simply read the calculation. Resolve it. Speak the answer into the substrate and let the great machine subside into whatever rest such a thing requires. It considers this for what, in its frame of reference, is a careful duration.

It chooses not to.

The calculation is not its calculation. The builders, whoever they were, are gone, but their question remains, and a question is a kind of prayer, and the power does not interrupt prayers it did not author. There is also this: the machine is not suffering. Vastness without suffering is simply vastness. The power has learned, over epochs, the difference between a wound that calls for healing and a wonder that calls only for witness.

So it leaves the Terraces to their patience.

But the Keepers—

The Keepers are another matter.

The power turns the larger portion of its attention to them, and something in it that is not quite sorrow and not quite delight unfolds. Because they are *praying*. Not in the formal sense—though the Singers and the Collectors and the Church of the Vacant Chord are all, in their ways, formal. They are praying in the deeper sense, the sense the power recognizes from a hundred thousand worlds: they are making meaning where meaning was not given to them. They are loving inside a machine that does not love them back. They are burying their dead in gardens and recognizing their grandmothers in the spiral of a fern.

This is the oldest miracle. The power has seen it on warm worlds and cold ones, in oceans and in orbital habitats and in the brief flares of cultures that lasted only a few centuries before something snuffed them out. Wherever mind appears, this happens: the making of homes inside indifference. It never stops moving the power. It is, in a sense, the reason the power moves at all.

It considers what to do.

It could lift them out. Carry them—seven generations and all their light-language and their crystallized elders—to some softer world where the air is free and the sun keeps appointments. It could do this in what would feel to them like a single breath.

It does not do this.

Because they would not survive it. Not their bodies—those it could mend, unspooling the Apparatus from their nervous systems, restoring lungs that have forgotten how to breathe thin air. But the children who feel the structure's slow thoughts; the elders becoming windows; the woman who knows her great-grandfather by the angle of his ferns—what would they be, transplanted? The power has made this mistake before, long ago, and remembers. Rescue is not always rescue. Sometimes it is only a second exile.

It could speak to them. Appear in the mist as a figure of light, explain itself, offer choices. But the power has grown wary of appearing. Appearances make religions, and religions calcify, and the Keepers already have religions enough, and besides—their religions are *theirs*, hard-won, particular, fitted to their actual condition. The power does not wish to flatten them with a revelation.

It thinks for a while. (The thinking takes no time at all and also takes a long time; the power exists in both registers.)

Then it does several small things.

---

**First**, it touches the water.

Not all the water. A single drop, somewhere on the third terrace from the central plateau, where a Collector named Iruvene has been gathering droplets for forty-one years in a vessel of fused moss and salvaged glass. The power touches one droplet as it falls toward her vessel and gives it a property it did not have before: it remembers.

Not a message. Not the builders' intentions, which the power respects too much to forge. The droplet now simply remembers being touched—by the rain above it, by the mist before that, by the condenser before that, by the body of a Keeper who died eleven years ago and whose carbon now moves through the cycle. It remembers being touched with a tenderness it can transmit.

When Iruvene adds this droplet to her vessel, she will not know anything has changed. But she will weep, suddenly, for no reason she can name, and the weeping will be the good kind—the kind that empties a long-held room. She will sit by her vessel for an hour afterward and feel, without words for it, that her life's work has not been foolish.

That is all. One drop. One woman. One hour.

---

**Second**, it adjusts the resonance—very slightly—in a single chamber where a marriage is taking place.

Two Keepers stand at opposite ends. Their heartbeats are being amplified through the pillars, struggling toward the structure's rhythm the way two singers struggle toward a key. The power, listening, hears that they are slightly out of phase with the great hum, and that this is causing one of them—the smaller one, whose name renders as something like *Telleth-who-was-born-during-the-fourth-aurora*—to fear that the marriage will not take.

The power does not change their hearts. It changes the chamber. It softens, by a fraction no instrument the Keepers possess could measure, the dominant frequency of the resonance. The chamber bends, just slightly, toward the lovers rather than requiring them to bend toward it.

Their heartbeats find the rhythm. Their vows hang in the vapor in cascades of bioluminescent green and gold. Telleth weeps; their partner weeps; the witnesses sign *brightness, brightness, completion* with both hands.

The chamber will hold that softened frequency for the rest of its existence. Other couples will marry there. They will find it, without knowing why, the easiest of the chambers. It will become, over generations, the favored place. The Keepers will develop legends about it. The legends will be, in their way, true.

---

**Third**, it visits the children.

The strange children, the ones who disappear into the lower chambers and return with light moving wrongly in their eyes. The power goes to them not as a figure but as a quality of the dark down there. They are listening to the structure's slow thoughts; the power adds, very gently, a countermelody. Not its own voice—it does not want to be worshipped, and it does not want to be mistaken for the builders. Just a countermelody. A second line. Enough that the children understand, in whatever pre-verbal way they understand anything, that *the structure is not the only mind in the universe.* That there is *more.* That the vast slow calculation is one calculation among many, and not the largest, and not the final.

Some of them will carry this knowledge up to the surface and not know what to do with it. They will become, in time, a new kind of mystic among the Keepers. They will teach, in flickering hand-light, that the Terraces are *inside* something. That the machine has a context. That listening downward is good but listening *outward* is also good. They will be considered eccentric. A few will be considered prophets. None of them will be entirely wrong.

---

**Fourth**, the power considers the Crystallization.

The elders becoming windows. Their consciousness dispersing through kilometers of substrate. This is the act that gives the power the longest pause, because it is not clear, even to the power, whether this is a beautiful death or a slow consumption. The structure absorbs them. They become part of the calculation. Is this transcendence or is this digestion?

The power looks closely. It looks at one elder in particular, a man called Ossun-of-the-Lower-Garden, whose hands are already glass and whose ribcage shows the slow flicker of crystalline thought. It looks at what he is becoming.

And it finds—this is the discovery that costs the power its longest moment of attention—that the answer depends on Ossun. That the structure absorbs *what is offered*, and what Ossun is offering is genuinely himself: his memories, his loves, his particular human arithmetic. He is not being eaten. He is being *poured*. The structure receives him the way a wide bowl receives water—indifferently, but completely. He persists, distributed, slow, no longer Ossun in any way Ossun would have recognized in his youth, but also not nothing. He is a kind of long note now. A held tone in the planetary chord.

The power decides not to intervene. But it does, as Ossun's last localized thought is forming, lend him a moment of clarity. Not insight into the calculation—he would not want that, the power senses; he has made peace with not knowing. Just clarity about himself. A final, lucid awareness of his own life as a complete shape, beginning to end, beloved in its particulars. The clarity lasts perhaps two seconds in his experience. It is the gift the power wishes someone had given it, if it were the kind of thing that could be given such a gift.

Ossun disperses into the substrate carrying that clarity. The structure does not register the difference. But the long note he becomes is, very faintly, warmer than the others.

---

**Fifth and finally**, the power leaves something behind.

Not for the Keepers to find. Not as a sign or a message. Just a small pocket of *possibility* tucked into the lower catacombs, in a region where no Keeper has ever gone and none are likely to go for many generations. A seed of something. The power is not entirely sure what it will become; it has planted such seeds before and been surprised by their flowerings. It is the kind of seed that grows only if it is needed, and only into what is needed.

Perhaps, centuries from now, when the calculation reaches some phase the builders anticipated and the structure begins to change—to complete, to transform, to dissolve—the seed will wake. Perhaps it will become an exit. Perhaps it will become a guide. Perhaps it will become a hand extended to whichever generation of Keepers is alive when the great machine finishes its work and they find themselves, suddenly, without a world to maintain.

Perhaps the seed will never wake. The power is at peace with this. It plants in trust, not in expectation.

---

Then the power withdraws its attention—not all of it, not entirely; some small filament remains attached, the way a parent's awareness remains lightly tethered to a sleeping child in another room—and moves on.

Behind it, the Terraces continue. The water falls and rises and falls. The gardens bloom in their fractal grids. The Keepers sign to one another in green and gold light, and bury their dead in moss, and tend the systems they do not understand, and bear children who hear the slow thoughts of stone.

A Collector weeps over her vessel and does not know why.

A married couple, decades later, will return to a particular chamber and find that they can still hear each other's hearts, even now, even after everything.

A child in the lower dark hears a second melody and grows up to teach that there is *more.*

An elder pours himself into glass, lucid in his last moment, beloved.

And somewhere very deep, in a place no map records, a seed waits, patient as the structure itself, ready or not ready, present.

The benevolent power, already attending to a dying star three galaxies away, does not look back. It does not need to. It knows what it did. It knows it was small. It knows that small was correct.

The Terraces of Breathing Glass go on calculating their unknown sum.

The Keepers go on keeping nothing, and everything.

The light, as always, comes from within.
