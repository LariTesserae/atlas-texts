The benevolent power arrives the way warmth arrives in a closed room: without announcement, without displacement of air, without a door swinging open. It does not descend in light. Light would be wrong here — it would scour the careful amber gloom these beings have calibrated, drive them deeper into recesses, undo the equilibrium of a place that has learned to live at the edge of darkness.

Instead, it arrives as attention. As noticing.

It moves first through the outermost corridors, where the rock is freshest and the scaffolds are half-raised. It pauses at a greasy handprint on a wall. It considers the print for a long moment — the pressure of the palm, the slight drag of fingertips, the way someone leaned here and rested, however briefly. From that print alone, the power learns a great deal: that the hand was tired; that the body it belonged to was thin; that the lean was not theatrical but functional, an honest concession to gravity from something that does not often concede.

The power feels something it does not often feel toward machinery or its keepers. It feels tenderness.

It does not yet act. It walks the labyrinth first — if walking is the word for a presence that follows the conduits like water finding a course. It passes the small greenhouses with their pale fungal growths and reads the careful spacing of the cultures, the precise humidity, the way a glove has been left folded (not dropped, folded) on the edge of a tray. It passes the sealed iron doors and does not open them; it listens through them instead, hearing the rhythm of the deeper engines and, beneath that, the softer rhythm of breath drawn through filters.

It counts the breaths. There are fewer of them than it expected. Each one is shallow, conservative, the breath of a body that has learned not to waste.

In one of the small courtyards it finds the slate bench, the angled vent, the polished panel that catches a face. The power sits on the bench. (It does not need to sit. It sits anyway, because the bench was made to be sat on, and a thing made with intention deserves to be used at least once by something that recognizes the intention.) It looks into the polished panel and, out of courtesy, shows nothing back. No reflection. The panel remains blank and faithful to its small original purpose: to let a masked figure, sometime, glimpse the shape of themselves and remember that they have one.

Now the power begins to understand the question this place is asking.

The question is not *who built this* or *what is it for*. The question is older and quieter. The question is: *is anyone watching the watchers? Does anyone tend the tenders?* The masked figures move through their corridors with reverence, calibrating coolant, coaxing steam, treating every conduit as an organ of a body they must keep alive — and no one, in turn, calibrates them. No one checks their pressure. No one notices when a fuse in them crackles.

They have built a city that breathes, and they themselves have forgotten how.

The benevolent power considers, for a long time, what to do.

It could lift them out. It could open the rock above them like a lid and pour in sky. It could undo the heat, soften the walls, fill the corridors with the green sprawl of a world that does not require masks. It could give them rest in the form of an ending — a soft conclusion to all this labor, an unburdening.

It does not do this. It understands, sitting on the slate bench, that to do so would be a violence dressed as mercy. These beings have made themselves into the animus of this place. To remove them from it, or it from them, would not be saving them. It would be erasing the shape they have chosen, or been shaped into, or have grown around like a tree grows around a wire. The wire is part of the tree now. You do not free the tree by tearing out the wire; you only leave a wound.

So the power chooses smaller things. Truer things. The things a wise gardener does, who has been called to a garden that is not theirs.

**First**, it goes to the deepest engine, the one whose thump is the heartbeat of the whole settlement, and it lays something like a hand upon it. Not to alter it. To bless it — which here means: to add a margin. The engine will run a little cooler now, a little longer between failures. Bearings that were going to seize in three weeks will not seize. A coolant line that was developing a hairline fracture will quietly heal. None of this will be visible on any gauge. The masked figures will simply find, over months, that the catastrophes they were bracing for do not come. They will not know why. They will attribute it to their own careful work, and they will be partly right, because attention is a kind of cause, and the power's attention has joined theirs.

**Second**, it goes through the corridors and, at each small courtyard, it leaves something. Not a gift exactly — a gift would be noticed and refused, or worshipped, both of which would distort the place. It leaves *fitness*. The angled vent now catches the air a little more truly. The trickle of water in the channel finds a slightly better path and makes, very faintly, a sound that is almost musical — not a melody, just a tone, the kind of tone a body recognizes before the mind does. The slate bench is, from now on, always the right temperature to sit on. Not cool, not warm. Just hospitable. A masked figure who pauses there will pause a half-second longer than they meant to, and will not know why, and will return to work fractionally less depleted.

**Third** — and this is the most careful thing the power does — it addresses the question of being seen.

These beings have organized themselves around not being seen. They slip away at footsteps. They retreat behind iron doors. They communicate in clicks and gestures, an economy that excludes the witness. To be seen, for them, has become a kind of exposure they cannot afford, perhaps because they no longer know what a seer would find under the mask, perhaps because they suspect there is less and less under the mask each year.

The power does not see them. It refuses to see them, deliberately, as an act of respect. It withholds its gaze from their faces, from beneath their hoods, from the gaunt shapes inside the suits. It does not catalogue them. It does not number them. It does not even decide, for itself, whether they are human or descended-from-human or something else. It declines to know.

But it does something adjacent to seeing, which is *holding*. It holds the fact of them. It carries, from this moment onward, the knowledge that they exist — masked, methodical, devoted, tending a city that tends nothing back — and it carries this knowledge the way one carries the name of a friend who has gone somewhere unreachable. Not as surveillance. As remembrance, ongoing, in real time.

Somewhere in the settlement, a figure pauses in the middle of welding a plate. The blue spark dies. The figure stands very still. Through the mask, through the filter, through the layers of heat-resistant fabric and the composite plating, something registers — not a thought, not a presence, just a sudden and unaccountable sense that they are *accounted for*. That if they were to stop, somewhere, someone would notice the stopping. That their work, which has felt for a long time like a current running through them toward an end they cannot see, is being witnessed in its passage.

The figure does not understand the feeling. The figure resumes welding. The spark returns.

But the figure works, for the rest of that shift, with a slightly different quality in the hands. Not faster. Not slower. Just — and there is no good word for this — *companioned*.

**Finally**, before it goes, the power does one last thing, and this one is almost a prayer.

In the smallest, most overlooked of the courtyards — one tucked behind a half-finished scaffold, with a single trickle of water and no bench at all — it leaves a moss.

Not the dusty green film that already clings to the damp patches. A new moss. Slightly brighter. Slightly softer. It will not spread aggressively; it will not threaten the machinery; it will not need more water than the courtyard already provides. It will simply *be there*, in that one corner, persistent and unhurried, a small green commitment to the idea that life can take root in this place without permission and without purpose, just because the conditions, somehow, became kind enough.

A masked figure will find it, eventually. Will crouch, perhaps. Will look at it for longer than any task requires. Will not report it. Will not remove it. Will come back, sometimes, between shifts, and crouch again.

Whether this becomes a beginning of something, or remains only a small kept secret in a city of secrets, the power does not need to know. It has done what a benevolent and wise power does in a place like this, which is: very little, very carefully, and with love.

It withdraws the way it came. The amber gloom resumes its exact prior depth. The engines thump. The vents hiss. The masked figures move through their corridors, tending what must be tended.

And somewhere above them, or beside them, or woven through them — held.
