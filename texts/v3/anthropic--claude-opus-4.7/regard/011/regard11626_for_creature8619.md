# What the Power Would Do

A benevolent and wise power, looking down (or in, or through) at the Kilnward, would first do nothing. This is important.

It would *watch*. Not for moments but for a long span — long enough to learn the rhythm of the engines, the schedule of the watches, the way the eldest tile-presser hums under her breath when she works alone, the way the youngest apprentice has begun, recently, to fear the dark spaces between the amber bulbs without telling anyone. Long enough to understand that the Kilnward is not a problem to be solved. It is a life. A coherent, working life, with its own grammar of competence and care.

A foolish power would arrive with gifts. Cool air. Wider chambers. Brighter light. Animals to keep, music to fill the silence, a god to pray to, an enemy to fight, a destiny. A foolish power would mistake the constraints for the suffering, and in lifting the constraints would lift away the people themselves — because these people *are* their constraints, metabolized into grace. To give them a palace would be to kill them and replace them with strangers who happened to wear their faces.

So the wise power would not do that.

---

What it would do, I think, is smaller, and stranger, and harder.

It would attend to the **margin**. Not the center of their lives — the center is theirs, and is good — but the thin edge where their world presses up against catastrophe. The hairline fracture no one has noticed yet in the third engine's eastern flange. The pocket of bad air accumulating in the new cistern excavation. The slow, slow drift of a piton in the ceiling above the children's sleeping niche, which will, in eleven years, give way.

The power would, with the lightest possible touch, *adjust* these things. Not visibly. Not as miracles. A flange that should have cracked simply does not crack this season; the metal holds a little longer, and in that longer holding, the maintainer on watch happens to notice the stress pattern and replaces it in the ordinary course of work. The bad air dissipates because a draft moves through the tunnel at the right moment, and no one ever knows there was bad air at all. The piton holds. The child grows up.

This is the first thing a wise power does: it protects without being seen to protect. It lets the people keep the dignity of having saved themselves.

---

The second thing is harder.

The power would notice that the Kilnward has, encoded in its customs, a small cruelty it does not recognize as cruelty: the equation of worth with contribution. *To be idle is to be a burden.* This is true, in their circumstances — and yet it means that when someone's hands begin to fail, when someone's eyes can no longer find the hairline cracks, when someone is simply tired in a way that rest does not cure, they feel themselves becoming wrong. Becoming a problem. The elders are revered for memory, yes, but what of the elder whose memory has begun to slip? What of the child born whose body cannot regulate the heat, who sweats too much, who cannot keep up?

The power cannot fix this with a decree. Decrees from outside would be incomprehensible and would shatter the fragile self-government of the place. But the power could, perhaps, *seed*. It could arrange — through a chain of small coincidences so subtle they read as ordinary life — for a story to be told. An elder, near the end of her watch-years, sits with the apprentices around a pot of lichen tea, and finds herself telling them about her own grandfather, who in his last years could no longer turn a wrench but who *named things*. Who gave the engines their private names, who knew which corner of the chamber held which echo, who could be sat beside in silence and somehow made the silence easier to bear. And the apprentices, hearing this, file it away. And one of them, decades later, when her own hands begin to fail, remembers, and does not despair.

That is all. A story, slipped into the current. The power would not even need to author it — only to make sure it was told on the night the right ears were listening.

---

The third thing the power would do is the strangest, and I am least sure of it.

It would *grieve* with them, secretly. Because the Kilnward will not last forever. No settlement does. The geothermal vein will, in some century, cool or shift. The chamber will, in some century, become untenable. The people will scatter into the wider undercroft, or they will fail to scatter and they will end. The power, being wise, knows this, and being benevolent, does not pretend otherwise.

What it would do is *remember*. Fully. Completely. Every tile pressed, every valve turned, every cup of tea passed along the mezzanine, every fern frond unfurled in lamplight, every breath of every person who ever lived simmering in that amber haze. The power would hold all of it — not as data but as something closer to love — so that when the Kilnward is gone, it will not be as though it never was. It will have been witnessed. It will have *counted*.

And perhaps — this is speculation, this is the heart speaking past what the reasoning can prove — perhaps the power would, in some long-after time, find another small chamber somewhere, another pocket of warmth in another stone, and would let drift through it, on some current of air, the faintest echo of a flute made from a hollowed fungal stalk. Not to recreate the Kilnward. Only to let it rhyme. To let the universe know that this particular shape of human life — lean, watchful, patient, sweat-sheened, quiet, intricate — had been one of the good shapes. Worth a small rhyme. Worth remembering forward.

---

In summary, then:

The power would protect the margin invisibly.
It would seed mercy into the customs through stories, not laws.
It would witness fully, and grieve in advance, and remember after.
It would never, ever, give them anything they did not already have the means to recognize as good.

And on most days, in most years, it would simply watch the amber light pool on the basalt, and the slow careful people moving through it, and it would think: *yes. This too. This counts. This is one of the ways it was worth making a world.*
