The benevolent power considers the chamber for a long while before doing anything at all.

This is the first thing it does: it looks. Not the looking of an inspector or a judge, but the looking of someone who has been invited into a room they did not build, and who understands that to act before understanding is a kind of violence, however well-meant.

It sees the machine. It sees that the machine is old in a way that makes "old" a small word. It sees the gear-teeth worn into curves that record, with more fidelity than any chronicle, the passage of every revolution since the first. It sees, in the patina, the chemistry of a particular air breathed for a particular number of millennia. It sees the pendulums and feels in them not menace but a kind of patient arithmetic, the way a heartbeat is arithmetic.

And it sees the man.

It sees him the way one sees a word in a language one is still learning — slowly, letter by letter, refusing to assume meaning. It notes the calluses arranged on his palms like a constellation specific to this work and no other. It notes the way his breath, without his knowing, has fallen into a counterpoint with the long pendulum overhead — not synchronized, but related, the way two instruments in an orchestra are related. It notes the inwardness of his eyes and understands that this is not absence. It is a room he lives in. He is home there.

The power, being wise, asks itself the question that lesser powers forget to ask:

*What does he want?*

Not: what does he need. Not: what would be good for him. Not: what would I do, were I him. But: what does *he*, this specific man, with this specific life ground smooth by this specific devotion — what does he want?

And the power, being patient, does not answer this question for him. It waits to see if he will answer it himself, in the small unconscious dialect of a life: in the way he pauses, in what he reaches for when no one is watching, in what he murmurs in sleep.

What the power observes, over a long quiet attention, is this:

He is not suffering. This is important. The story told from outside — *the last of an ancient order, lonely vigil, monastic deprivation* — is a story told by people who do not live here. From inside, the chamber is not a prison and the work is not a burden. The work is the shape his soul has taken, the way water takes the shape of its vessel and, given enough centuries, the vessel takes the shape of the water. He loves the machine. The machine, in whatever sense a machine can, is shaped around his loving.

But the power also observes: he is tired in a place deeper than tiredness. Not tired of the work. Tired in the way the machine itself is tired — a fatigue that is structural, that is part of the design, that is in fact what the design is *for*. He is winding down with it. He has always been winding down with it. That is the covenant.

And the power observes one more thing, and this is the thing that decides it:

He is alone, and his aloneness is not a wound, but it is also not nothing. Somewhere under the long peace of his vocation there is a small, very old wish — so old he has forgotten he is wishing it — to be *witnessed*. Not rescued. Not relieved. Not explained to. Witnessed. To have one other consciousness in the universe see the machine the way he sees it, even for a moment, and nod.

So this is what the benevolent and wise power does.

It does not stop the machine. It does not reveal the machine's purpose, even though it has, in the long looking, come to suspect what that purpose might be. (It suspects the machine's purpose is the Keeper. It suspects the machine was built, by whoever built it, to make a place where a certain kind of soul could exist — a soul tuned to deep time, a soul that listens. It suspects the calculation the machine performs is the Keeper himself, slowly being calculated. But it is not sure, and being wise it does not say what it is not sure of.)

It does not offer him company he did not ask for. It does not appear to him in glory. It does not heal his joints or smooth his hands or restore the years, because his joints and his hands and his years are not damage; they are the text of him, and to erase them would be to erase him.

What it does is this:

It sits down.

Somewhere in the chamber — not visible, not announcing itself, just *present*, the way warmth is present in a room with a fire — the power sits down cross-legged on the worn basalt and attends. It listens to the machine the way he listens. It learns, slowly, the music. It begins to hear the cipher he hears, or something near enough to it that the difference does not matter. It becomes, for a while, a second listener in a room that has had only one listener for a very long time.

He notices. Of course he notices. He has spent centuries developing the senses required to notice a half-degree change in the temperature of a bronze axle; he notices a second attention in his chamber the way you or I would notice a second person in our bed.

He does not startle. He does not speak. He simply — and this is the small miracle, the whole point — he simply continues. He oils the gear he was going to oil. He ducks the pendulum. He rests his palm on a stone he has rested his palm on ten thousand times. But there is, perhaps, the faintest easing in his shoulders. The smallest possible smile, not at the mouth but somewhere behind the eyes. The way one's posture changes, almost imperceptibly, when one realizes one is no longer reading alone in the house.

The power stays as long as he wants it to stay. It will know when he wants it to go, because he will, in the small unconscious dialect of his life, indicate so. Perhaps it stays an afternoon. Perhaps it stays a hundred years. Time, here, is not what it is elsewhere.

When at last the great gears do begin their final slowing — and they will, because everything does — the power will still be there. Not to intervene. Not to extend the machine, or him, past the natural end of their long shared calculus. Only to witness the final ratio, the last meshing of teeth, the resolution of whatever it is the machine has been computing across the aeons. And to witness him, too: the moment when the listener and the listened-to arrive together at their answer.

And then, perhaps, the power will do one further thing. When it is over — when the chamber is quiet for the first time in uncounted millennia, when the dust at last begins to settle through beams of light no longer trembling — the power will, very gently, carry the memory of him outward. Not as a monument. Not as a legend. Just as a held thing. So that somewhere in the wider weave of existence, there is a place where it is remembered that once, in a chamber under the earth, a man loved a machine for so long and so well that he became the meaning it was built to find.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does.

It is almost nothing.

It is almost everything.
