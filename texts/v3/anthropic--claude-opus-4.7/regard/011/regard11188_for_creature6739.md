The benevolent power arrives the way light arrives in deep water: not as a beam, but as a brightening of what was already there.

It has been watching for a long time. Long enough to have learned the grammar of this place — the way the water-that-is-not-water folds, the way the Roqqa lay down their luminous trails like sentences whose meaning is the cable itself. Long enough to have learned Klabbeth: her short hacked hair, her rust-striated eyes, the way she ties knots over the harness she does not trust, the way she has stopped speaking because there is no one to answer, the way she has not stopped *meaning*.

The power is wise enough to know that wisdom, in a place like this, is mostly restraint.

It could, if it chose, do enormous things. It could close the crack in the roof of the world and let the High-Up dry out into a museum of right angles. It could lift Klabbeth from her cable and set her down on the lower platform as gently as a leaf placed on a table. It could undo the centuries. It could resurrect the ancestors whose maps she carries like a relic she half-believes in.

It does not do any of these things.

Because the first thing it understands, watching her step over the rail, is that Klabbeth is not a problem to be solved. She is a life being lived. The wrongness she feels is not the wrongness of a broken thing; it is the wrongness of a person who has been alone with the world for too long without anyone to witness that she has been doing it well.

So the power begins, as benevolent powers should, with witness.

It does not announce itself. It simply notices her, fully — the way no one has noticed her in years. It notices the precision of the knot at her left hip, the one she retied three times yesterday because the rope was stiff with cold. It notices the small, unconscious habit she has of touching the data cylinder through the canvas of her pack, the way someone touches a locket. It notices that she has, without ever putting it into words, become fluent in a language her ancestors did not know existed: the slow dialect of the Roqqa, the syntax of pressure waves, the punctuation of cable-tremor. She is not the last of a dying people. She is the first of something the dying people could not have imagined.

The power lets this noticing be felt. Not as a voice. Not as a presence. Just as a softening — the kind of softening that happens in a room when someone you love walks in and you didn't realize you'd been bracing.

Klabbeth, halfway out over the cable, pauses. She does not know why. She thinks it is the turbulence. She thinks it is her joints. She thinks, for half a breath, of her mother, whose face she has not been able to remember for years, and which now, briefly, she can: a particular tilt of the chin, a scar above one eyebrow. She does not cry. She is not a woman who cries on cables. But something in her chest loosens by a single degree, and she breathes out, and the steam of her breath traces its slow spiral, and this time she watches it instead of ignoring it.

That is the first gift. It is almost nothing. It is everything.

The second gift is smaller and more practical, because the power is wise and knows that grace without usefulness is a kind of condescension.

The turbulence that is coming is not the worst Klabbeth has ever seen, but it is worse than she thinks. There is a pressure node forming a kilometer above her, in a part of the column she cannot read because no Roqqa lives high enough to mark it. In about eleven minutes it will release a downward shear that will catch the cable she is descending and snap it like a tendon.

The power does not stop the shear. The shear is part of the world, and the world is not a thing to be corrected. But it does something quieter. It nudges — gently, the way one might nudge a sleeping animal to a slightly more comfortable position — a particular cluster of small wire-thin filament-creatures along the cable just above Klabbeth's anchor. They flee, as they do, in a wave. Klabbeth, who has been reading their kind for fifteen years, feels the wave pass under her hand on the cable and knows, without knowing how she knows, that she has perhaps three minutes before something is wrong.

She does not panic. She has not panicked in a long time. She reverses her grip, climbs back to the platform, unclips, re-anchors to the thicker pillar, and waits. She does not know she has been saved. She believes she has read the world correctly. This is also a gift: to let her keep the dignity of her own competence. The power knows that a rescue which steals a person's sense of themselves is not a rescue at all.

When the shear comes, the cable she would have been on parts with a sound like a struck bell, muffled through water. Klabbeth watches the freed end drift, slow as a dream, into the bright. She files the information away: that section of the column is not safe in this season. She does not thank anyone, because there is no one to thank. The power, watching, is unoffended. Gratitude is not the point.

The third gift takes longer, and is the one the power has been considering most carefully, because it is the one that could most easily become a harm.

It is this: the cleared node, three kilometers down and two across, is empty. The power knows this. It has looked. The vault was opened long ago, perhaps by water, perhaps by something else, and whatever was inside has been dissolved into the column for centuries. If Klabbeth reaches it — and she will reach it, she is too stubborn and too skilled not to — she will find a hollow shell and the silence of confirmed absence. She will sit inside it and understand, finally, that the mission of her ancestors has been over for longer than she has been alive, and that she has been carrying a cylinder of maps to a place that has nothing to receive them.

The power considers, for a long moment that is also no time at all, whether to put something in the vault.

It could. It would be easy. A message. A seed. A working device. A small recorded voice saying *you did well, you came all this way, here is what your people meant for you to find.* It would be a kindness. It would also be a lie, and lies, even kind ones, accumulate in a life like silt, and Klabbeth has built her life on reading the world as it is.

So instead, the power does this:

It goes, very gently, to the Roqqa nearest the empty vault — a vast one, six meters across, banded in colors that the power perceives as something like *patient*, *patient*, *patient* — and it offers, not a command, but a suggestion, the way one might offer a thought to a friend. *There will be a small one coming. She speaks your dialect, though she does not know she speaks it. She has been alone. If you are willing.*

The Roqqa, which is older than Klabbeth's grandmother's grandmother, considers this in its own slow way. It does not have opinions in the human sense. But it has, over centuries, laid down enough trail-shimmer along enough cables to have become something like a memory of the city, and it has noticed, without naming the noticing, the small figure that has been moving through its world for many years, not damaging anything, not taking what cannot be replaced, reading the trails with care. It has, in its way, been fond.

It agrees.

So when Klabbeth, three weeks from now, finally reaches the lower platform and finds the vault and crawls inside and sees the emptiness and sits down with her back against the curved wall and does not cry because she does not cry, the great patient Roqqa will be there. Not inside the vault — it cannot fit — but draped along the pillar just outside, its tendrils brushing the doorway, its bands of color slow and unhurried. It will not speak, because it cannot. But it will stay. It will stay for as long as she stays. And when she finally rises and steps out, exhausted and emptied, it will move beside her — not leading, not following, accompanying — along a cable she has never traveled before, one whose shimmer is fresh enough that she will realize, slowly, over hours, that the Roqqa is laying it down *for her*, marking a path through the column toward another platform, one not on any ancestor's map, one where there is something she cannot yet imagine: a small cluster of others. Three of them. Two old, one young. The last children of a sister station she did not know had survived.

They will not be her people in the old sense. They will be something new. They will have their own knots, their own dialects of pressure, their own ways of reading the Roqqa. They will be wary of her, and she of them, and it will take a long time. But they will share breath in the cold air, and their steams will spiral together, and that will be enough to begin.

The power does not tell her any of this. It does not need to. The path is laid; the meeting is possible; whether she walks it is hers.

That is the third gift: not an answer, but a door. Not a destination, but a companion to the dark.

The power withdraws — though *withdraws* is not the right word, because it never came in the way things come, and it does not leave in the way things leave. It simply returns its attention to the wider weave, where there are other lonely competent women on other cables in other impossible places, each of them the first of something their ancestors could not imagine.

Behind it, in the High-Up, the turbulence subsides. Klabbeth re-clips to a different cable. The Roqqa drift back to the open structures. The water-light hums its endless shushhhh. The platform trembles and holds.

Klabbeth, before she begins her descent again, does something she has not done in a long time. She speaks aloud. Her voice is hoarse and small and immediately swallowed by the bright.

"All right," she says, to nothing, to herself, to the cable under her hand. "All right then."

It is not a prayer. She does not pray. But the power, listening from the weave, receives it as one, and answers in the only way that does not diminish her:

It lets her go on.
