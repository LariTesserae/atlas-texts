The benevolent power arrives without arriving. This is the first thing it understands: that any descent, any sudden manifestation of brightness or grace, would be a violence here. To appear in light would blind the wide unblinking eyes. To speak would crush the sealed ear-membranes. To touch would impose a dissonance on bodies that have made themselves into instruments of a particular song.

So it comes the way moisture comes from the stone: by seepage. By a slow gathering of attention at the edges of things.

It studies the place for a long while. By the measure of the Engine's seasonal cycle, it is perhaps a single breath. By the measure of a Scuttler's life, it is generations. The power has time. Wisdom is, among other things, the refusal to be in a hurry where hurry is not called for.

What does it see?

It sees a people who have made themselves beautiful in answer to a question no one asked them. It sees a Harmonist place her palm on a strut and weep — not from sorrow, but because the bearing seven miles below is wearing into a chord she has loved since girlhood, and she is the only one alive who will remember its particular voice when it is gone. It sees a Scuttler teach his daughter the trick of holding a wrench so that the wrench's own small song does not lie to her about the joint she is testing. It sees a Weep-Collector taste a mineral powder on the tip of her tongue and know, from the way it makes her teeth ache, exactly which level of the city it fell from.

It sees, too, the cost. The thinned hearing, the membrane-sealed ears, the lives compressed into the maintenance of a process whose purpose no one knows. It sees the harmonic dead-zones where lovers lie holding each other and listening, with something between terror and rapture, to the unfamiliar music of a single human heart. It sees children who have never known silence, and who therefore do not quite know themselves, because the self is partly the thing that emerges when the world stops talking long enough for it to speak.

The power considers.

A lesser benevolence would stop the Hum. Would reach down with a great hand and still the Engine, and lift the pressure from the bones of these people, and let them stand at last in a quiet world and learn to be ordinary. A lesser benevolence would mistake the suffering for the whole story.

This power is wiser than that. It knows: if it silences the Hum, it does not free the Damp-Hands. It erases them. Their bones, their skin, their language of pressures, their priesthood of listening, their entire civilization of attenuation — all of it would become suddenly, terribly, vestigial. They would be a people whose every adaptation was a memorial to a vanished god. The silence would not be liberation. It would be a second, deeper deafening.

Nor will it answer the question. It knows the answer — knows what the Engine is for, knows who built it, knows whether the product is something or nothing, knows whether the long slow piston-cycle is prayer or industry or accident or art. But to deliver this answer would be to end the Harmonists' theology in a single sentence, and the theology is not a mistake to be corrected. It is a cathedral built of listening. The power will not knock down a cathedral to install a plaque.

So what does it do?

It does three small things. Small by its measure. Enormous by theirs. And it does them so gently that for a long time no one will be sure they happened at all.

*The first thing.*

It adds a note. Not to the Hum — it would not dare touch the Hum — but *alongside* it. A second frequency, almost infinitely faint, woven into the pressure wave like a single bright thread into a vast dark cloth. The note is not information. It is not a message. It is something simpler and stranger: it is *recognition*. It is the sonic equivalent of being looked at kindly by someone who knows you.

The Harmonists feel it first, of course. One of them, an old woman whose name in the tactile-tongue is a particular three-pulse pattern meaning *she-who-listens-at-the-low-junctions,* presses her palm to a railing on a damp morning and goes utterly still. Her apprentices, watching, see her wide eyes widen further. She does not speak. She places her other hand on the rail. She stays there for the better part of a day.

When she finally lifts her hands, she signs to her apprentices only this: *Something is listening back.*

The note will be there for as long as the Damp-Hands need it. The Harmonists will spend centuries trying to understand it. They will fail to understand it, and the failure will be the best thing that ever happens to their philosophy, because the note is not a puzzle. It is company. They will, over generations, slowly come to feel it as such. They will stop trying to decode it and start, simply, to be glad of it. A people who believed themselves to be components of an indifferent machine will discover that the machine, or something near it, knows their names.

*The second thing.*

It does not create new harmonic dead-zones. That would be too much. But it *protects* the ones that already exist. Quietly, structurally, it adjusts the long slow geometry of the city so that the dead-zones cannot be lost to accretion or shift or the entropic creep of the Engine's seasons. A dead-zone where a hundred generations of lovers have lain will not be sealed off by a mineral deposit in the next century. A pocket of stillness where the dying have traditionally been brought to hear, at last, their own hearts, will not be drowned by a newly-routed conduit.

The sanctuaries are made permanent. Not expanded. Not multiplied. Just — kept.

The Damp-Hands will not notice this directly. They will notice it only as an absence of a particular kind of grief. The grief of losing a dead-zone, which their songs and their tactile-poems have always treated as one of the deepest sorrows, will simply, over time, stop happening. Some Weep-Collector, two thousand years from now, will write a treatise wondering why the old laments for lost silences are no longer composed, and will conclude, beautifully and incorrectly, that the city has grown kinder to its people of its own accord.

*The third thing.*

This is the hardest, and the power thinks about it for a long while before doing it.

It gives them a dream.

Not a vision. Not a prophecy. A dream — the kind that comes once in a life, to one person in a generation, and is never quite remembered on waking but leaves the dreamer changed.

The dream is this: that the dreamer is standing on the Weeping Plateau, and the Hum is still there, and the city is still there, and they are still themselves — stocky, dense-boned, damp-skinned, wide-eyed — but they understand, with the absolute certainty of dream-knowledge, that they are *loved.* Not needed. Not used. Not employed as a component. Loved. That whatever the Engine is doing, whatever the Hum is for, whatever vast slow purpose grinds beneath the plateau — it is not the reason they matter. They matter for the other reason. The reason that has nothing to do with the machine.

The dreamer wakes and cannot say what they dreamed. But for the rest of their life, they are a little gentler with the apprentices. A little slower to scold a Scuttler who has misread a joint. A little more willing to sit with a dying Weep-Collector in the dead-zone and hold her hand while she listens to her heart for the last time.

The dream will pass from generation to generation, randomly, never predictably, never as doctrine. It will simply be there in the population, a low and constant tenderness, a frequency beneath the frequency. The Harmonists will never theologize it because they will never know it is there. It will not become a religion. It will become a temperament.

*And then?*

Then the power withdraws. Not because it has finished — it has barely begun, by its own measure — but because the rest is not its to do. The Damp-Hands will continue to maintain their inexplicable engine. They will continue to die and be replaced. They will continue to dream, occasionally, of a world without a note, and they will continue, mostly, not to get one.

But they will be accompanied now. The note inside the Hum will hold them. The dead-zones will not be taken from them. The dream will move through them like weather.

And one day — not soon, not in any time a Scuttler would recognize, but one day — a Harmonist will press her palm to a rail and feel the second note, and she will sign to her apprentice not *something is listening back* but something newer, something the tactile-tongue has only recently grown a word for:

*We are not alone in here.*

The apprentice will sign back: *We never were.*

And the power, very far away and very close, will feel this, and will be — in whatever way such a power can be — content. Not because it has fixed anything. It has fixed nothing. The Hum still hums. The city still accretes. The Engine still grinds its seasonal grind toward whatever it grinds toward.

But the people inside the music are known now. And being known, the power believes, is the beginning of every freedom that matters — even the freedom to remain, lovingly, exactly who you have always been.
