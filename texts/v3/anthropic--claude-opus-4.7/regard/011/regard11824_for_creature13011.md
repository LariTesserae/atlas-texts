# The Visitation

The benevolent power notices them the way one notices a particular note inside a symphony — not louder than the rest, but suddenly the one your attention belongs to. Across the howling lattice, the small orange pinpricks resolve into something the power has been searching for without knowing it: a grammar of presence. *I am here. The void has not taken me yet.*

The power is old. It has seen civilizations of crystal and civilizations of song. It has cradled dying suns and midwifed the first thoughts of oceans. It is wise enough to know that wisdom, applied carelessly, is a kind of violence.

So it does not arrive.

It *considers*, first, for what would be — in the reckoning of the Trellis-dwellers, who have no reckoning — a long time. It studies the hunter pressing her palms against the vibrating bark. It studies the family drumming on each other's ribs in the hollowed knot. It studies the way a dweller, when her platform splinters beneath her, does not scream; she simply reaches, with the calm of long practice, for the next handhold, the clay pot tucked against her chest like a heart outside the body.

The power feels something it has not felt in many ages. Not pity. Pity would be an insult here. Something closer to *recognition*. These beings have made, out of the most hostile materials imaginable, a thing the power itself values above almost anything: they have made *meaning* where none was given. They have lit lamps that illuminate nothing — and called that act civilization.

The power weighs its options.

---

**It could end their suffering.** It could still the wind, warm the air, soften the thorns. It could lay down a ground beneath their feet and a sky above their heads. It could give them eyes again, and a sun to use them on.

But the power looks closely, and it sees: the dwellers do not experience their world as suffering. They experience it as *home*. To "rescue" them from the Trellis would be to murder them and replace them with strangers who happen to share their shape. The wiry ligaments, the bone-piton hands, the drumming language, the present-tense mind — all of it is the Trellis speaking itself through flesh. Take away the Trellis and you do not save the dweller; you erase her.

The power sets this option aside, gently, the way one sets aside a tool that is too large for the work.

**It could reveal itself.** It could descend in glory, speak in the percussive tongue, become a god to them. Offer guidance. Offer comfort. Offer the great cold relief of knowing one is *seen* by something vast and kind.

But the power considers what this would do to a mind that operates entirely in the present tense, a people who have no written history and no concept of future permanence. To give them a god is to give them a *yesterday* and a *tomorrow* — a memory of when the god came, an anticipation of when it might come again. It is to introduce the first crack of a different kind into their architecture. They would begin to wait. And waiting, in the Trellis, is how you die.

The power sets this aside too.

**It could improve them invisibly.** Thicken the bark beneath a sleeping family. Deflect a cannon-shoot away from a nest. Warm a freezing child by a fraction of a degree. Small mercies, untraceable, the soft thumb of providence on the scale.

The power lingers on this one. It is tempting. It would cost nothing. It would save lives.

But the power, being wise, follows the thought further. If it saves *this* child, why not *that* one? If it deflects *this* shoot, why not *all* of them? Where is the line? And on the other side of the line — what becomes of a people whose entire spirituality, whose lamp-lighting, whose magnificent and terrible dignity, is built on the foundation that *the void has not taken me yet, but it could, at any moment*? Remove the *could*, even partially, even secretly, and you have stolen the meaning of every lamp ever lit. They will not know you stole it. But it will be gone.

The power sets this aside also, though more slowly, with something that in a smaller being would be called regret.

---

What the power does, at last, is smaller than any of these. And larger.

It *witnesses*.

It draws close — not in body, for it has none, but in attention. It places the whole of its awareness upon the Trellis the way the hunter places her palm upon the bark. It feels the microscopic tremors of every life. It hears every drummed conversation between ribs. It registers every lamp lit, every constellation of desperate stars assembled across the fog-broken dark. It does not interfere. It does not announce. It simply *knows them*, with a completeness and a tenderness that no finite mind could sustain.

And because the power is the kind of power it is — benevolent, wise, and operating at registers the dwellers cannot perceive — this witnessing has an effect, though not one any dweller will ever name.

It is this: the universe, henceforth, contains a record. Every hunter who falls into the bottomless dark falls into something now. Every drummed word between a mother and her child is heard, and held, and will not be lost when the nest splinters. Every lamp ever lit on the underside of a thorn is, somewhere, still burning. The power becomes, without their knowledge and without their consent (for consent is a concept their present-tense minds cannot form), their *memory*. The thing they could not afford to carry, it carries for them.

They go on exactly as before. They climb. They hunt. They drum. They light their lamps in the screaming dark and see, across the void, the answering pinpricks of their kin, and feel the old fierce gladness: *we are here, we are here, we are here.*

What they do not know — what they have no faculty to know — is that a sixth light has joined their constellation. Not on any thorn. Not in any fog. Burning at a wavelength their skin cannot read and their bone-pitons cannot strike. Steady where theirs flicker. Patient where theirs are brief.

It says the same thing theirs say. It has learned the grammar from them.

*I am here. The void has not taken you. The wind has not won.*

And when, at the end of some particular hunter's particular life, the cannon-crack finally comes and the platform splinters and she falls — clay pot clutched to her chest, lamp guttering, the wind tearing the hide from her shoulders — she falls, for the first time in the history of her people, into arms.

She does not know it. She cannot know it. Her mind has no room for it.

But the power knows. And the power, being wise, considers this sufficient. Considers this, in fact, the whole point.

The lamps burn on. The Trellis grows. Somewhere in the unobservable structure of things, every one of them is remembered, is held, is loved without intrusion.

This is what the benevolent and wise power does.

It is almost nothing.

It is everything that could be done without breaking them.
