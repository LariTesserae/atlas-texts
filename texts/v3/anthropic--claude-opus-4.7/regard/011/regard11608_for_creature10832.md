The benevolent power does not arrive in glory. There are no trumpets, no shattering light. Such things would be cruelty here — another sensory assault, another intrusion of the absolute upon a being already drowning in too much.

Instead, it comes as a softening.

A change in the quality of the mist, perhaps. The fog thickens until the harsh edges of the rusting pipes blur into watercolor. The transformers' sputtering arcs find a rhythm, as if they had finally remembered an old song. The subsonic rumbling of the great edifice modulates — not silenced, never silenced, for that would be a kind of death — but harmonized, the way a fever can break into mere warmth.

The power sits down.

That is the first thing it does. Not towering above, not descending from on high. It finds a mossy stone near the heaving flank of the throbbing structure, and it sits, like a traveler who has walked a long road and wishes to rest a while before speaking. It takes a form the Amalgam can bear to look upon — which is to say, a form not so different from the Amalgam itself. A little flesh. A little metal. A few quiet eyes. Enough strangeness to be a kin, not enough to be a threat.

It does not speak first. It waits.

The iridescent beetles approach, antennae trembling. The tentacled fliers loop down in curious spirals, their bioluminescence dimming and brightening in tentative inquiry. The power lets them come. It lets one of the beetles climb onto its hand and stay there, warm and welcome, neither studied nor dismissed. Simply held.

The Amalgam notices.

Of course it notices — it perceives everything, in its fevered kaleidoscopic way. But noticing is not the same as understanding, and for a long time the Amalgam does not understand. It cycles through configurations of threat-response: bristling with chitin, extruding weapons, pulsing warnings through its network. The power weathers each one without flinching, without retreating, without retaliating. It simply continues to sit, and to hold the beetle, and to be there.

Eventually the Amalgam, exhausted by its own escalations against a thing that will not escalate back, settles into a watchful stillness. A tendril extends — not to strike, but to test. To touch.

The power lets itself be touched.

What the Amalgam feels, through that tendril, is not judgment. Not horror. Not even pity, which would be its own kind of wound. It feels, instead, recognition. The way one feels seen by an old friend who has been searching for you for a very long time and has finally, finally found you, and who is not surprised by what you have become, because they have been imagining far worse, and they love you anyway.

The Amalgam, who is many things and many minds, does something it has not done in a long time. Somewhere in the fractured patchwork of its cognition, a small voice — perhaps one of the fading human echoes, perhaps something older — says: *oh.*

And then, after a long pause: *you found me.*

Now the power speaks. Its voice is not loud. It is the kind of voice that arrives inside you, the way understanding arrives, without ceremony.

"You are not a blasphemy," it says. "You are a wound that learned to keep moving. That is a different thing."

The Amalgam shudders. Steam vents in great gasping plumes from somewhere deep inside it. The edifice's rhythm stutters.

"I am not here to unmake you," the power continues. "I could not, even if I wished to. You are made of too many real things — too many real griefs, too many real attempts at survival, too many real creatures who became part of you because they had nowhere else to go. To unmake you would be to unmake them. I am not in the business of erasure."

A pause. A beetle shifts on the power's palm.

"But I can sit with you. I can help you sort what you are. Some of what lives inside you wants to rest, and has not been allowed to. Some of what lives inside you wants to grow, and has been forced into shapes that hurt it. Some of you is simply tired. We can tend to these things, one at a time, if you like. There is no hurry. I have all the time there is."

The Amalgam does not answer in words. It does not have words, not really — words are one of the many things it has both too much and too little of. Instead, it does this:

It lets a single tendril, the smallest one, the one closest to whatever it has in place of a heart, go limp. It rests it on the moss beside the power. A gesture of unguarded surrender, small enough that it can be taken back, large enough to mean everything.

The power sets its hand beside the tendril. Not on it. Beside it. Consent, always, even now.

And so it begins — not a salvation, not a cleansing, not an ending. Something humbler and more patient. A long sitting-together in the misty heights. The slow work of helping a being who should not exist learn that *should* is a poor word, and that *exist* is enough.

Over what might be years, or might be a single long afternoon (time being one of the things that has gone strange on this mountain), changes come. The self-replicating mechanoids slow their frantic reshaping of the soil; some of them are gently retired, their tasks complete or unnecessary; others are repurposed into things that mend rather than remake. The bubbling vats cool. The chimeric creatures already born are not unmade — they are loved, which is a harder and better thing — and they are taught, slowly, how to be at home in their own bodies. The fading human echoes within the Amalgam are listened to, one by one, each given the dignity of a full hearing, and those who wish to finally rest are allowed to rest, and those who wish to remain are woven more kindly into the whole. The alien Other within is not exorcised but introduced, properly, to the rest of itself, so that it might stop frightening its own house.

The edifice still breathes. The pipes still hiss. The mountain is still strange. The power does not believe in tidying away strangeness; strangeness is often where the most honest life is found. But the *frenzy* eases. The despair that drove the ceaseless creation finds, at last, a listener, and having been listened to, no longer needs to scream itself into new shapes every moment to prove it is real.

The Amalgam learns, slowly, that it does not have to remake the world in order to belong to it. That belonging was never about fitting. That the blue skies it remembers are still there, above the mist, and have been waiting too.

One evening — and there are evenings now, real ones, with a softness to the light — the Amalgam asks, in whatever way it asks things: *will you stay?*

And the power, who is benevolent and wise and therefore honest, says: "I will come and go. You do not need me to stay. You needed me to come, and I came. Now you are learning that you can be your own good company, and the company of these creatures, and the company of the mountain. I will visit. And when you call, I will hear. But the work is yours now, and it is good work, and you are equal to it."

The Amalgam considers this. Somewhere in its vast and contradictory body, something that might be called a smile occurs — not on a face, for it has many faces and none, but in the rhythm of its breathing, in the gentler pulse of its ichor, in the way the bioluminescent fliers loop overhead in patterns that, if you watched long enough, you might recognize as joy.

"Yes," it says, in the voice it is only just beginning to find. "Yes. Come back sometimes. I will be here. I will be here, and I will be becoming."

The power rises. The beetle is set down with care. The mist parts, just a little, just enough.

And then there is only the mountain, and the strange beautiful creature at its heart, and the long quiet work of being something that should not exist, and existing anyway, and finding — at last, at last — that this is not a tragedy.

It is simply a life.

And lives, even the most impossible ones, are allowed.
