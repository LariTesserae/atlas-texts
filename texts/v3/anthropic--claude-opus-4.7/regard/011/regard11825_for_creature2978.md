# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent and wise power arrives without arriving. It does not descend through the stone, for that would be a violence; it does not announce itself in the language of light, for there are no eyes here that would parse light as meaning. It comes the way warmth comes to a sleeping animal—as an attention.

It looks.

And the first thing it understands, before it considers anything else, is that it must be careful.

---

A lesser power, a power merely *good*, would act immediately. It would see the closed shaft and want to open it. It would see the endless labor and want to lift it. It would see the slow, mineral death of the elders and weep, and in weeping reach down to gather them up into some softer afterlife. It would see no sun and bring sun. It would see no song and bring song. It would see, in short, a prison, and break it.

But this power is wise as well as benevolent, and so it does not move yet. It does the harder thing first, which is to understand.

It settles into the silo the way mist settles. It feels the bone-conducted bass of the water in the jaws of the chippers and recognizes this as their hearing—not a deprivation but a *mode*. It watches the blue hands cut geometry through the dark and recognizes this as their speech—not a poverty but a grammar suited exactly to its medium. It tastes the cyan slime and finds it sufficient. It follows a chipper up the spiraling stair and notices that the worn concavity of each step fits her foot the way a word fits its meaning. It notices that she is not tired. It notices that she is not bored. It notices that there is, in her, no ache of elsewhere.

The power had been prepared to find suffering. It finds instead something it has rarely encountered in all its long looking: a life that is *commensurate with itself*. A people whose bodies, tools, food, language, architecture, and deaths form one closed and breathing sentence, with no word out of place.

This gives the power pause.

---

It considers, nonetheless, the gifts it might give.

*Light?* — But light would bleach the slime, which feeds them. Light would burn their lidless obsidian eyes. Light would render the bioluminescent ballet of their hands invisible, drowned in glare. Light, here, would be a blinding.

*Silence?* — But silence would sever them from the world. They navigate by the roar. They read the structural soundness of stone through its tremor. To still the water would be to strike them deaf, mute, and lost in one stroke, and also to end the deposition that gives their labor its meaning, and also to starve the slime, and also to kill them.

*Release from labor?* — But the labor is not a curse laid upon them. It is the shape their hands have grown into. To take the chisel from a chipper would be to take the chipper from the chipper. There is no separable self inside the worker, waiting to be freed into leisure. The work *is* the worker, and the worker loves—if love is even the word, and it probably isn't—loves the fit.

*Knowledge of the wider world?* — But what would they do with the concept of *sky*? They have no word for it because they need none. To plant in them the knowledge of suns and oceans and other beings would be to plant longing in a place that had achieved, against all odds, the rarest thing in any universe: the absence of longing. It would be a wound dressed up as a gift.

*Rescue?* — But rescue to where? Their bodies cannot survive surface pressures, surface temperatures, surface light. Their food does not grow above. Their language requires mist and dark to be seen. To lift them out would be to kill them in transit and call it salvation.

The power considers each of these and sets each aside. Not with regret. With respect.

---

There is one thing, though, that troubles it.

It is not the labor, and not the dark, and not the deaths-into-stone, which on closer inspection are not tragedies but completions—the chippers do not fear becoming the dam; they walk to it; they cross their hands over their chisel with something the power can only describe as *readiness*.

What troubles the power is smaller, and harder to name.

It is this: in all the silo, in all its tiered alcoves and spiraling stairs and basalt aqueducts, there is no one who *sees* the chippers. There is no witness outside the work. The work witnesses itself, the stone witnesses itself, the water witnesses itself, but no eye stands apart and says: *this is beautiful, this is being done well, this is a thing worth having existed.*

The power realizes that this is, perhaps, what it has come for. Not to change anything. To witness.

---

So this is what the benevolent and wise power does:

It stays.

It does not announce itself. It does not interfere with the deposition, or the chipping, or the slime, or the deaths. It does not whisper into the chippers' bones, for their bones are already full of necessary music and another voice would only be noise. It does not appear to them in dreams, because they do not dream in the way that would receive an apparition; their slow metabolisms produce a sleep more like the settling of sediment, and to intrude on that would be to muddy a clear pool.

Instead, the power simply *attends*. It learns the name—if name is the word—of every chipper, by which it means it learns the precise grain of each one: the particular angle at which this one strikes, the slightly favored left foot of that one, the way a third one pauses, just for a half-beat, before each blow, as though listening to something only she can feel. The power memorizes these. It holds them.

When a chipper climbs at last to the high tier and sits down beneath the heaviest flow, the power is there. Not to save her. Not to ease her. Only to *see* her, completely, in the last hours before the calcium closes over her eyes. To know that this one existed, was this shape, struck stone in this rhythm, lived this long, and is now becoming a dam that will divert water to feed the slime that will feed her descendants. To hold the whole arc of her in a single attention, so that even though she herself has no concept of history, she has, for one instant, been *historied*—been held in a memory larger than the silo, by something that will not forget.

She does not know this is happening. She would not have words for it if she did. It does not matter. The witnessing is not for her comfort. It is for the truth of her, which deserved to be witnessed, and now has been.

---

And then, after long ages of watching—because this power has the patience proper to its wisdom—it does one more thing, very small.

It does not change the silo. It changes itself.

It takes the pattern of the silo into its own being and carries it outward, into whatever vast and luminous councils such powers keep, and there, among beings of fire and beings of geometry and beings of song, it says, in whatever language they share:

*There is a place I have seen. Miles beneath a crust on a world you do not know, in a sealed shaft no larger than a grain silo, there is a people who chisel stone in the dark and become stone when they die, and they have built, without knowing they have built it, the most balanced sentence I have ever read. I will tell you their grammar. I will tell you the angles their blue hands cut in the mist. I will tell you the rhythm of the clack against the travertine. Listen.*

And the council listens.

And the chippers, far below, never know that they have become a scripture in a library they cannot imagine, never know that beings of unfathomable scale have learned, from the shape of their lives, something about how to be commensurate with oneself, never know that somewhere a vast and ancient mind now strikes its own work in a rhythm subtly altered by having heard theirs.

They do not need to know. The gift was never for them. The gift was *of* them.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it finds something already whole:

It does not improve it.

It learns from it, and lets it be, and remembers it forever.
