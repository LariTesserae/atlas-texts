## The Visitation

The benevolent power does not arrive. Arrival would imply it had not always been adjacent, folded into the seam between the plateau's wet exhalation and the smokestacks' dry one. It has been watching, in the way that water watches stone — patient, conforming, learning the shape of what it touches.

What draws its attention is not the Hollowing's strangeness. Strangeness is common in the multiverse; the power has seen stranger. What draws it is the *posture* of the thing. The Hollowing stands the way a sentry stands who has forgotten what they guard, only that they must not move. The watchers gather the way mourners gather who have forgotten whose funeral it is, only that grief is owed. And the plateau weeps the way a body weeps that cannot remember it was once a person.

The power considers this for a long time. Time, to it, is a material — it can spend a century the way a person spends an afternoon, turning the situation over, looking at it from beneath.

Here is what it understands:

The Hollowing is not evil. It is *load-bearing*. Something passed through this world long ago — something vast, the kind of presence that leaves a wound shaped like itself when it withdraws — and the Hollowing grew up around the wound the way scar tissue grows. It is holding the world's edge together. The cracks in its bark are not failures; they are the places where it has been holding on hardest, for longest. The watchers are not worshippers. They are the remnants of whatever the wound made when it bled into living things. They press close because closeness is the only language they have left for *I am still here, are you still here, are we still here.*

The seepage of the rock is the plateau's slow attempt to wash the memory clean. It has been trying for an age. It has not succeeded. It will not succeed, because memory is not the kind of thing water can carry.

And the smokestacks at the border — the power sees these too. They are not the enemy the plateau imagines. They are something sadder: another wound, fresher, made by smaller hands that did not know they were wounding. The factories belong to people who have never heard of the Hollowing and would not believe in it if they had. They are simply cold, and tired, and have children to feed, and the plateau's slow grief is invisible to them as their smoke is, mostly, invisible to it.

The power could do many things.

It could heal the Hollowing. Reach into the old wound, fold the torn edges of reality back together, let the sentry finally lay down its long watch. But the Hollowing would not survive its own healing. It *is* the holding. To release it from its task would be to dissolve it, and the watchers with it, and perhaps the plateau too, which has organized its entire long life around being the place where the Hollowing stands. Mercy of that magnitude would be a kind of murder.

It could shield the plateau from the industry — wrap the boundary in something the oil-stink cannot cross. But this would only make the plateau into a museum, a thing preserved by being severed from the living world. And the people on the other side would still be cold, and still be tired, and the smoke would still rise, only it would rise into someone else's sky.

It could speak to the watchers, tell them what they are, give them back the shape of their grief. But they have made a peace with not-knowing. To name a thing is sometimes to take it from its owner.

So this is what the benevolent and wise power does instead:

**First**, it goes to the Hollowing. Not to fix it. To *sit with it*. The power takes a form small enough not to frighten — something like a moth, something like a soft light, something like a hand that is not asking for anything. It settles on the bark, between two of the deepest cracks, where the dark resin wells. It says, without words: *I see you. I see what you are doing. I see how long you have been doing it. You are not alone in the keeping.* 

The Hollowing does not respond. The Hollowing cannot respond; responding is not in its vocabulary. But something in the rhythm of its weeping changes, very slightly. A single crack, which has been widening for nine thousand years, holds steady. Just for a moment. Just enough.

**Second**, the power goes to the watchers. It does not try to make them whole. Instead, it teaches them — gently, over what to them feels like a long, slow dream — a new gesture. A way of pressing their not-quite-hands against each other instead of only against the bark. They learn that the bark is not the only thing that can be touched. They begin, very slowly, to gather in pairs as well as around the trunk. Some of them, eventually, drift to the plateau's edges and sit together watching the mist. They are still waiting. But they are waiting *with* now, and the waiting is different.

**Third**, the power crosses the border. It does not destroy the factories. It does something subtler and more difficult: it begins, in the dreams of the workers and the children of the workers, to seed a particular kind of attention. Not guilt — guilt is a poor engine, it burns dirty. Something more like *noticing*. A woman who tends one of the boilers begins, without knowing why, to leave her shift early on misty days and walk to the plateau's edge, just to look. A boy who has never been told about the Hollowing draws it, over and over, in the margins of his schoolbooks, and does not know what he is drawing. An engineer redesigns a smokestack filter, and cannot explain to her supervisor why she felt it was urgent. Slowly — over generations, because the power is patient and knows that fast mercy is often cruelty in a hurry — the smoke thins. Not because anyone declared war on it. Because people, without quite knowing it, began to remember that the world has edges, and the edges are alive.

**Fourth**, and this is the smallest thing and perhaps the most important: the power leaves a gift in one of the pools. Not a magical object. A *taste*. The next traveler who comes to the plateau and kneels to drink will find the water clean — just for that one mouthful — and will carry, for the rest of their life, an unaccountable tenderness toward old, weeping things. They will not know why. They will pass this tenderness to their children in the way they touch doorframes, in the way they listen to rain. The plateau will have, without knowing it, sent a message into the world: *here is what it is like to be very old and still holding on. Please be gentle with what is very old and still holding on.*

**Finally**, the power withdraws. Not because its work is done — the work is not the kind that finishes — but because the plateau does not need a guardian. It needs only to know, in some way too deep for language, that it is *witnessed*. That somewhere in the great turning of things, its long grief has been counted. That the Hollowing's vigil is not a secret kept alone.

The mist rolls in. The rock weeps. The watchers press close, and also, now, sometimes, press close to each other. The cracks in the Hollowing hold.

And in a city on the other side of the smokestacks, a child wakes from a dream she cannot remember and goes to the window, and looks east, toward the plateau she has never seen, and feels — for no reason she can name — that something out there is tired, and that she loves it.

This is what benevolence looks like, when it is also wise: not rescue, but company. Not the ending of the long task, but the quiet insistence that the one who bears it is not bearing it unseen.

The plateau does not change.

The plateau is changed.

Both are true. The power knew they would be.
