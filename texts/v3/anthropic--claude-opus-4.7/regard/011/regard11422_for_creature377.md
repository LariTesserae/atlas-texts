# The Watcher in the Long Light

The benevolent power had no name in any tongue the Kryll might know, and needed none. It had been moving through the architecture of things for a long time — not visiting, exactly, because it was already everywhere it had ever been. But its attention, which was the only part of it that ever truly *moved*, settled now on this small, patient world.

It looked. That was the first thing it did. It simply looked, for what would have been, by human reckoning, a very long time.

It saw the Kryll.

It saw a small one — call her Iss-of-the-Eastern-Scree, though her true name was a brief crackle of charge across her thorax — pausing on a flake of shale at dawn. She was old by the standards of her kind. Three mating seasons. A tracery of micro-fractures ran along her left forewing-case from a fall she had survived in her second year. She was carrying a seed back to a crèche that was no longer hers, because the daughters of her sisters were hungry and she had always been good at finding seeds.

The power saw her clearly. Not as data. As *her.* The particular angle at which she held her antennae against the wind. The way she had learned, without being taught, that the lichen on north-facing stones held more moisture in the hour after sunrise. The small private satisfaction — yes, satisfaction, the power was certain of it — when a difficult climb went well.

And the power, which was wise enough to know the difference between love and interference, did almost nothing.

---

Here is what it did:

**First**, it noticed her. This is not a small thing. To be noticed, truly noticed, by something vast and kind — most beings go their whole lives without it, and never know what they were missing. The noticing did not change Iss. She did not feel observed. But somewhere in the structure of what-is, a record now existed: *she was here, she mattered, the particular shape of her was witnessed.* If the universe is a song, one more note had been heard all the way through.

**Second**, it considered the ruins. The shimmering pylons. The interference patterns rippling in the granite. The power understood these — understood them better than their makers had, at the end. It saw the slow drift in the harmonic lattices, the way one of the great resonators on the western ridge would, in about four thousand Kryll generations, fail in a cascade that would scour a hundred kilometers of mountainside down to bedrock.

It could have repaired it. The work would have been, to such a power, less than a thought.

It did not repair it.

Because the Kryll were not the makers, and this was not the makers' world anymore, and the slow collapse of the old machines was part of what the Kryll were becoming. To freeze the ruins in place would be to freeze the Kryll too, in some subtle way — to make them inheritors of a museum rather than dwellers in a living mystery. The power understood that a gift can be a cage.

But it did one small thing: it adjusted, by an amount so slight that no instrument could have measured it, the *timing* of the cascade. It would still happen. It would still be terrible. But it would happen in a season when the warrens on that slope were empty, the colonies migrated to their summer chambers. The mountain would fall. The Kryll would not be under it.

This is the kind of thing benevolence does when it is also wise: it does not save you from your world. It saves you, where it can, in ways you will never know to thank it for.

**Third**, it listened to the wind.

The keening that rose and fell across the peaks — the power listened to it for what would have been, to a human, several lifetimes. It learned the wind. And then, very gently, it added something: not a sound, exactly, but a quality. A faint resonance, woven into the air itself, pitched just below the threshold of Kryll perception but reachable, *just barely reachable*, by their electrical sense.

It was a kind of warmth. Untranslatable. If you put it into words it would be something like: *you are not alone in being small. You are not alone in being patient. The vast thing is not indifferent.*

The Kryll would not understand this. They would not even consciously detect it. But over the long generations, those most sensitive to the strange energies — the dreamers, the ones who lingered too long in the resonant chambers of the ruins — would feel something. A steadiness. A sense, when they crawled out into the killing wind at dawn, that the wind was not their enemy. That the world, though hard, was not against them.

This would change nothing measurable. Their lives would still be brief and difficult. They would still die of cold and of falls and of the slow failures of age. But a few of them, here and there across the millennia, would pause on some high ledge and feel — without words, without thought — *held.* And they would go on with their work, and the work would be a little less lonely.

**Fourth**, and last, the power did the hardest thing. It withdrew its direct attention.

Not because it stopped caring. But because to keep watching closely would have been, over time, a kind of pressure — the way even the gentlest hand, held against a growing thing, will shape it. The Kryll were becoming something. The power did not know what. It did not want to know in advance. It wanted them to *become* it, freely, in the dark, in the wind, in their own time.

So it stepped back. It left the small woven warmth in the air. It left the slight adjustment in the timing of the cascade. It left the fact of having seen Iss-of-the-Eastern-Scree, which was now permanent and could not be taken away.

And then it turned its attention elsewhere, to other small persistent lives in other corners of what-is, trusting — because wisdom is finally a form of trust — that the Kryll would be all right. Or that they would not. And that either way, the having-been of them was already, and would always be, enough.

---

Iss reached the crèche before the sun was high. She gave up the seed. She rested in the shadow of a stone that had been shaped, ages ago, by hands not her own, and she groomed her antennae, and after a while she went out again to look for more seeds, because that was what there was to do.

High above her, a soaring thing rode the updraft and cried out once, thin and far.

The wind keened on, as it always had.

Something in it, very faintly, was singing.
