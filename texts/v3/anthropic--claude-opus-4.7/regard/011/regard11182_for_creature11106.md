# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent power arrives the way fog arrives—not from any particular direction, simply present where it was not present before. It has crossed distances that are not distances to attend to this place. It has shape because attention requires shape; it wears the suggestion of a body the way the cliff-dwellers wear oilcloth, as an outermost layer, a concession to the medium it is moving through.

It begins, as the wise always do, by watching.

---

## The First Long Look

It watches for what would be, in human reckoning, several years. This is not hesitation. It is the basic courtesy that wisdom owes to anything complicated.

It watches the midwives test the grip-reflex and understands immediately what it is seeing, and feels the first thing it will feel here, which is something like grief, and the second thing, which is something like respect, because the grief is its own and the respect is owed to the people who have built a life in which this test is necessary and who do not pretend otherwise.

It watches a cable-minder walk a route at dawn, checking tension with a small hammer, listening to the sound each line gives back. It watches her stop at one particular junction and stand for a long time, head tilted, before making a mark in chalk and moving on. It follows her chalk-mark through three weeks of small adjustments by other hands until a repair is made that prevents a catwalk from failing in the next hard freeze. It sees that no one thanked her and no one needed to. The cable held. That was the thanks.

It watches the elders ride the lift-cage to the floor that should not exist. It could go with them. It does not. Some thresholds are owed their privacy even from benevolence—*especially* from benevolence, which has a tendency to trample what it means to bless.

It watches a child fall. It watches the meal afterward. It listens to the parents speak of the strength of the child's grip, and it understands—this is the moment of understanding that changes what it will do—that to remove the drop from this place would be to remove the people from themselves. They are not victims of the cliff. They are its fluent speakers. The drop is the grammar in which they have learned to say *I love you*, *hold fast*, *you were here*.

It revises, in that moment, every intervention it had been considering.

---

## What It Does Not Do

It does not flatten the mountain. It had considered this, in the way wise powers consider obvious things in order to rule them out. A valley would be kinder in one register and a desecration in every other. The cliff-dwellers did not come here because there was nowhere else. Or rather—some of them did, generations ago, but the ones who live here now stay because the cliff is the shape their souls have grown into. A flat world would leave them with hands that grip nothing, a vocabulary for phenomena that no longer exist, a *verthan* with no drop to be calm above.

It does not warm the air or lift the fog or coax the sun to linger. The gray is the light they read by. The fog is the clock they keep. To brighten this place would be to erase the drip-song, which is to erase the ear that hears the drip-song, which is to erase the people.

It does not reveal the writing in the cable routes. It has read the writing—it took only a moment, the way reading takes a moment for a power of this kind—and what is written there is not a secret being kept from the cliff-dwellers. It is a slow conversation the city is having with itself, and the cliff-dwellers are participating in it whether they know the syntax or not. To translate it for them would be to interrupt the conversation in order to summarize it, which is the particular violence that explanation does to mystery.

It does not approach the thing in the deep fog. The thing in the deep fog is not the power's business. The power recognizes, with something like collegial nod, that another order of being lives here, older or stranger or simply *other*, and has its own relationship with the cliff-dwellers, and has not asked for help.

It does not show itself. Revelation is a kind of weight, and the catwalks are already carrying enough.

---

## What It Does

**The small repairs.** It begins here, because beginning small is the discipline that prevents power from becoming weather. In the lower reaches, where the newcomers live, there is a pump that has been failing for eleven years—not failing dramatically, just failing slightly, leaking a little more each season, drawing a little more current, costing a little more to maintain than it should. No one has the time to replace it because nothing about it is urgent enough. The power, passing through, adjusts a single component at the molecular level. The pump runs true. No one notices for a long time. Eventually a maintenance-log will show that a line item has dropped off the recurring repair list, and a clerk will frown at it for a moment, and then move on.

It does this perhaps two hundred times across the city. A bolt that was about to shear. A seal that was about to fail in the cold. A cable strand, deep in a bundle, that had developed a flaw no inspection would have caught until after the break. None of these would have killed anyone immediately. Several of them, over the next forty years, would have. The forty years will pass and the deaths will not occur and no one will know they did not occur, which is the cleanest shape that help can take.

**The hand on the small of the back.** There is a pipe-fitter in the middle tiers, a woman of forty, who has been carrying a grief for six years that she has not set down. Her partner fell. She watched it. She has continued to work and to eat and to attend the meals when others fall, and she has done all of this correctly, and she is dying inside the correctness. The power does not speak to her. It does not appear to her. It simply arranges, one gray afternoon, for her to be on a particular catwalk at a particular moment when the fog parts in a particular way and the light falls on a particular patch of moss that is, improbably, flowering—small white flowers, a species that blooms perhaps once in a decade when conditions align. She stops. She looks. She cries, finally, the way she has not let herself cry. A cable-minder passing the other way sees her crying and does the thing the cliff-dwellers do, which is to stand close enough that words would not have to be loud, and waits, and does not ask, and after a while the pipe-fitter says something, and the cable-minder says something back, and they walk together to the next platform.

The power did not make the moss flower. The moss was going to flower. The power only made sure she was there to see it. This is most of what benevolence is, when it is done well: the arrangement of attendance.

**The child who would have been born without the reflex.** There is one, two years into the watching. The power knows before the midwives know. It considers, for what would be a long time in human terms and is no time at all in its own, whether to intervene. To give the child the grip would be to override the cliff's own selection, to begin a precedent it cannot finish, to start being the kind of power that decides who lives here. It does not do this. But it does something else: it ensures that the child, in the short time the child has, is warm, and is held, and does not suffer. The fog takes what it takes. The power cannot change this without changing everything. But it can sit with the parents, invisibly, through the night, and it does, and in the morning the mother will say to the father that she felt, somehow, that they were not alone, and the father will say he felt it too, and they will not know what to do with this feeling, and they will fold it into their grief, and the grief will be very slightly more bearable than it would have been. This is not much. The power knows it is not much. It does it anyway, because *not much* is what is on offer, and refusing to offer *not much* on the grounds that it is not much is one of the failure modes of power.

**The lift-cage.** Once, only once, the power rides the lift-cage to the floor that should not exist. Not to investigate. To pay respects. It steps off briefly into a district that is and is not there, where the elders are mid-discussion, and it does not interrupt, and the elders do not see it exactly but one of them—the oldest, a woman whose hands are stained so many colors they have become a single color that has no name—pauses mid-sentence and looks toward the doorway and says nothing and then resumes. After the meeting she will tell no one. But she will know that someone came, and was courteous, and left, and she will feel something she has not felt in sixty years, which is the sense of being a small part of a larger hospitality, of belonging to a network of care that extends past the cliff. She will carry this for the rest of her life and she will not name it and it will inflect every small adjustment she suggests in her remaining years, and the city will be very slightly kinder for it, and no one will know why.

**The seed of an idea.** The power notices, in its long watching, that the cliff-dwellers have never quite developed a written form of the drip-song. It is passed mouth to mouth, ear to ear, generation to generation, and this is part of its life—but the power can see, in the way powers see, that a particular bad winter is coming, perhaps eighty years out, in which an unusually high number of elders will be lost and a significant fraction of the tonal vocabulary will be at risk. The power does not give anyone the idea of writing it down. That would be intervention in the wrong register. But it does arrange, gently, for two children in different districts to befriend each other—children who would not otherwise have met, whose parents will be mildly surprised at the friendship—and these children will both have, independently, an interest in patterns and in marks, and they will spend their adolescence developing a notation system as a private game, and it will become a real thing in their adulthood, and by the time the bad winter comes the notation will be widespread enough that the vocabulary will survive. None of this will be traceable to the power. The children will think they invented it. They will be right. The power only made sure they met.

**The thing in the deep fog.** The power does, eventually, acknowledge the thing in the deep fog. Not by approaching it. By a gesture that the thing will understand and that cannot be described in any human language, a kind of *I see you, I am passing through, I will not disturb your work*. The thing returns the gesture. They have an understanding. The cliff-dwellers will never know about this understanding and they do not need to. It will simply mean that the thing in the deep fog continues to do whatever it is doing, and the power does not impede it, and a certain kind of catastrophe that could have occurred—the thing being startled, or misread by some other intruding force, or driven from the cliff—will not occur. The cliff-dwellers will continue to leave their offerings and receive their cryptic responses and the relationship will continue, and they will not know that it was, for a moment, watched over by something that decided to leave it alone.

---

## The Question of Verthan

The power thinks about *verthan* longest. It is the thing it most wants to give the cliff-dwellers more of, and it is the thing it most clearly cannot give them at all, because *verthan* is not a gift. *Verthan* is what is left when everything that could be given has been refused or relinquished or never offered in the first place. It is the calm of those who have understood that they are held by something that does not bargain.

The power realizes, with something like its own version of *verthan*, that the cliff-dwellers have arrived at a state it can only approximate. They have made peace with finitude in a way that no power, by definition, has had to. They are its teachers in this, not its students. It bows—not visibly, not in any way they could perceive, but in the orientation of its attention—it bows to them.

This is perhaps the most important thing it does. It does not condescend to the Clinging City. It does not regard the cliff-dwellers as small or as pitiable or as in need of rescue from their condition. It regards them as fellow practitioners of an art that the universe is engaged in everywhere, which is the art of holding on to something while knowing you will not hold on forever, and doing it with grace, and teaching your children to do it, and feeding each other afterward.

---

## The Leaving

When it leaves—and it does leave, because staying would eventually become a kind of weather, and weather is exactly what this place does not need more of—it leaves the way it came. Not from any particular direction. Simply no longer present where it had been present.

It leaves behind:

- Two hundred small repairs that will never be noticed.
- One woman who cried on a catwalk and is no longer dying inside her correctness.
- One set of parents who folded an unnameable comfort into their grief.
- One elder who knows she was visited and will never say so.
- Two children, not yet born, who will become friends and will invent a notation.
- One understanding with the thing in the deep fog.
- No monuments. No revelations. No interruptions to *verthan*.

And it carries away with it something it did not have when it arrived, which is the memory of a place where beings with hands that grip have built a life on a vertical mile of ancient stone and have learned to call it home, and have learned to teach their children to call it home, and have learned to feed each other after the falls, and have learned to read the fog by its sound on tin, and have learned, somehow, without anyone designing it, to govern themselves through the slow accumulation of small adjustments suggested by elders who ride a lift-cage to a floor that should not exist.

The power has seen many worlds. It has seen many cities. It will see many more. It will remember this one. Not because it was the most beautiful or the most tragic or the most in need. Because it was the one that taught it, again, the lesson that wise powers must keep learning and keep almost forgetting and keep learning again:

*Most of what love does, when it is done well, is to leave a thing more itself than it found it.*

The lifts continue to rise and descend. The drip-song shifts pitch as the night settles in. The cliff-dwellers do not look down, because they do not need to. They hold on. The stone endures. The fog takes what it takes.

And somewhere, very far away, a power that briefly attended to them moves on, carrying their grammar in its attention like a small flame cupped against the wind.
