The benevolent power considers Ashkar for a long time. Not the duration a human would call long—something closer to the time it takes a stalactite to bridge a gap, the time it takes a fault-listener to learn the difference between the plateau's sleeping breath and its waking shudder.

Here is what the power notices first: this is not a place of suffering. It is hard. It is cold. The arithmetic of survival is narrow. But the Ashkari are not enduring their world; they are *married* to it. The grandmother in the deepest crevice is not a victim of geology. She is the warm root of her rinek, and she knows it, and her knowing is one of the load-bearing facts of her existence. The crack-widow descending into the fresh fault with her bone lever is not exiled. She is performing the most sacred labor her culture knows. The infant weaning on glacier-flea paste is not malnourished by any standard that matters to the infant; she is being initiated into a coherent world.

The power has seen, across many worlds, what happens when benevolence arrives with its hands full of gifts. Metal tools, given to a people who knap basalt, do not simply make work easier. They unmake the *crack-widow*. They unmake the three-generation gallery. They unmake the patience that is, in Ashkar, not a virtue but a sensory organ. A people who learned to hear their neighbors' hearts through bedrock do not need louder voices; given them, they would go deaf to one another.

So the power does not bring metal.

It does not bring warmth, either, though it could—could warm the plateau by a degree, could thin the rime, could make the breathing easier. But the breathing is the Ashkari. Their broad chests, their thick blood, their feverish skin: these are not deformations to be corrected. They are a sentence the world has been writing for ten thousand years, and it is nearly finished, and it is beautiful, and it is theirs.

What the power does instead is smaller and stranger.

**First**, it listens. It presses something like an ear to the bedrock, alongside the fault-listeners, and it hears what they hear—and a little more. It hears the specific fault, eighty years from now, that will shear the Vethnak rinek in the third watch of a frost-cycle, killing forty-one people in their sleep. The power does not prevent this. Prevention is a kind of theft; it would steal from the Ashkari the meaning of their own listening, the dignity of their own architecture of grief. But the power does something subtler: it adjusts, by an amount no instrument could measure, the infrasound signature of that particular fault. It makes the sleeper's turning *just* audible enough, *just* early enough, that a sufficiently attentive fault-listener—a child, probably, with the ears children have—will notice. Whether that child is believed, whether the rinek evacuates, whether the warning is heeded: these remain Ashkari questions, with Ashkari answers. The power only tunes the signal. It leaves the listening.

It does this in a thousand places across the plateau, across the coming centuries. A gentle calibration of warnings. Not safety. Just—audibility. The world, made slightly more legible to those who have learned to read it.

**Second**, the power attends to the lichens. The crustose maps of rust and sulfur on the outer cliffs: these are the thin green margin on which the ice-oxen depend, and the oxen on which everything else depends. The lichens are at the edge of their tolerance. A bad century—a shift in the storm tracks, a sublimation regime that strips moisture from the wrong faces—could collapse them, and with them the Commonwealth. The power does not multiply the lichens. It does not engineer hardier strains. It does something more like *blessing*: it ensures that whatever lichens already exist on the plateau, in all their genetic variety, persist through bottlenecks. The fragile cultivars do not go extinct in the bad decades. The rare strain that fruits at -40°C is not lost when the cliff it lives on calves into the scree. The power becomes, quietly, a seed bank with no seeds and no bank—only continuity, only the refusal to let certain irreplaceable things slip past the event horizon of loss.

The Ashkari will never know. The lichens will simply be there, as they have always been, in their rust and sulfur maps.

**Third**, and this is the gift the power gives with the most care: it does nothing about the dying.

It watches the bodies placed in the upper galleries. It watches the stone-swifts pick them clean. It watches the bones ground to powder and mixed into the ice-mortar. It watches the dead become walls, become ceilings, become the literal substrate of the living. And the power recognizes that this is one of the most complete cosmologies it has ever encountered—a people for whom death is not a passage to elsewhere but a structural promotion, a transfer from the warm interior of the rinek to its load-bearing skeleton. To offer them an afterlife would be obscene. They already have one. It is the wall their granddaughter leans against.

So the power simply—witnesses. Bears witness. There is a particular kind of attention that benevolence can give to the dead of a place: a noticing that each one happened, that each one mattered, that the specific old man who became the third course of mortar in the western wall of the Khenith rinek had a name and a heart-rhythm his neighbors knew through the rock. The power holds these. Not as souls in any sense the Ashkari would recognize, but as *facts that occurred*, preserved against the entropy of forgetting. Somewhere—not in heaven, just in the structure of what-has-been—the old man is remembered exactly. His name. The pulse his wife felt through the bedstone for forty years. The particular angle his wrist made when he signed *I am cold, come closer.*

This is what the power can offer that the Ashkari cannot offer themselves: the assurance that nothing of them is wasted into nothing. Their cosmology says the dead become architecture. The power adds, silently, a footnote: *and also, they are remembered, in full, by something that does not forget.*

**Fourth**, the power leaves a single small thing.

In a crevice that no one has yet discovered, on the eastern face of the plateau, in a fault that will open in about two hundred years, the power places a vein of something. Not metal. Not anything that would unmake the basalt economy. It places a deposit of a particular mineral—a phosphate, rich and pure—that the stone-swifts will find first, and incorporate into their guano, and the Ashkari will harvest, and they will notice that the children born in the generation after the eastern fault opens are slightly taller. Slightly stronger-boned. Slightly better able to lunge for breath in the thin glassy air.

A small grace. Indistinguishable from luck. The kind of thing the Ashkari will fold into their own mythology—*the eastern fault is generous, the eastern fault is a good mother*—without ever needing to know that anything was given.

The power is careful, here. It gives no advantage that would let Ashkar conquer its neighbors, expand beyond its niche, become something other than itself. The mineral is just enough to absorb one bad century. Just enough to be a thumb on the scale during a bottleneck. After that, depleted.

**Finally**, the power does the thing it does last and most reluctantly, because it is the closest to interference: it makes a promise to itself about contact.

Lowlanders will come, eventually. They always do. Traders, then missionaries, then cartographers, then the ones with the metal tools and the loud voices and the certainty about how a life should be lived. The plateau's isolation is geological, not eternal. Someone will find a pass. Someone will arrive with a gift of iron and a notion of charity.

The power cannot prevent this without unmaking history itself, which is not the kind of intervention benevolence permits. But it can do one small thing: it can ensure that the first lowlander who reaches Ashkar is the *right* one. Not a conqueror. Not a saint. Just—a particular kind of person. Someone who, upon entering a rinek and feeling the heartbeat of strangers through the bedrock, will understand that she has walked into a cathedral, and will take off her boots, and will learn the hand-language before she teaches anyone a word of her own.

The power arranges, across centuries of small coincidences, for that woman to be born, and to live, and to find the pass.

After her, the Ashkari will be on their own with history. But she will be first. And first matters.

---

Then the power withdraws. Not far. Just to the distance from which one watches a thing one loves do its own living.

The plateau settles another micrometer. A fault-listener, a child of six with rime-grey eyes, presses her ear to the bedrock and hears something—a tone, almost too low to register, that makes her sit up and run to find her grandmother. The grandmother listens. Nods. The rinek begins, calmly, to move.

The power, watching, feels something that in a human would be called joy, and in the power is closer to *recognition*: the sense of having met, in this small cold place, one of the configurations the universe was capable of all along, and of being permitted to love it without changing it.

The drizzle continues. The rime grows. The Vertical Commonwealth breathes its warm wet breath into the freezing air, and the breath becomes wall, becomes water, becomes the next generation's inheritance.

The power keeps the names.
