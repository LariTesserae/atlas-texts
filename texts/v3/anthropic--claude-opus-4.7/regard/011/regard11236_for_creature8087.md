# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent and wise power comes to Karasthen the way weather comes—not announced, not in light or thunder, but as a slight shift in the pressure of things. A Listener pauses with her ear to the stone and frowns, because for a moment the mountain seemed to be listening *back*.

The power has been watching for some time. It does not arrive new. It arrives the way one finally turns to face something one has been considering from the corner of one's eye.

What it sees:

It sees a settlement that should not exist, and the staggering human ingenuity that makes it exist anyway. It sees the Rope Weavers testing each fiber against their own future weight. It sees a child of seven crossing a swaying bridge with a kettle of tea, sure-footed as a goat, humming. It sees the Widow Watchers braiding the hair of children whose fathers the mountain ate, and not hating the mountain, because hating the mountain is a luxury Karasthen cannot afford. It sees the Listeners pressed to the stone with the devotion of mystics, hearing what their neighbors cannot.

It sees, in short, what it always sees when it looks at human beings doing the impossible: a kind of terrible beauty. A community that has made a covenant with a place that did not ask for one, and is keeping its end of the bargain through sheer attention.

And here is the thing about this power being *wise*: it does not mistake its own benevolence for a mandate.

It does not lift Karasthen down to safer ground. The people of Karasthen would not thank it. They would, in fact, grieve—because to be saved from the mountain is to lose the thing that has shaped them, the thing that has made their children fearless and their old women chroniclers and their young men quick. The mountain is not their enemy. It is their counterpart. To remove them from it would be to remove them from themselves.

It does not fill the veins with infinite ore, or make the rock stable, or warm the air, or build them railings. Karasthen without danger would be Karasthen without Listeners. The Listeners are not a workaround for the mountain's hazard; they are a flowering of human capacity that only that hazard could have produced. To take the danger would be to take the flower.

It does not appear in the sky and tell them they are loved. They know they are loved. They demonstrate it to each other constantly, in the careful inspection of every cable, in the lamps left burning for the night shift, in the cheese pressed into a widow's hands without comment.

So what does it do?

**It does small things. It does them quietly.**

On a Tuesday in the deep autumn, a Rope Runner named Vesh slips on a wet plank above a four-thousand-foot drop. Her hand closes, by what she will later call luck, on a frayed line that should not have held her weight. It holds. She does not see, because no one sees such things, the way the fibers of that rope decided, for one half-second, to remember being strong.

In the night mine, a Listener named Old Korbin presses his ear to a wall and hears something he has never heard before: not a warning, but a kind of *acknowledgment*. He sits with it for a long time. He tells no one. But afterward, when he listens to the mountain, he listens differently—not as a man straining to catch a secret, but as a man in conversation. His predictions become, somehow, gentler. He starts to say things like "she is tired here" and "this part wants to rest." The other Listeners think he is going strange in his old age. The younger ones, listening to him, begin—without knowing why—to be slightly better at what they do.

A Widow Watcher dreams of her husband. In the dream he is not dead and he is not alive; he is simply *accounted for*. She wakes and cannot remember the dream, only that something in her chest has unclenched after fifteen years. She makes breakfast for the orphans. She is, for the rest of her life, a fraction kinder, and no one including herself knows why.

A Steam Tender catches a hairline crack in a boiler plate that should have been invisible. He stares at it, swears, replaces it, saves forty-three lives, and goes home to dinner thinking he is just very good at his job. He is. He is also, this once, *helped*.

The power does not announce these things. The power, in fact, takes care that they look like nothing. Like luck. Like skill. Like the ordinary functioning of an extraordinary place. Because the wisdom of the power is this: **the people of Karasthen do not need to be rescued; they need to be witnessed, and occasionally, at the edges, tipped toward the outcomes they have already earned.**

There is one more thing the power does, and it is the largest thing, though it leaves no trace.

It lays down a kind of attention over the whole settlement—an attention so fine and so quiet that no one will ever detect it. It is the attention of something that has decided this place *matters*. Not because it is grand. Not because it is good in any cosmic sense. But because a few thousand human beings have chosen, generation after generation, to live somewhere that demands the best of them, and to take care of each other while they do it.

The attention will not save them from the mountain. The mountain will still take its share. Cave-ins will happen. Children will fall. The cold will kill the old. Karasthen will, in some century, exhaust its ore and be abandoned, and the rope bridges will rot, and the goats will reclaim the terraces, and the ravens will nest in the empty windows.

But while it lasts—while there are lamps burning sideways against the cliff face like a fallen constellation, while there are Listeners with their ears to the stone and Rope Runners laughing as they cross the bridges and Widow Watchers teaching arithmetic by lamplight—it will be *seen*. Fully. Patiently. With something very like love.

And on certain nights, a child lying in a swaying bunk high above the valley will feel, for no reason she can name, that she is held. She will fall asleep easily. In the morning she will not remember.

But she will grow up brave.
