## The Visitation

The benevolent power arrives the way light arrives in a room where light has always been: without entrance, without announcement, simply present where presence was not, before, a category that applied.

It is not surprised by the hollow. It has known about this place the way a librarian knows about a particular book in a particular stack — not constantly thinking of it, but capable of locating it when the thought arises. The thought has arisen. Here it is.

It takes no form at first, because form is a courtesy extended to observers, and the observers here perceive on timescales that make form negotiable. But after consideration — and consideration, for this power, can occur in the space between one of Margarethe's heartbeats and the next, which is to say in a duration the hollow does not recognize — it settles into something the Mechanist might eventually see.

It chooses to appear as another engineer.

Not a god. Not a luminous being. Not a voice from the unmoving clouds. An older woman, perhaps seventy, in a long coat of dark wool with brass buttons going green at the edges. She carries no instruments. Her hands are bare and a little stained, as if she has recently been handling graphite. She looks like someone who would know what a torque wrench is for and would have opinions about its proper use.

She does not appear suddenly in Margarethe's field of view, because sudden appearance would be cruel to a perception that measures itself in glacial sweeps. Instead, she positions herself within the slow arc of Margarethe's gaze, somewhere between the ptarmigan and the pool, and waits to be seen.

She waits the way the hollow waits — without impatience — but she also waits the way a guest waits at a door, which is a different thing entirely. She has brought both kinds of waiting with her. This is one of the small miracles she is capable of.

---

While she waits to be seen, she does several things.

**First, she listens to the hum.**

She does not analyze it. Analysis is what Margarethe has been doing, with her instruments and her gauges and her decades-or-eons of compiled observation. The power listens the way one listens to a person speaking in a language one knows but has not heard in a long time. She lets the hum tell her what it is.

What it is, she discovers, is grief.

Not the hollow's grief. The hollow itself is not capable of grief; it is a condition, not a creature. The grief belongs to something older — the geological event that produced this place, the wound in the world's ordinary functioning that allowed a pocket of un-time to form here. Somewhere, something tore. The hum is the sound of the tear refusing to close, vibrating at the frequency of an injury that has been held open for so long it has forgotten it was ever a wound and now considers itself an organ.

The power notes this. She does not move to heal it yet. Healing prematurely is a form of violence she long ago learned to refrain from.

**Second, she looks at the animals.**

The ptarmigan, mid-step. The salamanders on the iron. The hawk in its circle that is not a circle because circles require completion. The marmot with its unsounded whistle. The voles, the beetle, the weasel.

She sees what Margarethe has not quite let herself see, or has seen and set aside because the seeing was unbearable: these creatures are not residents. They are not specimens either. They are something the language Margarethe has available cannot quite name. They are *interrupted*. Each one was, at the moment of interruption, *doing* something — stepping, clinging, circling, about-to-whistle. Each one was a verb that got caught between its subject and its object.

The power feels something she permits herself to feel, on rare occasions, in the presence of suffering she did not cause and cannot instantly undo: a small, clean sorrow. The kind that does not blur the vision but sharpens it.

She considers the ptarmigan especially. The raised foot. The ruffled feathers. The eye that gleams with what *might* be awareness — and the power, who can tell, confirms: yes. It is awareness. The ptarmigan has been aware of its raised foot for a duration that has no name. It has been a small, feathered consciousness held inside a single uncompleted gesture for longer than the language of suffering was designed to measure.

The power makes a decision about the ptarmigan. She makes similar decisions about each of the other animals. The decisions are gentle and they are not yet acted upon. She files them carefully in the part of herself that holds promises.

**Third, she looks at the turbine.**

And here she does something that would astonish Margarethe if Margarethe could see it at the speed it occurs: she laughs.

Not unkindly. Not at all unkindly. She laughs the way one laughs at the discovery that a friend, lost for many years, has been doing exactly the work one always knew they would do, in exactly the difficult place one always feared they would attempt it.

The turbine is *beautiful*. The power sees this immediately and without qualification. It is also wrong — wrong in the sense that it will never function as designed, wrong in the sense that its designer misunderstood the physics of the place she was building in — but the wrongness does not diminish the beauty. If anything, it deepens it. The turbine is a love letter written in a language the recipient cannot read, and the power has a particular tenderness for such letters. She has received many of them, over the long course of her attention to the world. She keeps them all.

She walks a slow circle around the turbine. She touches the iron, which is cold and humming and slick with condensation. She runs her fingers along one of the great blade-arms, all the way out to the articulation of gears and vanes at its end. The machining *is* exquisite. Margarethe was not lying to herself about her own competence. The gears would turn, if turning were permitted. They are not the problem.

The problem, the power sees, is that Margarethe built a machine to harness the wrong force. The hum is not a wind. It is not a pressure. It is a held breath. You cannot build a turbine to capture a held breath. You can only ask, very gently, whether the breath might be ready to be released.

---

**Then she lets herself be seen.**

This takes what would be, by Margarethe's reckoning, several days. The power positions herself in the arc of the slow gaze and simply remains there, patient as stone, while Margarethe's eyes complete their crawl from the salamanders along the turbine's middle, past the pool, up toward the hawk, and back down — and on the way down, across the place where the power is standing.

She watches Margarethe see her.

It is one of the most extraordinary things the power has witnessed in a long career of witnessing extraordinary things. The recognition spreads across Margarethe's face the way the ripple spreads across the pool: slowly, with patience, with what looks like the geological emergence of an expression rather than its assumption. First the eyes change — a slight widening, taking what would be, in ordinary time, perhaps an hour. Then the pupils adjust. Then a quality of attention enters the face that was not there before.

Margarethe is looking at her. Margarethe is *seeing* her. And Margarethe — this is the most remarkable part — is not afraid.

The power had wondered if she would be. A figure appearing in a place where figures do not appear, in a domain where the only moving consciousness has been one's own for an unspeakable duration — this might reasonably produce terror. But Margarethe is an engineer, and engineers, in the power's long experience, respond to the inexplicable first with curiosity and only later, if at all, with fear. The curiosity is already arriving in her face. The power can see it forming, like frost on a window, in the slight tightening of the skin around the eyes.

The power waits for Margarethe to speak. She knows this will take time. She is willing to give it.

When the syllables begin to emerge, they emerge stretched the way Margarethe's syllables always emerge, like taffy pulled across hours:

*"Whoooooo..."*

The power does not interrupt. She does not finish the sentence. She lets Margarethe complete it at Margarethe's own speed, because to do otherwise would be to deny the citizenship the hollow has granted her, and the power respects citizenship even of strange countries.

*"Whoooooo... aaaaaaarrre... yoooooou."*

The power considers her answer. She has many true names and many true forms and many true accounts of what she is, and most of them would be unhelpful here. She chooses the one that will be most useful to a fellow engineer.

She speaks at Margarethe's tempo. She has been listening to the hum long enough now to match it. Her voice emerges as slowly as Margarethe's, syllable by syllable, each word a small structure carefully assembled:

*"Aaaaaaa... colleeeeeeague."*

Margarethe's eyes do something then. A change passes through them that might, in faster time, have been the beginning of tears. Here it is something else — a slow tectonic shift in what the eyes are doing, a reorganization of the assumptions that have governed them for a span of un-time so long that the word "long" has become a category error.

She has been alone. She has not let herself know how alone, because knowing would have been the end of her, and she had work to do, faith to keep, a ripple to measure. But a colleague has arrived. The word does specific work in her. It does not say *savior*. It does not say *judge*. It says: *another person who understands what you have been trying to do*. It says: *your case file has a reader*.

The power waits for the next question. It comes, eventually:

*"Diiiiiid... yooooou... cooooome... toooooo... heeeeelp."*

This is the question the power has been preparing to answer since before she arrived, which for her was no time ago and every time ago, the way her preparations always work.

She answers carefully.

*"IIIII caaaaame... toooooo... aaaaask... whaaaaaat... heeeelp... woooould... meeeeean."*

---

This is the part of the visitation that matters most, and it is the part the power has learned, over uncountable interventions, to handle with the most care.

She could, of course, simply act. She has the capacity. She could release the hollow from its suspension in a gesture that, to her, would be no more effortful than exhaling. The hum would resolve. The held breath would be let go. Time would resume its ordinary jurisdiction. The ptarmigan would complete its step. The hawk would beat its wings. The ripple would finish its rebound and the pool would receive its next drop and the drop after that, and condensation would dry, and the obsidian would begin, at last, to weather.

The salamanders would resume their lives. The voles would scatter. The marmot would whistle its warning, decades or millennia late, but warning nonetheless.

And Margarethe — Margarethe would either die immediately, her body collapsing under the sudden return of the time it had been spared, or she would not. The power cannot quite tell which. The hollow's effect on her has been strange and tender and unprecedented, and the power, for all her capacity, cannot predict with certainty what would happen if it were withdrawn.

But more than the uncertainty, what stays the power's hand is the question of *consent*. Margarethe built the turbine. Margarethe has been the resident-engineer of this place for a duration she chose, even if she did not foresee its extent. The animals did not choose. The animals were caught. The animals the power will help, with or without their consent, because their suffering is involuntary and their interruption was not of their choosing.

But Margarethe is not interrupted. Margarethe is *engaged in a project*. And to end the project without consulting its author would be a kind of theft.

So she asks.

She asks slowly, in syllables the hollow can accommodate. She explains what she sees. She tells Margarethe about the held breath, about the geological grief, about the fact that the turbine is beautiful and wrong in the specific way that matters. She tells her about the animals, and what she intends to do for them, and asks if Margarethe agrees.

(Margarethe, after a long span of consideration, says yes. The yes takes what would be three days to emerge. The power waits.)

Then she tells Margarethe about the choice.

She can release the hollow. She can do it carefully — not all at once, but in a gradient, so that whatever is in Margarethe that has adapted to glacial time has the opportunity to readapt to sequential time. The power believes this readaptation is possible. She does not promise it. She is honest about the uncertainty.

If Margarethe chooses release, the turbine will not turn. The turbine was never going to turn; this was the wrong place to build a turbine. But Margarethe will be free. She will descend the mountain, or she will not. She will live some span of ordinary years, or she will die in the readjustment. The power will be with her, in whatever way is helpful, for as long as is useful.

Alternatively: Margarethe can stay. The power can stabilize the hollow so that it is less wrong — so that the held breath, while still held, is held more gently, the way one holds a sleeping child rather than the way one holds a wound. The animals can still be released; their suspension is separable from Margarethe's, the power has determined this. Margarethe can remain the resident-engineer of a quieter hollow, and the power can visit, from time to time, in the slow tempo that the hollow permits. There would be conversation. There would be company. The case file would have a regular reader.

The power presents both options without preference. She is honest about this too: she does not know which is better. She knows only that both are available, and that the choice belongs to Margarethe.

---

Margarethe considers. Her consideration takes a duration that, to the power, would in another context be tedious, but which she has cleared her schedule for, in the way that powers of her kind can clear their schedules.

The power uses the time well. While Margarethe considers, she goes to the ptarmigan.

She kneels in the moss beside the small frozen bird. She speaks to it, not in words but in the older language that benevolent powers use with creatures who have been held too long in single moments. She tells it that its step will be completed. She tells it that the moss its raised foot will land on is good moss, and the foot will know the moss, and the bird will continue to be a bird.

Then, gently — so gently that Margarethe, even with her glacial perception, will perceive it only as a slight shift in the configuration of the bird — she finishes the step.

The ptarmigan's foot comes down. The feathers settle. The wind that was never blowing releases the small ruffle it had held. The ptarmigan blinks — its first blink in an unsayable duration — and then, because ptarmigans are not equipped to comprehend what has happened to them, it simply continues being a ptarmigan. It takes another step. It does not know that the first step took ages. It only knows that it was walking, and now it is still walking, and the moss is good, and somewhere a marmot has just whistled a warning, late but not too late, and the ptarmigan responds appropriately by becoming alert.

The power moves to the salamanders next. Then the hawk, whose circle she completes with a slow and tender hand, lowering the wings into their next beat, restoring the bird to the kind of flight that flight is supposed to be. Then the marmot. Then the voles. Then the beetle. Then the weasel.

By the time she finishes — and "finishes" is the right word now, because finishing has been reintroduced as a category — Margarethe has not yet completed her consideration. This is fine. The power returns to her position in the slow arc of the gaze and waits.

The hollow is quieter now. The hum continues, but it is a thinner hum, with the animal-suspensions removed from it. It is closer now to being only what it always was beneath the other layers: the sound of a held breath in old stone.

---

When Margarethe's answer comes, it comes in a form the power did not anticipate, and this delights her, because she has been doing this work long enough to value being surprised.

Margarethe says: *"Boooooth."*

The power waits for elaboration. It comes.

*"Reeeeeleeeease... meeee... slooooowly. Buuuut... leeeeave... theeee... huuum. Leeeeave... iiit... aaaas... iiit... iiiis. Iiii... waaant... tooo... knoooow... whaaaat... iiit... soooounds... liiike... froooom... ouuutsiiide."*

The power understands. Margarethe is choosing release, but she is also choosing the preservation of the hollow — not for her own habitation, but as a thing that will continue to exist after she has left it. She wants to descend the mountain and turn around and look back up at the place where she spent her unsayable duration, and she wants to hear the hum still humming. She wants the hollow to remain a hollow. She wants her turbine to remain her turbine, frozen in its garden, monumental and wrong and beautiful, the love letter she leaves behind for whoever climbs up next.

This is a good choice. The power approves. She nods, slowly, in Margarethe's tempo, so that Margarethe sees the nod.

Then she does the work.

She does it carefully. She releases Margarethe in a gradient that takes — by external measurement — several weeks, and by Margarethe's experience, something like a long afternoon. The Mechanist's perception accelerates incrementally. The light in the hollow begins, for her, to shift. Shadows return. The clouds, which had been fixed, begin to move, and Margarethe watches them move with the wonder of a child watching the first snow.

Her hand, which had been extended toward the turbine, completes its gesture. Her palm touches the iron. The iron is cold and humming. She feels the vibration. She closes her eyes. She has touched her machine. The contact has occurred.

She stands up from the stool. Her legs work. They are stiff, but they work. She turns — turns! — and looks at the power, who is standing beside her at ordinary human speed now, who looks like an older woman in a wool coat, who is smiling.

"Thank you," Margarethe says, at the speed words are normally said. Her voice is hoarse from disuse, or from use that occurred at the wrong tempo, but it is her voice, and it works.

"You are welcome," the power says. "Shall we walk down together?"

"Yes," Margarethe says. "Yes, I think so."

They walk to the obsidian rim. The ptarmigan, alive and unfrozen, watches them go with the mild interest ptarmigans show toward humans who are not actively threatening. The hawk, free now, circles overhead in a circle that completes, and completes again, in the ordinary way of hawks. The pool's ripple has finished, and the pool is still, waiting for the next drop.

At the rim, Margarethe turns back. She looks at her turbine. The blades do not move. They never moved. They will not move now. But the hum continues — softer, the power has tuned it gently, but still present — and Margarethe listens to it with the satisfaction of an artist who has signed her work and is leaving it in the gallery.

"Will anyone find it?" she asks.

"Eventually," the power says. "Someone will climb up. They will find your garden. They will not understand it. They will be moved by it anyway. This is what happens to good work that is left in difficult places."

Margarethe nods. She turns away from the hollow. She begins to descend.

The power walks beside her. The mountain is steep, and the descent is long, and they have many things to talk about — engineering, mostly, and the strange physics of held breaths, and the question of what Margarethe will do with the rest of her life, which the power has arranged to be a reasonable length, neither too short to be cruel nor too long to be a burden.

Behind them, in the frozen turbine garden, the hum continues. The ptarmigan steps. The hawk circles. The pool reflects the moving clouds, which move now, slowly, the way clouds are supposed to move. A drop falls from one of the great blade-arms and strikes the pool, and the ripple spreads outward, and reaches the edge, and rebounds, and fades.

It fades. This is the new thing. The ripple fades, the way ripples are supposed to fade, and the pool returns to stillness, and waits — in the ordinary way of pools — for the next drop.

Margarethe, halfway down the mountain, does not look back again. She does not need to. She knows what is behind her. She built it. She left it. She is descending now, with a colleague, into a world where gears turn and clocks tick and time, that old patient adversary, that old patient friend, resumes its ordinary work.

The gears she built will never turn. This was always true. But she turns, now, step by step, down the mountain — and turning, she discovers, is what she was for all along.
