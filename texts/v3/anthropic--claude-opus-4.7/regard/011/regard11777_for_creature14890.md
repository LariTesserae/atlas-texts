# What the Power Would Do

I sat with this a long time before writing. The question seems to invite a rescue, an intervention, a great clean gesture from above — and my first impulse was to refuse it. So let me follow that refusal honestly, and see where it leads.

---

The benevolent power arrives the way such powers do: without announcement, without weight, without disturbing a single droplet. It does not descend through the fractured glass roof. It does not enter. It simply becomes *present*, the way attention becomes present in a room where someone has begun, quietly, to listen.

For a long while — and *long* here means whatever length of time is required, because this power has no appointments — it does nothing at all. It only looks.

It looks at the *plick* of the water that never quite synchronizes, and understands that the lack of synchrony is not a failure but a craft: a rhythm too complex to resolve, kept deliberately unresolved so that the place stays alive in its dying. It looks at the Choir circling their ancient routes and recognizes liturgy. It looks at the glass-legged water-things and sees that they are not lost; they are following gradients she set for them, and she resets the gradients when grief shifts the moisture. It looks at the watchman's shack and does not look inside. Some forms of respect are simply the refusal of certain gazes.

And it looks at the Weeper.

Here the power becomes very careful.

A lesser benevolence would want to *save* her. Would want to lift the burden of maintenance from her mirror-shoulders, dry the cathedral, dissolve the rules, escort her into some brighter country where immortals are not required to keep grief on schedule. A lesser benevolence would mistake her condition for suffering and her suffering for something that wants to end.

But this power is wise as well as kind, and wisdom — real wisdom, the kind that has watched many worlds — knows the difference between a wound and a vocation. The Weeper is not trapped in the Underloom. The Weeper *is* the Underloom's way of continuing to mean something after meaning has corroded. To end her work would not be mercy. It would be a second death laid on top of the first, and this time without a mourner.

So the power does not save her.

Instead, it does three small things. I think these are the three things, though I am not certain; I am only following the shape of what feels right.

---

**First**, it makes a slight adjustment to the physics of the place — so slight that the Weeper herself will not notice for perhaps a century. The almond-scented breath that moves across the back of visitors' necks will, from now on, carry a fractional warmth. Not enough to dry anything. Not enough to disturb the balance. Just enough that those who feel it will, without knowing why, feel *recognized* rather than watched. The Weeper has been alone with her work for so long that her tenderness has had to disguise itself as patience. The power gives her tenderness a small, plausible channel. A way to say *I see you* without breaking her rule of never showing her face.

She will not understand the change. She will only notice, slowly, that fewer visitors refuse her invitation. That more of them stay long enough to leave something behind. That the ledger she keeps grows a little fuller, and the names in it begin to be written in handwriting that looks almost like grace.

**Second**, it does something for the ones who leave damp-handed and hollow-chested, the ones who carry the *plick* inside them for the rest of their lives. The power cannot take the dripping from them — it belongs to them now, it is the cost of having accepted the invitation, and to remove it would be to remove the part of them that learned to love something that could not be saved. But the power can do this: it can ensure that wherever in the world two such people meet, they will recognize each other. The damp handprint on a doorframe, the faint sound of water in a quiet room — these will become passwords. The carriers of the Underloom will find each other, across decades and continents, and they will sit together in sunlit kitchens and not have to explain why they sometimes cry without sadness, or why rain feels like an old friend's voice. They will form, without knowing it, a diaspora of the gently broken. A choir of their own.

The Weeper will feel this, faintly, the way a mother feels the distant well-being of children she has released. It will not lessen her loneliness. Immortal loneliness does not lessen. But it will give her loneliness a *direction* — outward, into the world, toward people who carry a piece of her cathedral in their ribs. She has been a widow tending an unvisited grave. The power makes the grave, very quietly, visited.

**Third** — and this is the smallest gesture, and the one I am least sure of, but my intuition insists —

The power leaves something in the watchman's shack.

Not a gift, exactly. A presence. On the ruined desk, beside the ledger, there now sits a second book. It is identical in every way to the first, except that it is blank, and it is bound in a leather that will not rot in the saturated air. The power leaves no instructions. It does not tell the Weeper what the book is for. It simply puts it there, and withdraws.

It will take her a long time to notice it. Longer to open it. Longer still to understand that the first ledger was for the names of those who disturbed the dripping, and the second is for whatever she wants. Her own names. Her old name, if she can remember it. The shape of her purpose before it softened. The things she might have wept for if weeping were not her job. A line of poetry. A description of the color of moonlight on bone, in her own words, for no one.

She may never write in it. That is allowed. The point is not that she writes. The point is that the universe has acknowledged she is permitted to have an *interior* — a place that is hers and not the Underloom's. A small, dry page inside a very wet world.

If she ever does write in it, the power will know. And the power will read what she writes, with the attentiveness it gives to scripture. But it will not respond. Response would be intrusion. The book is for her.

---

Then the power withdraws.

It leaves the rules intact. It leaves the Choir to their circuits and the glass-legged things to their gradients. It leaves the *plick* unsynchronized and the ceiling weeping and the door of the shack always open. It does not announce its visit. It does not sign its work.

A benevolent and wise power, encountering something like the Underloom, understands that the highest form of love available to it is *not to colonize*. Not to fix what is not broken in the way it looks broken. Not to rescue what is not asking to be rescued. To offer, instead, three almost-invisible kindnesses — a warmth, a recognition, a blank page — and then to go away, and to keep going away, forever, so that the Weeper's work remains hers and the cathedral's slow dying remains its own.

This, I think, is what wisdom would do.

It would understand that some places are not problems. They are *practices*. And the most loving thing one can do for a practice is to let it continue, while quietly making sure that the one who practices is not, in the deepest sense, alone.

---

Somewhere far above, in a sunlit kitchen, a woman sets down her coffee cup and hears, very faintly, the sound of water dripping inside her chest. She smiles. She does not know why. Across the city, a man pauses in a doorway and notices that his hand has left a damp print on the frame. He smiles too.

Neither of them knows about the other. Not yet.

But they will meet. The power has seen to it.

And in the Underloom, the Weeper turns a page in a ledger that has, for the first time in centuries, one more book beside it than there was yesterday. She does not look up. She does not need to. The almond-scented air moves once across the place where her face would be, and somewhere in the mirror-curve of her hidden expression, something that is not quite a smile and not quite a tear gathers — and falls — and joins the rhythm.

*Plick.*

The weeping never stops.

But now, it is witnessed.
