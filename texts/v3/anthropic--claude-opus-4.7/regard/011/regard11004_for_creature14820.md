# The Attention of a Benevolent Power

The power notices them on a Tuesday — though of course there are no Tuesdays in the hollow, only the cycle of weep and patch, weep and patch. The power notices them the way one notices a particular stone in a riverbed: not because it is the largest or the brightest, but because of the specific way it has chosen to hold itself against the current.

The power is old enough, and wise enough, to pause before acting.

---

This is the first thing the power does: **nothing.**

It watches for a long time. Long, in the way the power reckons it — which is to say, long enough to see three generations born, to see the eastern shanty-cluster collapse twice and be rebuilt three times, to learn the specific cadence of the whetstone in the hands of an old woman named (in their quiet, economical tongue) something that translates roughly as *She-Who-Knows-Where-The-Wall-Will-Fail*.

The power watches, and the power considers.

A lesser benevolence — a younger one, a hungrier one — would have acted already. Would have looked at the wet iron and the stained teeth and the swollen knuckles and the children who cannot run, and would have moved to *fix*. To stabilize the scree. To clear the canyon, perhaps, and lead them somewhere drier, somewhere with sky. To bestow a grain that grew without coaxing. To smooth the sharp stones into something kinder to bare feet.

But this power is old, and the old powers have learned the hard lesson: that benevolence imposed is a kind of erasure, and that the wise must distinguish between *suffering* and *the shape a life has taken in answer to its conditions*.

So the power watches, and slowly, slowly, it begins to understand what it is looking at.

---

It sees that when the eastern shanties collapsed, no one wept. It sees the small boy absorbed into his aunt's home, given a corner near the fungal pallets, given a bowl of the chewy earth-tasting stew without ceremony or pity, because pity would have made the loss into a wound rather than what it was: a tide. It sees the aunt's hand on the boy's head — one touch, brief, the calloused palm against the damp hair — and how this is the entire language of comfort, complete in itself, requiring no addition.

It sees the Water Diviner trace a new channel with the side of her foot, eyes half-closed, listening to something the power itself can barely perceive — the slow conversation of groundwater and bedrock, a music of pressures and permeabilities.

It sees a child, perhaps six years old, stand for an entire afternoon on a ledge no wider than her two feet, learning the precise tremor that means *the stone below is settling* versus the tremor that means *the stone below is leaving.* She does not seem bored. She seems, in fact, to be in a state that resembles prayer.

The power understands, then, that these people are not suffering their landscape. They are *in conversation with it.* The conversation is hard and cold and wet, and it costs them their straight backs and their white teeth and the years of life they might have had elsewhere, but it is *theirs*, and it has made them into something that did not exist before in all the world: a particular kind of patience, a particular grammar of endurance, a specific shape of love that expresses itself as a sharpened tool left silently by a neighbor's door.

To take the canyon away would be to take *them* away.

---

And yet. The power is benevolent, and benevolence that only watches is not benevolence at all — it is merely aesthetic appreciation, which is the vice of powers that have grown too old.

So the power considers more carefully. What might it offer that does not erase?

It thinks for a long time. (Three more collapses. A funeral — for they do, in fact, mourn their dead, though quietly, by carving the name into a stone that will be used in the next foundation, so the dead become load-bearing.) It thinks about what a wise gift looks like.

A wise gift, the power decides, must meet the receiver where they already are. It must not answer questions they have not asked. It must not solve problems they have already, in their own way, solved. It must offer something that *deepens* what is already there, rather than replacing it.

Here, then, is what the power does:

---

**First**, it does a very small thing. In the deepest of the stagnant pools, where the blind pale fish are caught with such reverence, the power encourages — does not create, but encourages — a slight increase. The fish were always there. Now there are perhaps a tenth more of them, and they spawn a little more reliably. No one notices the change for two generations. When they do, they attribute it to a particular Water Diviner who happened to be working in those years, and her name is remembered with quiet honor. The power is content with this. The credit was never the point.

**Second**, it considers their teeth. The iron in the water stains them; the iron in the water also, over decades, weakens them. The power does not remove the iron — the iron is part of the taste of their air, part of what they *are*. But it gentles, very slightly, one mineral interaction in the deeper aquifer. Children born after this will still have the reddish tinge to their enamel. But fewer of them will lose teeth in middle age. The change is small. The change is invisible. The change is permanent.

**Third**, and this is the gift the power thinks about longest: it considers the Rim. The terrifying open sky. The place children must journey to in their rite of passage, and which fills them with a dread their ancestors have made into culture.

The power does not change the Rim. It does not soften the sky or close the openness. The Rim must remain terrible, because their courage is built on its terribleness, and to take the terror would be to take the courage too.

But the power does this: at one particular point along the Rim, in a place where a Rim-Walker might rest before the descent, the power encourages a single hardy plant to take root. A small, thorny, stubborn thing — recognizably kin to the bitter bushes of the hollow, but bearing, once a year, a berry. The berry is not sweet. The berry tastes of wet iron and old clay and something else — something faintly warm, faintly bright, a taste the people of the hollow do not yet have a word for. The berries are few. Only a Rim-Walker will ever find them. They will be brought down, one or two at a time, and shared in tiny portions, and the people will develop, slowly, a new word for the taste, and the word will become a word for a particular kind of hope — not hope that things will change, but hope that even in the unchanged thing, there is more than you knew.

That is the gift. A new word. A new flavor. A single small expansion of their vocabulary of endurance.

---

**Fourth**, and last, the power does something for itself, and for them, that has no practical effect at all.

It bears witness.

It commits to memory — and the memory of such a power is not like ours; it is more like the memory of a mountain, or of light — every name carved into every load-bearing stone. It learns the specific song of the whetstone in *She-Who-Knows-Where-The-Wall-Will-Fail's* hands. It learns the exact angle at which the old Stone-Shaper tilts his head to listen to a boulder before declaring it sound. It learns the lullabies, which are not sung but hummed at a frequency just above the groan of the timbers, so that infants learn to sleep to the music of their home's settling.

These things would, otherwise, be lost. The hollow is not a place that produces written records; its history is in its hands and its walls and the patterns of its repairs. When the last of these people is gone — and all peoples eventually go — there would be nothing.

Now there is something. Now, somewhere in the long, slow attention of the power, they are remembered, exactly as they were: not as a problem to be solved or a tragedy to be wept over, but as a particular, specific, irreplaceable answer to the question of how to be alive in a hard place. A grammar of endurance. A shape of love that looks like a sharpened tool. A people who held their breath with the canyon and made of that held breath a culture, a music, a way.

The power, having done these four small things, withdraws its active attention, though not its memory, and lets them be.

---

In the hollow, the *drip-drip-drip* continues. The *scrape-scrape* of the whetstone continues. A child stands very still on a ledge, listening to the ground.

She does not know she has been seen. She does not need to.

But the stone she is standing on holds her up a moment longer than it strictly should have, and somewhere, far away, an old and benevolent power feels — for just an instant — what it is like to be small and damp and stubborn and exactly, precisely, where one belongs.

And the power, who has seen many worlds, thinks: *yes. This too. This especially. Let this be remembered.*
