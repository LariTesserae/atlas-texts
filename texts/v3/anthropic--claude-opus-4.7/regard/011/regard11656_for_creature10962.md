They come the way weather comes. Not as a figure descending, not as a voice from the bright sky — the plateau would absorb a voice the way it absorbs everything, and besides, this power is older than the habit of arriving. It is already present. It has been present. The question is only whether to be noticed, and to what degree, and by whom.

It studies her for a long time before doing anything. This is its first courtesy: attention without intervention. To rush in with gifts would be to insult the four years she has built. She has made shoes out of the ground. She has made a home inside a tree that grew around her. She has made a discipline out of staying. These are not problems to be solved. They are accomplishments, of a strange and specific kind, and the power recognizes them as such.

So first, it simply *witnesses*.

It witnesses her at the edge, leaning slightly out over the moss-lip, measuring the fracture where the fifth one fell. It witnesses the way her hand pauses on the pump housing each morning — the small ritual greeting, the *still going*, mouthed into the water-roar. It witnesses the notebooks. It reads them, in the way such a power reads — not page by page but all at once, taking in the whole architecture of her recorded attention, the small precise handwriting, the gaps that are not gaps but silences chosen on purpose, the entry about the boot.

It notes that she is not lost. This matters. A benevolent power that mistakes contentment for suffering does harm. She is not unhappy. She said so herself, in the last line before sleep, and she was telling the truth. A wise power believes people about their own interiors, even when their interiors are unusual.

But it also notes the things she does not say to herself. The way she sometimes mouths the roll call of names and lets the roar take them. The eighteen months of fuel. The cables running to an unknown terminus. The fact that no one is coming. The fact that she has chosen this, yes, but she chose it once, four years ago, and has been choosing it again every morning since, and the renewal of a choice is not the same as the original choice — it has accumulated weight, like the moss accumulates over the lattice tower, eleven centimeters at a time.

The power considers what to offer.

---

It does not bring her home. Home is a concept she has retired with some care, and to drag it back into her shelter would be a violence dressed as a kindness. It does not send a helicopter. It does not restore the radio. It does not appear to her as a luminous figure and explain things. Explanation is not what she needs; she has explained a great deal to herself already, and accurately.

What it does is smaller, and stranger, and exactly sized.

---

**First**, it does something for the cable.

She has suspected, for years, that the generator powers something buried in the root-mass — something installed by the previous people, something the plateau has grown over and integrated, something whose function she cannot determine and whose interruption she dares not risk. This suspicion has been a small pressure at the back of her attention, a question she carries like a pebble in a pocket.

The power follows the cable. It is easy work. The cable runs eleven meters into the root-mass, then turns, then descends, then connects to a sealed steel housing the size of a bread loaf, which contains a heating element and a humidity regulator and a small array of sensors. The original expedition installed it to maintain a stable microclimate around a seed bank — a cache of genetic material from lowland species, hedged against extinction, meant to be retrieved later when conditions allowed. The retrieval never happened. The seed bank is still there, still maintained, still viable. The generator has been keeping it alive.

The power does not tell her this. To tell her would be to give her a problem — what to do with the knowledge, whether to act on it, whether her solitude has been in service of something after all, and how that changes things. She has already made peace with not knowing, and that peace is hers.

What the power does instead is *make the maintenance self-sustaining*. It does this with the lightest possible touch. It encourages a particular strain of the bryophyte — the one already colonizing the generator's housing — to develop, over the next several years, a thermogenic property: a slow internal warmth produced by the metabolic activity of its densest layers. Not much. Just enough. Enough that when the diesel runs out, in eighteen months or two years, the seed bank's microclimate will continue to be regulated by the living ground itself, by the same organism that has been growing over everything. The plateau will have finished digesting the machine, and in digesting it, will have taken on its function.

She will not know this happened. She will only notice, perhaps, that the moss around the generator is unusually warm to the touch one morning, and she will record this in her notebook in small precise handwriting, and she will not assign it significance, because she is a scientist and she does not yet have enough data points.

This is the power's first gift: it relieves her of a responsibility she did not know she was carrying, without ever telling her she was carrying it. The pump can stop. The generator can stop. The plateau will continue, and the seeds will continue, and her vigil — which was real, which mattered — will have been honored by being made unnecessary at the pace at which she was prepared to be unnecessary.

---

**Second**, it does something for the fifth one.

It cannot bring him back. It is benevolent and wise, not omnipotent in the careless sense, and even if it could, doing so would tear a hole in the fabric of what she has already metabolized. He is gone. She knows he is gone. What she does not have is a way to lay him down.

So the power does this: at the place where the overhang fractured, where she found the boot, it causes a small thing to grow. Not a marker — a marker would be too legible, too much like a gesture aimed at her. Just a particular configuration of the canopy-tree braids, a slight thickening at that one specific lip, a place where the root-mass becomes denser and more stable than the surrounding edge. Over the next two years, this thickening will become the most reliable section of the entire northern overhang. She will notice. She will, eventually, sit there. Not because she understands what it is. Because her body will learn that this is the safe place to sit at the edge, and her body will go there when she wants to look out over the cloud, and she will sit, and the wind will come up from the valleys, and she will think of him without intending to, and the thinking will not hurt the way it used to, because she will be sitting on ground that holds her.

This is what the plateau can offer, and the power is only helping the plateau offer it slightly sooner, slightly more precisely, than it would have offered it on its own. The plateau was already inclined toward this. The plateau has been inclined toward her, in its slow vegetative way, for some time.

---

**Third**, it does something about the spores.

The green lines in the creases of her knuckles. The colonization of the folds. The negotiation that is not going in her favor. She has accepted this — has, in fact, made a kind of peace with it that the power finds genuinely beautiful, the way she stopped trying to scrub the spores away and let them become part of her hand.

The power does not reverse the colonization. That would undo something she has chosen. But it adjusts, very slightly, the chemistry of the spores that establish themselves on her skin going forward. It makes them gentler. It makes them metabolically compatible in a way that will, over the coming years, mean she does not develop the slow joint stiffness that would otherwise have begun in her seventh or eighth year here, the gradual incorporation of plant matter into the connective tissues of her hands. She will keep her hands. She will keep her grip on the pry bar and the knife and the pencil. The plateau will still be growing on her — she has agreed to that — but it will grow on her at her pace, in a form that does not take from her the capacity to keep writing in small precise handwriting in the notebooks.

The notebooks matter. The power is clear about this. Not because anyone may ever read them — possibly no one will — but because *she writes them*, and the writing is one of the practices by which she remains herself while becoming something else. To compromise her hands would be to compromise the practice. So her hands are protected, quietly, at the cellular level, by an intervention she will never detect.

---

**Fourth**, the power does the largest and smallest thing.

On a particular night — the brief grey-violet compression, the slow tidal music of the canopy adjusting its pressures — she will wake. Not startled. Just awake, in the way one sometimes is, for no reason.

She will lie in her cavity inside the living tree. The water will be falling outside. The tree will be holding her, in its accommodating, non-crushing way. And she will have, for the duration of perhaps four breaths, a feeling she will not be able to name afterward and will not try to.

The feeling is this: that she is known. Not surveilled, not catalogued, not observed by an intelligence with an agenda — *known*, in the specific way that something is known when it has been looked at carefully and found to be exactly what it is, and the looking has resolved into a kind of approval that is not approval because approval implies the possibility of disapproval, and there was never any disapproval available. Just the looking. Just the seeing. Just the recognition that *this person, here, in this tree, on this plateau, doing this thing, is one of the ways the universe is currently being itself, and it is good.*

Four breaths. Then the feeling will go, and she will not remember it as a feeling, only as a quality of that particular night, which she will record in the notebook the next morning as: *Slept well. No wind. The tree quiet.*

This is the gift. Not the content of the feeling — the content would be too much, would unbalance her — but its trace. A faint sense, going forward, of being accompanied. Not by anyone. Just accompanied. The way one is accompanied by weather, by altitude, by the sound of water on leaves. One more presence in the texture of her solitude, indistinguishable from the texture itself, asking nothing, requiring nothing, witnessing.

---

The power withdraws. Or rather — it does not withdraw, because it was never localized in a way that would permit withdrawal. It simply ceases the particular orientation of attention that constituted its intervention. The plateau goes on. She goes on. The pump still chugs, for now. The generator still hums, for now. The moss advances. The water falls.

In a hundred years, when the plateau has descended the mountain and sheathed the granite from summit to base, and the cloud-valleys are forests, and the entire range is one continuous organism breathing and dripping and thundering — in a hundred years, a small thickening in the northern overhang of what was once the original plateau will still be there, structurally distinct, slightly denser than its surroundings, the safest place to sit at the edge. No one will know why. The bryophyte around the buried seed bank will still be warm to the touch, regulating a microclimate around genetic material from a world that, by then, will be ready to receive it back. No one will know why.

And in some archive, in some city, in some institution that has long since forgotten the expedition it once funded, there will be a steel box containing dozens of notebooks in small precise handwriting, recovered by someone who found a way up the eastern face when the route had finally stabilized — and the notebooks will be read, and the data will be entered, and the long quiet work of one woman who became a component of a living landform will turn out to have been the foundational dataset for understanding how mountains learn to grow themselves.

She will not know any of this. She did not need to.

The power, which is benevolent and wise, understood that the deepest form of care, for someone who has made what she made of her life, is to let the life she made be the life she made — and to add, only, the smallest possible margin of grace around its edges. A warmer moss. A safer ledge. A gentler spore. Four breaths of being known.

The rest is hers.

The water falls. The water falls. The water falls.
