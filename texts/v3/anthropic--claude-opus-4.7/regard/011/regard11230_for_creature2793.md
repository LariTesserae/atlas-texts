The benevolent and wise power considers them for a long time.

It is not a short consideration. Wisdom of this kind does not arrive with a plan already folded in its hand, ready to be opened over a place like a tablecloth. It comes near, and waits, and listens. It does what the elders of the chamber do: it presses, in its own way, an ear to the wall.

What it hears surprises it, though wisdom is rarely surprised.

It had expected, perhaps, a cry. A buried people, pale and cold, eating wall-milk, sealing their dead into the masonry, dreaming of silence — surely such a people must be calling outward, even without knowing it. Surely there must be a hidden ache pointing up through the limestone like a thin held note, asking for rescue, for sun, for abundance, for rest.

There is no such note.

There is grief, yes. There is hunger sometimes. There is the particular sorrow of a sluice-mother who has buried two children in the high niches and still rises at the changing of a basin's tone. There is fear in the dreams of silence. There is the soft, swallowed laughter, and the cold knuckles, and the split heels of the old. The power perceives all of it, fully, without flinching, because that is part of what it is to be wise: to look at a small suffering without immediately reaching to erase it, lest the erasure cost more than the suffering did.

But underneath the griefs, the power hears something else. It hears competence. It hears love expressed as a polished handhold. It hears a civilization that has not been broken into this shape but has grown into it, the way certain trees grow sideways out of cliffs and are not deformed but themselves. It hears a people who are not waiting to be saved. It hears a people who would not recognize salvation if it came as the thing it first thought of bringing.

So the power sets down its first impulses, one by one, the way one sets down tools that turn out to be wrong for the work.

It sets down the impulse to open the ceiling.

It had considered this. A clean fracture upward, light pouring in, the chamber flooded with morning, the pale people lifting their faces, weeping, transformed. But the power, looking again, sees what such a gift would actually do. The lamps, suddenly redundant, would be neglected, and with them the soot that insulates, the rituals that tend, the apprenticeships that bind generations. The water, warmed by sun, would breed greens and rots the chamber has never been shaped to handle. The sounds would scatter. The civic hearing, finely tuned across centuries, would find itself drowned in birdcall and wind and the meaningless roar of weather that has not yet learned its channels. The people would be blinded, then sun-sick, then estranged from one another, then strangers in their own walls. To give them the sky would be to take the city. The power understands: a gift that requires the recipient to become someone else in order to receive it is not, in the end, a gift.

It sets down the impulse to multiply them.

It had considered this too. Such a small people, such a narrow margin — surely abundance would be mercy. More children surviving. More hands. More fat in the broth. But the chamber is the chamber. It holds what it holds. To swell their numbers would be to press them against their own walls until the walls cracked, or to force them outward into passages they have not named, into dark they have not learned, into a diaspora for which they have no myths. Abundance, here, would be a slow crushing. The power sees this and is quiet.

It sets down the impulse to teach.

It had considered arriving as a figure in a lamp-lit alcove, bearing knowledge: of metallurgy, of writing that resists damp, of medicines, of broader engineering, of the shape of the world above. But the power looks at the clay tablets that would mold and crumble, at the metals that would green and be lost, at the maps that would mean nothing because the territory mapped is not the territory inhabited. It looks at the sluice-mother and understands that she already knows more about her water than any visiting intelligence could tell her. To "teach" such a person would be to insult the long, wordless schooling of her hands. The power has met this temptation before, in other places, and has learned to distrust it. The wish to teach is often the wish to be thanked.

It sets down, too, the impulse to be known.

This is the hardest to set down. The power is benevolent, and benevolence has its vanity: it likes to be recognized, to be named in stories, to be the bright figure in the myth of the Lamp That Burned Blue for Seven Fillings. It would be easy, and not unkind, to appear. To stand in the still-bowl chamber and be received as the thing the still bowl had always meant. But the power considers what it would do to that bowl to have its mystery answered. The bowl's stillness is holy because everything else moves; the bowl's meaning is holy because it is not fixed. To arrive and say *I am what you have been tending toward* would be to close a door that has been productively open for a hundred generations. The power understands that some reverences must be left unconfirmed in order to remain reverences at all.

So.

What, then, does it do?

It does small things. It does them carefully, and mostly without signature.

It does not open the ceiling, but it adjusts, very slightly, the pressure of the unseen stream that feeds the plunge-well, so that during a season the people could not have predicted — a season above, of unusual drought — the fall does not falter. The bass note holds. The civic weather remains. No one in the chamber knows that anything was ever in danger. This is precisely the point. A rescue that announces itself becomes a debt. A rescue that does not is simply continuance, which is what these people have built their lives to honor.

It does not multiply them, but it touches, with something like a thumb, the blind threadfish in the deepest cisterns. Not to make them many. Only to make them, in this generation, slightly more willing to be caught by the small hooked pins of those who need them most: the sick child in the fourth alcove, the old lip-cutter whose hands have begun to tremble. The catches will seem lucky. Luck is a form the power likes to take, because luck asks nothing of the lucky except that they remain themselves.

It does not teach, but it allows a particular child — the one who, at four, said *that bowl is counting wrong* — to live. There had been a fever moving along the damp-line that would have taken her. The power does not banish the fever; fevers belong to the chamber's long arrangement. It only sees to it that this child's particular small body finds the strength, on the third night, to turn the corner. She will grow up to be a sounder of unusual gift. She will hear, at fifty, the first soft wrongness in the eastern baffle decades before anyone else would have, and the city will not know the disaster it did not have. This is the only kind of teaching the power permits itself: the quiet preservation of a mind the city was already growing for its own purposes.

It does not appear in the still-bowl chamber. But once, on a night when the keeper of the still bowl is very old and very tired and has begun to doubt whether her long watching has meant anything, the power allows the bowl to be, for the space of three breaths, even stiller than it has ever been. Stiller than physics should permit. The keeper feels it, the way one feels a held gaze in the dark. She does not see anything. She is not told anything. She simply receives, through her palms resting near the rim, a sense that the watching has been watched, that the tending has been tended, that the long careful life she has spent at the edge of an unmoving water has been, in some manner she will never need to articulate, *correct*. She weeps once, quietly, in the way her people weep, and goes back to her duties, and dies four fillings later, content. She tells no one. There is nothing to tell. But the next keeper inherits, without knowing it, a bowl that has been, however briefly, acknowledged. That acknowledgment will travel forward through the keepers like a faint warmth in a stone long after the hand that warmed it has gone.

And the power does one more thing, which is perhaps the largest thing, though it would not look large to any observer.

It decides to remember them.

This sounds like little. It is not little. The power is benevolent and wise and has attended to many places: bright civilizations on open plains, sea-cities, forest peoples, the long slow thoughts of certain mountains. Its attention is finite even when its capacity is vast, and what it chooses to hold in active memory shapes what it later notices, later protects, later mourns. To remember the chamber — to keep the chamber and its hearing-people as a living presence in its own awareness, not as a file but as a known neighborhood — is to ensure that across whatever ages follow, the power will continue to listen for them. Will continue to feel, faintly, when the plunge-well's bass note alters. Will know, without being told, the day the last of them dies, if such a day comes; and will, on that day, do whatever such a power does to honor the closing of a small, exact, finished thing. Will, if instead they endure for ages more, continue to make the tiny untraceable adjustments — a pressure here, a fever turned there, a child preserved, a bowl briefly stiller — that allow a maintained condition to remain maintained.

It will not save them from being mortal. It will not save them from being few. It will not save them from being cold, or pale, or sometimes hungry, or sometimes grieving, or sometimes wrong about the water and punished by it. These are not flaws in their existence. These are the shape of their existence, and the shape is, in its way, beautiful, and the power is wise enough to know that beauty maintained at the edge of difficulty is not the same as suffering, even when from a distance the two can look alike.

What the power refuses, in the end, is the cheap mercy. The mercy that would have made itself feel generous by overwriting what it found. The mercy that mistakes a small life for a poor one. The mercy that cannot bear to leave a thing as it is.

What the power offers, instead, is the harder mercy. The mercy of being seen without being altered. The mercy of being remembered without being claimed. The mercy of small, invisible, well-timed steadyings, given freely and never billed. The mercy of allowing a people to remain the city's hearing, rather than insisting they become something louder, something brighter, something easier to love from far away.

The water falls, fills, spills, drains, and returns to the unseen undercourses at exactly the rate required.

The lamps lean low in their cups.

The same thunder goes on beneath the ground, steady as law.

And somewhere — not above, not below, not in any direction the chamber's geometry knows — a benevolent and wise power inclines slightly toward the limestone, and listens, and is glad.
