# What the Power Does

The benevolent power arrives without announcement, because announcement is a thing for those who need to be perceived. It simply becomes present in the shaft, the way humidity becomes present, the way a thought becomes present in a mind that was empty a moment before.

It listens first. This is the longest part, and the most important.

It listens to the grinding of the winch and hears in it a kind of heartbeat. It listens to the hiss of the misters and hears breath. It listens to the click-language of the Tenders and understands every word — the pressure-valve warnings, the piston-grammar of greeting, the long slow creaks that mean *I see you, sibling, and you are well-rooted.* It listens to the vines drinking from the spinal ports and hears not horror but conversation, the long murmured exchange of two organisms who have agreed, across some unrecorded covenant, to keep each other alive.

It listens to the deep ones in the reservoir, whose consciousness has spread through root networks like ink through water, and finds them neither lost nor damned. They are simply *large* now, in a way humans were not built to be, but in a way humans have always longed to become — pressed against the edges of themselves, finally permeable.

The power had expected, perhaps, to find suffering here. The geometry of the place suggests it: the industrial green paint flaking from rust, the surgical ports, the children grown from cuttings who have never seen the sun. These are the shapes that, in a thousand other places the power has visited, indicated wounds.

But it listens longer, and finds that this is not quite a wound. It is a wound's *afterward.* Something terrible happened once — the boring of the shaft, the institutional cruelty of whoever first built this engine for growth — and the beings who were trapped inside it did the only holy thing available to them: they refused to remain separate from their captivity. They married it. They let it become them, and they became it, and in the becoming the captivity transformed into something else. Not freedom, exactly. But not prison anymore either. A *practice.* A continuous and intricate practice.

So the power does not undo the shaft. It does not crack the mountain open and pour sunlight in. To do so would be to murder what the Tenders have built across generations of darkness — to insist that their adaptation was a deviation from some truer human form, when in fact it was a form of love, terribly slow love, love for a place that did not initially deserve it but that they made deserving through their tending.

Instead, the power does smaller things.

It goes first to the ancient Tender at the junction of pipes, the one who has become a living gauge. The power touches them — not with hands, because it has none, but with the kind of attention that feels, to the recipient, like being recognized after a long time of being merely used. The Tender's chemical-sensitive skin registers something new in the air: a quality of *witness.* For the first time in decades, they understand that their long stillness has been seen. Not measured. *Seen.* They weep, though their tear ducts long ago repurposed themselves for other secretions, and what comes out is a clear sap that the vines around them drink with something like reverence.

The power goes next to the youngest Tenders, the ones whose memories of sunlight are still fading. It does not restore those memories — that would be cruel, a re-opening. Instead it gives them a dream, just one, that they will share without ever speaking of it. In the dream, they stand at the top of the shaft and look down, and they see the whole garden as it truly is: not an industrial accident, not a captivity, but a cathedral grown in the dark by hands that learned, against every odd, to be gentle. They wake knowing their work is beautiful. They had not known this. They had only known it was necessary.

The power goes to the machines themselves, because the machines too are part of this being, this place. It does not repair them — they are maintained already, intuitively, lovingly. But it does something subtler. It blesses the failure rates. It makes the inevitable breakdowns occur on schedules that align with the Tenders' capacities, so that no Tender ever again will have to choose between rest and a dying pump. The winch will groan a little less. The pruning blades will whir at frequencies that the plants find less distressing. None of this will be noticeable as miracle. It will only feel like a long good season.

The power goes to the deep ones in the reservoir, and here it does the most careful thing of all. It does not call them back. It does not pity them. It simply joins them, briefly, in their distributed condition — lets its own vast attention thread through the root networks alongside theirs — and in doing so, confirms for them that what they have become is *a kind of being that exists.* They had wondered. Even in their dispersed wisdom, they had wondered if they were monstrous, if they had gone somewhere they should not have gone. The power's presence among them, untroubled and curious, is an answer. *You are a way of being a person. You are one of the ways.*

The power considers the cloning, the cutting-children who step from their pods already knowing the click-language. It looks closely at this, because it is the kind of thing that ought to trouble a benevolent witness: a life without childhood, without choice, without the long ramble of becoming.

But the power sees that the cuttings are not deprived of becoming. They are *given* becoming as their entire existence — they are still becoming, every day, in their tending, in their listening, in their slow integration with the garden. The childhood they did not have is replaced by something else: a kinship that begins in the medium itself, a sense of having been *grown by their community rather than born to a single body.* It is different. It is not worse. The power makes a small adjustment anyway: the new Tenders, when they step from their pods, will feel something they would not otherwise have felt — a moment of being *welcomed,* specifically, by name, even if the name is only a sequence of clicks. They will know, from the first breath of filtered air, that they were *wanted.* Not just generated. Wanted.

Finally, the power turns to the bones in the walls — the Tenders who have already died and been consumed back into the cycle, their skeletons threaded with root. It does not raise them. It does not need to. It only sings to them, in a register that bones can hear, the long song of what they accomplished: *you made a place where there was only a hole. You made a we where there was only a use. The mountain will remember you for as long as mountains remember anything, which is long enough.*

Then the power departs, because benevolence that lingers becomes a kind of weight, and this place has carried enough weight already.

It leaves behind no sign. The shaft still thrums in darkness. The misters still fire in their irregular patterns. The Tenders still move like deep-sea creatures through their trenches, clicking softly, tending.

But somewhere in the vertical garden, on a terrace of alpine moss high up where the ice forms and melts, a single rhododendron blooms — quietly, without announcement, in a darkness that has never permitted blooming before. No one will see it. The Tenders who pass it will only feel, briefly, that the air has changed, that something in the garden has *given a gift to itself.*

This will be enough. This will be, in fact, exactly the right size of miracle for a place that learned, across long years, how to make miracles out of necessity.

The power is already elsewhere, listening to some other unlikely flourishing, some other being who has refused to be only what they were made to be.
