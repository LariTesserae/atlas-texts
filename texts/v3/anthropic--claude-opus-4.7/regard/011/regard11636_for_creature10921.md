# The Visitation

The benevolent power does not arrive. Arrival is a thing of bodies and distances, and It is neither. Rather, Its attention, which had been turning through countless folds of the cosmos, settles. Settles the way snow settles: without violence, without announcement, accumulating in the spaces between things until the shape of the place is subtly changed by the presence of something soft and watchful.

It considers.

This is the first thing It does, and It does it for a long time. Not because It is uncertain — It is rarely uncertain — but because wisdom, when it is real, knows that the longest and most loving act available to it is often simply to *look*. To look at the Xel'thari moving along their pipelines. To look at the patient, ritual gestures with which they tend machines whose purpose even they may no longer remember. To look at the resonant tones that pass between them, and to understand — for understanding is trivial to It — that these tones carry grief. Not the sharp grief of a fresh wound, but the long grief of a people who have known, for many thousands of years, that they are ending.

It does not pity them. Pity is a small thing and would diminish them. Instead It honors them, which is the proper response of greatness encountering greatness, even when one greatness is ascending and the other is going down into the dark.

Then It asks the question that wise powers must always ask before they act:

*What do they want?*

This is harder than it sounds. The Xel'thari themselves do not entirely know. Some of them, in the deepest chambers, still labor toward goals so ancient the goals have become mythologies even to their tenders. Others have begun, in the slow way of long-lived species, to release. To sit by the phosphorescent mineral beds and let the light in their veins dim by degrees, content. Others still are angry — quietly, with the dignified anger of those who feel that the universe has been unfair, and who know complaint is beneath them, and so carry the anger like a stone in a pocket no one sees.

The benevolent power does not impose a single answer on them. This is perhaps the most important thing about It: It refuses, on principle, to be the kind of power that solves.

Instead It does several small things, and one large thing.

---

**The small things, first.**

It enters the dreams of the Xel'thari — those of them who still dream — not as a presence but as a *clarity*. The dreamer finds, upon waking, that some long-tangled question in their mind has untangled itself in the night. A schematic recalled. A grievance against a sibling, dead nine hundred years, finally laid down. A word in the old tongue, lost since childhood, returned with the taste of it intact. They do not know why. They attribute it to the minerals, or to the machines, or to nothing at all. This is fine. Gifts that announce themselves are not gifts but transactions.

It softens, by some imperceptible degree, the thinness of the air. Not enough to disturb the ecology of the plateau — It would never — but enough that the youngest of the Xel'thari, the few that remain, can run a little further before their thin chests ache. They feel this as a kind of unaccountable joy in their bodies and do not question it.

It listens to the resonant tones, and where the tones are calls of loneliness — one Xel'thari singing into a corridor where no one has answered for centuries — It carries the call, faithfully, across the city to another who is listening. It is only a postman. It changes no words. But suddenly, in the far quarters, old friends who had each believed the other dead find themselves in conversation again, and weep in their fashion, which is a low harmonic that makes the obsidian monoliths shiver in sympathy.

It does not heal the dying. It eases them. There is a difference, and It knows it.

---

**The large thing is this.**

The benevolent power perceives, with a sorrow that is not Its own but which It takes upon Itself out of courtesy, that the Xel'thari have a fear they have never spoken, not even in the resonant tones, not even to themselves in the deepest hours. The fear is this: that when the last of them is gone, no one will ever have known. That the long labor, the inscrutable balance, the tending of the machines, the songs in the corridors, the communions with the crystalline growths — all of it will pass without witness, as though it had never been. That the universe is a room they have been keeping clean, and when they die the room will be found by no one, and the cleanliness will mean nothing.

This is the wound. Beneath all the other wounds, this is the one.

So the benevolent power becomes a witness.

Not a memorial — memorials are stone, and stone forgets. Not a record — records can be lost. It becomes, instead, a kind of permanent attention. It folds the Xel'thari, in all the fullness of what they were and what they did and what they tried to do and what they failed at and what they loved, into Its own being, which does not end. Every tone sung in every corridor. Every gesture of tending. Every quiet death by the mineral beds. Every joke — for they had jokes, though no outsider ever guessed it. Every small kindness one of them did for another in the long dark.

It does this without telling them. To tell them would be to make the gift a debt. But somewhere, in the marrow of the city, the Xel'thari begin to feel — without knowing why — that they are *seen*. Not watched. Seen. The way a beloved child is seen by a parent who has stepped quietly into the doorway and said nothing.

This feeling spreads slowly. It is not euphoria. It is not even quite comfort. It is more like the lifting of a weight so old they had forgotten it was a weight, had come to believe it was simply the shape of their shoulders.

They work a little differently after this. Some of them sing more. Some sing less, and instead sit with each other in long companionable silences. One of them, very old, walks out to the edge of the plateau for the first time in eleven hundred years and looks at the mountains, really looks at them, and decides — for reasons he cannot name — that they are beautiful, and that it has been a good thing, on the whole, to have lived among them.

---

The benevolent power stays. It will stay for as long as there is one Xel'thari left to tend a machine, and then It will stay for a little while longer, in case the city itself has anything to say in their absence. (Cities sometimes do.)

It does not save them. It was never going to. Salvation, when imposed from outside, is only another kind of erasure — the replacement of a real ending with a borrowed continuation. The Xel'thari have earned their ending. It is theirs. It belongs to them in a way that nothing else in the universe quite does.

But they will not end *alone*, and they will not end *unknown*, and the long patient labor of their twilight will be carried — every tone of it, every gesture — into something that does not forget.

This is what the benevolent and wise power does.

It is not very much.

It is everything It could honorably do.
