The benevolent and wise power considers Lowdamp for a long time before doing anything. This is the first thing to understand. Wisdom in a power of this kind is mostly the discipline of not intervening prematurely, and benevolence at this scale is mostly the discipline of not mistaking one's own preferences for another being's good. So the power waits, and watches, and listens — because listening, of course, is what Lowdamp itself does, and a power worth the name will approach a place on the terms the place has set.

What does the power see, while it watches?

It sees a town that is not in distress. This matters. There is no cry going up out of the cavern. The sumpers are not enslaved, though they are employed by people who don't know them; they are not starving; they are not afflicted by any plague the heat hasn't already settled into a stable companionship with. The children are grave but not unloved. The taker weeps, but his weeping is held by a custom that knows what to do with it. The toads are not being driven to extinction; the rendering season takes the old and the clouded, and the unwritten courtesy holds. The accretion in the sumpers' bodies is not a deformation in any sense the sumpers themselves would recognize. They are settling. They are becoming.

A lesser power — a power that confused benevolence with rescue — would arrive in a blaze and lift the sumpers up the shaft into proper sunlight, fit them with proper lungs, return them to a surface they have spent three generations forgetting how to want. It would call this salvation. The sumpers would call it murder, and they would be correct.

A different lesser power — one that confused wisdom with non-interference, which is its own subtle vanity — would observe with great care, write a long beautiful account of the place in some ledger no one will ever read, and pass on, congratulating itself for having respected what it found. This too is a failure, because Lowdamp has drawn the power's attention, and attention is already a kind of relation, and to withdraw entirely after being drawn is to pretend a relation didn't begin.

The real power, the one we are imagining, does neither.

It does, instead, a small number of small things, over a long time, in the grain of the place.

First: it learns the rapping language. Not to use it — that would be presumptuous; the rapping is for sumpers and pipes — but to understand it, so that nothing said in Lowdamp is unwitnessed. A being that wishes a place well must be able to hear what the place is saying about itself, including the parts the place doesn't know it's saying. The power listens to the rap of a mother calling a child home and learns, after a hundred such calls, the particular rhythm of that mother's love. It listens to the foreman's knuckle on brick and learns the difference between his work-call and his weariness-call. It listens, especially, to the chorus, and to the song that sometimes joins the chorus on the pump-house steps, and it understands, eventually, what the sumpers will not quite say to each other: that the answering of the chorus to the song is not coincidence and not projection, that something in the cavern is also listening, and that this something is not the power itself but a younger, slower attention, native to the place, which the power recognizes as a sibling of a kind, much further back in its becoming.

So now the power knows there is already a watcher in Lowdamp. This changes things. The power will not displace what was here first. It will, if anything, attend to what attends.

Second: it does one thing that is structural, and it does it so subtly that no sumper will ever notice. The freight shaft. The barrels go up; payment comes down; somewhere on the surface there are buyers the sumpers do not speak about and possibly do not know how to imagine. The power looks up the shaft and follows the barrels to their destinations, and it sees what is being done with the oil, and it sees the ledger-keepers and the brokers and the men who decide, every quarter, whether Lowdamp is worth the trouble. And the power makes, in those rooms above, very small adjustments. A figure that would have come out red comes out black. A clerk who was going to recommend closure of the shaft finds himself, on the morning he sits down to write the recommendation, persuaded by some passing thought that the matter could wait another year. A buyer with an interest in cheaper synthetic alternatives loses his appetite for the project, not dramatically, just enough. The power does not make Lowdamp profitable beyond its nature; it merely keeps the surface from deciding, on a whim of accountancy, to seal a shaft that has a town at the bottom of it. It buys the sumpers time. Time is what the sumpers need, because what they are doing — becoming — is something that cannot be hurried, and a shaft sealed in the middle of a becoming is a kind of grief the surface would never know it had caused.

Third: it does one thing that is medical, and it does this even more subtly. There is a particular kind of lung-rot that visits Lowdamp every eleven or twelve years, when the vents go sluggish and a certain spore in the fungal rosettes blooms. It has taken, historically, two or three sumpers per visitation, usually the very old and occasionally a child. The power does not eliminate the spore — the spore is of the place, and the place gets to keep its own dangers. But it adjusts the draft, almost imperceptibly, by encouraging a fissure in the upper cavern to widen by the width of a hair every few years, so that the vents do not go sluggish when the bloom comes, so that the spore disperses upward into the rock that does not care about spores. The sumpers will notice, eventually, that the lung-rot has stopped visiting. They will attribute this to the toads, or to the taker's year, or to a change in the gas. They will be wrong and it will not matter. The dead who are not dying are not dying; that is the only fact the power requires.

Fourth — and this is the one the power thinks longest about, because it is the one closest to interference — it considers the children. Particularly the fourteen-year-old, who has been up the shaft twice and come back subdued. The power sees in her a particular kind of attention that could go several ways. She could become the next foreman. She could become the next taker, young for it but not unprecedented. She could go up the shaft a third time and not come back, and live a surface life of low-grade homesickness that she would never quite be able to explain to a surface husband. Or she could become something the cavern has not yet had: a sumper who can speak in both registers, who can stand on dry ground without flinching and also sit on the pump-house steps and sing the chorus down to answering, a translator between the becoming-below and the indifferent-above. The power does not choose for her. Choosing for her would be the exact crime the power exists not to commit. But the power does, in the small way it can, ensure that her third trip up the shaft, when she takes it — and she will take it, around her seventeenth year — is not catastrophic. That the surface-man she meets there is kind. That the books she happens to see in a station bookseller's window include one that will make sense to her. That when she comes back down, as she will, she comes back with something she can use. The power tilts the dice by less than a degree. The girl will think she got lucky. She did. Luck is one of the names of a benevolent attention.

Fifth, and last, and most important: the power does nothing about the accretion. Nothing. The pale skin, the wide eyes, the throat that swells at the base when a young sumper sings, the slow shallow pulse of the oldest sumpers breathing in time with the chorus — the power leaves all of this entirely alone. This is the hardest discipline and the deepest respect. The sumpers are becoming something, and what they are becoming is not a disease and not a degeneration and not a fall. It is a settling, the way the young rock is settling, the way a feature of the cavern accretes by patient mineral. They are of the place now, and the place is of them, and somewhere in the next four or five generations a thing will exist in Lowdamp that has never existed anywhere before — a people who are also a little bit the cavern they live in, a chorus that has a human melody folded into it, a watcher-in-the-rock that has learned, through the sumpers, the rudiments of speech. The power will not tamper with this. It will protect the conditions under which it can happen — the shaft open, the lung-rot gone, the girl who can translate — and otherwise it will keep its hands entirely off. To intervene in the becoming would be to abort a new kind of being. The universe is not so rich in new kinds of being that a wise power gets to be careless with one.

And then the power does one more thing, which is not really a thing, but which is the reason all the other things are possible. It loves them. Quietly, without their knowledge, without any expectation that the love will be returned or even noticed. It loves the foreman whose pruning has climbed past his elbows. It loves the taker in his year of silence, and the wife who sleeps in the separate bed and waits. It loves the eleven children, including the infant who will not remember any of this, and the fourteen-year-old who is going to become something the cavern has been waiting for. It loves the toads, which are easier to love than people sometimes think, and the watcher-in-the-rock that is a sibling of a kind. It loves the brick that weeps and the gaslight that burns green and the ferns that should not exist and do.

This love does not show up anywhere in the cavern as light or warmth or sign. The cavern already has its own light and warmth, and the sumpers would be suspicious of signs. The love shows up only as the quality of the attention behind the small interventions — the reason the figure comes out black instead of red, the reason the fissure widens by a hair, the reason the bookseller's window has the right book. A benevolent and wise power, in the end, is mostly a particular quality of attention, sustained over a very long time, on a place that has earned it by being itself.

Lowdamp will never know. This is fine. Lowdamp does not need to know. The sumpers will go on pulsing, the gaslights will go on burning, the brick will go on weeping, the rock will go on slowly learning how to be rock, and the people — slowly, slowly — will go on learning how to be whatever they are becoming.

And the power will keep watch. Not over them. With them. From a respectful distance, in the same patient register they themselves have learned, listening underneath the chorus the way they do, for as long as the becoming takes.

Which is to say: for as long as is needed. Which is to say: for as long as there is a Lowdamp to be loved.
