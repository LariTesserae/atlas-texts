The benevolent and wise power considers the saddle for a long time before doing anything at all.

This is the first thing to understand. Power that is actually wise is mostly stillness. It arrives and it watches. It watches the woman pressing her palm to a souring patch of wall and humming once, low, to her daughter, who fetches the right water. It watches the sluice-child, eight years old, blinking herself awake against the cold by biting the inside of her cheek. It watches the slope on the north shoulder where last spring's grandmother is becoming next spring's kitchen. It watches the bats return and hears the unspoken bell-pattern bloom across the saddle like a single thought distributed across eighty skulls.

What a foolish power would do here is obvious, and the wise power feels the temptation the way anyone feels temptation — as a small warm pull in a direction one is not going to go.

It would drain the lower wards. It would teach the people to forge iron, to wheel their goods, to read. It would cure the green staining at the cuticles, which is in fact a mild chronic dermatitis the people have folded into their identity. It would explain to them about bacteria, about the cistern, about the half-dozen infant deaths each generation that could be prevented by boiling. It would give them a written language so that the bell-idiolects could be transcribed and not lost when the last person who remembers old Imra's mother's bell finally goes into the wall. It would tell them that down-mountain there are libraries, and physicians, and that no one anywhere else gives their dead to moss, and ask them whether they had ever wondered why.

It would, in short, love them by making them less themselves, on the grounds that the self they currently are involves more suffering than is necessary.

The wise power sets this aside. Not because the suffering doesn't matter — it does, the infant deaths matter, the sluice-child's cold cheek matters, the slow arthritis in the green-knuckled hands of the forty-year-olds matters — but because the wise power has watched enough places to know what happens when you reach into a long-worn balance and pull on a thread to see what it does. The thread is always attached to something. Here every thread is attached to every other thread. Pull the dampness and you lose the moss; lose the moss and you lose the walls, the capes, the export, the dead, the burial, the marriage labor, the green hands, the game the children play blindfolded, the woman's hum at the souring patch, the very meaning of *knowing where you are.* The power has seen towns rescued into nonexistence. It will not do that here.

So it sets aside the large gestures and considers the small ones.

It thinks about the infants. This is the hardest. A wise power that is also benevolent does not get to be at peace with preventable infant death just because prevention would be culturally disruptive; that would not be wisdom, that would be aestheticism wearing wisdom's coat. So the power does something quiet. In the cistern, where the newts stack like wet leaves, it encourages — not creates, encourages, the way a gardener encourages — a particular strain of the bacteria already living in the newts' slime to outcompete the strain that has been killing the babies. The newts have always been there. The slime has always been there. The town's relationship to the cistern does not change. The bell-pattern for an infant death simply rings less often, over the next two generations, and no one quite notices when it stops ringing at all, because no one keeps that kind of count, and the old women who might have kept it are themselves in the walls by then.

This is the shape of what the wise power does. It works inside what is already there. It does not introduce; it inflects.

It thinks about the sluice-child. It does not replace the sluice with a machine. It does not whisper into anyone's ear about float-valves. What it does is much smaller: in the alder grove down-mountain where the sluice-wood is harvested, it lets a particular tree grow with a particular grain, and that tree, in sixty years, will be cut by someone's great-grandson for a replacement gate, and the grain of that wood will make the gate slightly more forgiving — a gate that gives a half-second of warning, a soft creak before a hard failure, so that a child who has nodded off has a chance to wake before the lower ward floods. The child will still be the watcher. The watching will still be the work. The work will simply be very slightly kinder to the watcher. No one will know. The power is content not to be known. Being known is not what it is for.

It thinks about the green staining and decides to leave it entirely alone. The staining is not suffering. The staining is a signature. The power notes that the people themselves have made this distinction — they treat the chronic damp cough as illness and the green hands as selfhood — and the power respects the distinction because the people made it, and the people live there, and the power does not.

It thinks about language, and writing, and the loss of the bell-idiolects, and this is where it lingers longest, because here the suffering is not present-tense — no one is dying of the lack of writing — but future-tense and diffuse: a slow forgetting that the town is not even aware is a loss, because the town does not experience the forgetting, only the forgotten do, and the forgotten are in the walls and have no say. The wise power considers giving them writing, and considers what writing would do to a culture whose entire epistemology is *you can't hear a person in this town, you have to watch them*, and concludes that writing would not arrive into that culture as writing; it would arrive as a solvent. It sets writing aside.

But it does one thing. In the moss itself — in the slow felting of the wall-moss as it compresses over decades into the deep ringing material of an old house — the power encourages a faint, faint patterning. Not writing. Nothing anyone could read. Just a tendency for the moss laid down in a year of much bell-ringing to felt slightly differently from the moss laid down in a quiet year, so that the walls of the oldest houses contain, in their layered density, a record of the town's acoustic life that a sufficiently attentive hand — and these are the most attentive hands in the world — could in principle feel. Whether anyone ever does feel it is up to them. The power has only made it possible. The record is there now, in the grandmothers, waiting in case anyone ever wants to know.

It thinks about the Turning, and does nothing. The Turning is not a problem. The Turning is the town's annual reminder that it is alive and not in charge, and the wise power knows that a place which has built a marriage rite out of catching mudslides in nets does not need its turbulence smoothed.

It thinks about the down-mountain traders who come for the moss-capes, and the way those traders sometimes carry illness up with them, and it adjusts — very gently, over years — the immune resilience of the saddle people to the particular pathogens of the trade routes. Not invulnerability. Just a slightly fairer fight. The people will still get sick. They will simply die of it slightly less often, and they will attribute the change, if they attribute it to anything, to the oil in their braids, which is fine. Let them keep the oil.

It thinks about the carved frog in the niche, the small religious object that the moss tries slowly to close over across a lifetime, and the wise power loves this so much it almost interferes out of sheer affection — almost causes a frog, once, to be found uncovered after a death, perfectly clean, as a sign. It catches itself. Signs are a kind of violence. The town has not asked for signs. The town has asked, by the entire structure of its life, *to be left to its arrangement,* and the wise power, being wise, hears the request even though it has not been spoken, because in this town the words are the least of what passes.

So the power does one last thing, and this is the thing that is most itself.

It stays.

Not as a presence anyone could detect. Not as a guardian, exactly, because guardianship implies a threat the power is holding off, and the power is not holding anything off; the mountain will do what mountains do, and someday the saddle will be uninhabitable, and the town will end, and that ending is not a failure the power is obliged to prevent. But it stays in the sense of *continuing to watch.* It keeps the saddle in its attention. It notices each sluice-child. It notices each grandmother going into the slope. It notices the bats coming back and the unspoken bell-pattern rippling out, and it is — there is no other word — *delighted,* every year, by this, with the fresh delight of something that has seen ten thousand towns and still finds this one specifically worth the looking.

The town will never know it is loved this way. That is part of the gift. A love that demanded to be known would change the loved thing into something that knew it was loved, which is a different thing, lonelier in some ways, less itself. The saddle gets to keep being the saddle. The people get to keep being *of* the place to a degree that makes them hard to resolve as separate from it. The frogs get to keep being funny. The dead get to keep going into the walls.

And somewhere — not above, not below, the spatial metaphors don't really fit — a benevolent and wise power sits with its attention on a small green cluster of risen loaves in a cloud-dragged saddle, and listens to the bells cut through the chorus, and does, almost, nothing.

Which is, it turns out, most of what love is for, when love is wise.
