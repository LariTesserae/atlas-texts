The benevolent and wise power considers the Undergarden for a long time before doing anything. This is the first thing to understand about benevolence that has had time to grow wise: it is mostly attention, and most of its attention results in no action at all.

It looks at the brick vault and sees the hands that laid it — not metaphorically; it can see them, because power of this kind reads the past in objects the way we read words on a page. It sees a workshop somewhere above, centuries gone, where the light-plates were quarried from a vein the miners thought was just a curious yellow rock, and it sees the one mason who understood what the rock was for, and it sees that mason's apprentice, who placed the plates, and it follows the apprentice's line down through time to the moment the upper world forgot the Undergarden existed. It sees the forgetting. It does not judge the forgetting. Forgetting is one of the ways the world makes room for things to settle.

It looks at the Keeper, who is in the second chamber, sitting. Not sleeping — sitting. The Keeper is doing the thing the passage at the end described: the quiet nothing of a being who has exhausted the need for doing. The power looks at this and feels something very close to recognition, because the power, too, spends most of its existence in a quiet nothing, and knows the texture of it.

So. What to do.

The first impulse, which the power notes and sets aside, is to *give*. To add. To bring water — no, the place does not want water. To bring company — no, the Keeper is not lonely and would experience company as a wound. To bring news of the upper world — no, the Keeper does not remember the upper world and the news would be a kind of violence, an unasked-for grief. The power notes how many of its first impulses would be, in effect, small acts of harm dressed in the clothes of kindness, and it is grateful, as it always is, for the discipline of waiting before acting.

The second impulse is to *preserve*. To set some ward around the chamber, some guarantee that it will continue. But the power knows that nothing continues by being guaranteed; things continue by continuing, and a guarantee is only another name for a cage. The Undergarden has lasted because it was tended, not because it was protected. The Keeper's mortality — and the Keeper is mortal, the power can see this, in a slow geological way; the Keeper has perhaps another two or three centuries before the dry-flake shedding outpaces the dry-flake making, and then the garden will be alone — the Keeper's mortality is part of what makes the tending real. To remove it would be to remove the Keeper.

The third impulse is to *understand*, and this the power permits itself. It spends some long while — by its own reckoning, which is not ours — simply learning the garden. It learns the clicking-language of the not-swallows, which has fourteen distinct calls, most of them concerning the positions of beetles. It learns the Keeper's internal taxonomy, the private names, the centuries of small griefs over composted rosettes. It learns the chemistry of the gold dust, which is doing something subtler than the Keeper realizes: it is not only feeding the leaves, it is slowly, slowly, over millennia, transmuting the loam itself into something that will, eventually, become a new kind of soil, and the new soil will support a new kind of plant, and the garden five thousand years from now will not be this garden, and that is correct, that is the slow shape the place is making, and the Keeper is its midwife without knowing.

Knowing this, the power feels a great tenderness, which is the form its love takes when it has nothing to do.

And then it does, finally, three small things. Very small. The smallest things it can do and still have done something.

The first: there is a light-plate, near the second door, that has been dimming for the past forty years. The Keeper has not noticed because the dimming is gradual and the Keeper's eyes have adjusted along with it. The plate is nearing the end of its long luminescence — it has perhaps a century left, where the others have millennia. The power does not replace it. It does not refresh it. It simply — and this is a very delicate operation, because the power is being careful not to be a god here, only a neighbor — it nudges the mineral lattice of the plate by a fraction, so that the dimming will continue at exactly its current rate rather than accelerating, as it was about to. The plate will still fail. But it will fail on schedule, gently, over the Keeper's remaining lifetime, dimming so slowly that the moss-shelf above it will have time to migrate to the next plate over, and no creature will be startled, and the Keeper will have the small sad pleasure of watching one light go out at the pace of a life, which is the correct pace for such a thing.

The second: there is a seed, in one of the clay pots, that the Keeper has been saving for a long time without quite knowing why. It is the seed of a plant the Keeper has never seen grown — a parent plant died before the Keeper could learn its habit, and the seed has sat in the pot for ninety years, and the Keeper turns it over sometimes and wonders. The seed is, in fact, viable. It only needs a particular small condition to germinate: a single night of cold, which the Undergarden does not provide. The power, passing through, allows a thread of cool air — no more than the breath of a sleeping child — to settle on the pot for the duration of one of the Keeper's rest-periods. By the next time the Keeper passes the pot, there will be a green thread rising from the soil. The Keeper will see it and stop and crouch down, knees creaking very slightly, and look at it for a long time, and then go and find the rake and lean on it, just standing there, just looking, because something new has happened, and new things happen perhaps once a century down here, and they deserve to be looked at. The power does not need to know what the plant will become. Neither does the Keeper. That is the point.

The third thing the power does is the strangest, and the hardest to describe.

It leaves a small acknowledgment. Not a message — the Keeper has no use for messages, has perhaps forgotten reading, has perhaps never read. Not a gift, exactly. The power simply arranges that, at some moment in the Keeper's near future — perhaps tomorrow, perhaps next month, the power does not specify — the Keeper will be sitting on the low bench, and a clicking-bird will land on the Keeper's shoulder, as they often do, and the Keeper will click back, as they often do, and the bird will click a thing it has never clicked before. A fifteenth call. A new one. Just a small sound, soft, like fingernails on glass but in a slightly different rhythm, and it will mean — to the bird, who will not know why it said it, and to the Keeper, who will not know why they heard it — *you are known*.

That is all. *You are known.* Not by me, the power thinks, fading back from the chamber, withdrawing its long attention with the care of someone closing a door on a sleeping animal. Not by me specifically. By the shape of things. By whatever it is that notices when a small brown being tends a green room beneath a forgotten city for nine hundred years. By the fact that tending is the kind of act that, in a sufficiently old universe, eventually gets noticed, because the universe has had time to develop the faculty of noticing such things, and the faculty has a name, and the name, if it has one, is something like love, and the love does not need to do anything, mostly, but sometimes it leaves a click in a bird's throat, just so that the tending will not have been entirely unwitnessed.

The power goes. The Undergarden continues. The Keeper, in the next room, stirs — not because anything has changed, but because it is time, the way it is always time, to get up and resume.

They push the small door open with a shoulder. They pause, as they always pause. They notice — though they could not say what they notice — that the room is very slightly more itself than it was an hour ago.

They pick up the rake.
