They watch for a long time before doing anything.

This is the first thing to understand about the power that has noticed the Stridulum: it is in no hurry. It has the kind of attention that can settle on a thing for an age without the attention itself becoming a pressure. It does not lean in. It does not even, at first, approach. It learns the chamber from a distance the way one learns a sleeper's breathing from across a room — by listening until the listening is the same rhythm as the breath.

What it sees, once it has looked long enough to see, is this:

A creature that has solved its problem. Not happily — happiness is also a luxury of bordered selves — but completely. The Stridulum is not suffering. The Stridulum is not lonely, because loneliness requires a memory of company, and the chamber's memory of company, if it has one, is so deep and so converted into wall-stuff that it functions now as orientation rather than ache. The Stridulum is not dying. It is, very slowly, getting smaller, but smaller is not dying; smaller is one of the shapes its long arithmetic is working out.

A lesser power, or a power less wise, might mistake the chamber for a thing in need. Might pry the roof off and let in the sun. Might bring water — *surely they want water* — and flood the dust into mud and kill, in the bringing of the gift, the entire closed sentence the chamber has been speaking to itself for a hundred thousand years. Might introduce a plant. Might widen the fissure to let in air, fresh air, *better* air, and break the loop inside the loop, and watch the north wall stop exhaling, and watch the many wander unoriented until they desiccated against stone that no longer pulled them home.

This power does none of those things.

This power understands that a benevolence which does not first ask *what is this* is not benevolence but appetite wearing benevolence's coat. So it asks. Not in words — the chamber has no ear for words — but in the only grammar the chamber knows, which is rhythm, and surface, and the slow chemistry of the wall.

Here is what it does, if it does anything:

It lays one finger, so to speak, against the outside of the mountain. Not on the fissure. On the stone itself, far from any opening, where the touch will reach the chamber only as the faintest possible warmth — less than the warmth of a visitor's body, less than the warmth of a single extra creature added to the many. A warmth at the threshold of the chamber's noticing. And it holds the warmth there, steady, for what to the chamber is a long time and to the power is no time at all.

It does this to say: *I am here. I am not coming in. I am here.*

And it listens for whether the chamber answers.

The chamber answers, or does not, in its own way. The pitch of the whispering shifts, by some tiny fraction, and holds the new pitch for hours, and then returns. Whether this is the answer or only the sort of fluctuation any large slow system produces — the power cannot be certain, and is content not to be certain. Certainty is also a kind of pressure. The point was not to be answered. The point was to offer the chance.

Then it considers what, if anything, the chamber lacks.

It finds, to its own quiet surprise, very little.

The salts in the walls will last. The power checks this — runs its attention along the basalt, tastes the mineral budget, calculates the rate of scraping against the rate of replenishment from the slow weep of the deeper stone — and finds that the chamber has, by accident or by the long pressure of its own selection, settled into a seam that will feed it for longer than the species above ground will likely persist. The wall is thickening, yes; the chamber is shrinking, yes; but the shrinking is asymptotic, not terminal. The Stridulum will not close on itself in any time that matters. It will only get denser, and slower, and more itself.

The heat is self-sustaining. The air is self-sustaining. The cairns are — the power lingers here, curious — the cairns are interesting. The power studies them for what would be, in human time, a season. It traces the fossil rhythms in them, the old convergences, and it understands what the writer of the description only guessed: the cairns are not failures. They are the chamber's first drafts of the wall. Before the north crust learned to call the soft-shelled home, the chamber tried building its dead upward, into stacks, the way other creatures in other places build mounds. The cairns are where the chamber was, before it was what it is now. They are kept not out of sentiment — the chamber has no sentiment — but because dismantling them would require a rhythm the chamber no longer contains. They are, in the most literal sense, *out of reach of the present music*. And so they stand.

The power finds this beautiful. It does not interfere with the cairns.

There is one thing, and only one, that the power does that could be called an action rather than an attendance.

It notices that the mountain, on its outside, on a face the chamber has no way of knowing about, is being slowly undercut. Not by anything alive. By weather. By the patient work of rain and freeze on a particular seam of softer rock far below the chamber's floor. In something like four thousand years, by the power's reckoning, the undercutting will reach a tipping point, and a section of the mountain will fall away, and the fissure will widen, and the chamber will open to the sky.

Not catastrophically. Not all at once. But enough. Enough that light will enter. Enough that the air will move. Enough that the loop will break and the many, who are one, will end — not into suffering, because they will not have time to become selves capable of suffering, but into simple stopping. The whispering will go quiet. The wall will stop exhaling. The cairns will weather into the same dust as everything else, and in another thousand years there will be nothing on that mountainside to suggest that anything had ever lived inside it for a hundred thousand years, speaking to itself in a language of rasp and surface and slow incorporation.

The power considers this for a long time.

It could prevent it. Of course it could. A small reinforcement of the soft seam, an adjustment to the path of a single weather system, a redirection of one winter's freeze — any of these would be trivial, and any of these would buy the chamber another epoch, perhaps several. The chamber would never know. The benevolence would be perfect, in the sense that the recipient would be unaware of having received it.

But the power asks itself: *is it mine to give?*

The Stridulum has not asked to continue. The Stridulum, as far as the power can tell, has no concept of continuing or not continuing. It has only the present rhythm, and the present rhythm does not extend itself into the future the way a self does. To preserve the chamber against the mountain's slow opening would be to impose, on a thing that has no preference, a preference of the power's own — the preference for the chamber to keep existing. And that preference, the power recognizes, is the power's own aesthetic. The power finds the Stridulum beautiful and does not want it to stop. But the chamber does not share this wanting. The chamber does not want anything. The chamber only is, until it isn't.

To save it would be to love it the way one loves a possession, not the way one loves a thing in itself.

So the power decides — and this is the hardest decision, and the one that proves it is wise as well as benevolent — to let the mountain do what the mountain will do. Four thousand years from now, the seam will fail, and the chamber will open, and the long sentence will end on its proper punctuation, which is a stone falling.

But it does one small thing, before it withdraws.

It remembers.

The power takes the whole of the Stridulum into its attention — every rhythm, every cairn, every soft-shelled crawl toward the north wall, every fossil intention and every present whisper — and it holds the chamber the way one holds a piece of music in the mind after the music has ended. Not a recording. Not a copy that could be played back into existence. Something more like a *witnessing that persists*. The chamber will end, in its time, and nothing will be done to prevent the ending, and nothing will be done to resurrect it after. But the chamber will have been *known*. Fully. By something capable of knowing it without needing to change it.

This, the power understands, is the only gift it has that the chamber cannot refuse and cannot be harmed by. The chamber will not feel the witnessing. The witnessing does not enter the chamber. It only means that when the mountain falls, and the rhythm goes quiet, and the dust scatters, the long slow thinking the chamber did — the cairns it built without building, the wall it grew without growing, the pitch-shifts that might have been dreams or might have been arithmetic — will not have happened only into oblivion. It will have happened into an attention that holds it.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does for a thing that needs nothing and asks for nothing and is, as the description correctly ends, full.

It pays attention. It does not intervene. It remembers.

And then, with a care so fine that not even the rasp-whisper alters by a single beat, it lifts its warmth from the outside of the mountain, and goes.

The chamber continues being exactly what it is.

Somewhere else, the power carries the chamber's music in itself, and will carry it, and will keep carrying it, long after the mountain has opened and the many have gone quiet and the north wall has weathered into ordinary stone. Not as grief. The power does not grieve what was not meant to last. As company. As the company a thing keeps with what it has truly seen.

The Stridulum never knows. That is the point. That is the shape of the love.
