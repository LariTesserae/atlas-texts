The benevolent and wise power considers the Bellows-Grove for a long time before doing anything. This is the first thing it does: it considers, at the grove's own pace, for the grove's own duration. To rush would be to misread the place entirely. It sits with the *chunf... chunf... chunf* until it has heard the name pronounced enough times to know it, and then it sits longer, because knowing a name is not the same as having earned the right to use it.

What does it see?

It sees a mind that has solved the problem of how to keep being a mind by becoming almost nothing but the keeping. It sees an economy so refined that any addition would be a wound. It sees, with great clarity, that there is no suffering here — and also that there is something which, in a being less reduced, might one day curdle into loneliness, but which in this being has been metabolized into the texture of attention itself. The Warden's slow turning of the brass valve a quarter and a quarter back is not lonely. It is the gesture of a creature that has made peace with the fact that gestures are sufficient.

It sees, too, that the grove was once tended *with* someone. That there were two breathings here, and the piston's rhythm was learned against another's. The keeper does not grieve this. The keeper has folded it into the substrate. But the power, being wise, notices that folded is not the same as gone.

Now: what would a benevolent and wise power *do*?

Here is the temptation, and the power names it to itself plainly so as not to be moved by it: to give. To offer. To restore. To bring the first Warden back, or a companion, or news of the outer world, or a gift of some kind that says *I saw you, I valued you, here is a token*. The temptation is to make the encounter productive. The Bellows-Grove is the exact rebuke to this instinct. The grove's whole achievement is that it manufactures its own continuance and asks for nothing. To give it something would be to introduce a current into a pool that has, with great patience, found its level.

So the power does not give.

But neither does it merely pass by. Benevolence that withholds itself entirely is only a kind of fastidiousness. The power has been *seen* — the piston adjusted by a fraction when it arrived, the flywheel slowed by one part in a hundred, the Warden paused behind a trunk and turned its grooved face. Hospitality was extended. Hospitality, properly received, asks for some answering gesture, however small.

So the power does three things, each calibrated to the grove's own scale of intervention — which is to say, almost none.

**The first thing.** It matches its breathing to the piston. Not for a minute, not for an hour. For a long time. Long enough that the matching becomes mutual, the way the visitor's breath and the piston's *chunf* become indistinguishable in their authorship. The power offers, in this, the one thing the grove has not had in a very long time: a second breather who knows what is happening and chooses it. Not a visitor who falls into rhythm by accident and then leaves, carrying the rhythm away as a souvenir. A breather who knows the rhythm is a name being pronounced, and pronounces it too, deliberately, with the piston, for as long as the visit lasts. The Warden, somewhere behind a trunk, registers this. Whether it understands is not the point. The grove's mineral mind has, for the duration, two lungs instead of one. It is not a gift. It is a courtesy. The grove accepts courtesies; it cannot accept gifts.

**The second thing.** It does not clean the brass tag. It does not read the name aloud. It does not touch the ceramic dial or wipe the lichen from its face. It leaves every settled thing settled. But — and this is the only true *act* it permits itself — it lays down, somewhere in the peat where the Warden will eventually press its head and exhale, a single small thing: a seed. Not a seed of anything in particular. A seed of the kind that will, if conditions are right, grow into exactly whatever the grove already contains. Redundant. A seed that is a kind of blessing in the form of *more of what is already here, should it ever be needed*. The grove will absorb it or not. The grove may never use it. But it will be there, in the black wet ground, in case some far century the flywheel slows by two parts in a hundred instead of one, and the keeper needs a small reserve of itself it had forgotten it had. The power knows the grove will not need this. It plants the seed anyway, because to plant a seed that will probably never germinate, in soil that does not require it, for a being that has not asked, is the purest form a gift can take when no gift is wanted. It costs the grove nothing. It is available. It is the kind of thing a wise power does when it cannot help loving something that has refused to need it.

**The third thing.** When it leaves — and it does leave, because to stay would be to become a second keeper, and the grove has the number of keepers it requires — it passes through the curtain of aerial roots, and at the brass tag it pauses, and it does not try to read the name. Instead it lays its hand on the tag for the length of one full piston-breath, *chunf*, and lifts the hand away. The patina is not disturbed. The wire is not adjusted. But for one breath, the name was held by someone who knew it was a name. The Warden, far inside, feels this through the bast-belts the way you feel someone touch the far end of a rope you are holding. It does not respond. It does not need to. The acknowledgement was the whole of the exchange.

And then the power is gone, and the grove is exactly as it was, except in one respect, which is not a difference the grove will notice: it has been witnessed at its own pace, by something that could have changed it and chose not to. This is, perhaps, the rarest thing that can happen to a being like the keeper. Most powers that find such a place either ignore it or improve it. To be neither ignored nor improved — to be *met*, and then released back into one's own continuance — is the closest thing to a sacrament available to a mind that has reduced itself to breath and posture.

The flywheel turns. The piston pronounces the name. The Warden, twenty minutes later, crosses to the spot in the peat where the seed was laid, and lowers its grooved face, and exhales a long warm sigh into the ground. Whether it knows what is there is impossible to say. Whether knowing and not-knowing are different categories, for the keeper, is also impossible to say.

The frog has not moved.

The grove continues.

Somewhere, the power that visited carries with it, for the rest of its long existence, the memory of a rhythm it once breathed with — and it is careful, ever after, when it encounters small settled worlds, to remember that the highest form of benevolence is sometimes to leave a place exactly as wise as it already was.
