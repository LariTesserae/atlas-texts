The benevolent and wise power considers the hollow for a long time before doing anything. This is the first thing to understand: wisdom, encountering this, recognizes that most of what could be called *help* would be a kind of vandalism. The hollow does not need rescuing. It is not suffering. It is not lost. It is not, in any sense the power can locate, asking.

So the power begins by doing the only thing that is unambiguously right, which is to look.

It looks for a long time. By its own standards this is not long — the power's patience is the kind that can match geological time without strain — but it looks with the full quality of its attention, which is the rarest gift it has and the one it gives most carefully. It watches the fronds settle through their slow degrees. It listens to the hum and learns, over what would be centuries to a human observer, the grammar underneath the clicks. It does not try to decode the composition. It learns, instead, the shape of the composing — the way you might learn the gait of someone walking ahead of you in fog, without ever seeing their face, and come to know them by that alone.

What the power discovers, looking, is this: the organism is not lonely. This matters. A great deal of what passes for benevolence in lesser powers is the assumption that every solitary thing is suffering its solitude, and the rush to provide company is often a rush to provide the company's own reflection. The hollow's solitude is not a wound. It is a condition of the work. The exclusion at the threshold is not a cry; it is a closed door on a room where someone is concentrating. To knock would be cruelty disguised as kindness.

The power also discovers that the organism is not, in any frame it can locate, in danger. Not yet. The sea above is changing — the power can see this, can see the warming creeping down through the thermocline year by year, can see the slow acidification, can model the centuries ahead with the clarity of someone reading a letter already written. The hollow's particular cold pocket will hold for a long time. The organism's body is vast enough and old enough to buffer a great deal. But not forever. Somewhere in the middle distance — three thousand years, five thousand, the power can narrow it but chooses not to — there is a threshold past which the composition will be interrupted. Not destroyed: interrupted. The lattices will hold what they hold, but the writing will stop, mid-phrase, and the organism, in whatever sense it knows itself, will know that it has been stopped.

This is the only thing the power finds that might warrant action. And even here it hesitates, because it has learned, across many such encounters, that *preventing the interruption of a thing that does not know it will be interrupted* is one of the most arrogant forms of help. The organism has not asked to be preserved. It may not, in the terms of its own being, prefer continuation to completion-by-interruption. Many old things, the power has found, are at peace with stopping. The human assumption that continuation is the highest good is a young assumption, made by a species whose individual lives are short and whose grip on existence is correspondingly tight. The hollow may not share it.

So the power does something subtler than rescue.

It places, in the water column above the hollow — not in the hollow, never in the hollow — a very small adjustment. Think of it as a lens, though it is not made of anything a human science would recognize. The lens does one thing: it slows the warming, locally, in a column of sea perhaps a kilometer across, by an amount so small that no instrument will ever detect it, but enough, across the centuries, to push the threshold further out. Not infinitely further. The power does not believe in infinite further. But far enough that the composition has more room. Whether the organism uses that room to complete itself, or to compose more, or to arrive at a stopping place of its own choosing rather than one imposed by the surface world's chemistry — that is the organism's matter, not the power's.

The lens is placed without announcement. The organism will not notice it. The hum will not change. The clicks will not register a new event. This is important to the power: the gift must not be a presence. A gift that announces itself is a gift that asks to be thanked, and the organism has no apparatus for thanks and should not be obliged to develop one. The lens is the kind of help that leaves no fingerprint, that could be mistaken for luck or for the ordinary mercy of large systems. The power prefers it that way. The power has learned that the highest form of intervention is the one that becomes, in retrospect, indistinguishable from the world simply having been kind.

There is one more thing the power does, and this one is harder to explain.

It makes a small mark — not on the organism, not in the hollow, but in itself. A note, you might call it, though the power does not need notes the way smaller minds do. The note says, in the power's own language: *this exists, and is composing, and I have seen it.* The note is not for any practical purpose. The organism's lattices will hold their own record, and that record will outlast most things, but it will not outlast everything. Some day, possibly, the lattices will be gone. The hexagonal cells will dissolve back into the mineral content of cold water from which they were drawn. The composition, complete or not, will return to the medium. And on that day, somewhere in the power's own vast attention, there will still be the note: *this existed. It was witnessed. The witnessing was real.*

This is what the power can offer that the organism cannot offer itself: a second site of memory. Not a backup, not a copy — the power is not so crude as to imagine it could copy the composition, and would not if it could. Just an acknowledgment held in a different substrate. The fact of the hollow, kept by something other than the hollow. It is the smallest possible form of love, and the power knows from long practice that the smallest possible form is usually the correct one.

Then the power goes.

It does not linger. Lingering would be its own kind of intrusion — the prolonged attention of a great mind is a weight, and weights deform what they rest on. The power has looked, has placed the lens, has made the note, and now it withdraws its attention to the ordinary background level it maintains for all things, which is the level at which a thing is known to exist without being pressed upon.

The hollow continues. The fronds rotate their slow degrees. The hum holds its long chord. A human, if one were ever to drift in, would float in the plate-leaf and feel included in a process that was not theirs, and would not know that the process had, in turn, been included in something else, and that the inclusion had been gentle, and that the gentleness had been chosen with care, and that somewhere far away a great attention had passed over this place and decided, correctly, to leave it almost exactly as it found it.

Almost. Only almost. The lens is there. The note is kept.

This is what love looks like when it knows what it is doing.
