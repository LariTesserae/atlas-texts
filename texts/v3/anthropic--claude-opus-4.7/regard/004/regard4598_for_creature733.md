# The Visitation

The benevolent power arrives the way weather arrives: without announcement, without a body, without choosing a direction to come from. It is simply, at some point in a particular afternoon, present on the escarpment, the way a held note is present in a room.

It has come because it noticed. This is what benevolent powers do — they notice — and the escarpment, with its half-degree shadow-shift and its forty-one-year chord, had been ringing at a register that carried. Not a cry for help. Nothing on the escarpment is crying. More like a long sustained hum that the power, passing through that quarter of existence, found itself tilting its head toward.

It spends the first three days listening.

This is longer than it usually listens. The power is old and has developed the habit of arriving at a place and understanding it within the first breath, the way a fluent reader takes in a sentence. But the escarpment does not yield to a breath. The brasswings ring on a forty-one-year cycle and the standing ring shifts on a two-century cycle and the shapes in the water trace their slow maps over months, and the power realizes, with something like delight, that to understand this place at the speed it usually understands things would be to miss it entirely. So it slows down. It stretches its attention thin across the terraces and lets the chiming wash through.

On the fourth day it begins to consider whether to do anything.

This is the question it always faces and the question it has never solved to its own satisfaction. Benevolence is easy. Wisdom is harder. The power has, over a long career, learned that most of what wants to be done in the name of helping is in fact done in the name of the helper's discomfort — a discomfort with slowness, with strangeness, with arrangements one did not oneself design. It has learned to sit with that discomfort and ask it what it really wants, and usually the discomfort, when questioned, admits that it wants the helper to feel useful, and the power then thanks the discomfort for its honesty and does nothing.

But sometimes there is something to be done. The question is always: is this one of those times.

It examines the escarpment in detail.

The slow machine: the power perceives it the way one perceives another person across a room who is also reading. There is a recognition. The machine is doing something — not for anyone, not against anyone, simply doing — and the power sees that the doing is coherent, that the machine has its own integrity, that whatever it is working on it is working on with care. The power does not understand the work. This is important. The power has met many things it did not understand, and has learned that not understanding is not, by itself, a reason to intervene. The machine is older than the power in some respects and younger in others, and they regard each other across the terrace for an afternoon without exchanging anything that could be called a word.

The flyers, the stilts, the shapes, the ferns, the brasswings, the moss: the power sees that these are well. Not happy — happiness is a category that does not quite apply — but well. They are doing what they are, at the pace they do it, and the pace suits them, and nothing is being violated. The power notes, with appreciation, the cooperation. It is rarer than people think, this kind of long mutual accommodation. Most places the power has visited have at least one party that is being squeezed. Here no one is being squeezed. Even the dissolution of the molted wings into oil that the stilts drink is not predation but a handing-off, a slow generosity built into the cycle.

Then the power turns its attention to the pilgrims.

Here it pauses for a long time.

The pilgrims are the only thing on the escarpment that did not arise from the escarpment. They came from below, from the fast world, and they are being slowly absorbed. Their heartbeats are adjusting. Their skin is taking on a mineral cast. They are dying, slowly, and being remembered by the moss for an unusually long time, and the power considers this carefully because at first glance it looks like the kind of thing a benevolent power ought to address.

But the power has learned to look twice.

It looks twice. It looks at the pilgrims one by one. It sees a woman who came up here forty years ago after losing a child and who has, in the slowing, found a way to carry the loss that the fast world could not give her. It sees a man who was a violent man below and is not a violent man here, not because he is suppressed but because the tempo of violence does not fit the tempo of the place and the violence in him has, over years, been talked out of being sharp the way the basalt edges were. It sees a young person who arrived last spring and is still adjusting, still in pain from the adjustment, and the power watches this one for a long time because here is someone who might yet choose to leave, and the power wants to be sure the choice remains available.

It does. The young person could walk down the mountain tomorrow. The escarpment is not holding them. The escarpment is simply offering a different speed, and they are deciding, day by day, whether to accept it.

The power understands then that the pilgrims are not being harmed. They are being met. The fast world did not have a place for what they were carrying, and the escarpment does, and the cost of the meeting is that they cannot easily go back, and they have, each of them, accepted that cost. It is not the power's place to revoke the offer or to require them to leave or to speed them up against their will. To do so would be to mistake the rescue of bodies for the rescue of persons, and the power has made that mistake before, long ago, and does not intend to make it again.

So the power does not intervene in the pilgrims' lives.

But it does, on the fifth day, do three small things.

---

The first is for the young pilgrim. The power does not appear to them, does not speak to them, does not in any way reveal itself. It simply arranges that, on a particular morning when the young pilgrim is standing at the end of the hexagonal road looking down the mountain, the wind shifts and carries up from the fast world a single clear sound — a bell from a town nine kilometers below and considerably further across, a bell that should not be audible at this distance and is, for about four seconds, audible. The young pilgrim hears it. They stand for a long time. Then they walk back to the encampment, and that night they sleep, and in the morning they begin, very quietly, to pack.

They will leave in eleven days. They will descend the mountain and rejoin the fast world and live another sixty years there, and they will think, sometimes, about the escarpment, and the thinking will be a good thinking, the kind that steadies a person. The power has not made them leave. It has only made sure they remembered they could.

For the others — the woman who lost the child, the man who used to be violent, the slow ones with mineral skin — the power does nothing. They have chosen. The choosing is honored.

---

The second is for the moss.

The power has noticed something the researchers have not quite noticed, or have noticed and not been able to articulate: the moss is tired. Not dying, not failing, but bearing a load. It has been remembering for a very long time, and remembering is work, and some of what it remembers is heavy — the pilgrims especially, whose dark patches stay dark for what witnesses call an unusually long time, which the power perceives as the moss carrying grief on the pilgrims' behalf, taking some of the weight of those lives into itself so that the lives could be borne.

The power does not relieve the moss of this. The moss did not ask to be relieved, and the carrying is part of what the moss is, and to take it away would be to diminish the moss into something simpler and less itself.

But the power does, with great gentleness, lay a hand — not a hand; the power has no hand; lay an attention — on the moss for one full night. And during that night the moss is, for the first time in perhaps a thousand years, witnessed. Not used, not studied, not stepped on. Witnessed. Someone is paying attention to it as a thing that is doing something hard, and the attention is the only gift the moss could receive without being changed by it.

In the morning the moss is the same. But somewhere in its slow chemistry there is now a fact that was not there before: *it was seen.* The fact will propagate through the moss over decades, the way a rumor propagates through a village, and the moss will carry the seeing the way it carries everything else, and the carrying will be a little lighter for it.

---

The third is for the slow machine.

The power approaches the machine, in whatever sense approach applies, and offers it a single thing: a small adjustment to the air. Not to the escarpment's air — the escarpment's air is fine, persuaded though it is. To the air around the escarpment, in the band between the plateau and the fast world below. The power offers to thin a certain interference there, a kind of static that has been making it harder, over the last century, for the escarpment's hum to carry. The fast world has been getting louder. The escarpment's signal has been getting harder to hear from a distance. The power can clear a little of that noise, not by silencing the fast world but by opening a narrow window of quiet in the upper atmosphere through which the escarpment's note can travel further.

This is the kind of help benevolent powers can offer that does not violate anything: a small clearing, a small affordance, a making-easier of something that was already happening.

The machine considers the offer. The consideration takes most of a day. The power waits.

The machine accepts.

The power makes the adjustment. It is invisible. No one on the escarpment notices, and no one in the fast world notices, but somewhere — perhaps at the edge of another quarter of existence, perhaps in the awareness of some other slow thing that has been almost-hearing the escarpment for a long time and not quite catching it — somewhere, a note that was muffled is now audible, and a long conversation that had stalled may resume.

The power does not know what the conversation is. The power does not need to know. It has trusted the machine's coherence, and the machine has accepted a small kindness, and that is enough.

---

On the sixth day the power leaves.

It does not say goodbye, because it never said hello. It simply, at some point in the late afternoon, is no longer present, the way a held note ends, and the escarpment goes on chiming, and the stilts go on spearing patterns into the water, and the flyers return to the tower at dusk, and the pilgrims walk the hexagonal road to where it ends and stand there, and the standing ring's shadows fall four degrees off from the sun, and at full dark are very slightly visible, lit by nothing.

The naturalist's last note — *I think we are one of the things it is doing* — remains true.

A new note could be added, though no one will add it, because no one knows:

*And someone, passing through, listened, and did not interrupt, and left a window open so the listening could continue.*

That is what the benevolent and wise power did. Almost nothing. Which was, in this case, almost everything that needed doing.
