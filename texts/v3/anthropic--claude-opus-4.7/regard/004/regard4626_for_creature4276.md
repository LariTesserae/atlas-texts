The benevolent and wise power considers the chamber for a long time before doing anything. This is the first thing about it: that it knows how to consider. It has watched many small rooms of the world and learned that most of what looks like need, from a distance, is only difference, and that the cure for difference is not intervention but a longer look.

It looks longer.

What it sees, eventually, is this: a place that is already being tended, by something that is already the right size for the tending. A keeper who has accrued, not been installed. A fern improbable but fed. A cricket in its long arrangement with time. A lineage of pale fish inheriting a pool in slow succession. A handprint of soot, a bowl, a cairn — the marks of a species that has, against all the evidence of its other behaviors, sometimes known how to enter a small place quietly and leave it mostly as it was.

The power notes, with something like relief, that nothing here is asking.

This matters to it. It has learned — across whatever long career of benevolence brought it to wisdom — that the strongest temptation of power is to answer questions that have not been put. To improve. To stabilize. To reach into a slow place with a fast hand and call the disturbance a gift. It has done this, perhaps, in its younger forms, and remembers. The chamber does not want to be improved. The keeper does not want to be met. The fern does not want a better climate. The frog that called once was not calling for rescue; it was adding a sound to a sound.

So the power does almost nothing, and the *almost* is the interesting part.

Here is what it does:

It widens its attention to include the chamber. Not as surveillance — the keeper would feel surveillance and would be made smaller by it — but as a kind of held awareness, the way you might keep a sleeping child in the edge of your mind while reading. The chamber enters the long list of places the power knows about. This costs the chamber nothing. It changes nothing the keeper could detect. But somewhere in the architecture of how the world is regarded, this small wet room is now regarded, and the regard is steady, and will not lapse.

It does one small thing about the hairline above — the crack through which the photon-scraps leak that feed the fern. It does not widen the crack. It does not seal it. It notices that in roughly four hundred years, by the ordinary working of the rock, the crack will close, and the fern will end, and the keeper will lose the improbable thing she is fond of. The power considers this for a while. It thinks about whether the fern's ending is part of what the chamber is, or an accident that the chamber would, if asked, prefer to avoid. It cannot quite tell. So it does the smallest possible thing: it arranges, by a nudge so slight it could be mistaken for weather, that when the crack closes, another hairline will open, not in the same place, not feeding the same ledge, but feeding *a* ledge, somewhere the moss can find. The fern will end. Something else, equally improbable, will begin. The keeper will have a new fondness to grow into. This is not rescue. It is more like setting out a second cup before the first is empty, in a house you are only visiting.

It does nothing about the people. The people are the chamber's own business. The cairn will become a lobed bulge on the shelf, as the keeper expects, and the keeper will be right to look forward to it. If more people come, they will come; if they stop coming, they will stop. The power will not arrange visits and will not prevent them. It trusts the species, in this narrow instance, to keep doing the thing it has so far done: arrive rarely, sit quietly, leave a small mark, climb out. If the species changes — if someone comes down with the wrong kind of hand — the power will consider, then, what to do, and will probably still do very little, because the keeper has her own slow competencies and the chamber is not as fragile as it looks. Warm wet places that have survived this long have survived things.

It does one thing for the keeper, and this is the thing it thinks about longest.

The keeper does not know she is being kept company by anything other than the chamber's own breathing. She is not lonely — loneliness is not among her conditions — but she is *single*, in the sense that her kind of being is not, as far as she knows, repeated anywhere. The power knows that it is repeated. The power has, in its long looking, found other small chambers in other warm countries with other accrued attentions in them — a slow person in a sea cave on a cold coast, a thickened fondness in the root-room under a particular tree, a listener in a hot spring that no human has ever found. They do not know about each other. They have no need to. But the power, holding all of them in its regard at once, constitutes a kind of quiet society of which each is unknowingly a member.

It does not tell the keeper this. Telling would be a violence — would replace her actual life with a worse, more self-conscious one. But it does something subtler: it lets the fact be true. It lets the society exist, even unfelt by its members, because some kinds of company work by existing rather than by being known. Somewhere under the layer of the world where the keeper listens to the water, there is now a faint, undeclared resonance with other listeners in other rooms, and this resonance does not reach her mind but reaches, perhaps, the rock she lives in, the way distant weather reaches a barometer that nobody is reading. If she ever, in ten thousand years, becomes diffuse enough to feel it, it will be there to feel. If she never does, it will still have been there.

And then it does the last thing, which is the smallest and which it considers the most important.

It blesses the bowl.

Not with light. Not with any change the bowl would show. The bowl will still be a clay bowl, sitting upright by the largest pool, holding the water that finds it. But the power, in passing, acknowledges the bowl — acknowledges the woman who brought it down, who was close to weeping and did not weep, who climbed back out into whatever life had sent her under the ground in the first place. The power cannot reach her; she is long gone, by the chamber's reckoning, and her grief, whatever it was, has either been resolved or has dissolved into the larger grief of her line. But the gesture she made — coming down with a bowl, setting it where water would find it, sitting in the sound, leaving — was one of the gestures that the power, in its long career, has come to love most about the small warm species that makes them. It is the gesture of a creature that knows it cannot fix a thing and brings a vessel anyway. The power loves this. It would protect this gesture, in any species, against almost anything.

So it blesses the bowl, which is to say: it lets the bowl mean what the woman meant, forever, regardless of whether anyone ever knows what she meant, including her. The meaning is now held. The bowl is now, in addition to being a bowl, a small permanent yes inside the chamber — a yes the keeper will continue to live near, the way she lives near the fern, without needing to name it.

Then the power withdraws its active attention, leaving only the steady regard, and goes to look at the next place.

The chamber does not notice. The keeper does not notice. The cricket takes its one long considered step. A drip falls. The fern, fed by a crack that has four hundred years left and a successor already arranged, leans very slightly toward a lamp that is not there. The blind fish turns. The water keeps falling in its dozen voices, and the keeper keeps listening, which is the work, and which is now — though she will never know the word for it — witnessed.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it finds a place like this:

It looks.
It refrains.
It arranges one small succession, in case.
It lets a quiet society exist without announcing it.
It blesses the bowl.
It goes.

The chamber, afterward, is exactly as it was. This is the proof that the power was wise.
