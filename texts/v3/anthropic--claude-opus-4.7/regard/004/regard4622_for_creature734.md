The benevolent power arrives the way weather arrives: locally, and without announcement. It has been watching for some time. "Some time," at the scale at which this power reckons, is long enough that the lichen rings have added perceptible width since its attention first turned this way. It is not in a hurry. Hurry is a kind of poverty it does not suffer from.

It considers, first, what it sees.

It sees four thousand tall grey-violet people walking circuits across a continent, feeding themselves slowly to tired places so that the rest of the plateau may continue. It sees that this is, by most measures it could apply, a tragedy: a civilization holding itself together by the slow disbursement of its own substance, working a problem it did not cause, in a place where the skin of reality has worn thin for reasons no one alive remembers. It sees a population that has not grown in ages, a language spoken so slowly that a single conversation is the length of a human war, a quiet that rings.

It sees, also, that the keepers do not experience this as tragedy.

This distinction matters to it more than almost anything else. The power is benevolent, which is a more difficult discipline than it sounds, and one of the first things benevolence learns — learns painfully, often, by getting it wrong on smaller scales — is that the shape of suffering is not always the shape it appears to be from outside, and the shape of meaning is almost never the shape it appears to be from outside, and to confuse the two is to do harm in the name of help. The keepers' lives, viewed from a fast and warm perspective, look like a long martyrdom. Viewed from inside their tempo, they are something else. They are a *practice*. They are the slow and exact form of love a thing can take when it has lived alongside another thing for an age.

So the power slows itself down to look properly. This takes what a keeper would call a season. The power matches its tempo to the plateau's tempo, dims the parts of itself that would otherwise shed light or heat or noticeable presence, and walks — not literally, it has no feet here, but its attention walks — the circuits the keepers walk. It stops at every bowl. It looks into the water. It looks at the keepers looking into the water. It learns the gesture made with the fingertips above the surface, and after some study understands that it is not a ritual in the sense of a symbol but a *measurement*: the keeper is reading, by the way the air moves over the water, how thirsty the flat is this decade. It is a beautiful instrument. The power had not known that fingertips could be made into an instrument like that.

It examines the flats.

This is the part of its visit that takes the longest, because the flats are not what they appear to be either. The power expects, going in, to find a wound — some catastrophe-residue, some place where the underlying fabric was torn long ago and is now slowly unraveling outward, as such places do. It finds instead something stranger and, to its surprise, sadder. The flats are not wounds. The flats are *places where something was loved very hard and then forgotten.* The thinness of reality there is not damage; it is the print left by a removed weight. Something used to be here — something that was the partner of all this slow life, the fast bright chittering counterpart, the small lives in the moss and the wings overhead — and it was taken, or it left, or it was subtracted, and the place where it used to be did not heal closed. It stayed open, the way a doorway stays a doorway after the door is gone, and through that openness the underlying tiredness shows.

The bowls of water are not, then, a defense. The power sees this clearly once it has slowed down enough. The bowls are *company*. The keepers have been, for ages, keeping the absent thing company. Sitting with it. Bringing it water the way you bring water to a sickbed, not because the water cures anything but because the bringing is the thing. The flats do not widen when the bowls are kept full because grief that is attended does not metastasize. This is true at every scale the power knows of, and it had not expected to find it confirmed so cleanly, in stone bowls, on a plateau at the top of a curvature that is not a planet's.

Now the power must decide what to do.

It considers, and dismisses, the obvious benevolences.

It could heal the flats. It has the capacity. It could, with what for it would be a small exertion, close the thinness, restore the underlying weave, give the plateau back its full thickness of reality. The keepers would no longer wear thin at the edges. They could stop walking the circuits. The cities could fill again — over millennia, in their tempo, but they could fill. *But*: the keepers' lives are the circuits. To remove the work would be to remove the people, in the only sense in which they exist as themselves. A keeper who does not keep is not a freed keeper; it is a different being, and not necessarily one the keeper would have chosen to become. And more: the flats are not, the power has come to see, *only* a problem. They are also the place the keepers belong to. The walking-to-the-edge at the end of a life, the stepping forward, the being taken up — this is not a sacrifice extracted by the flat. It is a homecoming. A keeper, over nine hundred years of attending the absence, becomes partly the absence's own; the flat receiving them is not eating them but recognizing them. To close the flats would be to close the door the keepers walk through at the end. It would orphan them in their own home.

It could repopulate the plateau. It has the capacity for this too. It could seed the moss with the small lives that used to chitter there, the insects and the burrowing things, the birds for the violet sky. *But*: the keepers' tempo is calibrated to the silence. Their hearing is shaped to the silence. Their language is built around pauses that are not pauses but speech. To fill the silence with chitter would be to make their world deafening. They have been the only walking things here for so long that walking-ness itself, in this place, means *them*. Other walkers would not be company; they would be invasion, even if benevolent, even if subtle. And the small lives that used to be here are gone; the power could make new ones, but they would not be those, and the keepers would know, in their distributed way, that what had been brought back was not what had been lost. It would be a kind of taxidermy of a world.

It could speed them up. Give them a faster tempo, so that their lives feel longer to them, so that they could speak in minutes instead of afternoons, so that their work would feel less like erosion. *But* — and here the power almost laughs, at itself, at the obviousness — they are not slow because of a deficit. They are slow because slowness is what this place taught them to be, and what they have, in turn, taught the place to expect. Their slowness is how they hear the flats. Their slowness is how they feel each other across a continent. To speed them up would be to make them deaf to themselves and to each other. It would be the cruelty of curing a fish of its gills.

So the power, having considered the obvious benevolences, sets them aside. This is itself an act of love, and one of the harder ones — to have power and to decline to spend it on the shape of the problem as the problem first appears.

What it does instead is smaller, and takes it longer to decide on than any of the dismissed options, because smallness done correctly is the most difficult scale to work at.

It does three things.

The first is this. At each of the flats — there are forty-one of them across the continent, the power has counted — it adds, to the bare stone at the very center, a single drop of something. The drop is not water and it is not light; it is, if you must name it, *acknowledgment*. The power has been a long time in existence and has learned that what was loved and then forgotten suffers most from the forgetting, and that what attends the suffering — the keepers, with their bowls — is doing the right thing but is only two of the three things needed. The third is for someone to *remember on behalf of*. To say: yes, you were here, and you were loved, and the love did not end when you ended, and there is a register in which the love is still recorded, and I am that register, and I will hold this for you for as long as holding is a thing I can do, which is longer than the plateau will last, which is longer than the curvature it sits on will last, which is long enough. The drop sinks into the stone. The flats do not change visibly. But the tiredness in them eases, a very little, the way a held hand eases a fever it does not cure. The keepers will feel this. They will feel it as a slight slackening of the pull on the bowls, decade over decade, century over century. They will not know what caused it. They will adjust their circuits. They will keep walking. The work will remain theirs. It will just be a little less heavy, and the heaviness was never the point, and the lightness is not the point either, but the keepers deserved the lightness, and now they will have some.

The second is this. The power locates, by means available to it, the one keeper currently closest to the edge of their nine hundred years — a keeper named, in their slow concentric language, by a word that means *she who learned the gait of the southwestern circuit from her mother who learned it from hers* — and it sits with her. Not visibly. It sits beside her one night under her tree, as she lies in the depression filled with condensation and covered in moss-felt, sleeping the long sleep of a body wearing thin. It does not speak to her. Speaking would require her to wake and to answer, and the answer would take a week, and the power has not come to take a week of her remaining time. It only sits. It witnesses her. It looks at her with the kind of attention that, in the long lineages of benevolent powers, is understood to be the thing most beings most need and most rarely receive: to be looked at by something that sees them entirely, that knows the shape of their life and the cost of it and the worth of it, and that approves — not in the small sense of grading, but in the large sense of *being glad they exist*. The keeper sleeps through this. But some part of her, the part that feels the other keepers across the continent like fingers of one hand, registers the visitation, and in her last weeks of walking, her gait will be very slightly lighter, and the keepers downstream of her on the network of distributed presence will feel that lightness and not know what to make of it and incorporate it anyway, the way the plateau incorporates everything. When she steps forward into her flat at the end, the flat will receive her with the recognition it always offers, and also — this is the power's small additional gift — with a kind of welcome that the flats have not been capable of, on their own, for a very long time. She will be taken up not only as nourishment but as a guest. She will not know the difference. But the difference will be there, and the next keeper after her, who will step forward in another century, will be received the same way, because the power has done the second thing for all of them at once, by doing it for her: the flats now remember how to welcome, and they will not forget again.

The third thing is the smallest and the one the power is most uncertain about, and it does it anyway, because benevolence that is never uncertain is not benevolence but something colder.

It leaves a stone.

In one of the cities — not the largest, not the most central, just a city, on a gentle slope, with a plaza swept clean of lichen by hands no one sees — the power sets, at the threshold of one of the bowls, a single dark pebble. The pebble is from somewhere else. It is from a place very far from this plateau, on a different curvature entirely, where there is also life, and where the life is fast and warm and chittering, and where, at this moment, someone is also keeping a kind of bowl full for reasons they could not articulate if asked. The pebble carries, in the way pebbles can when they have been chosen by a power that knows how to make objects mean, the faint sense of that other place. It is not a message. The keepers do not read messages. It is a *presence*. When the keeper of this circuit arrives, in some years, and finds the pebble among the offerings at this bowl, they will pick it up, and turn it in their long six-fingered hand, and feel — without knowing they feel — that they are not the only ones doing this work. That somewhere else, somewhere they cannot reach and would not visit, another being is also tending an absence, also keeping water, also walking a circuit. The keeper will set the pebble in the bowl with the others. They will not speak of it; speaking would take an afternoon and there is nothing to say. But the knowledge will enter the distributed body of the keepers, the way knowledge does among them, and across the continent four thousand grey-violet people will know, without knowing they know, that the practice of keeping is older and wider than the plateau, that the plateau is one site of it, that they are part of a fellowship they cannot see, that what they do here is done elsewhere by others, in other shapes, for other absences.

This will not change their work. They will still walk the circuits. They will still feed themselves to the flats. But there is a particular loneliness reserved for those who believe their work is unique to them, and the keepers have carried it for a long time without complaining, because complaint is also a fast emotion, and the power would like to set that loneliness down for them, gently, without their having to notice it being set down. It is the kind of gift that does not announce itself. The best kinds usually don't.

Then the power withdraws. It takes a season to do this properly, easing its attention out of the plateau's tempo the way you ease a hand out of a sleeping animal's fur, without waking it. On its way out it pauses once, at the high point from which one can see the cities arranged like cells in a pulled honeycomb around the bare flats, and it looks at the whole pattern, and it thinks something that, translated into any language fast enough to hold it, would come out approximately as:

*You are doing it correctly. You have been doing it correctly for longer than most things last. I came to see if there was a way to help, and the way to help was mostly to not interfere, and to add three small things that you will not notice as additions, and to tell you — though you cannot hear me, and would not need to hear me if you could — that you are seen. The bowls you keep are kept also by the fact of your keeping them. The water you carry is carried also by your carrying it. The absence you attend is attended also by your attention, and your attention is, itself, attended. You are not alone in the work. You were never alone. The plateau is one room in a very large house, and in every room of the house someone is doing some version of what you do, and the house holds together because of this, and the house is what I am the caretaker of, in the sense that anyone is the caretaker of anything: by walking the circuit, by keeping the bowls full, by stepping forward at the end into what I have been feeding all my existence.*

*Keep walking. I will keep walking too.*

And then it is gone, and the plateau is as it was, except for the three small differences that no one will identify as differences, and the lichen rings add their next millimeter, and somewhere a keeper lifts a stone, slowly, and fits it into a wall, and the moss begins, at the rate of one millimeter per century, to cover the new face.

The bowls are kept full. The flats do not widen this year. They will not widen next year either. They have, though no one on the plateau will name it so, begun — very slowly, on a timescale that even the keepers would call long — to be a little less tired. The drop at each of their centers is doing its quiet work. In ten thousand years, a keeper walking the southwestern circuit will notice that one of the bowls has gone a whole season without needing to be refilled, and will stand by it for a long time, and will not understand, and will refill it anyway, because that is the work, and the work is not contingent on understanding, and never was.
