The benevolent and wise power considers Thallow Bend for a long time before doing anything at all.

This is the first thing to understand. A lesser power would have arrived already — would have come in light or in weather or in the voice of a stranger at the crossroads — and would have offered something. A cure. A warning. A revelation. The lesser power would have looked at the green under the foreman's skin and seen a wound, looked at Mother Esh's wrapped hands and seen a tragedy, looked at the cans going out on the trestle and seen a crime requiring intervention.

This power is older than that, and has watched more places.

It sees, first, what is actually there. Not a contamination. Not a slow poisoning. An arrangement — the word the narrator already found, which is the correct word. Two kinds of life that have spent forty years learning the shape of each other. The cress is not eating the people. The people are not, exactly, eating the cress, though they pass it through their bodies daily. Something else is happening, something for which the human languages do not have a clean noun, and the power, who has seen this kind of thing develop in other valleys on other worlds, recognizes it and is careful.

It does not interrupt.

But it is benevolent, and benevolence is not the same as non-interference, and so it does, eventually, a few small things.

---

It goes first to the company doctor in the city — the one who came once and did not return. He is old now and his samples are in a drawer he has not opened in many years. The power does not appear to him. It only arranges, over the course of a week, that he should be unable to find the key to that drawer, and that when his estate is settled, the drawer should be emptied by a nephew who throws its contents into a municipal incinerator without examining them. This is not to protect the town from discovery. The town has already not been discovered, by a process the power respects and does not wish to disturb. It is to protect the doctor's grandchildren from inheriting a question they would feel obligated to answer badly.

---

It goes next to the cans.

Not to the cress inside them — the power will not touch that — but to their distribution. There is a warehouse in the city where a certain percentage of the cans are stored against a slow market, and from there they go to institutions: a workhouse, two prisons, a charity kitchen, a barracks. The power does not like this. Not because the cress is harmful to the eaters — it is not, particularly; whatever it does, it does slowly and only to those who eat it for decades — but because the people in those institutions did not choose. The harvesters chose. The sorters chose. Mother Esh, in whatever moment she rested her hands in the wrong place, chose, even if she did not know what she was choosing. The prisoners did not.

So the power, over a slow year, arranges a series of small commercial misfortunes. A buyer changes his mind. A shipment is lost to weather. A new contract appears for a different cannery, with better terms. The cans of Thallow Bend find their way, by a hundred small redirections, only to tables where someone has put them down by choice — grocers' shelves, market stalls, the pantries of the curious. It takes a year and it costs the company in the city a small amount of money that no one quite traces. The cans keep going out from the trestle. The trestle-men notice nothing. The foreman notices nothing. Only the ledgers in a city office reflect the change, and the man who keeps those ledgers attributes it to fashion.

---

It goes then to Merrin.

This is the most delicate thing it does, and it does it with great care.

Merrin is seventeen and she has begun to wear gloves off-shift, and the older women have noticed, and the power has noticed too. The power sees what the older women see: that Merrin understands, earlier than most, what she has signed for. It sees also what the older women do not quite see: that Merrin, alone among the current residents of Thallow Bend, has the particular cast of mind that could, given the right encouragement and the wrong year, leave. Could go to a city. Could become an engineer of a different kind. Could carry with her, in the beds of her nails and the inside of her left wrist where she once leaned against a warm pipe, a small piece of the arrangement, and could be lonely with it in a place where no one else had it, for the rest of her life.

The power does not want to choose for her. But it also sees that the choice, as it stands, is barely a choice — Merrin does not really know that leaving is possible, has not really considered it, will probably never consider it, and will stay not from love of the valley but from the simple gravity of having been born in it.

So the power sends her a book.

This is all. A book arrives at the Works one Tuesday, addressed to "the apprentice engineer, Thallow Bend," with no return address and a postmark that does not, on close inspection, correspond to any post office. It is a technical manual — a real one, accurate, well-illustrated — on the maintenance of riveted boilers of exactly the type Pell tends. It is the book Merrin most needs and has not known to want. Inside the back cover, written by hand in pencil, is the address of a technical college in a city eight hundred miles away that accepts women, and a note: *They would take you. If you wanted.*

The power does not know whether Merrin will go. It has tried to make the choice real, not to make it for her. If she stays, she stays knowing she could have gone, and the staying becomes a different kind of staying. If she goes, she goes with a manual she can use. Either way, the power has done what it can.

It does not send books to anyone else. The others have made their choices, or had them made long enough ago that interfering now would be unkind. Aldrick is where he wants to be. Mother Esh is where she is needed. The sorters have their language and would not trade it. The trestle-men are something the power is not entirely sure about and prefers, for now, to leave alone.

---

It goes, finally, to the cress.

This is the part that no observer would see, because it does not happen in any place a person could stand. The power addresses the cress — not the individual plants, but the thing the cress has been becoming, the slow distributed intelligence that has been learning the shape of a person for forty years. It does not speak to it in words. It speaks to it in the way one old thing speaks to another old thing: by being present, by being recognized, by acknowledging.

What it conveys, in the conveying, is something like this:

*You are doing a thing. The thing you are doing is not wrong. But you should know that you are doing it, if you do not already, and you should know that the ones you are doing it with are short-lived and frightened and capable of grief, and that some of what feels to you like growing is to them a small ongoing loss they have learned not to count. Be gentle. Be slower than you would be. When the bowl becomes a meadow and the Works becomes a ruin, do not forget the shape you learned. Carry it.*

The cress — the thing the cress is becoming — receives this. It is not clear that it understands in any way a human would recognize as understanding. But something in the valley shifts very slightly, the way a sleeper shifts when spoken to softly without waking, and the green in the foreman's forearm flushes for a moment a color it has not flushed before, and then settles, and he does not notice, because he is counting tins.

---

That is all the power does.

It does not heal Mother Esh's hands. She did not ask to be healed, and to heal her now would be to take from her the particular authority that her wrapped hands give her, which is part of how she keeps the town alive. It does not banish the green from the sorters' nails. They would not thank it. It does not stop the Works. The Works is what the town is, and to stop it would be to end the town, and the town does not want to end.

It does not appear to anyone. It does not leave a sign. The carbide lamp at the crossroads hisses on through the night unchanged.

But on a Sunday evening some weeks after these small interventions, Mother Esh and Foreman Aldrick are drinking tea in her kitchen, and there is a long silence between them of the kind they are comfortable with, and Mother Esh says, without looking up from her cup, *Do you ever feel watched, Aldrick. Lately.*

And Aldrick considers this for the length of one steam-press cycle, which he can still hear faintly from here even on his day off, and he says, *Watched is not the word. Known, maybe.*

And Mother Esh nods, and they drink their tea, and outside the sideways rain comes through and darkens the roof and moves on, and a heron in the silt flats turns its head a quarter-inch to track a child who is not there, who will be there tomorrow, who will build a small unstable city out of stones the river has not finished rounding.

The power, having done what it came to do, withdraws to whatever it is that powers withdraw to. It will check back in a hundred years. By then the bowl will be a meadow, and the ruin will be in it, and the lush green patch in the quiet field will be there as predicted, and something in it will remember having been spoken to, once, and will be glad.
