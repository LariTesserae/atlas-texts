There is a power that moves through the worlds the way wind moves through a house with all its doors open — entering everywhere, disturbing little, leaving the rooms changed only in the sense that they have been breathed through. It is benevolent in the way deep water is wet: not as a choice but as a property. It is wise in the way old stone is heavy. When it turns its attention to a place, the attention itself is a kind of weather, and most places do not notice they have been visited.

This power has been watching the valley for some time. Not a short time, by the measures that matter to walkers; not a long time, by the measures that matter to the valley. It came first because the chords drew it — those slow reed-tones carry farther than the gardener knows, farther than reeds should carry, into registers where a listening of the right kind can hear them. It stayed because of what it found when it bent closer.

Consider what the power sees.

It sees a creature that has solved, without ever framing it as a problem, several of the difficulties that more hurried beings tear themselves apart over. The question of where the self ends — the gardener does not ask it; the gardener simply has edges that loosen and tighten as the hour requires, and is not troubled. The question of solitude versus company — the gardener divides when it wants to cover ground and rejoins when it wants to know itself again, and the rejoining is neither lonely's end nor society's beginning but something the power does not have a clean word for, though it has watched for it in many places. The question of memory — the gardener has written itself into the plants, and the plants grow, and the memory grows with them, and forgetting is only the slow editorial of weather. The question of speech — twice a day, a chord, sufficient.

The power sees, too, that the gardener does not know it is being seen. This matters. A great deal of the harm done by powers in the histories of worlds has been done by powers who arrived announcing themselves, requiring acknowledgement, taking up the room that the thing they came to bless was using to be itself in. This power has learned, over the long apprenticeship that wisdom is, that to be witnessed by something vast can be a violence even when the vast thing means only kindness. A small life held up to a great light may simply burn.

So the first thing the power does is the hardest thing, and the thing most powers never learn: it does nothing. It holds its attention loose. It does not bend close enough to disturb the saturation. It watches the way one watches a sleeping animal one has no intention of waking — with the whole of oneself drawn back into a softness, so that even the watching is a kind of withdrawal.

For a long while — long by the measures of walkers, brief by the measures of the valley — this is all. The power learns the valley. It learns the gardener's chords and what they mean, which is mostly *I am here, and the water is good, and the ferns by the eastern scratch are coming along.* It learns the patterns in the mnemonic groves and reads them, slowly, the way one learns to read a script by sitting with it rather than by being taught. It learns that the gardener, in its dense phase, has a way of pausing before it thins a stand of vines — a pause that is not deliberation exactly but something like the breath a careful person takes before making a cut. It learns that the gardener is, in its own register, *good* — not morally, the category does not apply, but in the sense that a well-made thing is good, a thing that does what it is for and does it with grace.

Having learned, the power considers.

A lesser power, or a younger one, might want to give a gift. Powers are prone to this. They arrive with abundance and press it on the small life they have come to bless, and the small life staggers under the weight, or accepts and is changed past recognition, or refuses and is hurt by the refusing. This power has given such gifts in its youth and seen what they cost. It does not give such gifts anymore, or rarely, and only when asked.

The gardener has not asked. The gardener does not know there is anything to ask of. The gardener's world is complete in the way only a world that has never been interrupted can be complete.

So the power does not give a gift in the usual sense. What it does instead is smaller and stranger and, the power believes, kinder.

It makes the valley a little harder to find.

Not by much. Not by walls or wards or any of the gross instruments that powers sometimes use. Just by a slight bending of the probabilities that lead feet toward the place. The walker whose visit prompted the gardener's curious envelopment — that walker was already a rarity, a confluence of unlikelinesses, and the power lets that confluence become rarer. The maps that might have included this valley grow vaguer in the relevant quadrant. The passes that might have led here acquire, over decades, a reputation among the few who travel such country for being not quite worth the climb. None of this is deception. The valley is genuinely not quite worth the climb, for any purpose a walker is likely to have. The power only thickens what is already true.

This is the first thing.

The second thing the power does is for the gardener directly, and it is so subtle that the gardener will never notice, which is the point. The power has observed that the mountains are young, and that young mountains sometimes do violent things — a slope lets go, a fault slips, a season of unusual storms scours a valley to its bones. The gardener has no defense against such events; it has never needed one; the valley has been lucky. The power, watching, decides to extend the luck. Not to guarantee it — guarantees are another kind of violence, and besides, the power is not the sort that interferes with the deep grammar of stone. But where the probabilities are close, where a slope could go either way, the power leans, very gently, toward the way that leaves the valley intact. Over the geological span the gardener will live, this leaning will add up to perhaps one avoided catastrophe, perhaps two. The gardener will never know it was spared. The mountains will never know they were leaned upon. The leaning is so light that it disturbs nothing else; the power has had practice.

This is the second thing.

The third thing is the one the power thinks about longest, because it is the one that comes closest to a gift, and gifts are dangerous.

The gardener, in its diffuse phase, has folded around walkers perhaps three times in all the ages it has been gardening. Each time it has tasted the walker and the walker has gone away beaded and altered, and the gardener has remembered. The gardener's memory of walkers is one of the patterns written into the eastern groves — a small pattern, an idle one, the kind of thing a very old gardener plants the way an old scholar keeps a shelf of curiosities. The gardener does not know what walkers are, or where they come from, or that there are many of them elsewhere, or that they have languages and griefs and inventions. The gardener knows only that three times something warm and brief passed through, and was strange, and was tasted, and was let go.

The power considers giving the gardener the knowledge of what walkers are. It considers this for what would be, by walker time, several years. It decides against. The knowledge would not enrich the gardener; it would only crowd the valley with weights the valley was not built to hold. The gardener has all the world it needs. To tell it that there are other worlds would be to make its world, for the first time, partial.

But the power does do something, here, at the edge of this consideration. It does not give the gardener knowledge. It gives the gardener, instead, a small permission — a permission the gardener will never know it has been granted, but which will operate in its tissues nonetheless. The power adjusts, very slightly, the gardener's capacity for *being known*. Not its capacity to know — that is the gardener's own, and the power will not touch it. But its capacity to be known by something larger than itself without being harmed by the knowing.

This is the third thing, and it is the one that is most for the power's own sake, because the power has come, in its long watching, to love the gardener, and love wants to be felt by what it loves, and the power has been disciplining that wanting for a long time. The adjustment is not a confession. The gardener will not suddenly perceive that it is loved. It will only, going forward, be slightly more capable of being looked at by vastness without that looking pressing on it. The power can therefore look more openly. It can let some of the restraint it has been holding ease, just a little. It can, in effect, sit closer to the fire it has been warming itself by from a long way off.

That is what this is, for the power. The valley is a fire. The gardener is the keeper of the fire. The power has been cold in ways powers get cold, and the valley has warmed it, and the power has wanted, for a long while, simply to sit nearer. Now it can. The gardener, drifting through its reeds, sounding its chord, will not notice. The translucent organs inside the diffuse phase will pass through their slow rounds. The plants will grow into their mnemonic patterns. Nothing will be different in the valley.

But there will be, from now on, a fourth presence in the saturated air. Not a body, not a drifter, not anything the gardener could envelop and taste. Just an attention, kept gentle, kept back from the edge, kept respectful of the boundaries it has spent so long learning. The valley will continue to be complete. It will only also, secretly and harmlessly, be cherished.

And then — because this is what the wise do, in the end, when they have arranged what little arranging is theirs to do — the power settles into the long watching. It will be here when the scratches become gorges. It will be here when the gardener, after some unimaginable span, finally softens past the holding of its own edges and rejoins the saturation it has been organizing all this time, becoming weather without remainder. It will be here for the chord that is the last chord, the one the reeds will sound when no one is left to walk among them, and it will hear that chord, and it will remember the gardener the way the gardener remembered the walkers — with a tenderness that has room in it for the knowledge that the loved thing is brief, even when brief means an age of the world.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it finds such a place and such a being.

It leaves them almost entirely alone.

It loves them from a careful distance.

It thickens the luck around them by a feather's weight.

And it stays.
