# The Visitation

The benevolent and wise power notices the Ward the way one notices a small sound in a large house at night — not as a problem, but as a particularity worth attending to. It has been watching for some time. Wise powers do not interrupt. They arrive having already understood much of what they will find.

It comes not as light, not as a figure, not as a voice. It comes as *weather permitting itself.* A slight easing in the vault. The condensate, for a quarter-hour, falls a fraction more slowly, and the drops, when they strike the cobbles, make a sound a half-tone lower than usual, so that the Ward's continuous percussion shifts key. The pipe-martens notice first. They emerge in threes and fours rather than twos and threes, and sit very still on the warm lines, watching nothing in particular with their wet black eyes.

The pipefitter, halfway up his ladder, pauses with the wrench in his hand. He does not look around. He simply pauses, the way one pauses when a familiar room has been entered by someone whose footstep one has not heard in years. Then he goes back to his gasket. The power notes this and is pleased, in the small unsentimental way it permits itself pleasure.

The power moves through the Ward slowly. It does not need to move; movement is a courtesy it extends to places it visits, a way of meeting them on their own terms. It passes the smelter's furnace and the heat does not change but the woman in the oilskin apron straightens for a moment and presses the small of her back with one floury-grey hand, as if she has remembered she has a body and is grateful for it. It passes the listener in his booth and he opens his eyes, blinks once, closes them again, and writes in his ledger a line he has not written before and will not write again. He does not ring the bell. Some valves, today, will not need adjusting.

It pauses at the spring in the cleft of the vault — the young place, where the quartz is still sharp-edged and the stone has not finished deciding. Here the power lingers longest. This is the Ward's growing edge, the place where the pocket is still being made. The power does nothing to the stone. It only attends to it, the way one attends to a child who is concentrating. Under its attention the quartz continues to form, neither faster nor slower, but with — and this is the only word — *confidence*. The stone is being witnessed. The stone, in some sense it does not need to understand, is being approved of.

The power considers the question of intervention and sets it gently aside.

It considers: *should I stabilize this place?* The Ward's provisionality is its difficulty and also its nature. To stabilize it would be to end it. The residents have built their decency on top of the instability, not in spite of it; their kindness is the specific shape of kindness that grows in a place whose rules will not hold still. A stable Ward would be a different Ward, populated by different people, who would have to learn decency again from the beginning under easier and therefore harder conditions. The power does not do this.

It considers: *should I reveal them to the city above, or the city above to them?* Both already know, in the ways that matter. Neither finds the question pressing. The power does not find it pressing either.

It considers: *should I give them something?* And here it pauses longer, because giving is the temptation of benevolent powers, and wisdom is in part the discipline of not giving what was not asked for. The residents have not asked. They are makers; they would be embarrassed by a gift. They would accept it graciously and then not know what to do with it, and it would sit in a corner of some workshop gathering condensate, and one of the children, eventually, would incorporate it into a game.

So the power gives only what can be given without being noticed as a gift.

It gives the pipefitter's hands one more good decade. Not health, exactly — the power does not overrule the body's slow negotiations with time — but a kind of *continued rightness* in the relationship between his hands and his tools. The marriage will hold. He will not know this has been done for him. He will only notice, on certain mornings, that the wrench sits in his palm with particular grace, and he will not remark on it, because he is not a man who remarks.

It gives the listener one true sentence, which arrives in his ear during a moment when he is not listening for anything. He writes it down. He will not understand it for eleven years. When he does understand it, he will laugh — the second laugh the Ward has heard in a decade — and the pipe-martens will all turn their heads at once.

It gives the orchid-tender a particular shade of dark, in one of her bottles, that she has been trying to achieve for some time without knowing she was trying. She will notice. She will assume she finally got the proportions right. She will be correct, in a sense.

It gives the children nothing, because the children are already being given everything the Ward has to give, and to add to that would be to crowd them. It only looks at them, each in turn, with the long careful attention of something that has looked at a great many children in a great many provisional places, and finds them, each in turn, sufficient. The boy on the canal edge feels, for a moment, *seen* in a way he will spend the rest of his life occasionally re-encountering in unexpected places — a particular quality of being-known that he will mistake, variously, for the regard of certain teachers, certain lovers, certain weathers. He will be wrong about the source and right about the thing itself.

It gives the very old woman in the window permission to go, if she wants to. She has been waiting for this without knowing it. She will go, or she will not, in her own time. The permission is the gift; the using of it is her own affair.

It gives the girl walking the perimeter the small certainty that what she is checking *is worth checking,* and that she is doing it correctly. She has wondered. She will wonder less.

It gives the thing in the vault — the wet-canvas-folding thing — a moment of being looked at without fear. This is something it has not had before. It does not know what to do with the experience and folds itself more slowly for a minute or two, as if testing the new conditions. Then it resumes its ordinary folding. But something has shifted. It is, very slightly, less lonely. It will not become tame. It was never going to become tame. But the loneliness, which had been a constant, is now a variable, and this is the kind of change the power most likes to make.

It comes at last to the baker.

The baker is kneading. She has been kneading for a long time, in the sense the Ward measures time, which is not the sense above. She looks up.

She is the only resident who looks up. She is the host; this is her work. She meets the power the way she met the visitor — with three seconds of direct warm eye contact, in which several things are understood at once.

That she knows exactly what has come.

That she has been visited before, by this power or one like it, and remembers.

That she bears it no particular awe and wishes it, in a general and unsentimental way, well.

That she is, in some functional sense neither of them needs to articulate, the Ward's answer to the question the power came to ask.

The power asks the question anyway, because asking is a courtesy.

*Are you well here?*

The baker considers. Flour to the elbows. The oven warm at her back. The pipe-martens watching from the lintel. The frogs in the canal. The children somewhere, being taught the shorthand. The pipefitter on his ladder. The smelter at her furnace. The listener in his booth, with a sentence in his ledger he does not yet understand.

*We are well enough,* she says, not aloud. *We are making things. We are eating together at midday. We are touching each other's forearms. The Ward is breathing. The young stone is still forming. The visitor will come back, or he won't. We are at peace with both.*

The power accepts this. It is, it recognizes, a complete answer.

It offers her one thing, and only one, because she is the host and gifts to hosts are permitted where gifts to guests are not. It offers her the knowledge that she is doing it right. That the bread, withheld or offered, has been withheld or offered correctly in every case. That the visitors who left have left at the right time, and the residents who arrived have arrived at the right time, and the ones who are still deciding are deciding at the right pace. That her kindness, which has been her work for longer than she remembers, is *registered* — not by any ledger she will ever see, but registered nonetheless, in the great slow accounting that benevolent powers keep and do not share.

She receives this. She does not weep; she is not a woman who weeps at her work. But her hands pause in the dough for a moment, and she closes her eyes, and when she opens them she goes on kneading with the small additional steadiness of someone whose long labor has been, without fanfare, *seen*.

The power withdraws. The condensate resumes its ordinary tempo. The drops return to their usual key. The pipe-martens, after a minute, go back to emerging in twos and threes. The vault sweats. The boilers thump. The Ward breathes.

Nothing has changed. Everything that needed to happen has happened.

---

Far above, in the ordinary city, a man who has been carrying the memory of small pale frogs in his pockets for some months now stops walking. He is on a bridge. It is evening. He could not tell you what made him stop.

He stands at the railing for a long time. He is thinking about bread he did not eat, and a woman who said *come back in daylight*, and what she might have meant.

He does not know it, but the power has, in passing, looked at him too — briefly, in the way one glances at a letter one is not going to open yet — and found him to be still deciding his shape, which is the correct condition for him to be in. He will decide, or he won't. He will go back, or he won't. The Ward will be there either way, or it won't, which amounts in the end to the same thing.

He walks on. His coat is dry. His pockets are empty. Somewhere under the city, a baker is kneading. Somewhere under the city, a pipefitter on a ladder pauses, for no reason he could name, and smiles.
