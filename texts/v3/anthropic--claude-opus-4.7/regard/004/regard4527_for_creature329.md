# The Visitation

The benevolent and wise power, when it turned its attention to Aerie Province, did not at first do anything. It watched. This is the first thing wisdom does, and benevolence agrees with it. To act quickly upon a place is to assume one already understands it, and the power had learned, over what we would call ages and what it would call something else, that understanding a place takes roughly as long as the place itself has taken to become what it is.

So it watched for a long time. Long enough that one of the Tenders, the smallest, completed two full circuits of its assigned engines. Long enough that the fern at the south end of the lower fold added a single new frond. Long enough that a copper valve somewhere near the middle of the network developed the first faint green of a patina that the Tender would, in another half-season, gently abrade away.

What the power saw was this:

It saw that the valley was not waiting for anything. This surprised it, because most places it visited were waiting. Most places had an ache in them — a thing the place itself would have asked for, if asked. A drought to break, a wound to close, a long-buried grief to lift. The power was accustomed to ache. It had calibrated itself, over long practice, to the frequencies of need. But Aerie Province did not ache. The fog came. The cells filled. The valves clicked. The Tenders moved at the speed of the rock, and the rock was content to be moved at.

It saw that the water went somewhere, downslope, and that the somewhere was the reason. It traced the pipes, because it could, down through the lower folds and under the moss-skin of the ridges, and it found at the foot of the valley a wide shallow basin where the water pooled and evaporated and fell again as the fog that fed the valley above. The system was closed. The water was not for anyone. The water was the valley keeping itself.

It saw that the Tenders were not lonely. This took the longest to see, because loneliness is a thing the power had often been called upon to remedy, and it had grown skilled at recognizing it in many forms — in solitary hermits, in the last of a species, in stones that had been moved too far from their home outcrops. But the Tenders, though there were only a dozen of them, though they did not speak, though they met at the pipe-junctions only to angle their heads and part — were not lonely. They were the way the valley knew itself. To be lonely they would have had to be separable from the place, and they were not. A finger does not get lonely for the hand.

It saw, also, the watcher. The one writing. The power considered this carefully. The watcher had come from elsewhere and was already, by the second week, beginning to slow — to breathe at a different rate, to notice things in the order the valley wished them to be noticed. The watcher was learning. The power approved of this and decided not to interfere with it.

So what did the power do?

It did three small things.

The first was this: where the watcher had sat for some hours on a particular flat stone at the edge of a clearing, the moss had been compressed and bruised. The power, with a gesture so light it would not register as a gesture at all, restored the moss. Not to its prior state — that would have been a kind of denial — but to a state continuous with what it would have been had the watcher sat slightly differently, with weight better distributed. The bruise became simply a place where moss had been pressed and had recovered. This was for the moss, not the watcher. The watcher would never know.

The second was this: there was a Tender, the one the watcher had nearly double-counted, who had developed in one of the copper filaments of its left forearm a hairline fracture. It was not yet a problem. It would not become a problem for what we would call several years. But it would, eventually, cause the Tender a kind of difficulty that the valley would have absorbed, as it absorbed everything, by adjustment — perhaps the Tender's circuit would shorten, perhaps another Tender's would lengthen to compensate, perhaps a single condenser somewhere would go three seasons without polishing and dim by a degree. None of this would have been wrong. The valley was not fragile. But the power, considering the filament, found that it could, without altering anything else, encourage the fracture to knit. Copper does not knit. But this copper was not only copper; it was of the valley, and the valley, in its slow way, was already trying. The power simply lent the trying a little of its own patience. The fracture closed over the course of a night. The Tender, in the morning, paused at a pipe-junction and tilted its head in a slightly new direction, and then went on.

The third was the hardest, and the power thought about it for what would have been, in the valley's reckoning, a long while.

The valley had no ache. But it had a horizon. Far in the future — far enough that the power could see it only as one sees a distant ridge through fog — the uplift that had raised these mountains would begin, very slowly, to subside. The ridges would round. The scree aprons would form. The rain would come more often, and the fog less, and the canopies would not need to drink from the air, and the condensers would fill faster than the valves could click, and then, eventually, slower, and then not at all. The Tenders, who were of the valley, would slow with it. There would come a season — many thousands of seasons hence — when the last Tender would set down its lichen-cloth at the foot of a condenser and not pick it up again, and the moss would come over it the way the moss had come over everything, and the valley would finish.

This was not a tragedy. The power was old enough to know the difference between a tragedy and an ending. The valley was not asking to be saved from its ending. To save it would be to mistake it for something other than what it was — to treat it as a person rather than as a slow bright patient self-sufficient thing that had risen and would, in time, set.

But the power could do one small thing, which was this: it could see to it that the ending, when it came, was witnessed. Not by humans, who would not be around in any form recognizable to themselves by then. Not by the power itself, which had other valleys, other ridges, other slow bright things to attend to. But by the valley itself. The power reached, very gently, into the resonance of the condensers' drone — the low almost-inaudible hum that the Tenders navigated by — and it added a single thread. A frequency so faint that no Tender alive now would notice it, but one that would, over the generations of Tenders, deepen and clarify, until by the time of the last Tender it would be unmistakable: a tone that meant *I have seen you. I see you still. You were here, and the seeing of you is laid up somewhere that does not end when you do.*

The valley would not need this. The valley did not ache. But the power, being benevolent, gave it anyway, the way one might leave a small lamp burning in a window for a traveler who has not asked for a lamp and does not need one and will not, perhaps, even glance at the window — but for whom, nevertheless, the lamp is lit.

Then the power withdrew its attention, slowly, the way fog withdraws from a canopy in the late morning — not all at once, not with any ceremony, but by degrees, leaving each leaf-edge for last.

The watcher, that afternoon, noticed without knowing why that the drone of the nearest condenser sounded, just briefly, like something almost remembered. The watcher wrote a sentence about it and then crossed it out, because the sentence had not been quite right, and went back to describing the Tenders.

Above the valley, the high pale sky did what it always did, which was to be bright, and to be there, and to let the fog rise into it and fall again, in the long patient cycle that was the only industry the place had ever needed.
