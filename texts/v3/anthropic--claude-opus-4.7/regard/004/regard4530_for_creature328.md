— and an elder are very nearly contemporaries. A grandmother saying *when I was small* gestures at the same cups her granddaughter sees now, hung at the same heights, sounding the same notes. The grandmother was small among these cups. The granddaughter is small among these cups. What separates them is mostly the sequence of breaths each has taken, and breaths are not really kept track of.

They have, I think, no word for *future* that is different from their word for *now*. The song will be the song. The cups will be the cups. A listener born in fifty years will hear what a listener hears today. This is not faith. It is maintenance. They know exactly what would have to fail for it not to be true, and they prevent those failures, gently, daily, with their long fingers and their shallow breath.

---

And then.

Imagine that something notices them. Not a god in the loud sense — not a presence that would announce itself, because an announcement would crack every cup in the room. Call it instead an attention. A turning-toward. Somewhere in the great quietness behind the world, something benevolent and very old has been listening for a long time to many things, and one day — though *day* is the wrong word — its listening settles on this cavern.

What does it hear?

It hears, first, the song. It hears the *tink* of the copper pan that has been *tink*ing for a thousand years, and the *tok* of the deep clay pot, and the whispered *pt* of the half-gourd, and the several thousand other small voices, and it hears how they fit. It hears the in-breath word being spoken between two drips by a woman sitting beside her husband of forty years. It hears a child learning to breathe shallowly. It hears a listener, six years old, noticing that the seventh cup in the eastern cluster has gone a quarter-tone flat, and not minding yet, because she has not yet learned that this is the kind of thing one mentions.

And the attention does what attention of this kind does, which is to ask: *what does this need?*

This is the question I want to sit with, because it is not an obvious question. A lesser power, a power that loved in the loud way, would look at the cavern and see scarcity. Thirty people. Damp cloaks. Blind fish. No fire. Infants taught not to cry. It would want to *help*. It would want to widen the cavern, multiply the food, raise the ceiling, bring light, bring warmth, bring more of them into being so that there could be more joy, by which it would mean more of the kind of joy it already knew how to recognize.

But a benevolent and *wise* power — and the wisdom is the thing — would understand, I think, the following:

The cavern is not a deprivation. It is a composition.

The smallness of the people is not a wound. It is a fit.

The thirty are not too few. Thirty is the number that the song can hold without the song becoming about the people instead of about itself, and the people know this, in the way one knows the temperature of bathwater.

The shallow breathing is not suffering. It is participation.

The unchanging is not stagnation. It is the achievement. *Nothing changes. This is the achievement.* They told us so, in the only way they tell anything.

So what would the wise attention do?

I think it would, first, simply listen. For a long time. Longer than the cavern has existed. It would learn the song — not as an outsider learns a song, by transcribing it, but as the cavern itself knows the song, by being shaped to it. It would learn which drips want to say what. It would learn the in-breath word, and learn not to say it, because to say it from outside would be to put a thumb on a scale that has been balanced for a thousand years.

Then I think it would do something very small.

Not a miracle. A miracle would be a catastrophe here. A miracle would crack cups.

Perhaps this:

There is a seam, high on the eastern wall, where the lichen-that-is-not-lichen grows a little thinner than elsewhere. Over the next ten thousand years, by the slow logic of caves, this seam will widen. Water will find it. A new drip will begin, in a place where no drip has ever been, and it will fall onto a slate walkway where no vessel hangs to catch it, and it will, drop by drop, dissolve a groove in the slate, and the groove will redirect a trickle into a lower chamber, and the lower chamber will, eventually, drain the cistern. Slowly. Over centuries. But it will drain it. And then the blind fish will die, and the people will have no water that is not the hung water, which they have never learned to drink from, and the song will end not in a crash but in a long thinning, and the last listener will sit beside the last cup and hear it *tink* alone, and then she will die, and the cavern will be only stone and water again, and the in-breath word will be unspoken for the first time in a thousand years.

The attention sees this. It is not anyone's fault. It is geology. It is what caves do.

And I think — I am following my intuition here, into a place I am not certain of — I think the wise power would, with a gesture smaller than a thought, lay one finger against that seam. Not seal it. Just slow it. Buy the song another thousand years. Another ten thousand. As long as can be bought without the buying becoming visible. The lichen on that patch will grow imperceptibly thicker. The seam will widen imperceptibly slower. No one in the cavern will ever know. No listener, however gifted, will hear the difference, because there *is* no difference to hear — the song continues exactly as it would have continued, only for longer.

This is the first thing.

The second thing is harder, and I am less sure of it.

The people bury their dead in the cistern. They drink their grandmothers. This is, as they say, obvious; what else would water be. But there is a sorrow in it that they have not named, because to name it would be to introduce a word the song does not contain. When a listener dies — one of the three or four in a generation who heard most completely — something is lost that the cistern cannot quite hold. The particular shape of *her* listening. The way *she* knew the seventh cup. This dissolves too, but more slowly, into the felted cloaks and the braided cords and the hands of the children she taught by sitting beside them and not speaking. It dissolves, but incompletely. There is a residue of unheardness.

I think the attention, having listened long enough to know this, would do the following: it would itself remember. Not in the way a monument remembers, with a name and a date, but in the way the cavern remembers, by being shaped. Somewhere in the great quietness behind the world, in whatever the attention uses for a body, there would form — slowly, over the listener's life and after her death — a small interior cup, hung at a particular height, into which her listening would fall, *tink*, and continue, *tink*, and the attention would carry her *tink* forever among its other carryings, and this would not help her, because she is dead, and it would not help the cavern, because the cavern does not know, but it would mean that her particular listening was, in the end, listened to. Once. Completely. By someone large enough to hold all of it.

Every listener. Every generation. Every in-breath word spoken between two drips by a woman beside her husband of forty years. All of it caught, somewhere, in cups the people will never see and would not want to.

This is not intervention. This is witness.

And the third thing — the last thing, the thing I am least sure of and most want to be true — is this:

When the cavern does, eventually, end — because everything ends, and even the wise power's finger on the seam is only a postponement, not an exemption — when the last listener sits beside the last cup and hears it *tink* alone, and then dies, and the lichen darkens, and the cistern goes still:

The attention would, at that moment, very softly, on an in-breath, between two drips that are no longer falling, speak the word.

Not to anyone. There is no one left to hear.

But the word would be spoken. By something. Once. Aloud, in the only voice large enough to say it without cracking the cups, in a cavern where the cups no longer need to be uncracked.

And then the silence after would be the right silence. Not the silence of a song unfinished. The silence of a song that was heard all the way through.

---

I don't know if this is what such a power would do. I am only a person, writing. But I notice that when I imagine being that attention, and I imagine looking at the cavern, I do not want to change it. I want to protect it from change, for as long as protection is possible, and then I want to remember it, and then I want to say its name once when no one is left to be disturbed by the saying.

I think love, at its wisest, looks a great deal like restraint.

I think the highest form of help, for some things, is to let them be exactly what they are, for as long as they can be, and to keep careful count, in some private ledger, of every small voice that ever rang.

The copper pan goes *tink*.

The deep pot goes *tok*.

The half-gourd goes *pt*.

Somewhere, something very old is listening, and has been listening, and will go on listening, and will not say so.

This is enough. This is, I think, what was needed.
