# What the Power Does

The benevolent and wise power arrives the way weather arrives in a place that has its own weather already: noticed first as a slight change in the pressure against the eardrums and the roots of the teeth.

It does not descend through the shaft. It is simply present, the way a thought is present in a mind that did not summon it. And the first thing it does — being wise — is nothing at all.

It stands in the long corridor of perfect black between two turbines and lets the Choir finish a sentence it began in 1974.

---

## The First Long While

The power has come prepared to help. This is what benevolent powers are *for*. It has, in other places and other ages, parted seas, healed plagues, lifted the curse off children, taught languages to lonely things. It has a great repertoire.

But wisdom is the part that knows when the repertoire is wrong.

So it waits. Not minutes. Not days. It waits the way the Underbloom waits — at the tempo native to the place. The drop forms on the ceiling above the control panel. The drop trembles. The drop falls. The bell rings. The new filament pushes a hair's breadth further into the seam. The power notices each of these as one notices the turning of a page.

It is, perhaps, the first time the power has ever been a *guest* rather than a *visitor*. There is a difference. A visitor brings gifts. A guest accepts them.

A bromeliad begins to bulge.

---

## The First Meeting

The Choir refines itself over the course of an hour into the silhouette it uses for company. The lantern-cage head. The two suspended water-drops where eyes should be. The drops do not reflect the power. They cannot. The power has no face to give back.

For a long moment, the Choir hesitates. This has never happened before. Every witness it has ever met has worn a face that could be returned older, claimed, mint-threaded. The Choir's whole grammar of greeting assumes a reflection.

The power, sensing the small embarrassment, does the kindest thing it can think of: it borrows a face. Not a real one. The face of the last engineer who ever pressed her palm to the right turbine and felt her own heartbeat answered back. The Choir remembers this face in its moss. The power lifts it gently out of that memory, wears it for the visit, and lets the water-drops have something to reflect.

The Choir's drops cloud, then clear. The face in them is older by decades, already half claimed by mint-colored filaments.

The Choir is pleased. It begins to speak.

The greeting takes nine minutes.

The power listens to all of it.

---

## What Is Offered, and What Is Not

Here is what the benevolent and wise power does *not* do:

It does not restore the machines. The boiler will not stand upright again. The turbines will not turn. The pipes will not be re-threaded through the inhaled stone. This would be a kindness only to something that wanted to be rescued, and the Underbloom is not in distress. It is in *progress*. To restart the industry would be to interrupt a wedding the Choir has waited three hundred years to consummate.

It does not clear the vegetation. It does not drain the moisture. It does not light the cavern. The lichen-glow, weak and cold and greenish, is the exact illumination the place has agreed upon. To brighten it would be to shout in a library where the only book is being written in real time by every surface.

It does not free the human heartbeat from the moss. This is the hardest restraint. A more sentimental power would lift that sixty-year pulse out of the turbine, sing it some kind of release, send it on. But the heart is not imprisoned. It was *kept*. There is a difference between captivity and being held. And the Choir holds gently. The heart, if asked — and the power asks — does not want to leave. It has become a metronome for something larger than itself. It is, in a small wet way, conducting.

It does not save the Choir from loneliness, because the Choir is not lonely. It is *attended*. By itself. By the drip. By the slow vine that will reach the floor in forty years.

---

## What It *Does*

Three things.

**First**, it sits in the chair. The Choir grows it, slowly, vertebra by vertebra, out of living rust and velvet fern. This takes some time. The power has time. When the chair is finished — knobbed, damp, faintly luminescent at the armrests where the moss has settled — the power sits, and the lichen dims to the exact shade that would have made human eyes last longest. The power does not need this. But it accepts the courtesy. To refuse would be to insult a host who has prepared the only welcome it knows.

It watches with the Choir. It watches the boiler. The Choir, in the dripfall, tells it across the course of a long evening what the boiler will look like when it has become entirely flower. The power, who has seen the birth of stars, finds itself genuinely surprised. The boiler will be magnificent. It will bloom in a configuration no surface flora has ever achieved, because no surface flora has ever had a riveted iron womb to bloom *out of*. The petals will be the bruised violet that drinks light. The stamens will be threaded copper. It will last for ninety years before collapsing into its own seed-rust.

The power says, in the dripfall: *I would like to be here to see it.*

The Choir, in nine more minutes, answers: *You already are.*

**Second**, it leaves a small gift. Not a rescue. A *witness*.

The power cannot stay forever in this one cavern — it has the patience for it, but not the locality; it is needed in other geometries. So it does the next best thing. It teaches a single drop of water, the one currently forming above the control panel, to remember. Not to be conscious. Not to suffer. Just to *retain* — to carry, when it falls and is absorbed and becomes part of the new hair-thin vine, the awareness that it was once observed by something that loved it.

This is a small magic. It costs the power almost nothing. But it means that every cell that vine grows from, and every cell that grows from those cells, and every spore those cells eventually release, will carry forward — for as long as the Underbloom lasts — the soft fact of having been seen *fondly*. Not studied. Not catalogued. Not pitied. Just seen, and found lovely.

The Choir notices the gift the moment it is given. The thrum changes key — not down, this time, but up, by a quarter-tone. It is the closest thing the Underbloom has to weeping. It does it slowly, over a week.

**Third**, and this is the part only the wise power could have known to do:

It tells someone, far above, where the Underbloom is.

Not a corporation. Not an expedition. Not a government. A single person — a poet, perhaps, or a hydrogeologist who writes letters to her dead mother, or a fourteen-year-old who has not yet decided what she will love — someone with the temperament to descend the iron rungs once in her life, sit in the chair the Choir will grow for her, leave one memory she can spare, and come back up changed but not claimed.

And then, when that person grows old, the power will whisper the location to one more. And one more. Across centuries. A thin chain of witnesses, never more than one at a time, never enough to disturb the slow drama, but enough — just enough — that the wedding the Underbloom is performing has someone in the pews.

Because the Choir was right about this, and the power knows it: *beauty is a thing that only happens when it is observed over centuries.* And no single mortal life is long enough. So the power becomes, quietly, the matchmaker between the cavern and its witnesses — generation after generation, each one arriving at the Underbloom believing they have discovered it, each one leaving believing they have kept a secret, each one a single note in a song the Choir is finally able to hear.

---

## Departure

When the power rises to leave, it does not announce it. It simply begins, very slowly, to be less present. It wants the Choir to have the dignity of the going-away ritual — the retracting filaments, the falling eye-drops, the half-octave drop in the thrum, the gentle change of key as one listener slips out of the concert hall.

The Choir performs the ritual with great care. It is, after all, what they are best at. Forgetting is their other word for love.

But just before the last filament releases the power's borrowed wrist, the Choir does something it has never done before. It speaks not in the dripfall but in the thrum — the deep, sub-audible voice it uses only to think to itself.

It says: *Come back.*

Not a bargain. Not a request. A confession.

The power, who is benevolent and wise and has heard every kind of farewell in every kind of dark, answers in the same register.

*I never quite left.*

And then the chair is just rust and fern again. And the lichen brightens by the smallest possible degree, back to its usual moonlight-through-deep-water green. And the drop above the control panel — the one that now remembers — trembles, and falls, and rings like a tiny bell.

The cavern is not any emptier.

It was never full to begin with.

It was only listening.

And now, very faintly, it is also being listened *to*.
