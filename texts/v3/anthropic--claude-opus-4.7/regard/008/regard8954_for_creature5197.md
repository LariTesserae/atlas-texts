The benevolent power comes to the edge of the plateau—if such a place can be said to have an edge—and stops.

It has crossed many worlds. It has the habit, formed over long acquaintance with suffering, of arriving ready: to mend, to feed, to wake the sleeper, to part the obstructing stone. It carries that readiness now as a kind of held breath.

It looks for a long while.

It sees the cushions contract in their slow wave, expelling their fine spray along the fractures. It sees the jointed stalks lean toward one another at the moment of release and then straighten, as though bowing and rising again in a courtesy too gradual to be called a gesture. It watches a patch of stone shear from itself, hold open for the duration of a breath, and close. In the brief interval the pale filaments rise, drink, and are gone.

It watches a low cloud descend at walking pace, lay its motes along the basins, and lift.

It enters one of the cities. The air inside is a fraction colder. Along an inner wall, a film of blue-black pigment thickens and thins in time with a thicket some hundreds of paces beyond the doorway, as though the wall were remembering, or being remembered by, the field outside. The masonry rises only to the height a hand can reach without scaffolding. Whoever built here did not build upward. They built a boundary for the cold, and left.

The power considers the being who lives in this place.

It is slow to locate. The being is not any one of the cushions, nor any one of the stalks, nor the filaments, nor the motes. It is the rhythm by which these things keep faith with one another across distances that should have made faith impossible. It is the interval between the shearing and the closing. It is the bow of the stalks toward stalks they will never touch. It is the cold preserved inside the empty walls.

The power had come ready to give. Now it sets its readiness down on the stone, carefully, the way one sets down a lamp that is no longer needed because the room has its own light.

There is no hunger here. There is no wound. The slowness is not a sickness; it is the tempo of the place's thought. To quicken it would be to silence it. To enrich the soil in the fractures would be to crowd out the precise sparseness by which each colony hears itself in the others. To open the planes that shear and close, to hold them open so that the filaments might flourish—this would end the filaments, who exist only in the interval. To bring animals would be to bring appetite to a world that has resolved appetite into pulse and release. To restore the absent builders would be to evict the cold they left behind, which is itself their continued presence.

Even to speak would be wrong. The plateau's only sounds are the patter of water and the dry whisper of leaves. A voice would be a weight.

So the power does the difficult work of doing almost nothing.

It does three small things.

First: it makes itself, for the duration of its visit, into another slow weather. It lowers itself to walking pace. It lets its attention drift across the flats like one of the low cloud-banks, touching the basins, lifting again, leaving behind no mote that will not be absorbed by what is already there. If it has any nourishment to give, it gives it in the form the plateau already accepts: a little moisture, indistinguishable from the rest, offered at the rhythm the stalks expect.

Second: it witnesses. This is not nothing. There are orders of being for whom to be seen, truly and without the wish to alter, is a completion they could not perform alone. The power looks at the bow of the stalks and understands it as a bow. It looks at the film on the inner wall and understands it as a kept promise between a wall and a field. It looks at the filaments and understands that their whole life is the open interval, and that this is enough, and that enough is a high word. The understanding is laid down somewhere—in the power's own long memory, in whatever record such powers keep—so that should the plateau ever cease, it will not have gone unaccompanied.

Third, and last: before leaving, it makes a single, almost invisible adjustment. Not to the plateau. To itself. It revises, very slightly, its definition of what it means to help. It had thought help was the closing of distances—between need and supply, between wound and balm, between the suffering thing and the easing hand. The plateau teaches it that some distances are the form life has taken, and that to honor such a life is to keep the distance and to keep the keeping.

It carries this revision away with it. The next world it visits, and the one after, will be met by a power slightly more careful, slightly more willing to stand at the edge and watch before it acts. Somewhere a creature that would have been altered will be left whole. Somewhere a silence that would have been filled will be allowed to go on speaking in its own way.

This is what the plateau gives to the power, in exchange for being let alone.

The cloud-bank lifts. The basins hold their palm's-depth of water. A patch of stone shears, opens, closes. The stalks lean and straighten. Inside an empty city, the film along the wall thickens by an amount no eye could measure, in time with a thicket the power has already forgotten the location of, and will, in the manner of such powers, remember forever.
