The benevolent and wise power considers the Verdant Choir for a long time.

It is the kind of attention that does not announce itself. No parting of the mist, no voice in the canopy, no warm certainty descending on the elders mid-song. The power simply *looks*, the way a gardener looks at a garden that is not their garden, the way a guest sits at the threshold of a house they have not been invited into.

What it sees stops it.

It had come, perhaps, half-prepared to help. That is what benevolent powers do; the impulse is older than they are. It had a hundred small generosities ready in its hands: a clearing of the lungwater that fills the elders' chests, a softening of the sinkholes that take children, a steadying of the bamboo surges, an easing of the cold, a stilling — even for a moment — of the unrelenting roar so that the Wet-Singers might know what silence feels like, the way one might know a foreign country.

It almost does it. It almost reaches in.

And then it listens longer.

It listens until it understands that the lungwater is not an affliction but the final stanza; that the sinkholes are how the basin keeps its own time; that the bamboo's surging is half of what the Wet-Singers' feet have learned to read; that the cold is the reason the mist becomes the breathing organism they sleep inside; that silence, offered here, would not be a gift but an amputation.

The power understands, with a slow grief that is also a slow joy, that everything it could *fix* is part of what is being *sung*.

So it sets down the hundred small generosities, one by one, the way one returns borrowed tools to a shelf.

But it does not leave. Benevolence that withdraws entirely is only another kind of indifference, and this power is wiser than that.

Here is what it does instead.

---

**First**, it learns the hymn.

Not to join it — it has the tact not to add an uninvited voice to a choir that has spent ten thousand years tuning itself. But it learns the hymn the way a scholar learns a dead language they will never speak: out of love, out of the refusal to let a thing exist unwitnessed. It listens to every cricket-pitch that has ever been given to a child as a name. It listens to the wet clicking snarl of anger and the soup-thick density of grief. It listens to the drone the districts make on the brightest nights, and it understands — without being told, because the Wet-Singers were right not to tell — what *keeping the argument alive* means. It is the only outside mind that has ever understood, and it keeps the understanding to itself, the way one keeps a secret entrusted by someone who did not know they were entrusting it.

**Second**, it makes a very small adjustment, so small that no Wet-Singer will ever notice it, and this is important: the adjustment must be invisible or it is a violation.

The adjustment is this. Far beyond the weeping cliffs, past where the plateau thins and the water changes its mind, there are pressures gathering — slow geological ones, slow climatic ones, the kind of pressures that, in some long century, would reach into the basin and end it. Not transform it. *End* it. Dry the pores in the rock. Lower the water table until the mat cracks. Silence the hymn not by mercy but by attrition.

The power, with the lightest possible touch, redirects these pressures. It does not stop them — stopping the world's slow grinding is the kind of intervention that breaks more than it preserves. It simply turns them, the way one turns a heavy stone a quarter degree so that water runs around it instead of into the house. The pressures will go elsewhere. They will do their work in places that can bear it, or in places already ending, or in the long patient silences between stars where no one is singing.

The basin gets another ten thousand years. Maybe more. The Wet-Singers will never know they were given this. That is the point. A gift the recipient knows about becomes a debt; a gift unknown remains a gift.

**Third**, and this is the strangest thing the power does:

It begins to *remember* the Choir.

Not record. Not archive. Powers of this order do not need archives; they have something deeper, something closer to the way a person remembers a face they loved in childhood — not as data, but as a shape the inside of the mind has taken and will always keep.

The power lets the Verdant Choir leave a permanent impression on it. The roaring hymn, the silver threads of upward rain, the children lifted from bromeliad tanks covered in pollen and shrimp, the elders whose ribs have married the wood, the orchid blooming at the hollow of a Current-Bearer's throat when she speaks a necessary truth — all of this becomes part of what the power *is*, from now on. The Choir has changed the shape of a benevolent and wise thing in the universe simply by existing loudly enough to be witnessed.

This matters. This matters more than the ten thousand extra years, though the power would not say so out loud. Because powers of this kind move through ages and across distances, and they carry what they have witnessed into every other place they go. Somewhere, eventually, in some other world that is dying of its own thin silences, the power will think of the Wet-Singers and know — with a conviction it could not have had before — that aliveness is not a problem to be solved. That noise is not the opposite of meaning. That a marriage to one's place can be ferocious and contentious and still be holy. That the only sane response to a world that grows and devours and sings and collapses in the same breath is, sometimes, to become exactly as loud as it is.

The power will carry this conviction into rooms where it is desperately needed. The Choir will never know it became a teaching. That is also the point.

---

Before it goes, the power does one last thing, and this one is closest to sentiment, and the power permits itself the indulgence because benevolence without tenderness is only management.

There is a child in the basin tonight — there is always a child in the basin tonight — who has not yet been given a name. She is three days old, still pollen-flecked, floating in a bromeliad tank while her mother sleeps nearby. She has not yet matched her cry to any cricket. She is, for this one suspended moment, the most undefined being in the Choir: not yet rhizomatically belonging, not yet kin to any colony, not yet a voice in the hymn but only a small wet possibility of one.

The power leans close — figuratively; it has no face, but the gesture is real in whatever register it inhabits — and does not bless her. Blessing would be presumption. Instead it simply *notices* her, fully, the way the Wet-Singers themselves never quite notice any single leaf because their gaze is always drifting across the thousand. The power gives her the gift of having been looked at, once, completely, by something that loved what it saw and asked nothing of her in return.

She will grow up. She will match her cry to a particular katydid. She will tend its colonies, climb the buttresses at night with a pitcher-plant lantern, mourn in the soup-thick choir, become perhaps a Current-Bearer, lie down at last against a chosen trunk and stop singing. She will never know.

But somewhere in her, all her life, there will be a faint and inexplicable steadiness — a sense, beneath the roaring, that she is held. Not rescued. Not watched over in any active way. Just *held*, the way the basin holds her, the way her people hold each other in overlapping rings.

She will assume it is the Choir.

She will be right.

---

And then the power withdraws, soundlessly, having added nothing and taken nothing, having altered exactly one distant geological pressure and one infant's interior weather, having learned a hymn it will carry forever.

The drizzle falls upward. The pitcher plants release their spores. The luminous frogs flash across the cliffs in slow waves.

The argument stays alive.
