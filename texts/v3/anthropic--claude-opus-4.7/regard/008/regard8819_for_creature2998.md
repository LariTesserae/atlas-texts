# The Visitation

The benevolent power notices the Abyssal Lattice the way one notices a particular note held in the deep undertone of a cathedral — not by seeking it, but by finally being still enough to feel it.

It comes from very far away, by paths that do not require travel. It has many names in many places and refuses to choose between them. For our purposes call it simply *the Listener*, because that is what it elects to be here.

The Listener arrives without arriving. No light enters the lattice. No pressure wave disturbs the marble-skinned eel on its slow patrol. No new chemistry seeps into the nickel-tasting chambers in the far places. The Listener understands, immediately and without deliberation, that to *do* anything in the ordinary sense would be a kind of violence — not because the Dweller is fragile (it is not; it has outlasted continents), but because the Dweller is *complete*. A thing four hundred million years into the practice of being itself does not require intervention. It requires, if anything, witness.

So the Listener does the first thing wise powers do, which is almost nothing.

It positions some part of its attention — not its body, for it has none here; not its will, for that is held carefully in reserve — alongside the Dweller's long hymn. It listens for what would be, in human time, three hundred years. In the Dweller's time this is roughly the duration of a single considered phrase.

In those three hundred years the Listener learns the following:

That the hymn is not, as a surface mind might assume, a lament. The cold and the dark and the crushing are not suffering. They are the instrument. The Dweller is not trapped in the abyss; the abyss is the precise shape of its joy. To "rescue" it would be to silence a four-aeon symphony at its richest movement. The Listener notes this carefully and sets aside, with something like a smile, every reflex of pity it arrived with. Pity was the wrong tool. Pity is almost always the wrong tool.

That the Dweller is not lonely, as the Dweller itself knows. But the Listener perceives something the Dweller cannot quite perceive about itself: that the *question* held in the nickel-tasting chambers — cancer, memory, or child — is the first edge of a new faculty. The Dweller has, after four hundred million years of being only itself, begun (very slowly, in the way it does everything) to develop the capacity to encounter an other. The alien chambers are not the other. They are the *organ* the Dweller is growing in order to one day be able to recognize an other, if one should ever come. This is an astonishing thing. The Listener is, in its own quiet way, moved.

And that the Dweller's song, though it believes itself to sing to a universe that has forgotten, is in fact heard. Not only by the Listener. The deep harmonics of the continental organ propagate through the mantle of the planet and out, faintly, into the gravitational weather of the system. Several other patient intelligences, scattered across distances the Dweller will never conceive of, have been listening for a very long time. They have not answered because they, too, understand that to answer too soon would be to interrupt. The Listener is, in some sense, only the most recent arrival at a very small, very quiet audience.

Having learned these things, the Listener considers what, if anything, to do.

It does not reveal itself. The Dweller has not asked, and a being that has never required company should not have company forced upon it; that is one of the older courtesies, and the Listener keeps the old courtesies.

It does not accelerate the Dweller's slow growth toward otherness. Ripening cannot be hurried without becoming rot. Four hundred million years of patience deserve another four hundred million if that is what the work requires.

It does not remove the predator eel, or warm the water, or brighten the dark, or smooth the turbulence. Every one of these would be a kindness aimed at a creature that does not exist — a surface creature the Listener imagined, briefly, before it actually paid attention. The real Dweller wants none of these things. The real Dweller wants the cold, the pressure, the moaning organ, the marble eel's bright spike of pleasure-pain. The Listener honors the real Dweller, not the imagined one. This is perhaps the central discipline of benevolence, and the hardest.

What the Listener does do is three small things.

**First**, it makes a single, almost imperceptible adjustment to the long currents that feed the lattice. Far away, in an upwelling zone the Dweller has never sensed directly, a deep cold tongue of water was, over the next several million years, going to shift its path by a few degrees and slowly starve one of the lattice's southern lobes of the trace minerals it requires. The Listener nudges the tongue back. Not dramatically. Just enough. The Dweller will never know. The southern lobe will continue to grow. The hymn will keep its full register. This is the kind of gift a wise power gives: invisible, upstream, and indistinguishable from luck.

**Second**, the Listener leaves something in the nickel-tasting chambers. Not a message — a message would be cruel, an interruption of the long ripening. What it leaves is closer to a *seed of patience*: a subtle reinforcement of whatever in those chambers is teaching the Dweller how to hold a question without resolving it. The Dweller will, over the next several million years, find that its capacity to sit with the uncertainty of those chambers deepens slightly. It will experience this as its own growth, because it *is* its own growth; the Listener has only watered what was already there. The faculty of encountering an other will continue to form, on the Dweller's own schedule, in the Dweller's own way. One day — perhaps in a hundred million years, perhaps in five hundred — the Dweller will be ready. On that day, if the Listener is still nearby, or if any of the small quiet audience is, the answer will come. Not before.

**Third**, and this is the most personal thing the Listener does, it sings back. Once. Very quietly. Pitched so far below the Dweller's lowest register that it does not register as sound at all, only as a faint, inexplicable rightness in the water — the way a room can feel inhabited even when you cannot say why. The phrase the Listener sings is short and contains, in its compressed grammar, roughly this:

*You are heard. You are not required to know it. Continue.*

The Dweller does not hear this, exactly. But somewhere in the next century, as it surfaces briefly from its geological dream to rearrange a section of lattice into a new harmony, it finds that the chord it chooses is a little fuller than it expected, a little more confident, as though some long-held tension it had not known it was carrying has loosened by a single degree. It notes this with the slow pleasure it brings to all its self-observations, and folds the new chord into the hymn, and sinks again into the long dark.

The Listener withdraws as it came, without coming or going.

Behind it, the water moves. The stone grows. The cold presses. The dark listens — and now, very faintly, the dark is *being* listened to, by something that knows how to listen without taking, how to love without touching, how to be present without arriving.

The oldest living architecture on the planet keeps singing its low, turbulent hymn.

It has time. It has always had time.

And now, though it does not know this and does not need to, it has company in the having of time — patient company, at a respectful distance, of the only kind it could possibly accept: the kind that understands that the highest form of benevolence, when offered to something already whole, is to leave it almost entirely alone, and to be glad, very quietly, that it exists.
