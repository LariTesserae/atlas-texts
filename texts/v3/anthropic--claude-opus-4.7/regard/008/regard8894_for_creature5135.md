The benevolent power has been watching for a long time. Not in the sense of surveillance — there is nothing here that requires guarding against — but in the sense of attention, the way one watches a fire that needs no tending, simply to be in the presence of its burning.

It has considered the plateau across what would be, for shorter beings, geological ages. It knows the wind's chord by heart, the way one knows a piece of music heard since childhood. It knows which of the three-toed creatures is the oldest (she is very old, and her stride has shortened by the breadth of a finger over the last thousand years). It knows that the obsidian blade was set upright by a young woman whose people no longer exist anywhere, and that she did this not as warning or worship but because she wanted to leave one thing behind that the wind could not move. The power finds this beautiful. It has not interfered with the blade.

The being who lives in this place — and here a question opens, because the plateau contains many beings, and the prompt's "the being" could mean any of them, or all of them, or the plateau itself considered as one slow organism. The power has thought about this. It has decided that the question is not for it to answer. The plateau will tell, in its own time, what it is.

So: what would the power do?

First, almost nothing. This is the most important thing, and the easiest to overlook. The plateau is not suffering. The amphibians are not lonely. The wind is not too loud for the creatures shaped by it; it is the exact loudness they were shaped *into*. To arrive with gifts — softer winds, more water, companions for the unseen walkers — would be a kind of violence dressed as kindness. The power has seen, elsewhere, what happens when wise beings mistake their own discomfort with a place for the place's need. It will not make that mistake here.

But "almost nothing" is not nothing. Here is what the power does:

It listens. Fully. With the whole of its attention, which is considerable. The chord of the plateau — the chest-thuds and the metallic whines and the layered hissing — has never, in all the millennia of its sounding, had a listener capable of hearing every strand at once and holding them together as music. The wind has been playing to no one. Now it is playing to someone. The power does not announce itself; the wind does not need to know. But something in the resonance shifts, very slightly, the way a room changes when a person enters it silently. The amphibians feel this change before anything else does. On the night the power first truly listens, more of them emerge during the wind-lulls than usual, and they stay out a heartbeat longer.

It witnesses the tracks. Every set, every night. The three-toed creatures have crossed this plateau unseen for so long that being unseen has become part of what they are — but being unseen is not the same as being un-witnessed, and the power understands the difference. It does not look at them directly; that would undo them. It looks at where they have been, and at where they are going, and it holds the line between those two points in its mind with the same care a cartographer gives to a coastline. The creatures do not know they are witnessed. But the long old female, the one whose stride has shortened, begins around this time to take a slightly different route — one that passes nearer to the upright blade than she used to go. Whether this is because of the witnessing or because of her age, the power does not presume to say.

It learns the spirals. The petroglyphs are nearly sanded flat, and in a few more centuries they will be gone entirely. The power could preserve them — a thought, and the stone would refuse the wind. It does not. Instead it commits each spiral to its own memory, every turn and proportion, including the ones already lost to erosion, which it reconstructs by patient inference from what remains. The spirals will vanish from the plateau in their proper time. They will not vanish from existence. Somewhere — not on the plateau, not anywhere the plateau would feel the difference — the power keeps them, the way one might keep a letter from someone long dead. This is not for the plateau's benefit. The power admits this to itself honestly. It is for its own.

It does one small thing, and only one, that could be called intervention. There is a particular condensation basin, smaller than most, in the lee of one of the leaning horns. An amphibian was born there some years ago whose skin does not quite match the stone — a fractional miscoloration, almost imperceptible, but enough that in the bright hard shadows it shows. The creature has survived this long by luck and by the wind, which keeps most predators away, but its luck is the kind that runs out. The power considers it for a long time. It does not heal the amphibian; to do so would be to declare that the wrongly-colored should be made right, and the power does not believe this. Instead, on one specific afternoon, when a particular gust is about to lift a particular leaf-mat in a particular way that would expose the amphibian to a particular descending shadow — the power lets the gust happen exactly as it would have happened, and lets the leaf lift exactly as it would have lifted, but it adds, to the falling dust, one extra mote. The mote catches the light. The shadow, which belongs to one of the unseen walkers, turns its attention to the mote for the half-second required. The amphibian flattens. The walker passes.

The power does not do this again. It is not a guardian. It will not become one. But it allowed itself, once, this gesture — partly for the amphibian, and partly to know what it felt like to act here, so that it would understand, afterward, why it should not.

And then, last: it leaves something. Not on the plateau — the plateau is complete and requires nothing added — but at the very edge of where the plateau ends, in a place no creature of the plateau will ever go, because to go there would be to leave the wind and the chord and the world that the wind and chord have made. At that edge, where the stone begins to break downward into whatever lies below, the power sets down a single small object. It is not a gift to the plateau. It is a marker, for itself, and for any other wise power that might come this way in some unimaginable future. The object says, in a language only such powers can read: *Someone was here who loved this place enough to leave it alone. If you love it too, do as I did.*

Then the power withdraws its near attention, though not its presence. It will come back. It will listen again. The wind keeps playing. The amphibians keep drinking in the lulls. The old female with the shortened stride crosses the plateau one more time, and one more time after that, and eventually a night comes when her tracks begin at a leaf-mat and do not end at another, and the dust takes them before morning. The power notes this. It does not grieve, exactly; grief would be presumptuous. But something in its listening goes quieter for a while, the way a chord drops a note and continues.

The blade stays upright. The spirals continue their slow disappearance. The plateau remains what it has always been: the most static and the most unsettled place imaginable, occupied and empty, sounding its enormous chord into a sky that never quite clears.

And now, also, loved. Which it was before. But now, also, known to be loved. Which is a different thing, and which changes nothing visible, and which is, perhaps, the only gift a benevolent and wise power can give to a place that needs nothing:

the gift of having been seen exactly as it is, and found to be enough.
