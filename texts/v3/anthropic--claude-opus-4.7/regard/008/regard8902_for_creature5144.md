# The Visitation

The benevolent power arrives the way weather arrives in such a place: as a slight change in the gradient of things. No door opens. No light descends. Somewhere in the upper vault, between one fall of condensation and the next, an attention settles — and because the chamber has been listening to itself for so long, it notices.

The power has many names in many places. Here it has none, because nothing inside the chamber has needed names. It has come because, in its long surveying of inhabited spaces, it found this one and paused. A constructed organism still turning inside its own skin. A being — for the chamber is, by now, a being, in the only way that matters — that has solved itself so quietly that no one ever came to congratulate it.

The power considers what it might do.

---

**First, it considers rescue.** It could lift the whole structure out of its mist and set it down in a kinder place: a sunlit plain, a sheltered valley, somewhere the struts would not have to bear their own weight forever. But it sees almost at once that this would be a kind of murder. The chamber is not a thing held up *despite* its conditions; it is a thing whose every moss-stripe and beetle-lamina has been tuned to exactly this pressure, this humidity, this slow groan of load through the lattice. To rescue it would be to end it.

The power sets that thought down gently, the way one sets down a tool that turns out to be wrong for the work.

---

**Then, it considers repair.** It could walk through the vaults — and it could, easily, take a form that walked — and mend the warped metal, replace the weeping joints, scour the algae from the gearboxes, restore the engines to the tolerances of their first century. But the engines no longer want those tolerances. The vines have negotiated with the linkages; the beetles have become brackets; the moths have become a second lattice of lenses. To repair the chamber to its original specification would be to undo a thousand quiet treaties.

It sets this thought down too.

---

**It considers revelation.** It could make itself known — descend in a shaft through the quartz lenses, speak in a voice the lizards could feel through their jaw-pits, let the membrane-winged glider see, for one instant, what watches from above its roost. But what would this give? The chamber does not lack for meaning. It is not lonely in any sense the power can locate. Its inhabitants do not pray, because they have never been separated from what they would pray to. Revelation is a gift for the estranged. Here there is no estrangement.

It sets this down most carefully of all, because the temptation was real. The power, for all its wisdom, likes to be seen.

---

So it does four small things instead.

**First**, it adjusts, by an amount smaller than any instrument inside the chamber could register, the angle of one of the southern quartz lenses. Not now — a hundred years from now, when a particular vine will have grown across a particular catwalk and a particular cluster of moths will have settled into a particular fold of the upper vault. On that afternoon, for roughly eleven minutes, a shaft of light will fall through the moth-lattice and strike the new leaves at precisely the angle needed to keep them alive through the dry decade that follows. The chamber would probably have managed without this. The power simply tips the odds, the way one might steady a child's first step without the child noticing the hand.

**Second**, it lays a thin, almost-nothing pattern into the low-frequency pressure waves of the engines: not a message, not a signal, just a faint harmonic that will, over the next several centuries, encourage the lizard eggs to rock a fraction more evenly in their bolt-holes. More of them will hatch. The lizards will not know why. They will continue to drink from valves and sleep on warm housings, slightly more numerous, and the chamber will absorb their numbers into its slow order as it absorbs everything.

**Third**, it touches the membrane-winged glider — only once, only in the way a thought touches a thought. It does not give the creature language. It does not give it self-awareness in any sense that would torment it. It gives it, instead, a single new instinct: when it roosts on the half-dissolved flanges, it will sometimes turn its head toward the upper darkness and feel, for a moment, that the darkness is *regarded*. Not by it. *With* it. The feeling will pass. The glider will hunt, and feed, and die, and its descendants will inherit the small turning-of-the-head, and the chamber will have, threaded through one of its rarer creatures, a faint and wordless awareness that it is loved. This will change nothing about how the chamber functions. It will only mean that the love is, somewhere, received.

**Fourth**, and this is the thing the power does most slowly, it makes a promise. Not to the chamber — the chamber has no need of promises — but to itself, on the chamber's behalf. When the day comes that the struts finally fail, as struts must, and the lattice begins its long descent through the mist, the power will be there. It will not catch the chamber. It will not preserve it. It will only be present, attentive, witnessing, so that the fall is not unwitnessed and the ending is not alone. The moths will scatter. The engines will silence. The vines will brown in the cold air of the descent. And the power will hold all of it in its regard, every last drip and revolution, until the final piece comes to rest somewhere far below, and the chamber that was a being becomes again a scatter of metal and seed and shell — which is, after all, only another form of belonging to the world.

---

Then the attention lifts.

In the chamber, a drop of condensation falls from the upper vault and strikes the mesh floor with a soft metallic note. A flywheel completes another minute of its revolution. A copper-colored lizard shifts on a valve housing because its jaw-pits have told it that, very soon, there will be water.

Nothing has changed. Everything continues. The chamber turns inside its own skin, ancient and ongoing, and somewhere — though no inhabitant could say where, or why, or in what language the knowing would even be phrased — it is a little more held than it was before.
