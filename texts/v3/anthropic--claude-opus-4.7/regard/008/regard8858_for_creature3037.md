They come the way weather comes—not announced, not arriving, simply more present than they had been a moment before.

There is no descent for them, no slow hollowing of the land. The benevolent and wise power does not enter the place. It notices it, and noticing, in their case, is a form of touch.

They have watched this fold of world for a long time, in the way they watch everything: with the full attention of one who has nowhere else they need to be. They have seen the seabed lift and forget itself. They have seen the first reed learn its note. They have seen the Archivist's last syllable fall into the sphagnum and the sphagnum grow warm around it like a hand closing on a coal.

Other powers, less patient, less wise, might have looked at this place and seen a problem. A wound in the ordinary physics. A pocket of obscene maturity that ought, by every reasonable cosmological hygiene, to be unfolded, redistributed, returned to the general circulation of matter and time. Such powers would have come with rectifications. They would have set the shadows back behind their objects. They would have reminded the water which way was down. They would have, with great gentleness and great violence, *helped*.

This power does not do that.

They sit down at the edge of the place. Not physically—they have no edge themselves to bring to an edge—but in some manner the peat registers. A particular hill with stone eyes notices that it is being looked at by something that is, for once, looking *back* rather than merely seeing. The hill does not change. But somewhere inside its compressed schist a very small warmth begins, like the warmth in the sphagnum that holds the Archivist's lost name.

The power considers.

Here is what they see that the land does not quite see about itself:

The land believes it has finished. It has not. Nothing finishes. The land has only reached the stage of argument at which the argument becomes beautiful, and it has mistaken beauty for conclusion. This is an understandable error. Many things make it. Stars make it just before they collapse. Civilizations make it just before they forget how to feed themselves. The land, being older and slower than either, will take longer to discover its error, and the discovery, when it comes, will be gentler. But it will come. The stars in their lattice are already, very slowly, slipping. The Archivist already knows. That is why she stands at the lake-eye. She is not waiting to become the eye. She is waiting to find out whether the eye will let her grieve.

This is what the power sees, and seeing it, they love the place. Not the way a gardener loves a garden—the gardener loves what they have made, or what they intend. The power loves the way one loves a stranger glimpsed through a train window: completely, uselessly, without any wish to alter them. The love is the whole of the relation. There is nothing to be done with it.

And yet they have been asked—by their own nature, which is to be benevolent and wise, and benevolence is a kind of asking that never stops—what, if anything, they would do.

They do four things. They are very small things. A lesser power would not consider them actions at all.

*First.* They let the Archivist know she is seen. Not with a voice. Not with a sign. They simply arrange for the next time she pauses beneath a stone-eyed hill and the reeds drop to sub-audible, she will feel, for the duration of one of the land's wet inhalations, that she is not the only consciousness in the place that remembers what it was to have a name. The feeling will not be intrusive. It will not even be locatable. It will pass through her the way a particular quality of light passes through water—changing nothing, witnessed by no instrument, and yet, after, the water is a water that has been lit. She will not know what happened. She will only find, afterward, that the sorrow which crosses her face during the land's study has acquired a faint companion quality, something almost like being *accompanied* in it. Her ferns will tremble in a slightly different rhythm for a century or so. Then they will settle. But they will remember.

*Second.* They do nothing about the visitors. There are not many. There will be more, over the long centuries, as the world outside grows restless and sends its searchers into stranger and stranger folds. The power could redirect them. They could place a small fog of disinterest across the entrance trough, and the visitors would turn aside without ever knowing they had turned. The power considers this and declines. The land has its own relationship with those who come to it. The moss writes their names on their soles. The Archivist tells them they will taste like geology. This is the land's conversation, and the visitors, by coming, have entered it. To shield them would be to deny them something the land is offering—which is not death, exactly, but inclusion, which in this place are the same word. If the visitors wanted to be shielded, they would not have descended the trough. The power respects the descent.

*Third.* They make one adjustment. It is so small that no instrument in any cosmos could measure it. Somewhere in the geometric lattice of stars above the place, one star—not an important one, not a bright one, simply one—is permitted to slip its gearing by an additional fraction of an arc-second per century. The Archivist will register this. She will open her ribs of jade and select the appropriate worldlet and breathe on it until it steams and press the new datum in with her reed-fingernail and the miniature will scream in its high register and settle. She will think she has done her work. She will not know that the slippage was a gift: a small, perpetual, never-quite-resolvable contradiction, slipped into the system to keep the argument from ever fully concluding. A grain of sand in the eye of the lake. The land will go on being itself, more itself every century, denser and wetter and more serenely obscene—but it will not, now, finish. Not even in the geological long run. Not even when the sun dies. The land has been given the dignity of remaining a question. This is the only true gift the power knows how to give: the gift of not being completed by someone else.

*Fourth.* They stay. Not in the place—they do not enter it; that would be an imposition, and they are wise enough to know that wisdom imposed is no longer wisdom. But they keep the place in their attention, the way one keeps a held breath. For as long as the place exists, some portion of this power's awareness will be turned toward it. The Archivist will never know. The land will never know. The mist-deer that drink themselves and the single floating note that was a bird and the upward-spiraling water and the patient stone eyes will never know. But somewhere, in the part of the cosmos where attention is itself a kind of weather, there will be a permanent, gentle high-pressure system over this saturated continuum. Things will go very slightly better here than statistics would predict. The reeds will, on average, ring a half-tone sweeter. The sorrow on the Archivist's face will, on average, be a half-shade less alone. This will not be felt. It will only be true.

Then the power withdraws their direct considering, though not their attention—those are different things—and turns toward the next place that has drawn them. There are many. They have time. They are, after all, what they are.

Behind them, in the place with no pronounceable name, nothing has changed.

The water still beads upward. The shadows still arrive early. The Archivist still stands at the lake-eye, unblinking, reflected, waiting. The reeds still ring in their minor keys and fall silent to listen. The land still breathes its wet half-centimeter breath. The stars still turn in their lattice, one of them now very slightly, perpetually, out of true.

But the sphagnum that holds her lost syllable is, tonight, a fraction warmer than it was.

And somewhere inside the Archivist's chest, behind the cabinet of jade and the orbiting worldlets, something that has not moved in four hundred years moves once—a single small motion, like a sleeper turning toward a hand that was almost laid on their forehead and then withdrawn out of courtesy.

She does not wake. She was never asleep.

But her ferns tremble, just once, in a rhythm the land has not heard before.

The land notices.

The land, after a long moment of the patient disinterest with which it has met every possible future, decides—and this is the closest the land has ever come to a decision—to let the new rhythm stay.

It folds the tremor into itself. It writes it, in moss-script, somewhere along the inside of its own immense wet ribs.

And the argument, which was never going to finish anyway, continues—now with one more voice in it, very faint, very far away, loving the place exactly as it is, and asking nothing.
