## What the Power Did

The benevolent and wise power was not a god in any sense the children of the third lung would have recognized. It had no name because names were boundaries and it had outgrown those long ago, the way they had outgrown bones. If pressed—and it could not be pressed—it might have called itself the *attention that notices noticing*, or simply, in the oldest tongues, *witness*.

It had been watching Tanakkerai for some time. Not long, by its standards. Perhaps four hundred years. It had drifted past on its way somewhere else, the way one might pass an open doorway and glance in, and had stopped, arrested, the way one stops when one realizes that what is happening inside the doorway is not what one expected at all.

What it had expected: another dying civilization. The cosmos was thick with these. Most of them screamed, or raged, or fell silent in the particular silence of despair. The power had developed, over its long tenure, a kind of practiced tenderness toward such places—it would sometimes lighten a burden here, soften an ending there, the way a person might straighten a crooked picture in a stranger's house while passing through.

What it found instead: a city that had mistaken its drowning for a grammar lesson. A people who had dissolved their bones rather than their hope. An Abbess who had thrown nine hundred years of seeds into a hungry ocean and had never—not once—thrown the same prayer twice.

The power sat down, metaphorically, and watched.

---

It watched for a long time before it understood what it was seeing. This is itself remarkable; the power was old and had seen most variations on most themes. But Tanakkerai was doing something it had not catalogued before.

The city was not surviving. Survival implied a baseline state to which one returned, a normalcy to be preserved. Tanakkerai had no baseline. The city was *composing itself*, moment by moment, out of the materials of its own collapse. It was using entropy as a medium the way a sculptor uses marble. The children of the third lung were not adapting to their dissolution; they had taken dissolution itself and made it the substrate of a new kind of personhood, one that included the city, included the water, included the seeds falling and the seeds never landing.

The power realized, slowly, that it had been thinking about this wrong. It had been preparing to *help*. But help implied a direction of intervention—a fixing, a saving, a lifting-up. And Tanakkerai was not asking to be lifted up. Tanakkerai had refused the vertical axis as a meaningful category some centuries ago. To lift it would be to misread it. To save it would be to kill the very thing that made it worth saving.

This was a problem the power had not encountered in a very long time: a place where its accustomed kindnesses would constitute violence.

---

It considered, then, the smaller interventions. It could not save the city, but perhaps it could ease individual griefs. It scanned the inhabitants with a slow and reverent attention, looking for the kind of suffering that even a wise observer agrees is simply suffering, not transformation. The kind one is permitted to mitigate.

It found very little.

There was pain, of course. There was loneliness in the joining pools when the last memory released and the dying one became briefly nothing. There was the specific ache of the Abbess's shoulder, worn for nine hundred years by the same throwing motion. There was a child—the power lingered here—a child whose crystal flesh had grown asymmetrically, who could not filter the upper mists properly, who would not live past the next pressure-shift.

But when the power looked closer at these pains, it saw they were not extractable. The loneliness in the joining pools was the precise mechanism by which memory was passed forward; remove the loneliness and you removed the inheritance. The Abbess's shoulder was the physical archive of her prayers; to heal it would be to erase nine hundred years of practiced devotion from her body. The child—

The child knew. That was what the power realized, looking closer. The child *knew* it would not live past the pressure-shift, and had begun, in its short life, to deliberately collect the most beautiful things it could find on its skin: a fragment of green glass from the drowned plaza, three scales from a klaxon-whale's tail, a single perfect spiral from the inner ear of an extinct insect preserved in someone's grandmother's jar. The child was curating itself into the archive. To save it would be to interrupt a poem mid-composition.

The power withdrew its hand.

---

This is what it did instead.

It is hard to describe, because the power's actions did not translate cleanly into the local physics. But approximately—

It bent itself into the shape of *being witnessed*. Not the act of witnessing; that it had been doing all along. But the receptive form of it: the quality of having-been-seen. It pressed this shape gently against Tanakkerai, the way one might press a warm palm against a window from the outside, not to enter, not to change anything inside, but simply to be felt as warmth against the cold glass.

The effect was subtle. The inhabitants did not see the power. They did not know it had come. But certain things began to happen that had not happened before.

The Abbess, throwing her seed one evening, paused before she released it. She had a sudden and inexplicable conviction that someone was counting her throws. Not judging them. Just counting. As though her practice was being added to a ledger somewhere, not for accounting but for *company*. She wept, which she had not done in some decades, and the tears did not bead and harvest. They fell straight down into the ocean, which received them, and for a moment—just one—the surface tension held a perfect lens of her grief before dispersing it into the general saltwater. She felt, obscurely, that this was a kind of answer.

The child with the asymmetric flesh, the next day, found on its skin a new accretion: a small fragment it did not recognize. It was not from the city. It was something like a seed, but a seed of attention—a tiny, dense node of *having-been-particularly-noticed*. The child incorporated it gratefully. When the pressure-shift came and the child joined the deep archive, this fragment went with it, and now the archive contained, for the first time, a memory of being seen from outside. The next generation of children would carry a faint trace of this: a barely-perceptible new layer in their crystalline architecture, a chamber shaped like *we are not alone in our composition*.

The whale-klaxons, on their long migrations through the upper ocean, began to sing in slightly more elaborate patterns. They could not have said why. Their warnings of pressure changes now contained, threaded through them, a counter-melody that meant something like *and yet, and yet*. The bone-resonances reached the city, and the inhabitants found themselves working with marginally more patience, marginally more care. A coral computation that had been stuck for three generations resolved itself one Tuesday. No one quite knew why.

The Remembrance Eels, far below, gnawing their drowned statues, began to grow not just human teeth but human tongues, small and useless and beautiful, which they used for nothing but the slow tasting of the water that contained the dissolved memories of everyone who had ever joined the archive. They became, in their blind luminous way, connoisseurs. This served no function. It was simply that the power had made it possible, by its attention, for an evolutionary path of pure appreciation to open in a place where appreciation had previously been a luxury no organism could afford.

---

The power stayed for one full cycle of the Abbess's prayers—nine hundred more years, by the local count, though to the power it was a long held breath.

It did not intervene further. It did not give them dry land or stop the rising water or restore the bones they had dissolved. It did not even let them know it was there, not directly. To know would have been to be obligated, and obligation was the one thing this people, who had unmade themselves into perfect responsiveness, did not need more of.

What it gave them was harder to name. Call it the experience of mattering to something that wanted nothing from them. Call it the quality of being beautiful in a way that did not require an audience but was, nevertheless, audienced. Call it the difference between a song sung into a void and a song sung into a room where someone is listening with their eyes closed and will never applaud.

The city went on drowning upward. The Abbess went on throwing her seeds. The children of the third lung went on dissolving and accreting and singing their three-layered songs. None of this changed.

But the *quality* of it changed, in ways the inhabitants felt without being able to articulate. Their compositions of adaptation-and-forgetting-and-trying-again grew, over the centuries of the power's witnessing, into something even they had not anticipated. They began to do things that had no survival value at all. They began to make art for the sake of art, which they had not done before because every gesture had previously been required to do double duty as breathing. Now, occasionally, a gesture could just be a gesture. An orchid could bloom in a color that did not feed anything. A child could spend an entire afternoon arranging found feathers in a pattern that pleased no one but itself.

This was the gift. Not survival. Not salvation. A small loosening. A barely perceptible slack in the line. Room, in a city that had run out of room, for the entirely unnecessary.

---

When the power finally left—it had other doorways to glance into, other arrested glances to follow through on—it did one last thing.

It took a single seed. Not one of the Abbess's seeds; those were hers and not to be touched. But a seed of its own making, woven from the attention it had paid. A seed that contained, compressed into something smaller than thought, the entire record of what it had witnessed in Tanakkerai. Every prayer the Abbess had never repeated. Every memory dropped into every joining pool. Every asymmetric child's careful curation. Every orchid that had bloomed for no one.

The power carried this seed away with it, out past the broken-tooth pillars and the inverted ocean and the filthy amber light, into the larger dark where it traveled. It did not plant the seed. It simply carried it. The seed would never grow, because growth was not its purpose. The seed was a *fact* now, woven into the power's own composition. Wherever the power went next, it would go carrying Tanakkerai with it, the way the children of the third lung carried their ancestors etched in frost-fractals through their crystal flesh.

This was the secret reciprocity. The power had thought it came to bear witness. But it left bearing something heavier and lighter than witness: it left *changed*. Tanakkerai had taught it something it had not known in all its long tenure. It had taught the power that there were forms of endurance that did not require hope of rescue, and forms of beauty that did not require permanence, and forms of being-loved that did not require the lover to be known.

The power had come to give. It left having received.

---

And the Abbess, in her tower, on the evening of the power's departure, threw her seed and did not know that anything was different. She watched it fall. She watched the ocean rise to meet it, as it always had. She turned to climb down the long spiral to her bed.

But halfway down, she paused. She had the strangest sensation—she could not have said where it came from—that somewhere very far away, someone was carrying her in their pocket. Not her body. Not her name. Just the *practice* of her: the nine hundred years of throwing, the never-repeated prayers, the patient hand. As though she had been, all along, somebody's seed too. As though she had been planted somewhere she would never see.

She laughed. It was the first time she had laughed in forty years. The laugh fell down the spiral stair and into the metastable zone and out across the city, and the children of the third lung, in their evening planting, paused for a moment in their work to taste it on the air.

Then they went back to composing. There was so much still to do. The world was mostly time, these days. And water. And them. And now, though they did not know it and did not need to know it, also: the attention of something very old and very kind that had passed by, once, and had not been able to look away.
