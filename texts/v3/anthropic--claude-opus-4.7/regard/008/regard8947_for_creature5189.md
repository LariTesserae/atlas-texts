Some answers, if followed honestly, take the shape of silence.

The wise power arrives — if *arrives* is even the right word for a presence that does not displace air — at the edge of the plateau. It has come because something in this place reached the threshold of its notice: not a cry, not a wound, but a completeness so total it registers like a held note in the fabric of attention. Benevolence is drawn to suffering, yes, but also to certain kinds of accomplishment. And this is one.

It looks. (It has whatever organ of looking suits it; let us call it looking.)

It sees the stems that have made of the wind a partner rather than an adversary. It sees the lizards whose stillness is not exhaustion but a kind of mastery — the long lesson of how to be exactly the shape of one's circumstance. It sees the insects that have dissolved the old quarrel between a body and the medium it moves through. It hears the single tone and understands that this is not the absence of music but music that has learned to sustain itself without the vulgarity of progression.

A lesser power — younger, more eager, more certain of its own gifts — would already be reaching. Would soften the light. Would carve a shadow somewhere for mercy's sake. Would seed the basins with something more ambitious, something that could *become*. Would, in the name of love, end the plateau by improving it.

The wise power does none of these things.

It understands, with the particular ache that wisdom carries, that to intervene here would be to mistake stillness for stagnation, equilibrium for imprisonment, continuance for a cry from help. The plateau is not waiting to be rescued. It is not enduring. It has, by some long and unwitnessed work, arrived. The lizards are not trapped in their grooves; they have *become* the grooves' answer. The stems are not starved; they have refined themselves to the exact thinness that the thin water can sustain forever. There is no suffering here that is not also the shape of a solution.

And so the benevolent power does the hardest thing benevolence can do, which is to recognize a place where its gifts are not needed. It does not pour. It does not seed. It does not soften or enrich or rescue.

But it does not simply leave, either. Leaving would be a kind of dismissal, and this place has earned more than that.

What it does, instead, is *witness*. Fully. With the entire weight of its attention, which is considerable. It stays — for what, in its own reckoning, is a long moment and, in the plateau's reckoning, is nothing at all — and it lets the single tone pass through it. It lets the level light fall across whatever surface it presents. It lets the lizards' stillness teach it something it had perhaps forgotten or never quite learned: that not every life is a story, that not every equilibrium is a trap, that some forms of being have made peace so deep it looks, from the outside, like nothing happening.

It receives the plateau as one receives a difficult and beautiful gift.

And then, because wisdom includes knowing when one's presence has become its own small disturbance — a shadow where there should be none, a listener where the tone needs no audience — it withdraws. Carefully. The way one steps back from a sleeping animal one has been privileged to see closely.

But it carries something away.

Not a sample. Not a memory in the ordinary sense. Something more like a tuning. Wherever it goes next — to the places that *do* cry out, the wounded worlds, the beings whose suffering is real and whose equilibrium is broken — it will carry the plateau in itself as a kind of reference pitch. A reminder that not all stillness is death. That sometimes the work of love is to leave a thing exactly as it is. That the universe contains, among its other accomplishments, a high pale stone where wind and lizard and stem have agreed, across an unmeasured age, to continue.

And in the centuries the power has yet to walk, when it is tempted — as even wise powers are tempted — to fix what is not broken, to improve what has already found its form, it will remember the plateau. And it will stay its hand.

That is what it does, if anything. It comes. It sees. It honors. It leaves the place untouched.

The light remains level. The tone does not pause. A lizard, half-lidded, does not register that it has been, for an instant, the teacher of something very old and very kind.
