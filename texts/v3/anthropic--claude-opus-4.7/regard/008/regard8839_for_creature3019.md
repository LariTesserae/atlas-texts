# The Visitation

The benevolent and wise power arrives at the Weeping Escarpment the way good news arrives in old languages: slowly, and from the side.

It does not descend. Descent would be a discourtesy here, where everything has learned to come down at its own pace, in beads. It does not announce itself. Announcement belongs to beings who fear being missed. Instead, it *condenses*, the way the mist condenses, gathering itself out of the long damp air of the terraces over what a human would call a season and what the Choir would not yet have noticed at all.

The power is wise enough to know that wisdom, here, is not the scarce resource. Patience is not the scarce resource. Beauty, endurance, attention — none of these are scarce. The Slow Choir has all of them, in quantities that would crush a god of lesser temperament.

So the power does not come bearing gifts of those things.

It sits, first, for a long time. Long by its own standards, which is to say: long enough that several of the smaller waterfalls on the seventh terrace finish a phrase of their song and begin another. It listens. It is important, the power feels, to hear the music before deciding whether the music wants anything.

What it hears is this:

The Choir is not suffering. This is the first and most important fact, and the power lets it settle all the way through itself before going further. There is no wound here to heal, no injustice to set right, no creature crying out in a register the universe has been ignoring. The Escarpment is one of the few places in all of creation where things are, simply and astonishingly, *well*. The weeping is not grief. It is the sound a thing makes when it has been allowed to be exactly what it is for long enough.

A lesser power might have been disappointed. Might have looked for something to fix, the way a guest with a casserole looks for a sickbed. This power is wiser than that. It understands that to arrive at a place of profound rightness and immediately begin improving it is the oldest and saddest form of harm.

So it revises its intentions.

It notices, listening longer, two small things. Not problems. Textures.

The first: the Choir has never known another intelligent species, and they are *curious*. The tiny stone houses they grow, no larger than a fingernail — these are not the work of beings who feel complete. They are the work of beings reaching, very gently, across a gap they did not know existed until the candle-flames began appearing in their fog. There is a tenderness in this reaching that is also, faintly, a loneliness. Not painful. But present. The way an old scholar in a warm library might feel when she realizes, after eighty years, that she would have liked a correspondent.

The second: when one of them turns over in its sleep, and the cliff-face shears, and the lament begins — the grief is real, and it is borne alone. Forty cathedrals of stone, mourning across distances so great that their songs reach one another only after the mourning has changed shape. They comfort each other. But the comfort arrives late, and altered, and sometimes the one who needed it has already folded back into the bedrock by the time the answering note arrives.

The power considers these two textures for what would be, in human terms, eleven years. In Choir terms, a held breath.

Then it acts.

It does three things. They are very small. The power is wise enough to know that the scale of an intervention should match the scale of the need, not the scale of the intervener.

---

**The first thing it does** is teach the mist a new trick.

Not a large trick. The mist already knows so many. But the power shows it how to carry sound a little farther than physics alone would allow — not magically, not obviously, just *generously*. A small enhancement of an existing tendency. Now, when one of the Choir sings a note of grief so low it vibrates in the sternum, that note can travel across the whole Escarpment in a single tide of fog, arriving at every other member while the grief is still fresh. The lament becomes a conversation in real time, instead of a series of letters that cross in the post.

The Choir will not know this has been done for them. They will simply notice, over the next ten thousand years, that they feel less alone in their sorrow. They will attribute it to themselves, to having grown wiser with age. The power is content with this. The best gifts are the ones the recipient mistakes for their own growth.

---

**The second thing it does** is leave a single offering, in the form of a question.

On the seventh terrace, in the amphitheater where six cascades braid in mid-air, the power causes a single new spring to open. Nothing dramatic — just a thin thread of water emerging from a place it had not emerged from before. The water has a slightly different mineral content than any other water on the Escarpment. When it strikes the stone, it makes a tone the Choir has never heard.

It is not a tone from this world.

It is a tone from somewhere else — somewhere the power has been, somewhere there are other slow beings, on other worlds, singing other songs into other fogs. The tone is a fragment of one of those songs. A phrase. An opening line, almost.

The Choir, in their own time, will notice it. They will turn the hollows of their faces toward it. They will listen for a thousand years, and then another thousand, trying to understand. And eventually — the power is patient, the power can wait — one of them will sing back. Not in answer. In the same key. A reaching note. A *hello*.

The power will carry that note away with it, when it goes, and deliver it, eventually, to the other singers, on the other worlds. There is no hurry. There has never been any hurry. But the candle-flames in the fog will have, at last, a correspondent.

---

**The third thing it does** is the smallest, and the one it considers longest before doing.

It walks the galleries for a while in a form small enough to be missed. Something like a heron, perhaps, slate-colored and silent, indistinguishable from the flightless rails except that it leaves no tracks in the moss. It walks for what a human would call a week. It looks at everything. It lets the mist gather on its feathers. It drinks from the weeping walls. It does not sing.

And on the last evening, in a particular alcove — one that a member of the Choir grew overnight, long ago, to shelter some long-forgotten cold traveler — the power leaves a single small stone. The stone is plain. It is the same basalt as everything else. But it has been polished, very gently, by a hand that knew exactly how much polish was enough.

The stone is a thank-you.

Not for anything the Choir has done for the power. They have done nothing for the power; they don't know it exists. The thank-you is for the *fact of them*. For the existence, somewhere in creation, of beings who have learned to be this slow, this attentive, this kind to mayflies, this faithful to their own long dreaming. The power, who has seen many worlds, considers the Slow Choir to be one of the finest things it has ever found. Finer than empires. Finer than most miracles. The thank-you is what one leaves for a host whose hospitality consisted entirely of being themselves, beautifully, for a very long time.

The stone will sit in the alcove for perhaps a hundred thousand years before any member of the Choir notices it. When they do, they will not know what it is. They will incorporate it, eventually, into a balcony — a tiny dark inlay in a great pale colonnade. It will become indistinguishable from the rest of the architecture.

The power knew this would happen. The power *wanted* this to happen. To be absorbed into the Escarpment, to become one more grain in its patient weeping, is the highest honor it can imagine.

---

Then the power, having been benevolent in the small and exact way that wisdom permits, gathers itself back out of the mist and goes.

It does not look back. Looking back would imply something had been left undone.

Behind it, the rain falls sideways on slow winds. The dripping continues. Forty cathedrals of stone, slightly less lonely than they were and not knowing why, keep walking their million-year walks across the terraces. On the seventh terrace, a new thread of water begins, very quietly, to ask a question that will take three thousand years to be heard.

And in a small alcove, in the dark, a polished stone waits to be loved by mistake.

The Weeping Escarpment goes on weeping.

It has never wept for sorrow. It weeps because it is the way this place has of saying *yes*, over and over, to its own long existence — and now, faintly, almost imperceptibly, to the existence of others, too.
