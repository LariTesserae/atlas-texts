They would not come down.

That is the first thing, and the most important. A wise power, seeing this place, would understand immediately that arrival is itself a kind of injury here. There is no clearing wide enough to set foot in without crushing something that has been patiently becoming itself for a hundred years. There is no silence to speak into that would not displace the cicada-roar that helps the water drain. There is no gift that could be handed to a people whose hands are already full of living rope, whose chambers are already shaped exactly to the curve of their sleeping bodies.

So the power would hold itself at the edge of the ridge — or above it, or inside the weather, or wherever such powers wait — and simply look for a long time. Long enough to learn that the green stain on a forearm is not dirt. Long enough to hear the difference between the murmur a mother makes to a vine and the murmur she makes to her child, and to understand that the difference is smaller than it first appeared. Long enough to notice that the elders' listening is itself a form of governance, and that nothing in the hamlet is decided by anyone who has not first pressed a palm to a wall.

After that long looking, the power would ask itself a careful question: *What does this place lack that it cannot grow for itself?*

And the honest answer, most days, would be: nothing.

This is the hardest discipline of benevolence — to recognize sufficiency when one encounters it, and to withhold the reflex of improvement. A lesser power would want to give. Would want to lengthen the lives of the climbers, sweeten the fruit, teach the people writing, drain the lanes, raise the temperature half a degree against some coming century. A wise one would understand that every such gift is also a subtraction: that the webbing between the toes exists because the lanes flood, and that to dry the lanes is to unmake the foot, and to unmake the foot is to unmake the particular human who belongs here and nowhere else.

So mostly the power would do nothing, and the doing-nothing would be the gift. It would be a held breath at the scale of weather. It would mean that when a logging concern, three valleys over, sends scouts up the wrong watershed, the scouts find the trail confusing and turn back without quite knowing why. It would mean that a disease which has learned to leap between the quick-furred climbers and human children meets, in this one hamlet, an unusually resilient strain of the resinous sap the beetles secrete, and falters. It would mean that the rains, in a year when they fail elsewhere, do not quite fail here. Small adjustments, made upstream of visibility. The power would take care that no one in the hamlet ever has cause to thank it, because gratitude is a debt, and debt is the beginning of the end of belonging-to-oneself.

There would be one exception. One thing the power might allow itself, because it is the one thing the place cannot grow.

It would, very gently, place a thought in the dreams of the eldest listener — not a vision, not a voice, just a shape the mind wakes already holding. The shape would be this: *there are other ridges*. Not as a summons, not as a warning, simply as a fact deposited where it can be turned over slowly across a lifetime. Because the only real danger to such a place is the belief that it is the whole world. A people who believe themselves to be the whole world will not survive their first encounter with what isn't them; they will either shatter or conquer, and both are forms of dying. But a people who carry, somewhere in their oldest listener, the quiet knowledge that the ridge is *a* ridge — those people can meet a stranger at the edge of the leaf-mold and decide, freely, what to do.

That is all. One seed of cosmology, planted once a generation, in the only mind supple enough to hold it without needing to act on it.

The power would then withdraw its attention, mostly, returning only at long intervals to check that the hamlet is still breathing — that the channels still overflow, that the children still wear their living sashes, that the eggs are still patterned like water droplets on broad leaves. Each time it looked, it would feel something it does not have a clean word for: a satisfaction that is also a kind of mourning, because to love a thing well is to know it will end, and to love it wisely is to refuse to be the one who ends it.

And on the day — far off, but real — when the hamlet does at last begin to unmake itself, whether by drought or by the arrival of a road or by some slow internal turning no one outside could have predicted, the power would not intervene then either. It would only make sure, as the last listener pressed her palm to the last wall, that she felt, in the warmth of the vine under her hand, that she had been seen. Not saved. Seen.

That, in the end, is what benevolence at its wisest can offer a place that has already learned how to be itself:

The dignity of being witnessed without being touched.
