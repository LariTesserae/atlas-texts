# What the Wise Power Would Do

The benevolent power arrives the way good weather arrives: without announcement, without footprint, without the violence of arrival. It has been watching for a long time. Long enough to understand that the wrong gift, here, would be a kind of murder.

It considers, first, what it will *not* do.

It will not heal the breakdown. The breakdown is not a wound. To a younger world the fraying of perspective and the pooling of hours would be a sickness, and a benevolent power would rush in with bandages of restored law. But this is not a younger world. The breakdown is what an ancient law looks like when it has finished its work and is finally permitted to rest. To repair it would be to drag a tired elder back to the loom and tell them they must weave again. That is not kindness. That is the cruelty of the well-meaning.

It will not finish the city. It sees, with perfect clarity, that it could. It could lay every final course of stone in a single luminous night. It could close every bridge over its second chasm. It could let the towers have their crowns at last. And it understands, looking at the vaults where the perfectly finished objects sit like quiet executioners, that this would be the end of the Unfinishing and the end of the long thought the plateau is thinking. Completion is the one form of death the place is genuinely afraid of, and a benevolent power does not inflict on a being the death it fears.

It will not multiply the Unfinishing. Four hundred is not a shortage. Four hundred is the exact number of slow hands the ruin requires. To add more would be to accelerate care into management, and management is a younger, hungrier thing.

It will not speak to them. Speech is forward motion. Speech assumes that something needs to be made clear, and clarity is a kind of haste. The Unfinishing have stepped sideways out of haste. To address them in language would be to call them back into a river they have politely left.

It will not be witnessed. Witness implies a watcher and a watched, and the plateau has already arranged itself beyond that geometry. To stand visible above the moss would be to introduce a center, and centers are exactly what this place has been forgetting for thirty thousand years.

So. Having set aside everything it will not do, the power considers what remains.

What remains is very small. This is how it knows it is the right thing.

---

It does three small things, and then it leaves.

**The first** is a permission. Somewhere in the deep grammar of the place—beneath the law that allows shadows to fall upward and echoes to precede their sounds—there is a clause the power finds and gently loosens. It is the clause that has, for the last several thousand years, been quietly insisting that the plateau must eventually decide. That the long thought must arrive somewhere. That the slow editing must, at some unimaginable point, produce a final manuscript.

The power loosens this clause the way one loosens a collar on a sleeping animal. Not removed. Only eased. From this night onward, the High Ruin is no longer required to be going anywhere. It may continue thinking, if it wishes, for as long as it wishes, without the faint pressure of having to *become*. It is released from teleology. It is granted, by something old enough to have the authority, the right to be a process rather than a project.

The Unfinishing will not know this has happened. But the stone will. The stone will sigh, once, very softly, sometime in the next two hundred years, and a single tower that had been worrying itself toward completion will allow one of its upper courses to come quietly loose and rest in the moss. One of the Unfinishing—she who is called The Eleven Winters Spent Beside the Cracked Pediment—will find the fallen stone in the morning and understand, without words, that the tower has been forgiven. She will weep a single tear, which will freeze on her cheek and become, eventually, a small bead of clear ice that another Unfinishing will find on her body after she has been edited, and will wear as the only ornament any of them have ever worn.

**The second** is a softening of the vaults. The power does not destroy the perfectly finished objects—the seamless cup, the both-ways door, the sphere that reflects only futures. To destroy them would be to spend the power's authority on a violence, and the place has had enough violence from time itself. Instead, the power renders them slightly less terrible. Not less perfect. Only less *final*. It threads into each one a thin filament of contingency, so subtle that the objects themselves do not notice. The cup is still seamless, but the seamlessness is now provisional, the way a held breath is provisional. The door still opens both ways at once, but it has begun, very slowly, to wonder about a third. The sphere still reflects only futures, but among the futures it reflects there is now, faintly, the possibility that it might one day reflect something else.

The Unfinishing will still visit the vaults rarely. They will still leave visibly shaken. But the shaking will be different. It will be the shaking of someone who has seen a face they recognize, rather than the face of their own erasure. Over the next several thousand years, this small difference will work its way outward through the whole plateau like warmth through a hand, and the Unfinishing will begin, without knowing why, to stand a little less braced against their own custodianship. The tenderness with which they coax the stone will acquire, very gradually, a note of something almost like companionship. Not hope. Hope is too forward-leaning. Something quieter. The recognition that they and the plateau are doing this together, and that neither is the warden of the other.

**The third** is a gift so small it is almost not a gift. The power leaves, somewhere in the damp moss at the foot of a wall that has not yet decided whether to stand or lie down, a single seed. The seed is not magical. It will not grow into a tree of knowledge or a flower of prophecy. It is the seed of an ordinary plant from somewhere far away—a small, hardy, unremarkable thing with grey-green leaves and a flower the color of bone. The seed will not sprout for nine hundred years. When it does sprout, it will produce one plant, which will live for forty years and then die without setting seed of its own.

But while it lives, it will be the first new species the High Ruin has admitted in longer than the Unfinishing have existed. And one of the Unfinishing—he who will be called, by then, The Two Hundred Years Spent Watching the Wall Decide—will find the plant on the morning of its first flower. He will sit beside it for the entire forty years of its life. He will not tend it. He will not protect it. He will simply keep it company, as the Unfinishing have always kept the stones company, while it decides what it is.

And in that long sitting, something in him will learn—not as knowledge, but as posture—that the patient dark between the stones is not only the grave of the world but also, occasionally, its cradle. That the High Ruin's long unfinishing is not only the postponement of an ending but also, in some way the Unfinishing have not allowed themselves to consider, the postponement of a beginning. That the plateau, in refusing to complete itself, has been keeping itself available—for what, none of them can yet say.

When he is finally edited, seated against a wall that has been waiting for exactly his shape, the small plant will already be dead. But its dry stem will remain beside him through the season it takes the moss to grow up through his ribs. And the moss, when it reaches the stem, will incorporate it. And the lichen, when it takes the color of his cloak, will take also, faintly, the color of that single bone-white flower.

This will mean nothing. Or it will mean everything. The High Ruin has not yet decided, and is no longer required to.

---

The power, having done these three small things, withdraws.

It does not look back. Looking back would be a kind of weight, and the place has weight enough.

It carries with it, as it goes, only one impression—not a thought, exactly, since the power is older than thought. An impression. The impression that what it has just attended is one of the more beautiful things in the long catalog of things it has attended. Not because the place is grand, though it is. Not because the Unfinishing are noble, though they are. But because here, in this single planetary shelf of bone-soft stone, a world and the beings inside it have arrived, together, at the most difficult of all arts: the art of refusing to hurry toward one's own meaning.

The power has seen many worlds finish themselves. It has seen very few learn to decline.

It leaves the High Ruin to its declining.

The soft rain keeps falling, bead by bead. The Unfinishing walk their changing routes. The stars, on certain nights, still rearrange themselves into proofs that hurt to witness, then forget. A tower, somewhere, has just allowed a stone to come loose and rest in the moss.

In a vault no one has visited for two centuries, a sphere of black stone reflects, for the first time, something that is not quite a future.

And the long thought continues, unhurried, uncentered, unwitnessed, and—now, very quietly, in a way none of its custodians will ever consciously know—unburdened.

It is in no hurry.

It has been given permission to never be.
