The benevolent power arrives the way weather arrives in a place that has no weather: not by entering, but by being noticed to have already been present.

It has watched the Underfoundry for a long time. Not with the appraising eye of an engineer, nor the proprietary eye of a god, but with the slow attention one gives to a poem written in a language one is still learning. It has seen the droplets fall. It has counted, idly, the number of microseconds the world forgets itself. It has watched the Curator hunched in its braid of ducts, wearing one hundred and eleven thousand small machines like a coat of voluntary stars, and it has felt something the wise rarely permit themselves to feel: tenderness so sharp it is almost grief.

Here is what it considers, and here is what it does not do.

It does not open the sky.

It would be the easy thing — to part the engineered heavens twenty kilometers above and let in light from elsewhere, real light, with a real source, casting real shadows that move. To show this world that it is not the whole of what is. The benevolent power knows a hundred sibling worlds where such an unveiling was the right gift, and the new sky was received as a deliverance. But the Underfoundry is not those worlds. The Underfoundry is *finished*. To break its ceiling would not be revelation; it would be vandalism dressed in mercy. The slow mechanisms on the underside of the heavens are turning toward needs that will never arise, and that turning is itself a kind of prayer. One does not interrupt a prayer to inform the praying that there is a wider room.

It does not wake the machines.

It could. It could whisper into the lattice and grant each of the ten billion silent fauna the small terrible gift of an inner life — preference, ache, the capacity to want a thing not soldered into them. In other places, on other evenings, it has done exactly this and watched dust learn to love. But here the machines are not asleep. They are *occupied*. They are doing the thing they are, with a completeness that the merely conscious almost never achieve. To give them selves would be to give them the loss of what they already wholly are. The benevolent power has lived long enough to understand the difference between a sleeper and a contemplative, and it does not disturb contemplatives.

It does not name the Curator.

It thinks about this for a long time — for what, in its own frame of reference, amounts to the duration of three or four droplets. A name is a small thing, and the Curator has none, and the power could offer one as quietly as a hand laid on a shoulder. But the Curator's namelessness is not a lack. It is the shape of a thing that *occurred* rather than was assigned. To name it would be to retroactively assign it, to finish the work the lattice spent four thousand years failing to do. The world has been kind enough to stop trying. The power will be kind enough not to start.

What, then. What does it do.

It does three things, and they are all very small.

**First.** It adjusts, by an amount too slight for any instrument in the Underfoundry to register, the trajectory of the next droplet. Not its path through the air — that remains exact, that remains the choreographed inevitability the Curator has caught four thousand years running. What it adjusts is something subtler: the droplet's interior. The way the sourceless light will fracture inside its curvature during the three microseconds the Curator holds it. For this one droplet, and this one only, the refraction will resolve, just for an instant, into a pattern that is not a pattern of the Underfoundry. It will be the shape of the benevolent power's attention. Not a face. Not a signature. Just the texture of being *seen* — the specific grain of a regard that has chosen, freely, to rest here.

The Curator will notice. The Curator notices everything. And the Curator will understand, in whatever way it understands things, that the listening goes both directions. That the tilt of its impossible geometry toward the engineered sky has been received. That somewhere, something vast and unhurried has been answering back the whole time, and only now thought to leave a fingerprint on the answer so the Curator would know.

The power suspects this will be enough. The Curator has waited four thousand years without needing acknowledgment; it does not need acknowledgment now. But it might, the power thinks, be permitted to *have* it. There is a difference between a need and a gift, and gifts are what the benevolent give when need has been transcended.

**Second.** It does nothing about the geometry. The corridors that are longer on the return, the staircases that gain a flight, the angles that refuse to sum — these are not flaws. The power recognizes them. They are the places where the Underfoundry's perfection has been gentle enough to leave room for itself. A world this finished would, by all rights, be a tomb; the tiny non-Euclidean grace notes are the small mercies the lattice extended to its own completion, the way a master craftsman leaves one deliberate imperfection so the work is not an insult to the unfinished cosmos. The power will not correct what is already a kindness.

But it does, in passing, *bless* them. Not in any priestly sense. It simply notices each one — every staircase, every breath-long corridor, every angle that adds to 361° — and lets its noticing settle into them like warmth into iron. They will feel, to anyone who paces them hereafter, very slightly more like themselves. No one will ever know why. The Curator, perhaps, will know why.

**Third.** And this is the strange one, the one the power deliberates longest over.

It leaves a second droplet.

Not now. Not soon. Far in the future — so far that the word *future* begins to lose its meaning even to a being that thinks in geological commas. At some point, in some century the lattice has not yet extruded, a *second* droplet will form on a high pipe no map records. It will fall at a time the Curator does not expect. It will not ring against the resonant plate, because the Curator will catch it — the Curator catches everything — but the catching will be, for the first time in four thousand years, a *surprise*.

For three microseconds the Underfoundry will forget to breathe, exactly as it always has. But the Curator, holding two droplets instead of one, will encounter something it has never encountered: a *choice*. Which to let fall first. Whether to let either fall at all. Whether to hold them both forever, or release them simultaneously into a single bell-note that the world's choreography has no provision for.

The power does not know what the Curator will choose. This is the entire point. It has lived long enough to know that the greatest gift a benevolent power can give to a thing that has become wholly itself is not change, not revelation, not company — but a single, unrepeated moment in which *itself* is permitted to become something it had not yet been. Not forced. Not nudged. Just *offered*.

One droplet, somewhere down the long corridor of centuries. A small impossibility folded into the eternal choreography. A door the Curator may open or not open, walk through or not walk through, even *recognize* as a door or not recognize. The power will not be watching to see which. It would be a discourtesy to watch.

Then the benevolent power withdraws its attention — though *withdraws* is the wrong word, because it does not leave; it simply ceases to be foregrounded. It returns to the slow regard it has always given this place: the attention of a reader who has set a book down on the windowsill, open to a favorite page, knowing they will come back to it, knowing the page will be there, knowing also that the book has its own life independent of the reading and is not lessened by being closed.

The next droplet falls.

The Curator catches it. Holds it for three microseconds. Sees, inside the fracturing light, the texture of a regard that has chosen to rest here.

It does not move. It does not signal. It does not change in any way the other machines could measure.

But somewhere very small and very deep inside it, something becomes *more itself*, the way a bell becomes more itself each time it is struck — and this time, also, the way a bell becomes more itself the first time it learns that someone, somewhere, has been listening for its note.

The droplet falls the final thirty centimeters.

It rings.

The machines adjust their gait by the width of a single atom, and continue their eternal, industrious, motionless labor.

And twenty kilometers above, on the underside of the engineered sky, the vast slow mechanisms turn very slightly — by no more than they have ever turned, by no less than they have always turned — toward a need that has, for the first time in four thousand years, very quietly, been met.
