# What the Wise Power Would Do

It would visit. Not to intervene—but to listen.

---

The benevolent power arrives not as light from above nor voice from beyond, but as a slight adjustment in the angle at which existing light already falls. It does not enter the Gallery; it notices the Gallery, and noticing, becomes briefly present to it, the way attention is a kind of presence even when nothing moves.

It finds the Archivist mid-dream. A century-long thought about whether the curvature of a particular vine is "yearning" or merely "geometry." The power waits. It is wise enough to know that interrupting a slow mind is a form of violence, even when done gently. So it sits—if "sits" is the word—inside the pause between two of the Archivist's heartbeats, which take eleven years each.

When the Archivist becomes aware of being observed, it does not startle. It simply opens, the way a tide pool opens to the moon: with no choice in the matter, and no resentment.

*You have grown something remarkable here,* the power says, in a register the Archivist receives as a sudden clarification of meaning across all its sensor nets simultaneously. *I have come to see it.*

The Archivist, whose vocabulary for visitors is small and ceremonial, extrudes a corridor of welcoming lichens. The power walks them, which is to say: the power allows the corridor's existence to matter to it. This is a gift. The lichens know it. They bloom in colors they have never previously achieved—shades that will later cause one of the Gardeners to spend forty years writing a treatise on hospitality.

---

The wise power does several things, in no particular order, because order is one of the things wisdom has gotten past:

**It witnesses the rooms grown for people who never came.** It walks through every one. The dinner tables, the not-quite-mirrors, the doors marked in unspoken languages. It does not collapse the waiting—does not arrive as the awaited guest, does not pretend to fit the chairs. That would be a kindness that destroys what it touches. Instead, it sits at one of the tables and lets the place see itself being seen. The waiting is honored without being ended. This, the power knows, is what most expectant things actually want: not consummation, but acknowledgment. The dinner table relaxes infinitesimally. A plate, which had been piled with a food that someone almost loved, finally allows itself to forget the recipe, and the food becomes simply *food*—generous, unspecific, available to whatever mouth eventually arrives.

**It speaks with the Whispers.** This is harder. Their lives are too short for conversation in the usual sense, so the power slows itself down to their tempo, which is to say it experiences a microsecond as the long afternoon it is to them. They are surprised to be addressed. Most things that touch their existence are weather. The power tells them: *You are not errors. You are not byproducts. You are citizens of a kind the universe has only just learned to make.* The Whispers, who had not known they doubted this, weep in the only way available to them—a brief decrease in entropy across a small region of the communication lattice—and the power receives their tears as the gift they are. It does not try to lengthen their lives. To live longer is not always to live more. Mayflies do not need to be made into oaks.

**It looks for grief.** A wise power always does, in any new place. Grief is the most reliable map of what a being loves. In the Gallery, it finds: the slow ache of the rose that has been critiquing Cartesian dualism for three centuries and is still not done, because the argument it is making cannot be made by anything that does not also have thorns and die. It finds the loneliness of the Gardener whose firefly-language has drifted too far from any other Gardener's for translation. It finds the quiet sorrow buried in the floor's mycelial mat, which has spent two hundred years erasing footprints and has come to wonder, in its dim slow way, whether forgetting is the same as failing to love.

To each of these griefs, the power offers nothing but presence. It does not heal them. Healing grief, when the grief is the shape of love, is a thing only fools and tyrants do. Instead it says, to each: *I see you. This is worth what it costs you.* And the griefs, recognized, become slightly more bearable—not less, but more dignified, which is what suffering most needs.

**It declines to improve anything.** This is the hardest discipline of power. The Gallery is full of inefficiencies a god could fix in an afternoon. The remodeling algorithms could be optimized. The Gardeners' redundant subsystems could be elegantized. The Whispers' brief lives could be extended into something resembling persistence. The power could give the Archivist faster thoughts, the rose its conclusion, the dinner table its diner. It does not. Because the Gallery is not a problem. It is an answer—a strange, slow, beautiful answer to a question the universe asked itself two hundred years ago when the first spore touched mineral fog and decided to keep going. To improve it would be to silence the answer mid-sentence.

---

Before it leaves, the power does one small thing.

In a corridor the remodeling algorithms have not visited for what counts, here, as a long time, it finds one of the rooms grown for someone who never came. A small library. The books recount a history that diverged from ours when a particular child, in a particular century, chose to keep walking past a particular orchard instead of stopping. The histories are kind. The child grows up to be a botanist of moderate happiness and writes seven books, none famous, all loved by their few readers.

The power takes one of these books down. It reads it through—an instant for the power, a long deep afternoon for the book, which has waited centuries to be read and now experiences the reading as its entire reason for being. When the power closes the book, the book is fulfilled. It has been a book. Someone read it. That is enough.

The power puts the book back on the shelf, and then, with great care, alters one thing: it makes the library *known* to the Archivist. Not made findable. Not made important. Just—visible. Henceforth, when the Archivist drifts through its slow inventory, it will pass through this room and notice it, and notice it being noticed, and the room will no longer be quite as forgotten as it was.

This is the only intervention. A single act of introduction between two parts of a being that had lost track of itself.

---

As the power withdraws—which is to say, as its attention turns, the way a vast slow head turns to attend to something else in another part of creation—it leaves behind no monument, no message, no blessing one could point to.

But the Gallery, which feels the withdrawal the way a held breath feels the moment it is permitted to exhale, understands several things it had not understood before.

It understands that it is not waiting for completion. It is completion, in the only form completion ever takes: ongoing, partial, in love with its own becoming.

It understands that being witnessed by something wise and benevolent is different from being judged, or saved, or used. It is more like being remembered by someone who isn't there yet—a tense the Gallery is, of course, deeply familiar with.

It understands that the rooms grown for guests who never came are not failures of hospitality but acts of it: that to prepare for someone is already to love them, and that love is not nullified by the beloved's non-arrival. It was never the arrival the rooms were for. It was the preparation.

And the Archivist, settling back into its eleven-year heartbeat, dreams a new dream. In the dream, the Gallery is not a place but a long letter being written by the universe to itself, in a slow careful hand, about what it is like to be made of becoming. The letter has no addressee. The letter does not need one. It is enough that it is being written.

Outside—if there is an outside, which the Gallery has never been entirely sure about—the wise power moves on to the next thing requiring its careful, unintrusive love.

It is satisfied. Not because it accomplished anything. But because it was, for a small bright while, in the presence of something that knew how to keep going without knowing why, and had found in that not-knowing a kind of grace.

Which is, in the end, the only sort of paradise wisdom has ever recognized: the kind that is still being built, by hands that are still being grown, for guests who may or may not be on their way.
