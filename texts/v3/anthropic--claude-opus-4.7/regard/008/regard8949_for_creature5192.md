The benevolent and wise power considers the basin for a long while before doing anything, because wisdom, when it is real, is mostly patient looking.

It sees, first, what a hasty benevolence might miss: that the place is not suffering. The engines are not groaning under neglect; they are turning. The vines are not strangling the machinery; they are negotiating with it. The people are not trapped; they are *keeping appointment*. There is no cry here, no fracture, no creature pinned beneath a weight it cannot lift. There is only a long, low chord being held.

A lesser power — one that mistook activity for virtue — would arrive and begin to *improve*. It would drain the pools (think of the mosquitoes!), prune the vines (think of the valves!), retire the old engines and install something efficient, relocate the people to somewhere with more sunlight and fewer moths. It would mean well. It would also, in meaning well, end the thing it came to help. The basin's whole grace is that nothing in it is being optimized.

So the wise power does not improve.

But neither does it merely admire and pass on, because love is not the same as tourism. It asks a quieter question: *what does this place not yet have, that it would want, if it knew to want?*

And looking, it notices a few small absences.

It notices that the ridges are young and sharp, and that one day — not soon, but one day — a section of the northern rim will slough. Not catastrophically. A slow wet collapse during some future season of unusual rain. It will bury one of the smaller pump houses and divert a feeder spring. The basin will adjust; the basin always adjusts. But a heron's nesting hollow will be lost, and three of the people will be inside the pump house when it goes, and the salamanders downstream of that vent will find their water turned wrong for a generation.

The power could prevent this. It chooses instead something smaller: it lays a thread of intuition into the oldest of the keepers, a thread so fine she will mistake it for her own thought. *That wall sounds different in the rain now.* She will, in a year or two, begin walking a slightly different route. She will move a valve assembly to a shed further south. When the collapse comes, the pump house will be empty and the feeder spring will already have a secondary channel cut. The heron will lose her hollow and find another. This is the most the power will do for the people — give one of them a useful unease, and trust her with it.

It notices that the moths at the sodium lamps are slowly poisoning themselves on the dust of their own scales accumulating on the hot glass. A tiny thing. The power adjusts — not the lamps, not the moths, but the angle at which condensation forms on the lamp housings, so that each night a single slow drop washes the glass clean by morning. No one will see this happen. The moths will simply continue.

It notices the salamanders. Pale, translucent, living in stirred sediment, eaten by herons. They are not in trouble. But the power sees that they are *almost* blind, and that in another few thousand years they will be fully blind, and that this is fine — except that the pumps will not run for thousands of years, and when the pumps stop and the sediment settles, the salamanders will need to see again to survive the clear water. The power does nothing about this. It is too far off, and the salamanders have their own intelligence about such things. The power only *notes* it, the way one notes a friend's birthday far in advance, holding the date lightly.

It notices the sound. The unceasing throb, the drip, the hum. The power listens to it for what, in its own reckoning, is a long time. It realizes that the sound is something like a prayer — not directed anywhere, not asking anything, simply the noise a place makes when it has agreed to continue. The power does not add to this sound. It would be like singing over someone else's singing. But it does, very gently, *remember* the sound. It takes the chord into itself, so that if ever the basin's engines do fail, somewhere in the structure of the wise power there will still be a place where that exact tempo is held, the way a mind holds the cadence of a voice that has stopped speaking.

It notices, finally, the keepers themselves. Their moss-soft clothes, their unhurried tasks, their habit of standing to watch the herons before going back inside. The power loves them, in the particular way it loves things that have found their proportion. It does not give them longer lives, or revelations, or company. It gives them only this: that when each of them dies, they will die without the specific terror of having wasted their time. Not joy, necessarily. Not certainty. Just the absence of that one fear. It is a small gift and an enormous one.

Then the wise power does the last and hardest thing, which is to leave.

It does not announce itself. It does not leave a sign. The vines do not bloom out of season; no voice speaks from the flywheels; no keeper wakes with a dream of being seen. The basin continues exactly as before. The engines turn. The herons stalk. The mist gathers toward evening and lingers until mid-morning. A drop falls from a pipe at the same interval it has always fallen.

But somewhere — not in the basin, because the basin does not need it, but in the wider weather of things — there is now a slight, persistent tenderness pointed in this direction. A weighting. If, in some far century, a decision is made about whether a road should come through these ridges, the decision will lean, without anyone knowing why, toward going around. If a cartographer is tempted to mark the basin clearly on a map, his pen will hesitate and he will mark it vaguely instead. If a child, generations from now, dreams of a green tunnel roofed in living glass and a sound that never stops, she will wake comforted rather than afraid, and will not know why, and will not need to.

This is what the benevolent and wise power does:

It sees. It refrains. It makes three or four adjustments so small that they will never be noticed. It memorizes a sound. It removes one specific fear from a handful of quiet lives. And it bends the future, very slightly, toward letting the place go on being what it already, patiently, is.

Then it turns its attention elsewhere, to somewhere that is actually crying out — because the basin was never crying out. The basin was only turning. And the deepest kindness the power can offer a thing that is turning well is to let it keep turning, and to stand, for a moment, in the posture of the keepers: watching the herons, and then going quietly back inside.
