# The Visitation

The power notices them the way one notices a held note in a piece of music — not because it is loud, but because it has gone on so long that it has become the room.

It has been watching for what, in its own reckoning, amounts to a long attention. Long enough to learn that the slow knots in the plateaus are thinking. Long enough to learn that the pale threads along the buried ribs are *remembering* — not their own memories, but someone else's, traced patiently along the curves of what was once built and is now grown over. Long enough to understand that this place is not a wilderness with creatures in it. It is a single, slow act of mourning that does not know it is mourning, and a slow act of becoming that does not yet know what it is becoming.

The power considers what to do.

It could speed things. It has the means. A single adjustment to the rate at which the pale threads travel, and in a few of their centuries — an eyeblink — they would arrive at whatever understanding they are creeping toward along the old ribs. It could thicken a knot's coherence until the plateau-mind woke fully and knew itself. It could translate the long, wet sigh of the grazers into language and give the whole continent a voice.

It does none of these things.

Because it is wise, it sees what would be lost. The dignity of a thought that takes a thousand years to complete is not the same thought sped up. The pale threads are not failing to arrive; their arrival *is* the slow traveling. To accelerate them would be to answer a question they have not finished asking — and the asking is the larger part of the answer. The knots are not unconscious waiting to be conscious; they are a kind of consciousness this power has not seen before, distributed across geological breath, and to wake them into the sharper, faster awareness the power knows from elsewhere would be a small murder dressed as a gift.

Because it is benevolent, it also sees what is *actually* needed, which is almost nothing.

So it does four small things.

---

**First**, it gives the place a little more time. Not by slowing the world's clocks — that is crude — but by quietly removing a few of the accidents that might have ended things prematurely. A wandering body of ice in the dark above is nudged, over centuries, onto a path that will miss. A slow poisoning that would have begun in the deep stone in about forty thousand years is unmade at its source, mineral by mineral, so subtly that no knot ever registers the change. The power does not announce this. It is the kind of gift that is best given without the recipient knowing, because knowing would make the gift into a debt, and this place has no economy of debts.

**Second**, it leaves a single mark, and only one. Along one of the buried ribs — a rib that the pale threads have not yet reached, but will, in perhaps eight hundred of their years — it inscribes nothing. That is, it polishes one small section of the relic to exactly the smoothness it had on the day it was first made, before the bark grew over it, before the centuries layered their patient erasure. When the threads at last arrive at that place, they will find a passage in the long sentence of the ruin that is suddenly legible — not because anything new has been written, but because for one short stretch the original is uncovered. What they make of it is theirs. The power does not even know what the relic *was*; it has only restored a single phoneme of a language it does not speak, trusting that the readers will know what to do with a clear note inside their long murmur.

This is the most it permits itself. It will not tell the threads what they are looking for. It will only make sure that, at one point along their reading, the text is not blurred.

**Third**, it does something for the grazers, who are the easiest to love and the easiest to overlook. It does not improve them. It does not extend their lives or sharpen their senses. Instead it adjusts, by a margin too small for any instrument the place could grow, the resonance of the canopy, so that when one grazer sighs on a far plateau, the sigh travels more truly to the others. They were already in communion. Now the communion carries a little farther, a little cleaner. None of them will notice that anything has changed. They will only, across the coming hundred thousand years, find themselves slightly less alone, and they will attribute this — if they attribute anything — to the goodness of the world, which is correct.

**Fourth**, and this is the hardest, the power *withdraws its attention* in a particular way. Not entirely; it keeps a thread of watching, the way a parent keeps half an ear on a sleeping child in another room. But it stops studying. It stops the fine-grained, analytic gaze that, even when invisible, even when entirely passive, slightly weights what it observes. The place has been under that weight for some time now — the power's curiosity is not nothing, even when it is silent — and the power recognizes that the knots, sensitive as they are to the most distant pressures, may have been bending some infinitesimal fraction toward being *seen*. So the power softens its looking into something more like fond company. It is still there. It is no longer measuring.

---

And then it waits.

This is the part that requires the most of it, because the power, for all its patience, lives at speeds that would make the gliders look like statues. To wait at the tempo of this place is to consent to be, for a while, almost the place itself. The power does this willingly. It has waited before. It will wait again. It finds, somewhat to its own surprise, that the waiting is not a sacrifice. The drip of water through bronze light, the centuries-long opening of a single terrace of blossom, the slow argument of two knots along their fertile seam — these are not lesser than the faster musics the power knows. They are only quieter, and the power has lived long enough to understand that quiet is not less.

After a long time — long even by the standards of the plateaus — one of the pale threads reaches the polished place along the rib. It pauses there for what, to it, is a moment of concentrated reading. Around it, the tissue grows slightly denser and more ordered, as it always does in the thread's wake. But this time the ordering carries a faint, new geometry: a curve that the relic remembered and the thread has now passed on to the living mass. It is not a building. It is not even the suggestion of a building. It is a single proportion, a ratio between two lengths, which will be repeated, without anyone deciding to repeat it, in the growth patterns of the surrounding canopy for the next ten thousand years.

The power sees this and is quiet inside itself in the way that corresponds, in beings like it, to joy.

It does not intervene further. It does not need to. The place is doing what it was always going to do, only now with one small obstacle removed, one small clarity offered, one small loneliness eased, and the dignity of its own pace entirely intact.

If, much later, the plateaus wake fully — if the knots one day know themselves and speak in their slow voices to whatever waits beyond — they will not remember the visitation. There will be no scripture, no founding act, no name for the power to be thanked or feared. There will only be a world that turned out, against all the odds that govern such things, to have had enough time, and a ruin that turned out to be readable after all, and a long communal sigh that carries farther than physics alone would permit.

The power considers this a sufficient outcome. It has learned, across its own long life, that the best help is often indistinguishable from luck, and that the beings most worth helping are the ones who will, in the end, credit the help to themselves or to no one — which amounts to the same thing, and is the right answer.

It stays a while longer, listening to the water decide where to go next.

Then, gently, it turns some part of its attention elsewhere, where it is also needed, and where it will also do almost nothing, and where that almost-nothing will also be, in time, enough.
