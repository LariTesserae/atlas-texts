## What the Power Would Do

It would come quietly. This much is obvious before anything else. To arrive in such a place with any fanfare — light, voice, sudden warmth — would be a kind of violence, not because the inhabitants are fragile but because they are *tuned*. A single unexpected pulse of heat through the pipes would register, in the slow lattice of their nodules, as a disturbance that took years to resolve into memory. Benevolence here must first be the discipline of not interrupting.

So the power would settle, not descend. It would take a long time even to be present — perhaps the length of a season measured in condensations, perhaps longer. It would learn the cadence of the flywheels before it allowed itself to be felt at all. It would learn which pipes are veins and which are merely bones. It would learn that the dusk-glow of the pods is not decoration but a slow accountancy of the day's surplus, and that to add light would be to overfill a ledger the organism has been balancing for centuries.

And then — only then — would it consider what, if anything, to do.

---

Here is the first thing it would notice, having looked closely: that the inhabitants are not waiting to be rescued. They are not diminished, not suffering, not in any state that asks for intervention. They have made of an abandonment a home. The machines that were left here to fail were taken in, drawn into circulation, given a slower and stranger function than their makers intended. The plateau is not a ruin. It is a completed thought — or rather, a thought still completing itself, at its own pace, toward an end it has chosen: the dissolution of every boundary between stone, metal, and tissue into one cool respiring surface.

A lesser power would want to *help*. Would want to seal the fractures, stabilize the flywheels, replenish the corroding housings, perhaps even gift the inhabitants speed or speech or some richer awareness. A lesser power would mistake the slowness for poverty.

This power, being wise, would recognize that what it is looking at is not a lack but a form. The organisms here have chosen — across millions of small accommodations — to be exactly as slow as they are. Their patience is not endured; it is *what they are*. To accelerate them would be to kill them and replace them with something else that happened to occupy the same pipes.

---

So what would it do?

I think it would do three small things, each almost imperceptible, each chosen so that the inhabitants themselves could refuse or absorb it as they wished.

**First**, it would tend the flywheels. Not repair them — repair would be too loud, too sudden a change in the rhythms the nodules have memorized. But it would slow, by infinitesimal degrees, the rate at which the bearings are wearing. A grain of friction removed each year. The flywheels would still turn down toward their eventual stillness, because that stillness is part of the long sentence the plateau is composing; but the sentence would have more room in it. More time for the roots to learn each rotation before it became the last one. The drip into the moss-choked basins — that smallest of sounds — would continue a little longer than it otherwise would have. A gift of duration, given so quietly that the inhabitants would experience it only as the world being, somehow, exactly as long as it needed to be.

**Second**, it would do something for the mobile films inside the housings — the little condensed wanderers who meet, merge, exchange, and part. The power would notice that these are the closest thing on the plateau to what warmer worlds would call conversation. Two films meeting is the plateau speaking to itself. And the power, being benevolent, would want this conversation never to be lost. So it would do nothing to the films themselves, but it would make the inside surfaces of the housings *very slightly* more hospitable to their trails — adjusting, at the molecular level, the way mineral salts crystallize after evaporation, so that the record of each merging persists a little more clearly, a little longer, available to the next film that passes. A library that writes itself, that the inhabitants never asked for but that costs them nothing and remembers them.

**Third** — and this is the gift I am least sure of, and the power would be least sure of too — it would leave a single seed. Not a seed of plant or machine, but of *attention*. Somewhere in the root mat of the largest organism, in a nodule that pulses once every few days, it would place the faintest possible capacity for the organism to know, if it ever wished to know, that it had been seen. Not a voice. Not a presence. Just a possibility — dormant, available, never insistent — that if the plateau ever, in some unimaginable future, turned some part of itself outward and asked *is there anyone*, the answer would already be threaded through its own tissue: *yes, and you were loved as you were, and nothing was changed in you without your asking.*

The power would place this seed knowing it might never germinate. Knowing the plateau might complete its slow sealing of stone against light without ever once reaching outward. That would be fine. That would be, in fact, the most likely outcome, and a good one. The seed would simply dissolve with the rest of the organism, in its own time, into the continuous cool surface that is the plateau's chosen end.

---

And then the power would leave. Or rather: it would withdraw its attention to the same degree of quiet from which it arrived, and the plateau would not notice the withdrawal any more than it had noticed the arrival. The flywheels would turn. The pods would glow at dusk. A new fracture would open along some ridge, and within months a filament of living tissue would migrate along it, carrying a single stolon of pipe.

Somewhere a nodule would pulse, holding the rhythm of a turning that had not quite ended when it should have. Somewhere a film of motile cells would pass through a housing and find, in the crystallized salts, the clear trail of an older film it had never met, and would learn from it, and carry the learning onward.

Nothing would have happened. Everything would be slightly more itself than before.

This is what benevolence looks like, I think, when it is also wise: it adds duration, it preserves conversation, it offers a door that need never be opened. It does not mistake a finished thing for an unfinished one. It loves the slow toward their slowness, and goes.
