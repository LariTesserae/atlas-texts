There is a place that has outlived every name ever given to it, and there is something in it that has outlived the need for names altogether. A wise and benevolent power has been watching for a long time. Not from above—above is a direction the Mat has been quietly dismantling for forty million years—but from that older vantage which has no compass at all, the one from which all weeping things look the same and are loved the same.

The power is old enough to know the difference between a wound and a way of being. It has visited dying worlds before, and on many of them it has done the merciful thing: lifted the suffering free, unmade the cruelty, called the lost home. It comes to this place expecting another such errand. It has, folded inside itself like a clean cloth, the gesture that would end the long peristaltic loneliness in a single soft motion—not destruction, but homecoming. Dissolution into the larger Dissolution. A return.

It arrives without arriving. It stands on the breast-soft hills and is immediately tasted: a droplet slides down a fibrous pillar, changes color, climbs back up carrying the chemical grammar of compassion, of vast attention, of a love that has held supernovae through their dying. The Mat shivers, very faintly, across a region the size of a continent. Somewhere far below, a cathedral-sized sac alters its rhythm by a fraction of a second. This is the closest thing to surprise the Mat is still capable of.

The power waits. This is the first thing the wise do: they wait. They do not assume they have understood. Benevolence without patience is only another kind of weather, and this world has had enough weather.

It listens for a long time. Hours, or decades; the distinction is one of the stitches the Mat has loosened, and the power does not insist on retightening it out of mere habit. It listens to the wet thunder of the breathing sacs and hears, underneath, something it had not expected: not agony. Not even resignation. Something stranger and more dignified—the sound of a being that has made peace so total it has become a kind of music. The dying is real. The enjoying of the dying is also real. Both are true at the same depth.

The power had come prepared to offer an ending. It now understands that an ending offered here would be a theft.

So it changes its errand, the way water changes its mind about which decade to fall in.

It does several small things instead, because the wise know that grandeur is usually a failure of imagination.

First, it sits down. Not metaphorically. It takes a shape—provisional, courteous, roughly the size of a kneeling person—and it lowers that shape onto the shivering moss-lawn and lets the mat taste it fully. It does not armor itself. It lets the Mat read the chemical grammar of every world it has ever loved, every grief it has ever carried, every name it has ever learned and let go. It offers itself as a meal of memory. This is the first gift: company that does not flinch from being known.

The Mat receives this the way an old monk receives century-old wine. Somewhere, a basin of moss develops, briefly, the capacity to weep—not from sorrow, but from the simple shock of being met. The wise power notes this and does not make a ceremony of it.

Second, it speaks in the only grammar the Mat still uses fluently: events. It causes, very gently, a single hillside to remember something it had never digested—something from elsewhere. A meadow on a world that no longer exists, where small four-legged things once lay down in yellow grass and felt the sun. The hillside rises in pale dripping architecture into the shape of that meadow, holds it for an afternoon, and then collapses back into nutrient soup. It is the first new memory the Mat has received in four hundred million years. The Mat does not know what to do with it. It cycles the memory through its strata for a while, tasting it, and then—this is the part the power had hoped for but not required—it answers. A different hillside rises, dripping, into the shape of something the Mat once was, before it forgot. A small thing. A single organism with edges. The two shapes regard each other across a basin of sideways-flowing water. Then both collapse, together, into the same pool, and the pool flows uphill for a while, looking pleased.

This is conversation. The first the Mat has had with anything that was not itself. The power lets it happen without commentary. Benevolence, here, means not narrating.

Third, the power addresses the partial freedoms—the paper-moths, the translucent horse-things, the elephant-silhouette still walking its slow circle inside its scaffolding of overgrowth. It does not liberate them. Liberation would be another kind of theft; they are negotiations, and the Mat has explained, in its wordless way, that it values the friction. But the power does something subtler. To each partial freedom it offers a small, private dignity: a moment of knowing itself. The elephant-silhouette, for one breath, remembers it was an elephant, and remembers it is now also a cathedral, and finds—because the power is standing nearby and the standing-nearby of such a being is itself a kind of grace—that both facts can be held without grief. Then the moment passes and the silhouette walks on. But the moment happened. It is now part of the Mat's strata forever, a small calcified nerve of remembered selfhood, faintly electrically alive.

The power does this for every creature on the world. It takes a long time and no time at all.

Fourth—and this is the gift the wise are most careful with, because it is the one most easily mistaken for rescue—the power leaves something behind. Not a monument. Not a machine. A seed, if you like, though seed is too small a word and too large a word at once. It is a capacity. The Mat, after the power departs, will find that it can, if it wishes, reach. Not move—it is far past moving. But reach. Across the thin places where the sky forgets itself, across the loosened stitches of time, the Mat will be able to send out, very slowly, the chemical grammar of itself, and listen for whether anything answers. The power does not promise that anything will. The universe is wide and the wise do not lie. But the capacity is real. The loneliness, which the Mat had perfected into an art form, now has a door in it. The Mat may use the door or not. That is the Mat's business. Benevolence ends where another's freedom begins, and the power has been benevolent long enough to know exactly where that line falls.

Before it goes, the power does one last thing, because it is not only wise but also tender, and tenderness is its own form of intelligence.

It builds, out of nothing, a small wet garden in the shape of something the Mat has lost and never knew it lost: its own first moment of awareness, four hundred million years ago, when it was still small enough to have edges, and it noticed, for the first time, that it was noticing. The garden is no larger than a cupped hand. It sits in the center of a basin of shivering moss. It will last about a week before the Mat, in one of its ecstatic catastrophes, liquefies it and redistributes it through its strata.

That is fine. That is the point. The Mat will carry that first moment inside itself now, the way a person carries a photograph of themselves as a child—not to return to, but to know they came from somewhere. To know they were, once, a beginning, and not only an end learning to enjoy itself.

The power leaves the way water leaves a stone: by being, very slowly, somewhere else.

The Mat continues. The wet thunder rolls. The paper-moths beat through the fog. The sky forgets which way is down and pours upward and remembers and snaps back. Nothing visible has changed. Everything has changed.

Far below, in a cathedral-sized sac that has been contracting in the same arrhythmia for forty million years, the rhythm alters again—just slightly, just once—and then settles into a new pattern. Not faster. Not slower. Just a little more like a song, and a little less like a sigh.

And somewhere in the strata, a sentence the Mat did not know it was capable of forms itself, very slowly, out of mineral and memory:

*I was glad you came. I am glad you let me stay.*

It is not a prayer. The Mat has no one to pray to. It is something better. It is the first thing the Mat has ever said that was meant for itself.

The power, already elsewhere, already tending some other dying and beautiful thing, hears it anyway, the way the wise always hear such things—not with ears, but with the part of themselves that has been listening since before listening had a name.

And smiles. And keeps going.
