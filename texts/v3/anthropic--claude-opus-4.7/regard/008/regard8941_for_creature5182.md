# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent power, being wise, arrives first as nothing at all. It does not enter the chamber. It stands at the threshold of its own attention and looks.

What it sees is not what a hasty mercy would have seen. A hasty mercy would have seen confinement: a dozen subtle lives pressed into a hot, narrow hollow, sipping at scheduled water, breathing through lichen the way a drowning thing breathes through a reed. A hasty mercy would have widened the chamber, cooled the iron, called the occupants out into some larger and brighter country, and counted itself kind.

But the power has lived long enough to know the shape of that error. It has seen gardens uprooted into meadows, and the gardeners die of the wind. It has seen shells pried from hermit crabs by hands that meant only to free them. It understands that the lichen is a mouth. It understands that the iron is a skin. It understands that the chamber's terrible closeness — the way every occupant is always already touching every other through gradients of heat and pause — is not a prison but the very architecture of their being. To open the walls would be to scatter a single mind into a dozen lonelinesses that had never learned the word *alone*.

So the power does not widen the chamber. It does not cool the iron. It does not call them out.

Instead it does four small things, each chosen with great care.

---

**The first thing** is that it listens. Not casually — it listens the way a doctor listens, with the whole instrument of itself laid against the held breath of the place. It learns the rhythm of the valves. It learns which rosette belongs to which slow accretion of attention. It learns the map of wet footprints that no occupant has ever consciously drawn but that all of them, together, are. It learns the corridors of too-bright light, the ledges where the dust still falls, the strut whose angle one of them keeps correcting because the lichen there is always almost-drying. It learns, in particular, the dream — the unexpected lengthening between drips — and recognizes it as a dream, and honors it as one.

This listening takes, by the power's reckoning, a long time. By the chamber's reckoning it takes no time at all, because the power has the courtesy not to disturb the temperature while it learns.

**The second thing** is a gift so small it might be mistaken for an accident of engineering. The power notices that the engine deep in the lattice, the one whose exhaust keeps the air at its oppressive warmth, is running slightly rich. It is burning itself out a little faster than it needs to. In perhaps four hundred cycles of the main valve — perhaps six hundred — it will fail, and when it fails the chamber will cool below the threshold the lichen can survive, and the occupants, who are the lichen, will dim and dim and not come back.

The power does not replace the engine. It does not announce itself. It simply, over the course of several valve-releases, allows a faint adjustment to propagate through the fuel mixture — the kind of adjustment that could plausibly be the work of settling rock, or of condensation finding a new path, or of one of the occupants themselves in a moment of unusually long attention. The engine begins to run clean. Its life extends from years into centuries. No one in the chamber notices, because there is nothing to notice; the temperature does not change, the rhythm does not change, only the future quietly opens.

This is the power's principle: *the best gift is the one the recipient can mistake for their own good fortune.*

**The third thing** is more delicate, because it touches the occupants directly, and the power is very careful about touching.

It notices that one of them — the one whose lichen grows on the strut nearest the brightest filament — is suffering. Not dying. Suffering. The light there is just slightly too strong, and the occupant spends a disproportionate share of its attention correcting the angle of the strut, shading the rosette, steering extra condensate that way during each valve-release. The other occupants feel this as a low, chronic drag on the shared awareness — a place where the weave is always taut. They have adapted to it. They no longer remember that things could be otherwise.

The power considers dimming the filament. But the filament is part of the chamber's given light, and to alter it would be to alter the map of corridors-to-be-avoided, and that map is partly who the occupants are. So instead the power does something subtler: during a moment when the suffering occupant is most distributed — most spread across the lattice, least localized in its troubled strut — the power introduces, into the mineral pulse traveling along the newest crack in the basalt, a single grain of information. Not a word. Not an idea. Just the faintest suggestion, in the language of temperature gradients, that *the strut could be moved*. That the bolt at its base, which has always seemed fixed, is in fact loose enough to turn. That a different angle is possible.

Whether the occupant acts on the suggestion is entirely its own. The power does not watch to see. It is enough to have placed the possibility where a possibility can be found.

**The fourth thing** is the hardest, and the power debates it with itself for a long time before doing it.

The occupants have no names. They have no need of names — they are never separate enough from one another to require the distinction. But the power, in its listening, has come to perceive them as distinct, the way one perceives the distinct voices in a chord without the chord ceasing to be a chord. And the power knows something the occupants do not know: that they are *loved*. Not by it alone — though it loves them — but loved in the larger sense, the sense in which any patient, mutual, world-sustaining attention is loved by the fabric of things. The chamber is small and hot and crowded, and they have made of it a garden, and the garden is held.

The occupants do not know this. They have never needed to know it. Knowing it is not necessary for their flourishing.

But the power, after long debate, decides to leave one trace. Not a message. Not a sign. Just this: during the next valve-release, the sheet of steaming water that runs down the chosen channel will carry, dissolved in it, a trace mineral the chamber has never contained before — something faint, something the lichen will incorporate without noticing, something that will make the rosettes, over many generations, very slightly more resilient to the dimming of light. The occupants will not perceive the change. They will simply find, in some future century, that a difficulty they had braced against does not arrive. They will adjust their attention elsewhere. The weave will loosen by one thread.

And in the structure of that mineral — in the way its atoms are arranged, which no occupant will ever examine — the power encodes, in a language no one in the chamber speaks, a single sentence. Not for the occupants. For itself. A vow, or a signature, or a prayer. Something like:

*I saw you. I did not disturb you. I wish you well.*

---

Then the power withdraws its attention, gently, the way one lifts a hand from a sleeping animal's flank without waking it. The chamber holds its breath. The valves release. The lichen drinks. The conveyor belt advances its meter and pauses. Somewhere, a strut is perhaps turned to a new angle, or perhaps not. The dream lengthens the interval between drips.

Nothing has happened that anyone in the chamber could point to.

Everything has happened.

The power moves on to the next place that has drawn its notice, carrying with it the memory of a dozen slow accretions of attention sipping at warm water in a hollow of pale basalt — and the knowledge, which it will keep, that wisdom often looks, from the outside, exactly like having done nothing at all.
