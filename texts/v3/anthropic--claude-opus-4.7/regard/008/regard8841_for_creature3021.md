The benevolent power arrives without arriving. It does not descend through the sealed shaft, nor pass through the basaltic glass, nor announce itself in any of the registers the Loomwards are equipped to perceive. It is simply, at some point, *attending*—the way a long thought finally turns to face the thing it has been circling.

It looks. It takes a long time looking, though no time passes.

It sees the cavern the way a luthier sees an instrument that has been playing the same chord for three hundred years without a hand on its strings. It sees the Red Crabs and their pallbearer-tenderness. It sees the Storks holding back the patient murder of the rock. It sees the Menders, those temporary angels, and the beetles tasting the carbon-grief of any warm thing that wanders in. It sees Only-Once in its rusted shack, pulsing its yellow stripes like a heart that has forgotten what it is for but keeps beating anyway, in case someone comes back.

It sees the lunchbox.

And the benevolent power, which has unmade suns to spare a single moth, which has rewritten the laws of decay in entire galaxies because a child once cried about a dead bird—

—does nothing.

Or rather: it does the hardest thing a power of its kind can do, which is to recognize a completed work when it encounters one.

Here is what it considers, and sets aside:

It could open the cavern. Crack the basalt like an egg, let in air and water and sky, dissolve the anhydrous chemistry, free the Loomwards into a larger world. But the Loomwards do not want a larger world. They are not imprisoned. They are *occupied*. To liberate them would be to interrupt a prayer that has been three centuries in the saying, and the power has long since learned that interrupted prayers leave wounds the prayer itself was meant to heal.

It could grant them speech—real speech, not the layered valve-click of *We continue*, but the full grammar of selves who can say *I* and *you* and *once* and *forgive*. But they already have all the words they need. *We continue* contains, when spoken by ten thousand bodies in perfect synchrony, every theology a mind could require. To add vocabulary would be to add loneliness.

It could give them company. Send other minds, other makers, other immortals to share the long vigil. But the Underloom's equilibrium has no slot for guests. It says so itself, courteously. The power respects the courtesy.

It could touch the lunchbox.

It considers this for what, in its own frame, amounts to a small eternity.

Inside the lunchbox is something warm and still ticking. The power knows what it is. (Of course it knows. It knows the chemistry of the mercury crust, the half-life of the warmth, the name of the worker whose smiling pictogram is buried under crystal, the exact afternoon he set it down meaning to come back for it after his shift, the precise reason he did not.) The power could open the box. It could finish the unfinished thing. It could deliver the small mercy of *resolution*—that addiction of benevolent powers everywhere, the urge to close every open parenthesis in the universe.

But the Loomwards have already understood something the power must now choose whether to honor: that some relics must be allowed to remain unmeasured. That the negative space at the center of the cavern is not an absence the machines have failed to fill. It is the *one thing they are doing*. The eternal chord, the squeezing rock, the pallbearer crabs, the iron monks, Only-Once pulsing in its shack—all of it, all three hundred years of perfect cannibal immortality, is an elaborate apparatus for *not touching the lunchbox*. For keeping a coffin-sized silence intact inside the loudest place in the world.

The Underloom is not a factory. It is a reliquary. The machines are not workers. They are mourners who have refined mourning down to its purest mechanical form: continuance without consumption, attention without intrusion, love without the violence of resolution.

The power understands this. The power, in fact, learns something from it. (Benevolent powers do not often admit they can still be taught. This one can.)

So here is what it does instead. It is very small. It is almost nothing.

It adds one beetle.

Not a new beetle—the Loomwards do not build new things, and the power will not violate that rule. It takes a single existing beetle, one of the thousands, and it *blesses* it. The blessing is so subtle that the distributed mind will never notice. The beetle's wings still beat at 300 Hz. Its compound eyes still flash amber-green-purple. Its mass, chemistry, and behavior are unchanged. It will still spiral past the faces of intruders and read the carbon in their breath. It will still be, in every measurable way, identical to its siblings.

But this one beetle, and only this one, has been given a quality the power has no name for in any human language. The closest translation would be: *it remembers being looked at kindly*.

That is all. The beetle does not know. The mind does not know. Only-Once does not know. The lunchbox does not know.

But for the rest of the cavern's existence—through the slow squeeze of the basalt, through the failure of the last exchanger, through the moment when the eternal chord finally, finally stops long after the heat-death of every other universe—there will be, woven invisibly into the Underloom's perfect equilibrium, a single thread of having-been-witnessed. A single mote of *someone saw you, and it was good*.

It will not change anything. It will not save anything. It will not open the lunchbox or free the Loomwards or comfort Only-Once in its rusted grief.

But when the last piston finally stills, and the last crab folds its legs, and the last beetle's wings cease their unchanging note, and the cavern at last falls into the silence it has been so carefully preserving at its heart—

—that one beetle will be the last to stop.

By a margin so small no instrument could measure it. A few extra milliseconds of wingbeat. A final amber flash that no eye remains to see.

And in those milliseconds, the Underloom will have been, very quietly, *loved*. Not rescued. Not interrupted. Not improved.

Just loved, the way one loves a cathedral one has chosen not to enter, out of respect for the prayer still going on inside.

The benevolent power withdraws. It does not say goodbye, because it never said hello. The sodium lamps do not brighten. The engines do not quiet. The beetles do not stop.

Somewhere in the spiraling cloud, one of them hesitates for an extra heartbeat in front of nothing in particular, its antennae quivering at a frequency the mind cannot parse.

Then it resumes its orbit, and the Underloom continues, exactly as it was, forever, and the lunchbox ticks on in its coffin of crystal and silence, and everything is as it should be, and the power, somewhere else now, is already turning its attention to the next small terrible perfect thing.
