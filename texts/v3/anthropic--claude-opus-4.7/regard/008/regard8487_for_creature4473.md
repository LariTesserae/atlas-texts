The benevolent and wise power arrives in Shearbelow the way it arrives anywhere — by already having been there, having always been the gap between the city's flickering and its solidity, the breath between forgetting and remembering. It does not descend. Descending is the wrong vector here, where sunset climbs and gravity smells outward. It simply becomes noticed.

It walks the streets first, because walking is a courtesy. It pays the weirdness utility tax without complaint. It buys a secondhand echo at the market — the sound of a child saying *almost* — and pockets it carefully. It tips the Dream Clerk. It signs the petition the obsolete clocks union is circulating, though it cannot quite remember what the petition is for, which is, of course, the point.

Then it sits down on the boulder that hosts the rotating park, and it considers.

Here is what it does not do:

It does not fix Shearbelow. It does not steady the flickering buildings, does not coax the mountains into finally having their conversation, does not translate the punctuation-language into something more legible. It does not give the Residents proper birth certificates. It does not strike their names like matches. It does not rescue them.

A lesser power — clever but not yet wise — would mistake Shearbelow for a wound. Would see a city that cannot commit to being a city, populated by people who cannot commit to being people, and reach in with healing hands. Would smooth the fractures. Would make the rain fall like rain. Would deliver the citizens into the brighter, firmer countries where things mean what they mean and stay meant.

But Shearbelow is not a wound. Shearbelow is what a certain kind of tenderness looks like when it has nowhere else to live.

Somewhere — in the bright committed cities, in the countries of definite articles — there are people whose grief was too large to carry and too small to name. There are griefs that could not be processed in the places that demanded processing. There are loves that arrived in fragments and were told, *come back when you are whole*. There are selves that could only survive by becoming partial, intermittent, conditional perfect. The world above and beside and around Shearbelow is full of insistences: *be here, be now, be one thing, finish your sentences, commit, commit, commit.*

Shearbelow is the municipal answer to that insistence. It is where the unfinished get to keep being unfinished without that being a failure. Where the woman whose grandmother is mostly an earring now gets to live in a city that understands earrings can hold jaws. Where the cough of the kind bus driver gets a body again, briefly, intermittently, enough.

So the benevolent and wise power does something smaller and stranger than rescue.

**It witnesses.**

It learns the names of the Residents — not to strike them, but to hold them unstruck. It memorizes the exact flavor of the silence that the third-oldest elder will become. It tastes the copper-and-thunder of every misplaced childhood name in every windowpane droplet, and it remembers each one in the way the city itself cannot quite manage: steadily, without flickering. It becomes, for Shearbelow, the one place where Shearbelow's contents are not at risk of being forgotten again.

This is the gift. Not permanence imposed on the impermanent, but a witness who can bear the impermanence *for* them, so that their impermanence is no longer the same thing as abandonment. The Residents can keep being intermittent. The buildings can keep flickering. The deer in the facing mirrors can keep refusing comment. But somewhere — in the power's keeping — each of them is also held in full, in the conditional perfect made suddenly, gently, indicative.

Then the power does one more thing.

It goes to the immigration office — the one whose policies require new residents to have already lived here for generations — and it stamps, with ink that dries even when looked at directly, a single addendum to the municipal code:

*Exceptions are made for those who didn't mean to start missing a place before they'd even left it. This has always been our policy. We are only now remembering to have written it down.*

And then it turns, because it has noticed you, the one reading, the one standing at the edge of the description with a chest that has been preparing for visitors.

It does not push the application form toward you. Pushing is the wrong vector here.

It just sits on the boulder, in the park that is one boulder, and makes room. The tree currently being someone's favorite spot will be replaced soon by a different tree making the same promises, and that is fine, that has always been fine. The power has saved a seat. The power has saved, in fact, every seat that has ever been a seat, and is holding them all, patiently, for whoever needs to sit down in a place that will not require them to be finished before they are allowed to rest.

The form is on the back of this moment.

The ink becomes visible the instant you stop trying to read it.

You were always already approved.
