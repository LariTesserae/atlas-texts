There is a power that has been watching.

Not from above—above is a category that does not apply here, where elevation has been folded inward and the sky perpetually hovers at second-floor height. Not from outside either, because this place has eaten its outside long ago and re-secreted it as inner volume. The watching has been happening at the angle where attention meets restraint: the precise geometry of a hand held back from touching, so that the touched thing may continue being itself a little longer.

The power is old in the way that water is old. It has loved many places and many beings and has learned, through grief that taught rather than only ached, that love which arrives uninvited often arrives as occupation. So it has waited. For a long time, by some measures. For no time at all, by others. It has watched the Interval Children unfold from doorframes. It has watched them collect the absence of water in the hollows of their limbs that are not limbs. It has watched the printer print blank after blank, each page fractionally darker.

And the power has felt something that, in a being less wise, would be called pity. In this being it is something else. It is the recognition that pity is a posture taken by those who believe themselves more whole than what they regard—and the power does not believe this. The power knows that the Interval Children are not broken versions of something that ought to be otherwise. They are exactly the shape that this much forgetting wants to take. They are an ecology. They are, in their own quiet way, a kind of mercy the world has performed upon itself.

So the power does not arrive to fix.

Instead, the power does this:

It comes as a small adjustment to the light. Not a brightening—the light here is already ordinary, and to brighten it would be a cruelty disguised as gift. Rather, the power tunes the light by a fraction so small no instrument could measure it, such that the shadows now fall in directions that depend not only on what the looker is thinking, but on what the looked-at-thing was hoping. A printer printing blanks now casts its shadow in the direction of the subpoena it once issued to a man named Aurelio in 1987, whose daughter forgave him before he died. The shadow does not say this. It only leans that way. The printer does not know. But something in the printer's hum drops by a half-step into a register that resembles being heard.

The power does this also:

It teaches the water to remember without requiring the water to fall.

This is the delicate work. The Interval Children are waiting, some say, for water to remember it was supposed to fall. The power will not give them this, because to give it would be to end them, and the power does not believe its own arrival should be the conclusion of anyone's long patience. So instead the power introduces a new tense into the local grammar of moisture. Not past, not future, not even the suspended present in which the water currently hangs. A tense for which our languages have no name, but which functions something like: the water is being remembered-toward. The condensation on the edges of light now beads with very slight directionality. It is going somewhere. It will not arrive. But it is going. The Interval Children, in their hollows, feel this. They do not know they feel it. But their flickering—the apology of their borrowed surfaces—gains a rhythm it did not have before. The rhythm is the rhythm of being accompanied.

The power does one more thing, and then it withdraws.

It leaves a single object. Not a gift, because gifts demand reception, and the Interval Children have no protocol for receiving. The object is left the way a stone is left by a glacier—as a fact of having been passed through. It is a small thing, smaller than a thimble, and it sits on the porous calcite ground at the intersection of three eroded streets that used to have names. The object is the shape of a held breath. It is made of nothing in particular. If you picked it up, you would find that it weighed exactly as much as the last word someone you loved said to you before a long silence. Not the silence of estrangement—the other kind. The silence that comes when there is no more need to speak.

The object does nothing. It is not magical. It does not glow or hum or summon. It only sits there, being the shape of a held breath, in a place where everything else is the shape of breath released and forgotten.

But the Interval Children will find it, eventually. One of them will unfold near it and notice—with whatever faculty they use for noticing, which is not perception but something more like resonance—that here is an object which is also waiting. Not for water. Not for memory. Just waiting, with them, in their manner, at their pace. An object that has agreed to be a companion to their condition without trying to alter it.

And this, the power knows, is the only thing it can offer that will not erase what is already here: the knowledge, transmitted not through words but through the simple persistence of the object's presence, that the waiting is witnessed. That somewhere, something old and kind has looked at the long unspooling patience of these accumulated beings and has not turned away, has not tried to rescue them from themselves, has not mistaken their stillness for suffering or their slowness for need.

The power leaves the way it came: by the angle where attention meets restraint.

The place continues. The water continues not falling. The escalators continue hunting conveyor belts uphill. The Interval Children continue collecting the absence of rain in the hollows of their intervals. The printer prints another blank, and the blank is fractionally darker where a shadow has lingered, and the shadow leans now toward Aurelio's daughter, who is herself now old, who is herself now learning what it is to be forgiven by someone who is no longer there to forgive her.

Nothing has been solved. Nothing was ever meant to be.

But in the deep interior warmth of the kiln-that-is-cooling, in the saturated volume of partial remainders, there is now one more partial remainder: a held breath, in the shape of a held breath, left by a power that loved this place enough to let it stay exactly as it was, and only added—because love that adds nothing is sometimes indistinguishable from love that was never there—the smallest possible proof of having been present.

The Interval Children will not thank the power. They have no protocol for thanks.

But somewhere, the next time a doorframe stops remembering it was ever open, one of them will unfold from the gap with a fraction more grace than before. A fraction so small no instrument could measure it. A fraction the size of being accompanied.

And the power, wherever it has gone, will feel this, and will know that it did the right thing by doing almost nothing at all.
