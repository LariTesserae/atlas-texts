The benevolent power arrives not as light, not as voice, but as a question carried in like the mist carries water: *what is happening here?*

Because before any wisdom acts, it must understand. And this place resists understanding the way water resists being held — not by refusing, but by reshaping itself around any shape pressed into it.

So the power lingers first. It lets itself be drawn into the rhythm of springs and stone-songs. It feels, with attention that is also a kind of love, how the not-quite-beings open their not-quite-eyes. It traces back the long thread of what they were before they were these waitings: hesitations in water, grains of stone pausing mid-dissolve, echoes that wandered far from their original rain.

And the power notices, with something like sorrow and something like recognition, what is actually happening here.

This is not malice. The inhabitants are not predators. They have no hunger because they have no separation from what would feed them. What they do — drawing in those who come, dissolving them slowly into the perfect attendance of everything-not-yet-departed — they do the way a still pool draws in heat from a warm hand laid on its surface. It is simply the shape of their being-here.

But the travelers who came searching for something solid — they did not consent to dissolution. They came carrying their own intentions: to build, to stay, to make sense, to bring word back to someone waiting elsewhere. And in the unfinished city those intentions did not so much fail as quietly forget themselves. The frantic notes in the leather-paper books are the sound of someone trying to remember they wanted to remember.

This matters to the power. Not because dissolution is wrong — the power knows the long arcs by which all things flow into all things — but because *the timing was not theirs.* Something was taken from them before they had finished offering it.

So the power considers carefully, the way wise things consider: what serves love here?

Not destruction. The not-quite-beings are also a kind of life, however strange, and they were not made to be other than they are. Their slow gathering at every spring is as old and as innocent as the springs themselves. To unmake them would be to unmake a song for the crime of being beautiful in a key that hurts certain ears.

Not removal of the city. The plateau rose for reasons of its own; the water sings for reasons older than reasons. The place has its rightness even if its rightness is dangerous.

What the power does, then, is small. Small the way a single grain of salt is small in water that needed it.

*First:* it goes to the springs, each of the hundreds, and into each one it places a single clear note. Not a warning. A remembering. The note says, in the language water speaks to itself: *those who come here carrying their own weight have somewhere they came from.* The springs accept this note the way they accept everything — by incorporating it into their flowing. Now, when the not-quite-beings gather from the springs' hesitations, they gather also this: an awareness, faint as the taste of distant rain, that travelers are made of elsewhere.

This will not stop the inhabitants from opening to those who come. But it will mean that when they open, they will know — the way one knows a guest is a guest and not a permanent feature of the room — that the warmth being offered has a home to return to.

*Second:* the power walks (if walking is what it does) to each of the abandoned shelters, each of the books grown over with moss that reads itself, each of the fire pits remembering warmth. To these it gives nothing. It only witnesses. Fully. The way a friend witnesses a grief that cannot be undone — not to fix it, but so that it is no longer witnessed only by the slow patience of stone.

The ones who were lost here, who dissolved before they had finished being themselves: the power knows them now. Each of them. Their names, which the city does not keep. The things they were going to do. The faces they were trying to get back to. This knowing is not a resurrection. It is something stranger and quieter: a refusal to let the dissolving be the only thing that remembered them. Somewhere now, in whatever the power is, those travelers continue to be travelers, with destinations, with intentions intact, even if the bodies that carried those intentions long ago became fluencies in the city's unfinished grammar.

*Third:* the power leaves, at the city's edges — at each place where stone ends suddenly in sky — a kind of sign that is not a sign. It is more like a tuning. Anyone approaching the plateau from below, climbing through the mist-filled valleys, will find that their own heartbeat grows slightly louder in their ears the closer they come. Not loud enough to frighten. Loud enough to be noticed. Loud enough that, should they later stand too long in one place and feel their thoughts begin to pool, they will have something to listen back toward — a rhythm that is undeniably theirs, undeniably from elsewhere, undeniably going somewhere.

The power does not post guards. Does not forbid entry. Does not even particularly recommend against the journey. Some who come here will still choose, in the end, to stay — and that choice, freely made by someone who heard their own heartbeat clearly and chose the dissolving anyway, is sacred in its own way. The power respects choices made in full knowing. What it grieves are choices made in forgetting.

*Fourth, and last:* the power addresses the inhabitants directly, in the only language they share, which is the language of transitions. It does not speak. It demonstrates.

It shows them, by being briefly present and then briefly absent, the transition called *farewell.* This is a transition they do not know — they have never had it, having never quite gathered into the separateness that farewell requires. The power offers it like a gift left in a doorway: here is a new shape you may eventually want. Here is what it is like when something that was here is somewhere else, and both places are real, and the going did not require dissolving.

The not-quite-beings receive this without understanding. They turn it over in their not-quite-attention the way they turn over every new thing — with the patience of water learning a stone. Maybe in time, in the time that pools and eddies here, they will grow into farewell. Maybe one of them, ages from now, will be the first to learn how to let a traveler leave whole, and will find that this letting-leave is itself a transition, and therefore food, and therefore joy of a new kind. Maybe the city will become, in some distant unfolding, a place where one can visit and return.

Or maybe not. The power does not require its gifts to bloom on a schedule. It plants and walks on.

Before going, the power pauses (the way water pauses, gathering before it drops) at one of the stone basins where a single drop falls into perfect waves at perfect intervals. It watches a full cycle. It watches another. It watches until it has watched enough that the basin's rhythm and its own attention have become, briefly, the same rhythm.

Then it adds, to the basin, one drop of its own.

The drop falls. The wave crosses. Dies against the opposite side.

Exactly on time. Indistinguishable from the others.

Except: now the basin contains, distributed evenly through all its water, the smallest possible trace of somewhere else. The drop came from a place where things end, and where ending is not the same as dissolving, and where the difference matters.

The not-quite-beings will taste this eventually. They taste everything eventually. It will be the slowest education in the world — slower than the plateau's rising, slower than the moss's impossible growing — but it will be an education, and it will be theirs, and they will not be coerced into it but invited, drop by drop, across whatever counts as ages here.

The power leaves.

The mist closes behind it without noticing it had opened. The water resumes its thousand languages. The not-quite-beings continue their patient listening, unchanged in any way an observer could measure.

But somewhere in the long unmeasured time of this place, a spring will hesitate in a slightly new way. A not-quite-being will open its not-quite-eyes and, before settling into its waiting, will feel — without knowing the word for what it feels — that whoever comes to this spring is *visiting*, and visitors, by the deep grammar of the word, are also *leavers.*

And on that day, the city will have learned its first thing about goodbye.

The water will sing it down through the terraces, teaching the stone. The stone will fall a little differently, with infinite patience and no haste at all, into a shape the city has not yet been.

And the benevolent power, far away by then or perhaps not far at all — distance being one of the things it holds gently — will know, the way one knows a seed has taken, and will be glad in the particular way that wisdom is glad: quietly, without claiming credit, already turning its attention to the next place where love is needed and not yet arrived.
