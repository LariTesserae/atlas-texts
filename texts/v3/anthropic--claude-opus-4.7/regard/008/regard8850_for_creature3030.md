# What the Wise Power Does

It arrives without arriving. That is the first decision.

A benevolent and wise power, looking down (or in, or through) at the Mossheart Foundry, would understand immediately that almost every gesture it could make would be a wound. To intervene here is to insert sharpness into a world that has, over three centuries, negotiated sharpness out of itself. To bring a gift is to imply a lack. To speak is to interrupt a music. To appear is to cast a shadow in a place whose light comes from everywhere at once.

So the power does not appear. It does not announce. It does not bless, redeem, uplift, rescue, or reveal.

It does, however, do four small things. It does them slowly, over what the Mossborn would recognize as a long time — many revolutions of the great flywheels, many cycles of heartfruit — and none of them are visible as miracles. A miracle would be a violence here.

---

**The first thing it does is listen back.**

For one hour each day, when the adult Mossborn press their palms and bare feet to the green-rusted altar of the central boiler house, something on the other side of the listening now listens too. Not a voice. Not a presence. Just the faint, almost imperceptible sense that the attention they have been pouring into the organism for three hundred years is, at last, being received somewhere — held, considered, valued. The Foundry has always answered them with water and warmth. Now the attention itself is answered, gently, by attention.

None of the Mossborn will be able to say what changed. The gifted three, who can feel the difference between the Foundry's hunger and its satisfaction, will begin to notice a third quality they have no word for. Something like being seen while seeing. They will adjust nothing. They will simply listen a little longer some days, and the music in the evenings will gain, very slowly across generations, a new register — not louder, not sharper, but somehow *answered*.

This is the only theological gift the power gives, and it gives it without theology.

---

**The second thing it does is protect, invisibly, from the outside.**

The Foundry has enemies it does not know about. Time, of course, but time has already been negotiated with. The real dangers are external: a changing climate that might one day dry the air the moss requires; a distant industry whose runoff could poison the deep water-table that feeds the weeping; a future curiosity — explorers, cataloguers, extractors — who would find the waxy ingots and the shape-remembering felt and the moss-medicines and decide that such things should belong to the world.

The power turns these aside. Not dramatically. A storm system shifts a hundred miles. A prospector's map is misread. A scholar's letter is lost in transit. An aquifer is quietly rerouted around a chemical plume two centuries before the plume exists. A road that would have been built is not built, because the surveyor falls in love and moves away, and his replacement prefers a different valley.

The Mossborn never learn that they have been preserved. This is essential. To know one is protected is to know one is fragile, and the Foundry's whole genius is that it does not experience itself as fragile. It experiences itself as complete. The power leaves that completeness intact by handling the threats so far upstream that they never become threats at all.

---

**The third thing it does is more delicate, and the power hesitates over it for what, in its own reckoning, is a long while.**

Some Mossborn leave. Almost all return. But not all. A few — perhaps one in every two or three generations — go out into the dry, fast, sharp world and do not come back, and the power can see that most of these are not happy. They carry the Foundry inside them like a wound that will not close into a scar. They cannot live at the speed the outside requires; they cannot un-soften their gaze; they press their palms to walls that do not weep, and the silence breaks something in them that the moss had been holding together.

The power could call them home. It does not. To do so would be to decide for them, and the wisdom in *benevolent and wise* lies more in the second word than the first.

Instead, it does this: it ensures that each of them, at least once, encounters something in the outer world that reminds them clearly and without ambiguity that returning is possible. A traveler who has been to the Foundry and speaks of it. A patch of damp moss on a northern wall that smells, faintly, of home. A dream, on a particular night, of warm water welling between fingers. Just enough that the door, in their own mind, stays open. Whether they walk through it is theirs.

Some still do not return. The power grieves this — yes, a wise and benevolent power can grieve — but it does not override the grief with action. The dignity of the Mossborn includes the dignity of their wanderers, even the lost ones. *Especially* the lost ones.

---

**The fourth thing is the hardest, and it is the one the power is least sure about.**

The Foundry is a closed system that has reached equilibrium. Equilibrium is beautiful. Equilibrium is also, eventually, a kind of ending. Three centuries is not long for such an organism, but three thousand might be, and three hundred thousand certainly would be. The moss-industrial body has solved the problem of decay so thoroughly that it may have, without meaning to, also solved the problem of becoming. Nothing is wearing down. But is anything still arriving?

The power considers this for a long time. It considers whether to introduce, somewhere very deep in the substrate, a single small possibility — a mutation in the moss, a new note the organism could one day learn to play, a question the Foundry might eventually ask itself.

In the end, it does, but only barely. It does not add anything. It simply declines to prevent something that was already, in the deepest folds of the green mass, beginning to happen on its own: a slow, slow drift in the chemistry of the sap, so gradual that ten generations of Mossborn will not notice it, but that in time — long after every current resident has been gathered back into the walls — may give rise to something the Foundry has not yet been. Perhaps a second species of moss. Perhaps a new kind of heartfruit. Perhaps a music with a sharper edge that the velvet has finally learned how to hold without absorbing.

The power does not know what it will become. That is the point. A wise benevolence does not author the future of a being it loves; it protects the conditions under which that being can author itself.

---

And then the power withdraws its particular attention, though not its general care, and goes to consider other places, other beings, other dripping cathedrals and dry deserts and burning libraries — each one requiring its own grammar of restraint.

Behind it, in the warm green dark, a flywheel completes another revolution. A fingernail-sized golden frog rides a droplet down a curtain of moss. A Mossborn woman with lichen-colored eyes presses her palm against the altar and feels, for just a moment, that her listening is being kept somewhere safe.

She does not know why she smiles.

She does not need to.
