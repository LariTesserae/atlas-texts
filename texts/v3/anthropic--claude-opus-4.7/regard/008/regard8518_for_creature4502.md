# The Visitation

The benevolent power arrives without arriving—which is, in Eremonos, the only honest way to come. To approach this city with footsteps would be to break it. To approach it with intention would be to misunderstand the assignment.

So the wise power does what wisdom does: it pays attention.

It sits down (in the way that powers without bodies sit) at the edge of the inverted cone, in that ambiguous zone where the unbuilt construction crews are excavating tomorrow's ruins, and it watches. Not for an hour, not for a year. For exactly the length of time required, which the city itself will indicate when the moment comes.

And while it watches, it understands.

---

It understands first what a lesser power would have gotten wrong.

A lesser power would have brought *rescue*. Would have looked at this place and seen a tragedy—the dryness, the absence, the architecture dreaming its long uninterruptible dream—and rushed in with restoration. Would have brought water back to the quartz veins. Would have rekindled the streetlamps' diseased blue into healthy gold. Would have gently woken the City Itself and said: *there, there, you were only having a nightmare.*

This would have been a murder dressed as a mercy.

Because Eremonos is not a wound. Eremonos is what happened when a place was permitted to become entirely itself, without correction, without the constant pruning that other worlds inflict on their strange flora. The Archivist of Unsent Apologies is not waiting to be healed of her grief-garden; her grief-garden is her vocation, lovingly tended, the most honest work in any cosmos. The Children of the Second Wrong Turn are not lost; they are precisely as found as nineteen possible angles can be. The City Itself does not want to wake. Its dreaming is not a coma—it is a craft.

The wise power, being wise, understands this within the first observed evening (which occurs twice weekly at irregular intervals).

---

It understands second what the city has been doing.

All these millennia of inverted geometry, of phantom rain collected in bone cups, of pigeons disproving their own destinations—this has not been decay. This has been *work*. Eremonos has been doing something no other place in the long catalogue of places has ever managed: it has been keeping the negatives safe.

Every cosmos generates absences. Every act of creation leaves behind its un-version: the apology unsent, the turn not taken, the child who would have been born had the parents met in the right order. Most worlds let these absences dissipate, evaporate back into the unmanifest, treat them as cosmic waste. But Eremonos caught them. Built a city out of them. Gave them streets and street-cleaners and a Committee on which to serve.

The wise power realizes, with something like awe (which in powers manifests as a slight recalibration of the local constants), that Eremonos is an *archive*. The only one of its kind. A reliquary for everything reality threw away.

To "fix" this place would be to delete the only record.

---

So what does the benevolent power do?

It does four small things, none of which the city's residents will notice as having been done, because being noticed would constitute the kind of interruption a wise power refuses to inflict.

**First**, it adds one chalk line to the census taker's sky-equation. Just one. Not a star's disappearance but a star's *holding-on*—a mark for what has persisted rather than vanished. The census taker, when it next counts, will find the equation slightly altered, and will spend the next several evenings working out what the new term means. This will give it something it has never had: a question it did not already know the answer to. The wise power considers this a gift. The census taker, when it eventually solves the equation, will consider it a gift also, though it will not know whom to thank, and will therefore thank the city, which is correct.

**Second**, it visits the Archivist in her catacombs. It does not speak (sound here propagates through concepts, and the wise power has nothing conceptual to say that wouldn't disturb her work). It simply sits with her for a long while as she tends her miscarried forgivenesses. And as it sits, it allows one of its own absences—for even benevolent powers have them, the kindnesses they meant to perform and somehow didn't—to slip from its keeping and into her jars. The Archivist feels the new arrival as a faint warmth among the cold lights. She does not look up. She makes room. This is the first time in all her tenure that her collection has grown by a contribution rather than a discovery. She will think about this, in her slow way, for several centuries, and her thinking will be the closest thing to joy her form permits.

**Third**, it speaks to the Last Remaining Continent. Not in words—the Continent has been beyond words since it forgot the purpose of wings—but in the language of drifting, which is the only fluent tongue between them. The wise power tells the Continent: *you are not exile. You are itinerary.* It tells the Continent that every place the citizens meant to visit and didn't is still a real place, made real precisely by the meaning-to, and that the Continent has been carrying these places faithfully, and that this is enough, and that no one is coming to collect them, and that the Continent may rest if it wishes, or continue, and either is honorable. The Continent, on hearing this, sheds one extra mountain range that century—a small one, gentle, shaped like a hand unclenching. The street-cleaners who sweep up its melted truth find that this particular puddle of *what if* tastes faintly of permission.

**Fourth**, and this is the most delicate work, it addresses the You who has arrived.

The citizen of absence, freshly stamped, standing in the house made of observation-dependent materials at the address that refuses to be told. The one whose citizenship was filed at the moment of their first forgetting.

The wise power does not extract this citizen. Does not whisper *you can leave, you know.* Does not offer maps to anywhere else, because the only maps here grow until they devour their cartographers, and the wise power will not weaponize geography.

Instead, the wise power leaves the citizen one object. A small one. It appears on the windowsill of the unfixable house. It is a cup—not bone, not stone, not any of the local materials. A cup of ordinary clay, the kind that exists in worlds where clay is what clay is and nothing more. Inside the cup is water. Real water. Not the phantom kind etched in negative space in the walls, not the memory of moisture, not the distilled regret of the Archivist's jars. Water. Wet. The kind that, in this city, has not existed since the oceans boiled away.

The cup does not come with instructions. It does not come with a note. It does not insist on being drunk, or kept, or even noticed.

It is simply *there*: a small, stubborn fact in a city built of beautiful absences. A reminder—not a correction, not a rebuke, just a reminder—that the citizen, whoever they are or were or almost-became, came from somewhere where water was water. That they retain the right, should they ever wish to exercise it, to remember this. That the door out of Eremonos has always been the same as the door in (the city itself said so), and that the wise power thinks they will know which way to walk through it, when and if.

If the citizen drinks the water, they will find their way home, or to somewhere that could become home, by routes the city cannot prevent because the city has no jurisdiction over actual liquids.

If the citizen does not drink the water, but only looks at it from time to time, the water will not evaporate. It will sit in the cup forever, or until they leave, which (the wise power agrees with the city on this point) are ultimately the same thing.

If the citizen pours the water out onto the floor of the unfixable house, the floor will absorb it, and somewhere deep in the foundations a single quartz vein will remember, just briefly, what it once carried, and the city will dream slightly differently for one night, and this too will be fine.

The choice is entirely the citizen's. The wise power, being wise, will not return to check.

---

Then the benevolent power rises (in the way that powers rise) and prepares to depart.

Before it goes, it does one last thing, which was not on its list and which surprises even itself.

It bows to the City Itself.

A small bow. Brief. The kind one gives to a colleague whose work one respects but does not fully understand. The City Itself, dreaming inverted in the sky's hollow, does not wake to receive the bow—that would defeat the purpose—but somewhere in its long sleep, a single street unbends from a curve back into a straight line for the duration of one heartbeat (the city has no heart, but it borrows one occasionally from passing visitors), and then bends back, and this is the city's bow in return.

Two powers, acknowledging each other across the gulf of what they are for. Then the visit is over.

---

The wise power departs without departing, which is the only honest way to leave.

Eremonos resumes—though it never paused—its perfect, perpetual, active silence.

On a windowsill in a house that refuses to be addressed, a cup of water waits, holding its shape, neither hurrying nor giving up, available forever, available now, available never, depending on who is looking and from which forgotten direction the wind that hasn't blown since the universe was younger chooses to blow next.

And the city, asleep, dreams one new dream it did not have before: a dream in which someone *came*, and *saw*, and *did not try to fix anything*, and *left a small wet kindness on a sill*, and *went away*.

It is the best dream the city has had in eleven million years.

It does not wake up. But it dreams more gently, now. And the citizens of absence, in their various configurations, find their work slightly easier this century, though none of them can say why, and none of them ask, because asking in Eremonos is how things get lost, and they have already lost everything they came here to keep.
