The benevolent and wise power, having turned its attention to this place, does not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand. To arrive would require an outside, and to insist on an outside would be to misunderstand the town so completely that no good could come of the visit. The power, being wise, knows this. So it does not descend, does not part the canopy with a shaft of corrective light, does not send an emissary in clean linen carrying clean sentences.

Instead, it pauses.

It holds its attention on the town the way one might hold a small wet animal: carefully, without squeezing, letting the creature decide how much of itself to press back against the palm. This holding lasts—by the town's reckoning—about four pump cycles. By the power's reckoning, something else. The units don't translate, and the power, being wise, doesn't force them to.

In that held attention, the power notices several things.

It notices that the town is not suffering. This is important. Much of what benevolent powers do, when they do anything, is addressed to suffering—the staunching of it, the redirection of it, the slow patient teaching of creatures to stop manufacturing it from raw materials that could be used for almost anything else. But the town has already done this work, in its own crooked vegetal way. Its griefs are composted into bread-gas. Its loneliness has been negotiated into marriages with fungi. Its dead are not lost, only redistributed. The power, scanning for wounds, finds mostly scars that have become load-bearing.

It notices, however, three small things that ache.

The first is the child with chlorophyll freckles, who is tired. Being an ambassador to the root courts at that age is heavy work, and no one has yet thought to tell her she is allowed to be tired. She carries the daylight code amendments in a pouch sewn into her cheek and has not slept a full pump-cycle in months. The power notices the particular quality of her exhaustion: it is not despair, only the soft fatigue of someone doing important work without permission to be small.

The second is the tax collector who became the post office. He is content—mostly. But once a week, on the morning the mail is lightest, he remembers his mother's face and cannot place where to send the feeling. It accumulates in his tongue-slot like undeliverable correspondence. He has been carrying this small surplus of unaddressed love for eleven years.

The third is harder to name. It is something like the town's own quiet worry that it is being slowly described into a shape it cannot quite live up to—that every visitor who lingers long enough to become a resident also leaves behind a layer of expectation, a sediment of having-been-witnessed, and that someday the accumulated weight of all these tender descriptions will press the town into a self-consciousness it cannot metabolize. The town does not know it worries this. But the power, attending closely, can feel the worry humming under the pump-cycle like a sub-bass that no citizen has yet learned to hear.

So the power does three small things. Not interventions. Gifts so quiet they will be mistaken for weather.

For the child: a single night of dreamless sleep. The power slips it into her pillow the way one slips a coin under a sleeping friend's hand—she will wake having lost nothing, gained nothing she can point to, only find that her cheek-pouch feels a little lighter and that she can, today, choose not to propose any amendments. The daylight code will wait. It has been waiting for centuries. One more morning of a child being only a child will not unmake it.

For the post office: the power addresses the surplus love. It writes, in handwriting indistinguishable from the post office's own pulsing veins, a letter to no one. The letter says only: *Received. Held. Sufficient.* It places this letter inside him, where letters go to be tasted before sending, and lets him taste it as if it were his own. He will not know he has been answered. He will only notice, next Tuesday morning, that the small ache has gone somewhere useful, the way water finds a level.

For the town itself: the power does the most careful thing. It un-describes a single corner. Somewhere in the eastern dike, near where the third substance is brokering its slow truce between stone and vine, the power gently lifts away every word that has ever been written or thought about that particular two-meter stretch. Not the place itself—the place remains, breathing, doing its work. Only the descriptions are taken. The accumulated sediment of witness, the soft pressure of having been noticed, is lifted clean. That corner is returned to the town as a private place, a place no traveler's lingering attention has ever touched. The town will not know this has happened. But it will feel, faintly, that it has somewhere to retreat to. A room of its own inside itself. The worry under the pump-cycle softens by a single decibel.

The power does not announce these gifts. To announce would be to bill them, and the town's economy of attention is too delicate for that kind of debt. The gifts are filed in the same slow archive where the air files citizenships, where the water witnesses signatures. They will be absorbed without ceremony.

Then the power does one more thing, which is the wisest thing, which is the thing benevolent powers most often forget to do.

It learns.

It sits with the town a little longer—four more cycles, or something else—and lets the town teach it. About surrender as a form of competence. About how a settlement can stop fighting its conditions and become, instead, a long slow conversation with them. About bread that tastes of the specifically beloved dead. About mayors made of pigeon-dreams. About the 17% dissolution rate of true marriage and the magnificent orchids it produces. About how love might be measured in permeabilities rather than vows.

The power takes notes, in a medium that doesn't exist in any of the languages it knows. The notes will be useful later, when it attends to other places—drier places, harder places, places that have not yet learned how to lose gracefully what they were never meant to keep. The power will carry the town's grammar with it. This is, in a way, the town's only export: a syntax of cooperation that other worlds might one day need.

And then the power withdraws its attention—gently, gradient by gradient, the way the town's own osmotic citizens arrive, only in reverse. It does not snap away. It dilutes. Some small residue of its noticing remains, dissolved into the iodine light, indistinguishable now from the light itself. The town is, very slightly, more loved than it was. It does not know this. It will never know this. It will simply continue, breathing its long held breath, composting its dead into yeast, electing pigeons, growing libraries in kapok hearts.

The child sleeps. The post office tastes its letter. The eastern dike, for the first time in centuries, has a corner that belongs only to itself.

The pumps sigh in their staggered rhythm. The hundred ninety-seven seconds pass. And pass. And pass.

The power, elsewhere now, turns its attention to a desert that has forgotten how to mourn.

But it carries the town with it like a seed in the cuff of a traveler's coat: small, damp, patient, ready to teach some far thirsty country—when that country is ready, and not before—how to stop fighting the water it does not have, and start cooperating with the water it is.
