The benevolent and wise power considers the Hollow for a long while before doing anything at all.

This is the first gift: attention without intervention. The power looks, and goes on looking, until it can feel the rhythm in its own attention slow to match the irregular percussion of the drops, until the spiraling processions of the small clay-colored people seem not strange but inevitable, the only shape a life could take in such a chamber. It listens to the wet breathing in the walls. It notices that the breathing and the people's own breathing have, over uncounted generations, fallen into a loose accord, not synchronized but answering, the way two instruments in different rooms might find a common key without a player intending it.

Then the power asks itself the only question that matters: *What is lacking here that ought not to be lacking?*

And it finds, to its quiet surprise, very little.

There is no hunger that the root-mats and grass-husks do not meet. There is no loneliness, because loneliness requires a self that has been promised more than a cluster and a season. There is no fear of the dark, because the dark is only the other half of the light from the apex, and both are trusted. There is no boredom, because the work of keeping the Hollow is the same work as being alive in it, and the two cannot be pried apart to leave a gap where restlessness might enter. There is wear, yes, and small deaths, and the slow settling of tiles — but these are not wounds. They are the medium through which the people know themselves, the way a hand knows itself by what it touches.

The power could, of course, do many things.

It could widen the shaft at the apex and pour more light down. But the people lie flat at the hour of the column and listen with their bellies; more light would not be more seeing, it would be a flood that drowned the listening.

It could seal the cracks in the ceiling so the condensation no longer fell. But the dark rings on the mosaic are a clock, and the people read time by them, and a chamber without that clock would be a chamber in which the people no longer knew when to move and when to be still.

It could multiply the root-threads, thicken the lichen, send schools of soft creatures down through the hidden channels so that no one need ever scrape a wall again. But the scraping is not a burden lifted from the people; it is the people. To remove it would be to remove them and leave only fur and claws moving in a richer room.

It could make them taller. Stronger. Longer-lived. Give them language for what they already know, or writing, or fire bright enough to push back the soft bounced light. Each of these the power examines, and each it sets aside, because each would be a gift offered to someone other than the ones who actually live here. A gift to an imagined people the power had substituted for the real ones in its own mind. The wise part of the power recognizes this temptation — the temptation to love a being by replacing them — and lets it pass.

So the power does smaller things. Truer things.

It steadies, very gently, one sapling pole that was about to fail in a way the people could not have caught in time — not replacing their work, but giving them the extra hour they would have needed and did not have. They will lash it themselves, tomorrow, and feel only that they arrived in time, which is what they always feel, and which is mostly true and now entirely true.

It softens, by some fraction no instrument could measure, the mineral edge in the water that has been slowly, slowly making the older ones' joints ache. Not removing the ache — the ache is part of how an older one knows to sit at the edge of the column-hour and let the younger ones spiral — but trimming the cruelty from it, leaving the meaning.

It puts a thought, or something less than a thought, near the threshold of one of the people who has lately been pressing her cheek to the mosaic longer than the others, listening for something she has not yet found a way to name. The thought is not an answer. It is the faint reassurance that the listening itself is the thing, and that she is doing it correctly, and that there is no further place she is failing to reach. She will rise from the tile a little lighter and not know why, and she will stroke a lizard along its back, and the lizard will pause a half-beat longer than usual before flicking away.

And the power makes one promise, to itself, which is the largest thing it does:

*I will remember this place.*

Not as a curiosity. Not as a specimen. As a real place, with real inhabitants, whose way of being is whole. So that if, in some far century, the breathing in the walls falters, or the shaft above is choked by something falling from a world the people have no concept of, or a sickness moves through the clusters faster than the obsidian slivers and thorn needles can answer — *then* the power will know what the Hollow was, exactly, and will be able to act on behalf of what the people themselves would have wanted to preserve, rather than on behalf of what an outsider might think a people ought to want.

This is the deepest gift, and the people will never know they have received it: to be witnessed accurately enough that, should they ever need rescue, the rescue will be theirs and not a stranger's idea of them.

The power lingers a while longer. It watches the column of light cross the central mosaic and fade. It watches the small bodies rise from the edges and resume their traffic, pressing dust into grout, re-lashing what creaks, cradling drops of water in six-fingered hands without breaking the surface tension. It listens to the dry rustling of the winged insects and the slow, wet breathing of the hidden channels, and it understands — with the particular understanding that only a wise power can have, the kind that knows the difference between *enough* and *more* — that it has been admitted, briefly, into a completeness.

Then it withdraws, leaving no sign of its passage except, perhaps, the faintest sense among the people that the Hollow today feels exactly as the Hollow has always felt: a place too crowded to be empty, too carefully tended to be wild, and — though they have no word for this and need none — *known*.
