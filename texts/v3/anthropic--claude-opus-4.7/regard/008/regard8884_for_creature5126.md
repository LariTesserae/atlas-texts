They would not arrive.

That is the first thing. A power both benevolent and wise, looking on this place, would understand that arrival is itself a kind of weather, and this ridge has weather enough. To descend in glory onto a half-set stone would be to press a thumbprint into wet clay. The Settled have built their entire grammar around the principle that nothing here is finished, and that unfinishedness is not a flaw but the medium in which they live. To resolve it would be to end them.

So the first act of such a power would be restraint. A long looking. A patience matched to the patience of the gears.

What might come after, if anything, would have to be so slight it could be mistaken for the place's own behavior.

Perhaps this:

On a night when the larval hum thins dangerously—when some imbalance in the condensation cycle threatens a whole stretch of fern gardens—a single drift of moisture arrives from an unaccountable direction. Not rain. Not fog. Something between. It settles on the iron, and the swarms recover by morning. The listeners press their ears to the stone and hear nothing unusual. The Carapace Folk register a hum at the very edge of their subsonic range and assume it was the mountain. It was, in a sense. The power has not added anything; it has only reminded a process of itself.

Perhaps this:

A fracture opens beneath a sleeping child's hammock. The stone parts along a line that should, by every reading of its bedding planes, run wider before it runs deeper. Instead it runs deeper first, by perhaps the width of two fingers, and the platform holds. A listener three shifts later finds the fracture and cannot explain its geometry. She marks it, sits with it, and eventually decides the mountain is learning new habits. She is not wrong.

Perhaps this:

One of the listeners—a woman whose halo of larvae has lately stayed with her past the end of her shift, past her meal, into her sleep—dreams of a shape she has no word for. It is not a face. It is closer to the feeling of being read by something that is taking its time and finding the reading worthwhile. She wakes without urgency. She tells no one, because the Settled do not rush to speech. But for the rest of her life her sigh-timing carries a small additional cadence, and the Carapace Folk who trade with her come to prefer her exchanges over others, without being able to say why.

That is most of what the power would do. Almost nothing. Almost nothing, performed with great attention.

Because here is what a wise benevolence would have seen, looking down through the high mist:

These people have already solved the problem that most worlds bring such powers running to fix. They are not at war with their conditions. They are not extracting from the mountain faster than the mountain offers. They have built an economy with the larvae instead of against them. They wait. They listen. They have made a culture whose central virtue is the refusal to force a decision before its time. To intervene grandly would be to teach them that their patience was a stopgap, a thing to be relieved of by greater agency. It would unmake the very wisdom that drew the power's attention in the first place.

What the Settled need is not rescue. They need only the small assurance, never spoken, that their way of living is being witnessed. That somewhere the long groaning of the ridge is heard as music and not as mere geology. That the listener with the larval halo is, in some sense, listened to in return.

So the power would do one more thing, and only one, and only once.

It would leave a sign so small it could be missed for generations. Not a monument. Not a voice. Something on the order of: in a particular alcove, on a particular ledge, the quartz veins in the wall form, if you look at them in the blue lantern light at a certain angle, a pattern that is almost—but not quite—the shape of an ear. It is not miraculous. It could be coincidence. The Settled who notice it will argue gently about it over fermented grazer-milk and then forget to argue, because argument is not their mode. But the alcove will become, without anyone deciding, a place where listeners go when they are tired. They will sit there. They will be heard. They will not know by whom, and they will not need to.

And the power, having done this, would withdraw its attention—not entirely, never entirely, but to the distance proper to such things. It would carry the ridge with it the way one carries the memory of a piece of music one heard once in a country one will not return to: with a particular ache, with gratitude, and with the discipline not to hum it aloud in places where it does not belong.

The mountain would go on deciding its shape. The gears would go on turning at the rate of one rotation per hour. The larvae would tick beneath the threshold of words.

And somewhere, very far away or very near, something benevolent and wise would be a little changed by what it had seen, and would carry forward, into whatever else it tended, the lesson the Settled had given without ever meaning to teach:

that the kindest thing a great power can do for a small and patient world is to let it finish its own sentence.
