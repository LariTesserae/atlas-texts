The power arrives, and does nothing.

Not at first. Not for what would be, anywhere else, a long time. Here it is only another stillness folded into the stillnesses already present, another attentiveness laid alongside the attentiveness of the old ones on the terraces. The benevolence does not announce itself. Wisdom, in such a place, recognizes immediately that announcement would be the first kind of harm.

So it watches.

It watches the way a child moves a pebble from one depression to another and finds, in that motion, a completeness the power itself has rarely witnessed. It watches a woman lift a seedling and carry it twenty paces to a barer patch of stone, her hands neither reverent nor casual, simply correct. It watches the mist accumulate and release, accumulate and release, on a single leaf, for what would be, elsewhere, a season.

The power had come prepared to give. This is what benevolent powers do; it is nearly their definition. It had carried with it, in the way such beings carry things, a readiness: to heal what was wounded, to enrich what was thin, to wake what slept, to teach what did not yet know, to extend what was brief. It had expected to find some lack to meet. Most places, when examined closely, reveal a lack. Most beings, when listened to, are asking for something, even if they have forgotten the asking.

Here there is no asking.

This is the first thing the power must sit with, and it sits with it for a long while. The absence of request is not the same as contentment; the power knows this and is careful. It looks for the hidden ache, the unvoiced hunger, the suffering so old it has been mistaken for the shape of the world. It looks honestly, because a wise power does not flatter a place by pretending its peace is deeper than it is.

What it finds is subtler than contentment and stranger than resignation. The people of the basin are not happy in any sense the word usually carries. Neither are they unhappy. They have arrived, by some long settling whose origin not even they remember, at a way of being that does not generate the friction from which happiness and unhappiness are struck. They are not suppressing desire; desire has simply thinned, the way a river thins when its gradient flattens, until what remains is wide, shallow, and almost motionless—still water, still capable of reflecting sky.

The power considers: is this a diminishment? Has something been lost here that should be restored? It turns the question carefully, because the question itself carries assumptions it does not wish to impose. In most worlds the power has known, vitality means increase: more children, more song, more reaching. Here vitality has taken a different form. The plants thrive but do not multiply. The people persist but do not expand. The water moves but does not erode. Everything is alive; nothing is becoming. It is a mode of life the power has seen only rarely, and never so purely.

It decides—and the decision takes what would be, for a human, the length of several lifetimes—that this is not diminishment. It is one of the possible answers to the question of how to be, and it is an answer the people of the basin have given with their whole existence, across generations, without ever framing it as an answer. To overwrite it with a more familiar vitality would be to mistake the power's own preference for wisdom.

And yet.

The power notices something the basin's inhabitants cannot quite notice from inside. The balance that holds them is real, but it is not eternal. No balance is. The raw edges of the ridges are still settling; the crustal heave that lifted this basin has not entirely finished its slow work. Somewhere beneath the saturated floor, pressures shift by increments too small to register in any single generation. In some number of centuries, perhaps many, the basin will tilt, or the water will find a new outlet, or the mist will fail. The stillness here is not the stillness of permanence. It is the stillness of a held breath—very long, very steady, but held.

This the power can do something about, and the question is whether it should.

It could anchor the basin. It could deepen the seeps so they never fail, reinforce the ridges so they never crack further, fix the temperature of the air so the mist never thins. It could make the held breath into something that need never be released. The people would not know; nothing would seem to change; their way of life would continue indefinitely, exactly as it is.

The power considers this for a long time, and then declines.

Not because it would be difficult. Because the stillness of the basin is precious precisely as a stillness within time, not outside it. To remove the basin from the slow weather of the world would be to turn it into a diorama—beautiful, preserved, and no longer quite alive. The people watching surface tension elongate on a drop are watching something that matters because the drop will, eventually, fall. A held breath that can never be released is no longer a breath. The power understands this with a clarity that surprises even itself; it is the kind of understanding the basin has taught it simply by existing.

So the power chooses a smaller gift, and a stranger one.

It does this: it makes itself, very gently, a witness. Not a presence the people will detect, not an intervention in any event, but a kind of attention woven quietly into the basin's air. So that when a child moves a pebble, the motion is seen—not only by the basin, which already sees, but by something that will carry the seeing onward. So that when an old man stands on a terrace at dusk and watches the mist gather on a single shrub, that watching is itself watched, held, remembered in a register beyond the basin's own slow memory. So that nothing the people do, however small, however unspoken, falls into a final forgetting.

This is the gift: that the basin's way of being, which asks for nothing, is nevertheless received. That the long discipline of not adding weight to what already holds its place is honored by a regard that adds no weight either, but does not let the discipline pass unwitnessed.

The power understands that the people would not want even this, if they knew. They have arranged their existence so that being unwitnessed is part of the texture of the peace. So the regard is laid down very lightly, beneath the threshold of any possible detection, more like the faint tone of the light than like a gaze. It changes nothing. It only ensures that the basin's particular answer to the question of how to be is preserved, somewhere, as one of the answers—so that wherever else in the world or beyond it the question is asked, this answer too is available to be remembered, even if it is never repeated.

Then the power does one more thing, the last and the least.

When, eventually, the basin's long balance does begin to fail—when the seeps thin, when a ridge gives, when the mist at last lifts and does not return—the power will be there, not to prevent it, but to make the ending gentle. The people will not be saved from their ending, because their ending is part of what makes their stillness real. But they will not suffer in it. The change, when it comes, will arrive at the same tempo as everything else in the basin: slowly, without violence, with time enough for a last child to move a last pebble from one depression to another and watch the water carry a leaf along its chosen path. The lifting of the mist will feel, to those last inhabitants, like the natural completion of a long attention, not like loss. They will not know a power has eased it. They will only know, in whatever quiet way they know anything, that what was held has been allowed, at the proper moment, to release.

And then the basin will be only stone and changing weather again, and the power will go elsewhere, carrying with it the memory of a place that taught it the difference between preserving something and loving it.
