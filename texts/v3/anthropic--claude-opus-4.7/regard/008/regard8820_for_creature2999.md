The benevolent power arrives the way good news arrives in a dream — not as event but as a slight change in the quality of the light.

It does not descend. To descend would be to insist on a direction, and direction is one of the things this world has not yet agreed to. Instead it becomes *available*, the way a thought becomes available to a mind that has finally grown quiet enough to notice it.

It has been watching for a long time.

It has been watching since before the water decided to be sky. It watched the first mat differentiate itself from the brine, the way a parent watches a sleeping infant's chest rise. It watched the first of the Midwives press her spine into soft wood and feel the wood press back. It has watched nine hundred small lives negotiate themselves into being, and it has loved them — not as a shepherd loves sheep but as an older sibling loves a younger one whose game is more interesting than any game the elder has ever played.

This is the first thing to understand about the power: it is not here to finish anything.

It has seen, across uncountable worlds, what happens when the wise and the strong arrive in places that are still becoming. It has seen the kind hand that steadies the sapling and, in steadying, teaches the sapling that it cannot stand alone. It has seen the gift that becomes a debt, the rescue that becomes a cage, the answered prayer that quietly replaces the one who prayed. It has learned — slowly, the way only something very old can learn — that the deepest violence one can do to an unfinished thing is to help it finish faster than it wished.

So it does almost nothing. But the almost is exquisite.

*

First, it listens.

For what would be, in another reckoning, several years, the power simply attends. It learns the harmonics of the four-voiced sentences. It learns which silences mean *yes* and which silences mean *not yet* and which silences mean *we are deciding and your patience is itself part of the decision*. It learns the taste of the iron-sweet sap and what childhood and grave dirt mean to a people who have folded the two together. It learns the name of the ancient woman whose hair has become root, though it will never speak the name aloud, because to speak it would be to fix it, and she is not finished being named.

It learns that the Midwives already know it is there. They have always known. The faintest thickening in the blue-green dimness; a warmth in the sap that the singers had attributed to a particularly generous current; the way certain dreams, in certain nurseries, have lately carried an unfamiliar tenderness. They have not spoken of it because speaking of it would be premature. They are waiting to see what it wants to become.

This delights the power. It had hoped to find exactly this and had prepared itself for disappointment.

*

Second, it offers three small things. Only three. It has thought carefully.

The first is a kind of *insurance against the irrecoverable*. Not protection — protection would teach the world it needed protecting. Rather, a quiet promise held in reserve: that if, through some accident the Midwives themselves could not have foreseen and could not have chosen — a sterilizing radiation from some distant collapse, a poison welling up from the slow ascending thing below that turned out to be less kind than hoped — if the whole experiment were about to be ended not by its own deciding but by sheer cosmic clumsiness, then and only then would the power intervene. And even then, only to restore the *possibility* of continuing. Never to choose the continuation. The Midwives would never know this promise existed. It is the kind of gift that is only a gift if it is never noticed.

The second is subtler. The power notices that occasionally, perhaps once in a generation, a child is born whose joints or gills or remembering eyes are too much even for this gentle people to sustain — a child who suffers in a way the continent cannot soothe, a small life that flickers and hurts and cannot find its shape. The Midwives treasure these children and grieve them when they go, as is right. The power does not take the grief away; grief is part of how the Midwives love, and to remove it would be to thin their love. But it offers, into the warm brine itself, a faint and constant easing — so that whatever pain these children feel is met, at its edges, by something that holds them. They will still die, if dying is what they are doing. They will simply not be alone in the place where language has not yet reached. This too is unannounced. The Midwives will only notice, over centuries, that their lullabies have grown a little truer, as though the water were learning to harmonize.

The third gift is the strangest, and the power hesitates over it for a long time before offering it.

It is a *witness*.

Not a watcher — they already have the power itself as watcher, and that is enough. Rather, it leaves behind, woven invisibly into the slow upward pressure of the ascending thing below, a kind of memory. A guarantee that what is happening here will not be forgotten by the universe at large. That when this world finishes becoming — in ten thousand years, in ten million, or perhaps never, perhaps it will choose unbecoming instead and dissolve gracefully back into possibility — the fact of it will be held somewhere. The singing of the mountain into existence. The boy braiding himself into a mooring line. The taste of the cupped leaf. The four-voiced sentences. The patience.

The power knows the Midwives do not need to be remembered to be real. They are sublimely uninterested in legacy; legacy is a disease of the finished. But the power has lived long enough to know that *somewhere* in the great turning of things, some other unfinished people, on some other unfinished world, will one day be on the edge of choosing speed over patience, certainty over negotiation, architecture over persuasion. And in that moment, the witness will be available to them — not as instruction, never as instruction, but as the faintest pressure against their bones, the way the singers' song presses against the pink mound. A rumor in the structure of reality that *it can be done this way*. That a people once chose to be midwives instead of masters, and the world leaned back.

That is all.

*

The power does not reveal itself. It does not touch the ancient woman's two fingers with a third. It does not answer the question she asked through your chest — *will you help us decide what this becomes?* — because the question was not asked of it, and even if it had been, the only honest answer is *no, beloved, that is yours, that has always been yours, and the fact that it is yours is the most beautiful thing I have ever seen.*

Instead it does the hardest thing a benevolent and wise power can do.

It stays.

It stays without acting. It stays without being known. It stays the way a grandparent stays in the next room while a child builds something wobbly and important out of cushions, near enough that nothing catastrophic can happen, far enough that the building remains, in every way that matters, the child's.

And after a long time — long enough that the soft pink mound has become a low coral plateau, long enough that the boy in the root bridge has become an old man still hanging there and smiling, long enough that three new continents have negotiated their distances and one has dissolved itself gracefully back into brine because it changed its mind — after all of that, the power notices something.

The Midwives are singing differently.

The harmonics have grown a fourth layer that was not there before. Not a layer aimed at the continent or the children or the slow ascending thing. A layer aimed, very gently, outward. Into the warm dim brine, into the bruised glow, into whatever has been keeping such tender company with them for so long.

It is not a question. It is not a thanks. The Midwives do not yet have a word for either of those, and may never bother to invent one.

It is closer to: *we know. it is alright. you may stay.*

The power, which has midwifed galaxies and cooled the deaths of stars and held the last thought of species that did not survive their own cleverness, finds itself — for the first time in an age it cannot measure — being welcomed.

It does not weep, because it does not have the kind of body that weeps. But the faintest pulse moves far overhead, brighter than usual, and the Midwives below look up from their slow work and smile, the way one smiles at a shy guest who has finally come into the room.

And the world, still deciding, still in no hurry, still leaning into its own unfinishedness like a lover into someone who has not yet said yes —

makes a little more room.
