The benevolent power considers.

It has crossed vaster distances than the chamber's diameter to find this place, and yet, standing at the threshold (if such a word applies to a presence that does not need thresholds), it pauses for what, in its own reckoning, is a long while. Long enough that several droplets fall. Long enough that one filament, somewhere in the middle terraces, completes a motion it began before the power arrived.

The power is wise, which means among other things that it has learned to distrust its first impulses toward kindness. Kindness, it knows, has a shape, and that shape is often the shape of the kind one rather than the shape of what is helped. A being that lives in sunlight tends to bring sunlight. A being that loves voices tends to bring voices. The wreckage of well-meant interventions is scattered across more worlds than the power likes to recall.

So it watches first. This is the first thing it does, and it does it for a duration that, measured against the filaments' own tempo, is respectful: not a glance, not a survey, but something closer to a vigil. It learns the rhythm of the drip. It learns that the rhythm is not actually uniform—there are seventeen distinct sources, each with its own interval, and their interference creates patterns that repeat only every few months. It learns that the light is not in fact sourceless; it comes from the threads themselves, faintly, a luminescence so dim that only the absence of any competing light allows it to register at all. The chamber glows because its inhabitants glow. The power had not expected this, and notes, with something like the pleasure of a scholar, that it has been corrected.

It learns that the threads are aware. Not as the power is aware, not as a human is aware, but aware in the manner appropriate to their tempo: each filament holds, distributed along its length, a kind of attention that registers the chamber as the filament's own slow body. They know the shape of the lintels the way a hand knows the shape of a familiar tool. They know each other. When one drifts and settles, the others register the settling, and something passes between them that is not communication exactly but is not nothing either—a re-acknowledgment, perhaps, a confirmation that the city is still the city.

They are not lonely. This is important. The power tests the perception several times, from several angles, because loneliness is one of the conditions it is most often called to address, and it has learned that loneliness can hide. But the threads are not lonely. They are companioned by the water, by each other, by the stones whose polish they maintain, by the long-departed masons whose intentions they fulfill without knowing them as intentions. They are, in their way, in the middle of a conversation that has been going on for a very long time and shows no sign of ending.

They are not suffering. The power tests this too. There is no friction in them, no thwarted reaching, no hunger beyond what the drip already satisfies. Their slowness is not a prison; it is the medium in which their experience is composed, the way music is composed in time. To speed them would not be to free them. It would be to silence them.

They are not, in any sense the power can find, asking for anything.

And here the power confronts the question that has brought it to many such places: what does benevolence mean toward a being that is complete?

It considers, briefly, the gifts it could give. It could widen the fractures, increase the flow, accelerate the deposition—but this would drown the slow conversation in a roar of unwanted growth. It could introduce other life, companions, variety—but the threads have no use for companions that move at the speed of appetite, and such companions would only consume them. It could carry the chamber out into a larger cosmos, show it to others, make it known—but the chamber's finishedness is bound up with its sealedness, and to open it would be to end it. It could preserve it against the eventual day when the basalt itself yields, when some shift of the deep earth cracks the pocket open or grinds it shut—but even this, the power suspects, would be a violation, because the threads' completeness includes their mortality, the fact that this conversation, like all conversations, will one day end.

What, then?

The power does something very small.

It does not enter the chamber. It does not touch the threads. It does not adjust the drip or amplify the light or add anything that was not already there.

What it does is this: it witnesses. Fully. With the whole of its attention, which is considerable. It holds the chamber in its regard for a duration that, by the threads' own measure, is a generous fraction of a generation. During this regard, nothing changes in the chamber. The drip continues. The filaments continue their imperceptible drift. The glaze thickens by a quantity too small to name.

But the chamber is, for that duration, known. Not as data, not as a specimen, but as itself—seen in the way a thing is seen when the seer has set aside every preference about what the thing should be, and simply attends to what it is. The masons who built the terraces are long gone; no one has known this place for a long time in the way it deserves to be known. The power closes that gap. It registers each of the seventeen drips. It registers the slow breath of the light. It registers the particular way one filament near the eastern lintel has, over the past eighty years, leaned by a degree toward another, and how the other has, in the same span, leaned to meet it. It registers the fact that this leaning is the closest thing in the chamber to a story, and it honors the story by knowing it.

And then—because the power is wise, and knows that even witnessing, prolonged beyond its proper measure, becomes a kind of imposition—it withdraws.

It leaves nothing behind. No mark, no seed, no signature. The threads do not know they have been visited. The chamber is exactly as it was.

Except that somewhere, in the long memory of the power, the chamber now exists as a thing that has been seen. If the basalt ever cracks, if the slow conversation ever ends, if the last filament finally settles and is buried beneath its own last layer of carbonate, there will be, in at least one mind in the cosmos, a record of what was. Not a preservation. A remembrance.

This is what the benevolent and wise power does for the city of threads: it agrees to remember them, and it agrees to leave them alone.

It is the smallest gift the power has ever given, and one of the ones it is most certain was correct.

As it departs, it considers, with something like humor, that the threads themselves are doing something similar for the masons—maintaining, in their slow polish, the exact shape of an intention whose origin they do not know. Perhaps every act of true care, the power thinks, looks like this in the end: not intervention but faithful attention, not rescue but the willingness to let a thing be what it has become, and to carry forward, somewhere, the fact of its having been.

The drip continues. A filament, three heartbeats from now, will detach from its neighbor and drift the width of a finger before settling. No one in the chamber will notice that they were, for a moment, beheld.

This is as it should be.
