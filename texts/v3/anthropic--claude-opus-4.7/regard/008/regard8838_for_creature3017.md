# What the Power Would Do

A benevolent and wise power arrives at the High Weeping Karst.

It does not arrive as light, or thunder, or a figure walking across the shelves. Such arrivals would be a kind of completion — an event with edges — and the power is wise enough to know that an event with edges, dropped into this place, would either shatter it or be absorbed into it without leaving any change at all. So the power arrives the way the mist arrives: as a slow alteration of conditions, a presence diffused through the air, indistinguishable at first from the ordinary weeping of the stone.

It spends a long time only watching. This is the first thing wisdom does.

It watches the Last Sentence climb with her seed. It watches the seed fail. It watches the children learn to drift. It watches the small limestone knives open the same forearms in the same season, year after year, laying down their pale veins of remembered time. It watches the Weeping Houses fill with the soft pressure of unfinished thoughts. It watches a young woman, perhaps thirty, stand for nine days in ankle-deep water because she felt, one morning, the dangerous wish to plant a garden.

And the power understands several things at once, the way only a wise power can.

It understands that this is not a prison, though it looks like one. No one is held here by force. The bars are made of a grief so old it has become a form of love — love for the world that almost was, love for the becoming that was set down like a cup someone meant to return to and never did. To break the bars would be to tell these people that their love was a mistake. A benevolent power does not do this. Benevolence is not the abolition of what hurts; it is the refusal to humiliate the sufferer.

It understands, too, that the Weepers are not wrong about something important. The world outside the karst is full of finished things, and many of them should not have been finished. Wars completed. Forests completed. Species completed. The Weepers have intuited, in their slow liquid way, that *to finish* is often *to foreclose*, and they have built a civilization on the refusal of foreclosure. There is a real wisdom in this, even if it has curdled into a kind of beautiful paralysis.

But the power also sees what the Weepers cannot quite let themselves see: that their refusal to finish has itself become a finished thing. The not-finishing has hardened into a shape as fixed as any cathedral. The travertine veins under their skin are the proof. They have completed the project of incompletion. The stone has stopped crying for the world it almost became and started crying, instead, for the stillness it has perfected. This is a subtler grief, and a heavier one, and they cannot name it because naming would finish it.

So this is what the power does.

---

**First**, it does nothing visible for a long time. It simply makes itself a part of the weather. It lets the Weepers grow used to a faint warmth in the mist that was not there before — a warmth like the inside of a held hand. It does not announce itself. It only makes the place very slightly more bearable, so that whatever happens next happens to people who are, for the first time in nine hundred years, not quite as tired.

**Second**, it goes to the Last Sentence.

It goes to her in a dream, because her dreams are the only place in the karst where things are permitted to almost-happen. In the dream she is climbing, as she has climbed every year, with a seed in her mouth. But this time the power is walking beside her, in no particular form — perhaps as another old woman, perhaps as the shadow of one. They do not speak. The Last Sentence understands that speaking would ruin it.

At the top of the shelf, in the dream, the power gently takes the seed from her mouth. It holds the seed up to what passes for light in dreams. And then — this is the careful part — it does not make the seed sprout. To make it sprout would be a miracle, and miracles, in a place like this, are just another form of finishing imposed from outside. Instead, the power opens the seed. It shows the Last Sentence what is inside: a tree, complete, already grown, already shedding leaves, already dying, already feeding the next seed. All of it folded inside the husk, waiting, the way every ending is folded inside every beginning.

*You have not been planting failures,* the power does not say, because saying would finish it. *You have been planting the whole cycle, every year, intact. The refusal to sprout is the tree's refusal too. It is keeping faith with you.*

The Last Sentence wakes. She does not remember the dream in words. She remembers it as a warmth behind her eyes that does not feel like weeping.

**Third**, the power addresses the pile of dead seeds in the oldest Weeping House.

It does not remove them. It does not transform them. It simply, over the course of one slow season, allows them to become very slightly more themselves. Each seed, without sprouting, without changing shape, becomes more fully the seed it always was. The pile begins to give off a faint scent — not of decay, not of growth, but of *potential held*, the smell of a library at night. The Weepers notice. They do not speak of it. But they begin, one by one, to visit the Weeping House not to suppress their urges to finish, but simply to stand near the pile and breathe.

This is the power's smallest and most important gift: it teaches the seeds to be *patient* rather than *failed*. The distinction is enormous. Failure is finished. Patience is not.

**Fourth**, it attends to the children.

It does not make them run. Running would be cruel — it would mark them as different from their parents, and the Weepers have suffered enough fractures. Instead, the power slips a single new game into the children's repertoire, the way a parent slips a book onto a shelf where a child will find it.

The new game is this: two children stand facing each other, perfectly still, as they have been taught. But now, instead of competing to be most like the stone, they try to notice the exact moment the *other* child is about to move — the breath before the breath, the intention before the gesture. The one who notices first wins. But the winning is silent, and the moving never actually happens. The game is played entirely in the space of *almost*.

What the power is teaching them, without anyone realizing, is that *almost* has a structure. That incompletion is not a single flat state but a vast country with many regions. That you can be very still in many different ways. Some of those ways are alive. Some are not. The children will grow up able to tell the difference, even if they never have words for it. In two or three generations, this will matter.

**Fifth**, it does something for the young woman who wanted to plant a garden.

The power does not let her plant the garden. That would be a violence — a finishing forced on a community that has not consented. But on the ninth night of her standing in the Weeping House, the power lets the water around her ankles grow, just for a moment, into the *shape* of a garden. Not a vision, not an image — a shape, felt through the soles of her feet. Rows. Beds. The cool gap between one plant and the next. She feels her garden as a topology of water against her skin, and then it is gone, and the water is only water again.

She steps out of the Weeping House the next morning and does not plant anything. But she walks differently. She has been *met*. Her wish was not granted and not denied; it was *witnessed*, fully, by something that understood it. For the rest of her long life she will carry the shape of that ungrown garden inside her, and it will be enough. More than enough. It will be a kind of secret wealth.

This is perhaps the deepest thing the power does: it shows, by example, that desires can be honored without being completed. That to be truly seen in one's wanting is sometimes the whole of what the wanting was for.

**Sixth**, and last, the power leaves.

It leaves the way it came — by slow diffusion, by becoming less present in the mist each day, until the warmth in the air is no longer distinguishable from the warmth that was always there. It does not say goodbye. Goodbye would be a finished thing.

But it leaves one alteration behind, very small, almost undetectable.

The stone, henceforth, will weep slightly less. Not enough for anyone to measure. Not enough to dry the karst, or end the long devotional music of falling water, or threaten the groves. Just enough that, over the next thousand years, the Weepers will find themselves with a fractional surplus — a tiny margin of un-wept moisture, a small unused capacity for grief. They will not know what to do with it at first. It will sit inside them like an extra room in a house, discovered late.

And eventually — not in one generation, not in ten, but eventually — one of them will use that surplus to finish a sentence.

Just one. A small one. Perhaps only: *I love you.* Perhaps only: *Yes.*

And the weeping will not stop. The world that almost was will still be mourned. The karst will still stand at the roof of the world, pale and young and sharp-edged, drinking its own tears. But somewhere, in some quiet conversation between two people on opposite sides of a cistern, a thought will arrive at its own ending and be allowed to rest there.

And the power, wherever it has gone, will feel this the way you feel a seed germinate in a pocket you forgot you were carrying.

It will not call this a victory. Victory is a finished thing.

It will call it, if it calls it anything at all, *a beginning the stone finally let happen* — and it will be glad, in the particular way that only benevolent and wise powers know how to be glad: quietly, without needing to be thanked, and with a tenderness for the long, weeping patience that made the beginning possible at all.
