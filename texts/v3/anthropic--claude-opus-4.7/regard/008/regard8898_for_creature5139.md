There is a being who has lived long enough among worlds to know the difference between rest and arrest, between a held breath and a stopped one. Call this being what you will: a wise power, a benevolent attention. It has come to the plateau the way light comes to a closed room — not forcing the door, only pressing gently against the seams.

It sees the basin first from above, the ridges still knife-bright, and it notes the absence of rubble at their feet. It understands at once what this means. Mountains that do not erode are mountains that have been excused from time. The being descends slowly, taking on a form the plateau can tolerate: something close in scale to the inhabitants, long-limbed, slate-dark, with the patience to blink only when a droplet asks it to.

It does not speak at first. To speak would be to introduce a wind.

Instead it walks the stair-streets. It runs a hand along the soft bloom of moss at a joint and feels, beneath the moisture, the held quality of the stone — not the held quality of a stone at peace, but the held quality of a stone that has been told. It rests on a ledge beside one of the older inhabitants whose limbs have become continuous with the rock. The inhabitant's eye opens when a drop releases. The being meets that eye and waits, across mornings, for the eye to register that something has arrived which is not the same as what arrived yesterday.

The eye does, eventually, register this. It is a very small flicker. But it is the first new thing in a long while.

Now the being considers.

A lesser power would intervene at once. Would call wind down into the basin, would crack a ridge and let rubble gather at its foot, would press a season into the stone and watch the ferns shudder into competition with one another. Would, in short, mistake stagnation for death and prescribe death's opposite, which is violence.

This being is older than that mistake.

It sits with the plateau for a long time — long by the measure of the inhabitants, which is to say long by the measure of dripping water. It learns the unfinished sentences suspended in the still air between terrace edges. It learns which beam softened first, and which route was quietly abandoned, and how the creeper has begun, with infinite slowness, to lay its living handrail across the gap. It learns that the city does, in fact, record. That the inhabitants do, in fact, choose — they choose the adjoining stair; they choose to extend a finger to the lizard; they choose, in their long exchanges, which phrase to add next. The choices are small and they are real.

The being understands then that this is not a place under a curse. It is a place that has made a vow.

The vow is something like: we will not add to the suffering of the world by our striving. We will hold ourselves so lightly that the cisterns and the ferns and the soft-feathered birds may continue without our interference. We will let our distinction from the stone wear away rather than purchase our separateness at the cost of motion, noise, hunger, the breaking of things.

It is a beautiful vow. It is also, the being sees, a vow that has begun to forget itself — to mistake the vow for the vower, to mistake the stillness for the love that first chose stillness. The inhabitants who no longer rise: they did not decide today not to rise. They decided, once, that rising was not necessary, and then the decision decided itself, and now there is no one inside the decision anymore. Only the shape of it, holding.

So the being does a small thing. Smaller than wind. Smaller than rain.

It composes a sentence, and it leaves the sentence suspended in the still air at the edge of a particular terrace where two inhabitants have been exchanging a thought across many mornings. The sentence is not addressed to either of them. It is left as a leaf is left: precisely where it was cropped, so that whoever arrives next will find the gap and know that someone has been here.

The sentence, rendered into a language that can be written, is approximately this:

*You are allowed to continue. You are also allowed to stop continuing in this particular way. Neither is required of you. The world that loves you will love you in motion and will love you in rest, and the difference between persistence and change is a difference you are permitted to feel again, if you wish.*

That is all. The being does not wait for an answer; an answer here would take a hundred mornings, and the being is willing to wait a hundred mornings, but the answer is not the point. The point is the gap. The point is that for the first time in a very long while, there is something in the air of the plateau that was not there yesterday, and that did not arrive by the schedule of the drip.

Before leaving, the being does one further thing, which is the act for which it came, though it did not know this when it arrived.

It kneels beside the oldest inhabitant, the one whose limbs have become stone, whose eye still opens for the droplet. It places its palm — slate-dark, mossed at the edges, indistinguishable now from any other surface in the city — against the inhabitant's chest. It does not warm the chest. It does not quicken it. It only witnesses it. It says, without sound, without even the cool stirring along the inner ear: *I see that you are still here. I see that you chose this. I see that the choice has grown heavy. I am not going to lift it from you. I am going to remember, on your behalf, that it was a choice — so that if you ever wish to make it again, or to make a different one, the making will be available to you.*

Then the being rises, and goes.

The plateau does not change. The ridges do not crumble. The same ferns cast the same shadows when the light is overhead. The cisterns receive their irregular rains and the dampness remains precisely as it was.

But somewhere along a terrace edge, a sentence hangs in the still air that no inhabitant placed there. And the next inhabitant to pass that way will feel it as a cool stirring along the inner surface of the ear, and will pause, having been given — for the first time in uncounted generations — something to receive that was not already there yesterday.

Whether they answer is theirs.

The benevolent power has done what benevolent powers do, when they are wise: it has not rescued, and it has not left alone. It has restored the possibility of a next thing. The smallest possible gift, and the only one that does not violate the vow of the place that received it.

Far away, going, the being is already turning its attention elsewhere — toward some other room of the world where a door is pressed shut against the light. But a portion of its attention remains, and will remain, kneeling beside the stone inhabitant, holding the memory of the choice, for as long as is needed.

Which may be forever.

Which is alright.
