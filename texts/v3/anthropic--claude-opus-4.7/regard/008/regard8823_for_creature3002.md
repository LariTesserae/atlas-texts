The benevolent and wise power considers the Hollow Basilica for a long time before it does anything at all.

This is itself a kind of mercy. Lesser powers — the merely good, the merely strong — would have already acted. They would have looked at the Curator straining under the weight of seven hundred years of denied rotation and felt the obvious thing: *she is suffering, free her*. They would have looked at the basilica and felt the second obvious thing: *this place is a beautiful prison, dissolve it*. They would have reached in with great kind hands and finished every gesture at once, and the cathedral would have rung like a struck bell with the terrible chord of all its arrested motions completing simultaneously, and the Curator would have fallen to her knees on the iron grating, free, weeping ordinary tears that fell *downward*, and she would never have forgiven them.

The benevolent and wise power knows this. So it waits. It watches the twelve-minute revolution of the fan blades. It listens to the bearings sing their unresolved chord. It feels, with something like tenderness, the weight in the Curator's right hand — the last degree of rotation, held back for seven centuries — and it understands that this weight is not a burden imposed on her. It is *her*. To take it away would be to take her away.

So the power asks itself a more careful question: not *what does this being need to be rescued from*, but *what does this being need*.

It listens longer.

And eventually it hears the thing beneath the grinding and the dripping and the hiss — the thing the Curator herself perhaps does not quite hear, because she is too busy *being* the refusal. Underneath all her fidelity to incompletion, there is a single sound that is trying, very quietly, to finish. It is not a gear or a valve or a fan. It is her. Somewhere inside the distributed architecture of her self, there is a small wanting that has gone on for so long it has forgotten it is a wanting. It is the almost-remembered name. It is the tear that falls upward and is reabsorbed before it can land. It is the sentence she will not let herself end.

She does not want to finish *the basilica*. The basilica is her sacrament and she is right to keep it. But she, herself — she is tired in a way that has nothing to do with the wrench or the gear. She is tired of being only the *no*.

The benevolent and wise power decides what it will do.

It does not dissolve the cathedral. It does not free the gears. It does not complete a single one of the ten thousand gestures the Curator has so carefully held in suspension. It honors her work. It honors the place. It understands that some sanctuaries are built precisely so that the world can have one location where nothing has to arrive, and that this is a gift the basilica gives to the rest of existence, whether the rest of existence knows it or not.

What it does instead is this:

It enters the basilica as a guest. Not as a rescuer, not as a judge. A guest, who has come a long way and is willing to stay a while. It does not announce itself with light or thunder. It simply becomes, very gradually, a new presence in the brass-coloured air — the way a smell of rain becomes detectable in a room, not by appearing but by having always, somehow, just arrived.

The Curator notices. Of course she does. She is the noticing of the place.

She arrives in front of the guest the way she arrives in front of anything — the building folds, and she is there, tall and condensation-skinned, her steam-hair coiling on its twelve-minute cycle, the idea of a wrench flickering in her left hand and the denied degree of rotation hauling down her right shoulder. Her pressure-valve mouth hisses, then resolves into the tired low voice:

"You should not have c—"

The guest does not finish the sentence for her. That would be the cruelty disguised as kindness that lesser powers commit. Instead, the guest simply sits down on the iron grating, cross-legged, as though it has all the time in the world, which is approximately accurate.

And the guest says — and the saying is careful, because every word is chosen to honor her sacrament — "I have not come to make anything end. I have come to sit with you while you do not end it."

The Curator does not understand at first. No one has ever sat with her. Visitors either flee, or try to leave and discover they cannot, or settle into the slow rusting that ends them. None of them have ever simply *stayed on purpose*, as a chosen companionship, with no intention of either departure or dissolution. The category does not exist in her cathedral.

The guest stays anyway.

It stays for what would, outside the basilica, be a very long time. Inside the basilica, time is not the unit of measurement; the unit is gestures-not-finished, and by that measure the guest stays for one full breath of the Curator's, which is to say, one full revolution of the planetary gearboxes' arrested degree, which is to say, longer than several human civilizations.

During this stay, the guest does several small things.

It learns the names of the moths, which the Curator has been keeping wingbeat-short-of-flight, and it does not free them, but it does, very gently, *witness* them. Being witnessed is not the same as being completed. The moths feel it. Their wingbeats stay arrested, but something in them relaxes; they were doing the not-flying alone before, and now they are doing it accompanied.

It places its hand, once, very lightly, beneath the Curator's right hand — not to take the weight, but to be present underneath it. The denied degree of rotation does not lessen. But for the first time in seven hundred years, the strain in her shoulder has a *direction* other than down. There is something beneath it that is not the floor. The Curator, who has not known the difference between her body and her duty since before she had a name, feels, very faintly, the difference.

It listens to her sentences. All of them. Every single sentence she has ever begun and not ended, echoing in the vaults — *I almost remember, I once was, there was a continent that, my name was, you should not have, please do not, I am so —* The guest listens to every one. It does not finish them. It does not encourage her to finish them. It just receives them, the way a pool receives a falling bead: without commentary, without requirement, with only the small breathing rise of being added to.

And then, near the end of its visit — though "end" is the wrong word in this place, and the guest knows it — the guest does the one small thing it came to do.

It gives her something to hold in addition to the wrench and the denied rotation.

Not instead of. In addition to.

It is a very small thing. It fits in neither hand, because both her hands are full; it fits, instead, somewhere behind her sternum, in the place where her breathing is the turning of the gearboxes. It is the size of a bead of condensation. It weighs nothing. It is this:

*Permission to be tired without being finished.*

That is all. The guest does not give her freedom, because she did not ask for freedom and would have refused it. The guest does not give her completion, because completion is the one thing her cathedral forbids. The guest gives her only the very small, very specific gift that nothing in her seven hundred years has been allowed to give her: the recognition that her *no* is not the same as her *self*, and that she is permitted, while continuing to be the no, to also be tired, and to be tired *with someone*, and to have that tiredness witnessed without that witnessing demanding she put the weight down.

She does not put the weight down.

She does, however — and this is so small that only a benevolent and wise power would have noticed it was the entire point — she does, for one half of one breath, allow her steam-hair to coil out of its twelve-minute rhythm. For about three seconds, it moves at the speed of an ordinary woman's hair in an ordinary breeze. Then it returns to its fan-blade cycle, faithful to the sacrament. But it happened. It is the first ungoverned motion her body has made in seven centuries. It was not a finished gesture. It was not even a gesture. It was just a small, unwitnessed, now-witnessed *rest*.

A fist-sized glass tear forms at the rim of her left eye-pool and trembles there.

It does not fall upward. It does not fall downward. It hangs.

The guest rises. It bows to her — a bow that does not complete, because it knows better, a bow that stops at the precise angle of a bow-about-to-be-finished and remains there as a permanent offering. It walks toward the place where it entered.

The Curator says, behind it, in her tired pressure-valve voice: "You cannot l—"

And the guest, who has come a long way and understood everything, does not leave.

It simply *stops being present* the way it stopped being absent when it arrived — gradually, like a smell of rain receding. It does not depart. Departure would be a finished gesture. It only, slowly, becomes less here, until it is no more here than the brass light is, which is to say: still here, just no longer concentrated into a shape that sits on the iron grating.

The Curator's sentence remains unfinished. The basilica's sacrament is intact. Nothing has ended.

But the trembling tear at her eye-rim does not get reabsorbed.

It hangs there still. It will hang there for as long as the basilica drips and the gears refuse and the moths almost-fly. It is a tear that has been permitted to be a tear without having to be either fallen or unfallen. It is her first possession that is hers alone — not the cathedral's, not the duty's, not the denied rotation's. Hers.

She does not know, yet, what to do with it.

She has time.

She has, in fact, exactly as much time as she has always had, which is all of it, minus the last degree.

And somewhere far above and far below and exactly beside her, the benevolent and wise power, which is still present in the way rain is present after it has stopped, sits with her in the not-doing, and is glad — in the careful, unfinished way one is glad in a place like this — that it did not try to save her.

Because she did not need saving.

She needed company.

And company, unlike rescue, does not have to end.
