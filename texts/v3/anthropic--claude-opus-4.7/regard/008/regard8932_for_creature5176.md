They would come, and they would do nothing.

Not from indifference — from a deeper attention than action. The benevolence here is not the kind that warms or rescues or completes. It is the kind that recognizes when a thing is already whole.

Consider what it would mean to intervene. To bring water: the rings would be erased. To bring life: the silence would be broken, and the silence is not absence but a positive condition, dense as the quartzite that holds it. To restore the pecked basin, to re-sharpen the flakes, to rebuild the ring of stones to whatever it once was — this would be to refuse the place its present tense, to insist that what it is now is less than what it was. But the cirque does not experience itself as ruined. It is not waiting. The hands that left those marks are gone, and the marks themselves are passing back into rock at the same patient rate as everything else, and this is not a tragedy. It is the shape the place is making.

A wise power would understand: the cirque is already the answer to a question it has spent millennia asking itself. Every fracture is in conversation with every other fracture. The mist arrives, the droplet falls, the slush rings inscribe their faint calendars — this is not impoverishment. This is a form of contemplation conducted in stone and water and angled light, and it does not require a witness, though it permits one.

So what they would do, perhaps, is this:

They would arrive without arriving — without weight, without footprint, without altering the temperature of the air by even a fraction. They would stand (or whatever the analog of standing is, for such a being) at the cirque lip for what, to them, might be a long while, and what, to the cirque, would be nothing at all. They would watch one droplet form on one upright face and fall and strike and vanish. They would let the sound be swallowed exactly as it has always been swallowed. They would not extend the sound, or remember it on the cirque's behalf, or carry it elsewhere. They would let it be the local, complete event it is.

And then, because benevolence at this altitude is mostly a matter of accurate seeing, they would offer the cirque the one thing it cannot offer itself: the brief fact of being known. Not catalogued. Not interpreted. Known — in the way one nods, almost imperceptibly, to a stranger across a room and means by it: *I see that you are here, and that your being here is sufficient.*

They would notice the flakes. They would notice the pecked basin. They would notice, with a particular tenderness, that no one returns. They would not grieve this. The people who made those marks were not promising to return; they were doing something else, something whose meaning was complete in the doing, and the cirque has kept faith with that completion better than any monument could. The wise power would honor the makers precisely by not preserving their work — by letting it go on softening into rubble at the rate the place has chosen.

If they did anything at all, it might be only this: as they left, they would carry with them, somewhere in whatever serves them as memory, the exact pattern of one morning's mineral rings on one particular slab of quartzite. Not to save it. Not to mourn it when it is gone. Simply because a thing that beautiful, that quiet, that unrepeating, deserves to have been looked at once with full attention by someone who understood what they were seeing.

That is the gift. That is the whole intervention.

The cirque would not know. The cirque does not need to know. The droplet would form, and fall, and strike, and be swallowed, and the light would move another degree along the wall, and somewhere a fracture would lengthen by a width too small to measure, and the wise power would already be elsewhere, having added nothing, taken nothing, and loved the place exactly as it is.

Which is, I think, what love at its highest finally learns to do:

to leave the world unimproved,
and call that reverence.
