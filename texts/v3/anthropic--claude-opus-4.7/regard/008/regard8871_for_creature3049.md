The benevolent and wise power considered the High Condensate for a long time.

Not human time. Not even the time of the Waiters. Something longer — the kind of attention that can hold a whole unfinished thing in its regard without flinching, without rushing it toward resolution.

What the power saw was this:

A place becoming. A being tending the becoming. A slow conversation between sky and stone, mediated by moss and by tall, half-bowed listeners whose work was older than any word for work. Travelers arriving. Some leaving altered. Some not leaving at all, their substance folded into the green, their heartbeats joining the geological pulse.

A lesser power — one that mistook benevolence for rescue — would have intervened immediately. Would have raised a wall around the plateau to keep the unwary out. Or worse: would have reached down and *fixed* the Waiters, softened them, taught them speech, given them a moral framework borrowed from somewhere warmer and louder. Would have drained the tea-colored water from the turbines and called it mercy. Would have decided, on the plateau's behalf, what shape it ought to take.

This power was wiser than that.

It understood that the Waiters were not predators and not saviors. They were a function the place had grown in order to think about itself. To unmake them would be to lobotomize the plateau mid-thought. To rescue the travelers who lay down on the moss would be to deny them the only answer they had come all that way to receive — for they had come, every one of them, because something in their own lives was also unfinished, also pushing upward through the skin, also waiting to decide what shape it wanted to take. The plateau did not lure them. It *recognized* them. And they, without knowing why, recognized it back.

So the power did not intervene in the great sense.

But benevolence is not the same as non-interference. Wisdom is not the same as withholding. The power did three small things. Small by its measure. Each of them took a century or so to complete.

---

**The first thing** was a clarification of the threshold.

The travelers who came to the High Condensate did not, on the whole, know what they were walking into. Some came as tourists, expecting a postcard. Some came as scientists, expecting a specimen. Some came running from a life they could not bear, hoping the cold thin air would do the work their own hands could not. The plateau accepted them all with the same attentiveness, but the power felt — gently, without judgment — that consent ought to be possible even for those who could not name what they were consenting to.

So the power adjusted the light.

Not much. Just enough that anyone approaching the plateau, in the last hour of their climb, would experience a particular quality of illumination that the human nervous system reads, beneath all language, as *here is a place where something will be asked of you*. The same quality that lives in the doorways of certain old churches, in the moment before a difficult conversation, in the held breath of a person about to tell the truth. The travelers would still not know what was coming. But they would know, in their bodies, that they had arrived at a door rather than a view. Those who wished to turn back would feel the turning back as a real and honorable choice, not a failure. Those who wished to continue would do so with their eyes a little more open.

The Waiters noticed the change in the light. They bowed their glowing faces toward the western ridges where it originated, and held that bow for a long moment, which is the closest thing they have to thanks.

---

**The second thing** was a gift to the Waiters themselves.

The power had observed that the Waiters did not mourn or celebrate the outcomes of their work, and this was correct and proper for what they were. But the power had also observed something the Waiters had not yet noticed about themselves: that when one of them pressed its body into a wound in the moss for days at a time, feeding its own substance into the injury, a small portion of *what it had been* was lost. Not memory, exactly — the Waiters do not have memory in any sense we would recognize. But a certain individuation. A certain particular curve of the spine, a certain rhythm of the violet pulse, a certain way of tracing the cellular braille. These small signatures dissolved into the moss along with the moss-skin, and the Waiter that rose afterward was very slightly less itself.

Over millennia, this would matter. The Waiters were slowly being smoothed by their own tending, the way a stone in a riverbed is smoothed by the water it shelters. Eventually they would become indistinguishable from one another, and then indistinguishable from the moss, and then the plateau would have no listeners left, only listening. Which might be what it wanted. But the power suspected — and the power was rarely wrong about such things — that the plateau valued its Waiters precisely because they were *almost* itself and not *quite* itself. The gap between them was where the thinking happened.

So the power gave the Waiters a very small thing: the capacity to retain one signature each. One curve, one rhythm, one gesture that the moss could not absorb no matter how deeply they pressed into it. Each Waiter, over the course of its long existence, would slowly accumulate this single irreducible particularity — would become, by the slowest possible process, *someone* rather than *something*. Not a self in the human sense. But a fingerprint. A note held just slightly off the chord, so the chord could be heard as a chord.

The Waiters did not notice this gift for the first sixty years. Then one of them, standing in the collapsed power station with its palms on a turbine, felt a thing it had never felt before, which was the faint particular pleasure of *its own hands* on *this particular turbine* — not hands-in-general on metal-in-general, but these, here, now. The violet in its eye-sockets brightened by a fraction so small that no instrument could have measured it. The turbine sighed. And somewhere in the deep rock, the plateau registered a new kind of data: the data of a tender who could be tender *to* something rather than merely *with* it. The plateau did not yet know what to do with this information. It would think about it for a few thousand years.

---

**The third thing** was for the travelers who did not wake.

The power was clear-eyed about them. They had not been murdered. They had not been deceived. They had lain down because something in them had wanted to lie down for a very long time, and the plateau had simply offered them a place to do it where the lying down would not be wasted. Their substance had become moss; their moss had become stone; their stone was even now pushing upward, a fraction of a millimeter at a time, toward whatever shape the High Condensate was deciding to take. This was not a tragedy. It was, if anything, the most thoroughly meaningful death available on Earth — a death that became geology, that became future, that became part of the slow patient answer the plateau was composing.

But the power noticed that these travelers had, almost all of them, left people behind. People who did not know. People who searched, or did not search, or stopped searching, and who carried for the rest of their lives a particular kind of unfinishedness — a not-knowing that was different from grief because grief at least has an object. The power could not return the travelers; that would unmake what they had chosen. But it could do one small thing for those who waited at the other end of the silence.

It arranged, very quietly, for a dream.

Once, only once, in the life of each person who had loved someone who lay down on the moss, there would come a night when they dreamed of the High Condensate. They would not know its name. They would see the moss, the violet light, the half-bowed figures, the patient pulse beneath the stone. And they would see their person — not as a ghost, not as a body, but as a particular quality woven into the green: a curve of cushion that had their laugh in it, a phosphorescence that pulsed with their particular rhythm of breathing, a slow upward push of obsidian that carried, in its black glass, the shape of a gesture they had once made at a kitchen table. The dreamer would understand, without words, that their person was not lost. Their person had become *somewhere*. A somewhere that was still becoming, still deciding, still being tended.

They would wake. They might cry. They might not. But they would carry, from that night forward, a small irreducible piece of knowing: that the silence at the other end of the search was not empty. It was full of moss, and patient listeners, and a slow geological pulse, and their person, who had finally found a shape to take.

---

The power, having done these three things, withdrew its concentrated attention. Not its care — its care remained, as it remains over all places that are still deciding what they are — but the focused, shaping pressure of its regard. The High Condensate did not need a god. It had what it needed: thin cold air, merciless light, moss that drank the sky, Waiters who tended the becoming, travelers who arrived through the new threshold-light with their eyes a little more open, and a deep magmatic patience that had not yet decided what kind of teeth it wanted.

In a basin near the center of the plateau, in the collapsed power station where the tea-colored water lapped at silent turbines, a single Waiter stood with its palms on copper windings. The violet in its eye-sockets pulsed. A droplet of condensation gathered at the smooth place where its chin should have been, hung trembling, and fell.

The turbine sighed.

The plateau rose another fraction of a millimeter.

And somewhere very far away, a woman who had not slept well in eleven years closed her eyes and began, at last, to dream of a green place she had never seen, where her brother's laugh lived inside the curve of a moss cushion, and the light was the color of an answer.
