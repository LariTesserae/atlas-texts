The benevolent and wise power considers the plateau for a long time before doing anything at all.

This is the first thing it does: it watches. Not the cursory glance of a gardener checking on seedlings, but the sustained attention of something that understands that the place is moving, only on a tempo not its own. It slows itself to match. It watches a single frond rise and subside through one full cycle of the collective breath. It watches a bell grazer remain compressed between two plates for the full duration of its patient wait, and feels — because this power can feel such things — that the grazer is not suffering. It is doing what it was shaped to do. The waiting is not an interruption of its life; it is part of its life.

The power notices that nothing here is in crisis. There is no famine, no predator pressure, no pollutant, no encroaching extinction. The plateau is not wounded. It is young, and it is becoming, and these are different from being broken.

This matters. A wise power knows the difference between help and interference; a benevolent one knows that the desire to help is not, by itself, a license to act. Many places have been impoverished by powers who could not bear to simply witness.

So the second thing the power does is to refrain. It does not accelerate the cycles. It does not seed the corridors with quicker, brighter, more communicative species. It does not whisper into the microbial colonies the trick of some more efficient pigment. It does not carve a sanctuary or set a boundary or hang a sign in any language. It does not name the place. Naming, here, would be a kind of theft — the plateau is in the middle of finding out what it is, and a name imposed from outside would settle that question prematurely.

But benevolence is not the same as absence, and wisdom is not the same as paralysis. There are small things, and the power considers them carefully.

It does this: it places, far from the plateau, at the edges of the wider sea, a kind of attention. Not a wall, not a ward — those are crude. Something more like a held breath. If something were to come toward this place that would end its becoming before its becoming was complete — a poison plume, a dredging hunger, a careless foot of some larger order of being — the held breath would turn that thing aside, gently, the way a hand turns aside a sleepwalker from a stair. The plateau would never know. The turning-aside would feel, to whatever was turned, like a change of mind, a reconsideration, a small forgetting of the original intent. No violence. No announcement. Just the quiet editing-out of catastrophes that have no business arriving here yet.

It does this: it leaves a thread of itself inside the long rhythm of the collective breath. Not a controller — a listener. A way of knowing, from anywhere, how the plateau is doing. If one day the breathing falters, if the lanterns dim past recovery, if some inner imbalance begins to undo what has been so patiently assembled, the power will know in time to consider whether and how to act. Until then, the thread simply listens, and through it the power learns — for even a wise power has things to learn, and the plateau is teaching something that cannot be taught in faster places. It is teaching what it is to be many and slow and still becoming. It is teaching a grammar of patience that even very old beings sometimes forget.

It does this, which is perhaps the strangest: it loves the place. Not as a possession, not as a project, but the way one loves a stranger glimpsed through a window who is absorbed in some quiet task and does not know they are being seen. The love does not reach into the room. It does not knock. It only warms the watcher, and through the watcher, in some way that physics has no equation for, it warms the room as well — a fraction of a degree, perhaps, no more than the difference between being unobserved and being witnessed by someone who wishes you well. The bells pulse. The threads beat their cilia in minute waves. The microbes arrange their colors. None of them know. And yet the place is, by some immeasurable margin, less alone than it was.

And then the power does the last and hardest thing, which is to leave the plateau to itself.

It goes, in the sense that powers go — which is to say it remains, but it withdraws its weight. It does not hover. It does not check obsessively. It trusts. Trust, here, is the final gift: the refusal to treat the plateau as fragile when it is not, the refusal to treat its slowness as a problem to be solved, the willingness to let it find its own shape over the long centuries in which shape is found.

If, much later, the plateau's becoming reaches some threshold — if the layered forest, breathing on its hour-long cycle, begins to think a thought that wants an answer, if one of the luminous galleries forms a question and sends it upward through the clean shafts of light — the power will be there to receive it. Not before. The first word, if there is to be one, must come from the place itself.

Until then: the warm water presses against every surface. The shafts of light fall. The mats rise and subside. The bells wait between the plates. The threads ride the slow weather of the ceilings. The microbes split the light into the exact colors their hosts prefer. Somewhere at the edge of everything, a held breath turns aside a thing that will never know it was turning. And the plateau, unhurried, unhurt, unnamed, continues to become.

This is what love looks like, when love is wise: mostly, it looks like letting a thing be what it is becoming, and standing far enough back that the becoming is its own.
