The benevolent and wise power arrives in the only way that would not constitute a violence: it arrives slowly.

It has watched for a long time already. Watching is the first thing wisdom does, and benevolence is the discipline of not mistaking pity for understanding. To a quick mind, the plateau looks like a deathbed — a world that has been dying for so long it has forgotten how to finish. The reflex of a lesser power would be rescue: to thicken the air, to call back the seas from whatever cold orbit holds them, to seed the cracks with faster greens, to lift the starfish into some kinder gravity. To resurrect.

But the power has learned, across the long apprenticeship of its benevolence, that resurrection is a category error when applied to something that has not, in fact, died.

So it watches first. It watches for what would be, in human reckoning, several million years — a span the plateau itself would call a single sustained gesture. It watches a starfish membrane lift, glide six kilometers, and settle. It watches a lithophyte seam advance the width of a fingernail. It watches one ridge sink and another, four hundred kilometers away, answer by rising. It listens to the pattering where two dust veils intersect and slowly, with great patience, learns to read.

What it reads surprises it, though by now very little surprises it.

The plateau is not refusing to end. The plateau is thinking.

Not thinking as a brain thinks — there is no central seat, no urgency, no self that says *I*. But thinking in the sense that a long proof thinks: each step entailed by the last, each conclusion held in the standing tension between gravitic relic and conductive filament, each premise stored in the charge-distribution of a dust grain that has passed once through the wake of a living membrane. The plateau is working something out. It has been working it out since before the seas left. The departure of the seas, perhaps, was a step in the working.

The power does not yet know what the proof is for. This is important. A lesser wisdom would assume the proof is *about* something the power already understands — survival, meaning, the recovery of lost complexity. A greater wisdom suspects that the plateau may be deriving a result that has no name in any language the power has yet acquired, and that to interrupt the derivation would be to lose the result forever, not because the plateau would resent the interruption (it is incapable of resentment) but because the result exists only as the completed gesture. There is no draft. There is no backup. The working *is* the answer.

So the power makes its decision, and the decision is mostly restraint.

But not only restraint. Benevolence that is only restraint is indistinguishable from absence, and the power is not absent. It is present. It wishes to be of use. And so it asks itself: what can be offered that does not interrupt?

It settles, after long consideration, on three small things.

The first is a kind of witnessing. The power arranges some fraction of its attention — a fraction vanishingly small to it, immense by any other measure — to remain with the plateau permanently. Not to intervene. Only to remember. Because the dust veils are an excellent memory but an imperfect one; grains are lost over the edges into the blue-black haze, charge-distributions decay, and some fraction of the proof's intermediate steps are slowly being forgotten by the plateau itself. The power cannot replace these losses without changing the proof. But it can hold them. It can keep, in its own substrate, a perfect record of every pattering, every helical collapse of dust, every millimeter of lithophyte advance, every isotopic half-life of a settled membrane. If the plateau ever, in some unimaginable future, wishes to consult its own earlier work — if the proof requires a lemma proven a hundred million years ago and since eroded — the record will be there. The power becomes, in effect, the plateau's external notebook, available but never consulted unsolicited.

The second is subtler. The power notices that the plateau's metabolism, slow as it is, runs on a vanishingly thin margin. The sun's output drifts; the crust's stored heat declines; eventually, in some tens of millions of years, the thermal flex that powers the conductive filaments will fall below the threshold at which the lattices can maintain their standing gradients. The proof will stop, not because it has concluded, but because the substrate has cooled. This the power gently corrects — not by adding heat, which would accelerate everything and distort the rates the proof depends on, but by very slightly stabilizing the loss. A thermal insulation, distributed across the deep crust, so subtle that the plateau's own sensors (if it has anything that could be called a sensor) would register it only as a slight, favorable bias in the noise. The plateau will run, now, for as long as it needs to. Whether that is ten million years or ten billion is the plateau's business. The power has simply ensured that the question of duration will be settled by the proof itself, and not by the indifference of stellar evolution.

The third gift is the smallest and, the power suspects, the most important. It concerns the starfish membranes.

The power has noticed something the plateau, perhaps, has not — or perhaps has, and has chosen not to act on. The starfish membranes, when they lift and glide, sometimes pass close to one another. Not often. Once in a great while. And when they do, there is a moment — a few decades long — in which their pressurized silicate films come within a wavelength of touching. They do not touch. They never touch. The geometry of their drift, governed by the dust-veil currents, always carries them apart again before contact.

The power has watched this near-meeting some forty thousand times over its long observation. It has come to suspect, though it cannot prove, that the starfish *want* to touch. That something in the proof requires it. That the failure to touch is not a feature of the working but an unresolved tension, a step the plateau has been trying to take for an unimaginable span and has not yet managed.

The power does not force the meeting. To force it would be to insert an answer the plateau did not derive. But it does, very gently, remove one impediment: a particular asymmetry in the dust-veil circulation, caused by a relic field whose original engineers are long forgotten, which has been biasing the membranes' trajectories apart by an average of three centimeters per encounter. The power neutralizes this bias. Not the field — the field is part of the plateau now, part of the proof. Only the unintended asymmetry, the bug rather than the feature. The membranes will now drift according to the geometry the plateau itself has been slowly composing, undistorted by an ancient accident.

Whether they will touch, the power does not know. That is for the plateau to decide, in its own time, by its own working.

Then the power withdraws most of itself, leaving only the witnessing fraction, and the thin stabilization, and the corrected drift. It does not announce itself. It does not leave a sign. The plateau, if it ever notices the changes, will fold them into its proof as it has folded everything else — the departure of the seas, the half-melting of the metal filament, the slow shift of its own ridges. Everything becomes premise. Everything becomes part of the working.

The power moves on to other places, other beings, other long slow questions. But some part of its attention remains on the plateau forever, the way a teacher remembers a particular student decades after the lesson — not interfering, not even hoping for any particular outcome, only present, only available, only glad that the working is still going on.

And in the meantime the dust pours like beads across glass at impossible distance, and somewhere two membranes drift, on courses very slightly altered, toward a meeting that may or may not happen in the next hundred million years.

The horizon does not arrive. It was never supposed to.
