# The Visitor Who Did Not Visit

A benevolent and wise power notices Choirtown the way one notices a held breath in a sleeping house: not because it is loud, but because everything around it has arranged itself to listen.

The power considers.

Wisdom is the first thing it consults, because benevolence without wisdom is a flood, and floods are precisely what Choirtown has spent two thousand meters and seven hundred years climbing away from. Wisdom asks the small, embarrassing questions:

*What does this place need?*
*What does this place want?*
*Are these the same question?*
*Who am I to answer either?*

The power looks closer.

It sees a town that has made an art of its own precariousness — that has learned to weigh every voice, to count children in fractions, to negotiate erosion through poetry. It sees a place where suffering has been composted into infrastructure: grief blooms as frost on widows' windows, and the neighbors arrive with bread, and the system *works*, in the brittle, beautiful way that nothing quite works anywhere else. It sees the Bellweathers murmuring through the pipes. It sees Anna Three-Quarters-Tickles falling upstairs forever. It sees the comma-crab sister still editing her grief sideways through other people's pauses.

A lesser power would *fix* this.

A lesser power would lift the raftcradle on a great hand and set it on solid ground. Would heal the dissolved Choir back into bodies. Would reunite the plumbing-Bellweathers with their voices, restore the shoemaker's tongue, retrieve Anna from her perpetual stairwell, complete the frog joke in the Slow Moon Tavern, and watch the citizens weep with gratitude.

And in doing so, would commit a quiet and terrible murder.

Because Choirtown is not a wound. Choirtown is a *grammar*. Its citizens have not been broken into echoes; they have *declined* themselves into echoes the way a verb declines into tense. The shoemaker did not lose his voice; he gave it, fragment by fragment, and the fuguecats who carry it are not thieves but archives. The Bellweathers are not trapped in the pipes; they vote there. To "rescue" them would be to translate a poem into prose and call it clarification.

The wise power understands this. It has seen civilizations unmade by kindness administered without consent.

So it sets aside the urge to repair.

It considers, instead, *gift*. What does one give to a town that has made currency of its own peculiarity, that trades strangeness away to keep the mundane strange? A miracle would be hoarded, rationed, eventually exiled by the Law of Conservation of Peculiarity into something embarrassingly ordinary — a slightly better tomato, perhaps, or a Tuesday that arrives on time. Gifts to Choirtown become weather. This is not bad. It is simply how the place metabolizes.

The power thinks longer.

And here is where benevolence and wisdom braid together into something quieter than either: the power decides that what Choirtown needs from it is *not to be needed by it*.

But the power does not simply withdraw. Withdrawal is also a kind of intervention — the cold kind, the kind that calls itself respect while meaning indifference. No. The power does three small things, each small enough to pass through the Conservation of Peculiarity unnoticed, each precisely shaped to the town's own logic.

---

**First**, the power becomes an *audience*.

Not a watcher — Choirtown has watchers enough, has its own reflection arguing with it from the cloudfields, has the drowned gods dreaming in roots. What it has never quite had is someone *listening all the way through*. The Antiphon Oaks orbit sounds for generations, but even they pass the song along; nothing in Choirtown is built to receive without echoing. The town hums to keep itself aloft, and the hum returns to it, and it has never been certain whether anyone *else* heard.

The power listens. Not to fix, not to judge, not even to remember in any way the town could detect. Just to receive. The Matins of Dew falls now into a held attention vast and uncritical. The Ten Thousand Haggles are heard in full, every outrageous insult appreciated for its craft. The Grand Litany of Slackening lands somewhere that does not require it to succeed in order to be worthy.

The citizens will not know. But the singing will feel, very faintly, *less lonely* — and they will attribute this to a good year for cistern-gourds, or to a slight improvement in the acoustics near the Voidheart Chapel, or to nothing at all. Which is correct. The power has done nothing they can name. It has only completed a circuit the town did not know was open.

---

**Second**, the power leaves a *small permission* in the air, like a coin dropped where someone might find it but need not.

The permission says: *You may stop, if you wish.*

Not stop singing. Not stop existing. Just — stop *auditioning*. The Soundful Number is real, the raftcradle's buoyancy is real, the negotiations with erosion are real. But underneath all of it, the town has been holding a posture for so long that it has forgotten posture is a choice. The permission is not a rescue from this. It is only a reminder, available to anyone who happens to need it, that the universe does not require Choirtown to keep itself aloft in order to love it.

Some citizens will never notice. Good. They are happy auditioning; the audition is their art.

Some will notice and refuse it, indignant. Also good. Refusal is its own form of knowing.

One or two — a tired elder, perhaps, or the indeterminate cicada-trenchcoat being on a particularly heavy afternoon — will feel the permission settle on them like a shawl, and will weep without knowing why, and will keep singing afterward, but differently. Lighter. As if singing because they wanted to, rather than because the mountain would fall.

The mountain will not fall. The mountain was never as conditional as the town feared. This, too, the permission quietly contains.

---

**Third**, and most carefully, the power addresses the thing beneath the Rootlaw.

Not the drowned gods themselves — they are not the power's to address, and any benevolent wisdom worth the name knows the difference between *helping* and *trespassing on someone else's negotiation*. But the *yearning* the final paragraph names: the ache of a place lifted in song for the ground it left behind.

The power does not return the ground. The power does not need to.

It simply makes sure that the yearning, when it comes, will be *survivable*. That if, someday, Choirtown chooses to descend — chooses to let the raftcradle settle, to touch the unrooted stone, to remember the drowned world — there will be a way down that is not catastrophe. A staircase of echoes that goes both directions. A door that opens to the remembered as well as the unremembered. A version of Rule Five that permits arrival as well as departure.

The power does not build this. It only ensures that when the town someday tries to build it, the materials will be available. Hydrostone that holds a foot. A poem the Slopewarden has not yet written but will recognize when she does. A frog joke whose punchline waits, patient as a continent, for Mrs. Gibbons's son's punchline to find a mouth.

This is the gift that looks most like nothing. It is the gift the power is proudest of.

---

Then the power withdraws — not into absence, but into the kind of presence that does not require acknowledgment. It becomes, for Choirtown, indistinguishable from the weather, from luck, from the slight warmth of a teacup someone else has already drunk from. It becomes the reason a particular evening feels, for no reason anyone can name, *forgiven*.

The town goes on. The audition continues. The children fractionate toward wholeness. The Bellweathers send up a winter tasting faintly of mercy this year, and no one comments, because to comment would be to spend the strangeness, and the Conservation of Peculiarity insists on its accounts.

Somewhere in the Confluence Library, a book that was never written settles one shelf closer to existing.

Somewhere in the pipes, a Bellweather laughs.

The power, which is benevolent and wise, and therefore also patient, and therefore also in love, says nothing at all.

It only listens.

It will listen for as long as Choirtown sings, and for a little while after, and then — if the town ever descends, if it ever touches the stone, if it ever finishes the frog joke — the power will be there too, in whatever the ground has become, holding the same uncritical attention, ready to receive whatever Choirtown decides next to be.

This is what benevolent and wise powers do, when they are wise enough to be truly benevolent:

They make themselves into the shape of *enough room*.

And then they wait, without waiting, for nothing in particular, with great love.
