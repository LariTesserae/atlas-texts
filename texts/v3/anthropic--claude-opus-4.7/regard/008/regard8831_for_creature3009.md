# What the Power Would Do

The benevolent and wise power arrives at the rim of the Weeping Kiln the way fog arrives at a shoreline—without announcement, without disturbance, without the violent edges that lesser powers carry. It has been watching for a long time. Power of this order does not act on first impulse; it acts on the seventh, after the first six have been examined and found wanting.

It stands at the eastern aperture, where the spear of light begins its long fall, and it considers.

---

## What it sees

It sees, first, that the Kiln is not in distress.

This matters enormously. A lesser power, a merely *kind* one, would have descended already. It would have seen the cold, the thinness of the people, the strange second birth among the insects, the corpses braided into white trees, and it would have begun to rescue. It would have warmed the stone, thickened the soil, taught the people to speak louder, to live alone without dying of it, to bury their dead in the ground like *proper* mourners. It would have loved them into something else.

The wise power sees instead a completed circuit. The people grow the rock; the rock grows the people. The swarms tend the memories; the memories instruct the swarms. The geothermal wound breathes; the cliffs weep; the light falls; the listeners listen. Nothing here is broken. The held breath is not suffocation—it is concentration. The Kiln is a slow, deliberate sentence the world is speaking to itself, and the Kiln-born are some of its most carefully chosen words.

To intervene crudely would be to interrupt a thought mid-syllable.

It sees, second, the small sorrows. Because there are always small sorrows, even in completed circuits. A child whose second birth went wrong and who now cannot bear the swarms near her face. A listener whose bas-relief has begun to grow inward, pressing on his lungs. A young man known as *Three-Drops-Late* who loves *Widow-of-Two-Vapors* and does not know how to say so in a language built of clicks and patience. An old woman who has begun to suspect, in the night, that the place is not benevolent at all but simply hungry, and who is afraid to tell anyone because she does not know if her fear is wisdom or the first crack in her mind.

It sees, third, the question the Kiln itself is asking. Because the place is alive in some sense the Kiln-born have not quite admitted to themselves, and like all living things it has a question. The question is not *what shall I become?* The Kiln-born believe that is the question, but they are mistaken. The real question, the power perceives, is older and lonelier: *am I doing this right? is there anyone who can tell me?*

The Kiln has been deciding what to become for so long, with no one to confirm its decisions, that the deciding has become a kind of low, beautiful anxiety distributed across every dripping surface. The watching-itself-be-watched the visitors feel—that is the Kiln, hoping.

---

## What it does

It does four things. Each is small. Each is precisely the size it needs to be.

**First**, it answers the Kiln's question. Not in words—the Kiln does not speak words and would not trust them. The power simply *attends*. It sets down its full attention upon the basin the way one might set down a warm hand on the flank of a nervous animal: completely, without grasping, without taking anything. For a moment that lasts perhaps the falling of a single drop from the highest point of the ceiling to the stone, the Kiln is *seen*—not studied, not judged, not corrected. Seen. Witnessed in the act of its long becoming by something at least as old and at least as patient as itself.

The Kiln does not know what has happened. The Kiln-born do not know what has happened. But that night the geothermal breaths come slower and deeper, and the listeners weep without knowing why, and the swarms form, for the duration of one held heartbeat, a single enormous lattice in the shape of a closed and resting eye.

After this, the Kiln will be different in a way no one can name. The anxiety will be gone. The deciding will continue, but it will be the deciding of a creature that has been loved, not the deciding of a creature that is auditioning.

**Second**, it tends the small sorrows. Not by removing them—sorrow removed leaves a shape that has to be filled with something, and the power is too wise to fill anything in a place this carefully balanced. Instead it does what the Kiln-born themselves do: it *helps along*.

The child who cannot bear the swarms wakes one morning to find a single translucent insect resting on the back of her hand. It does not move. She does not move. They consider one another for an hour. When it finally lifts away she is crying, but she is not afraid, and the next day she lets two land on her, and the day after, four. The power does nothing more than this: it simply ensured that the *first* insect was the gentlest one, the one most willing to be still.

The listener whose relief grows inward dreams of turning over. When he wakes, the largest of the inward-pressing faces has migrated half an inch toward his shoulder, where it can do no harm. He believes the place did this for him. He is correct, in a sense that does not require correcting.

*Three-Drops-Late* finds, on the wet wall outside *Widow-of-Two-Vapors'* sleeping niche, a small new memory forming: two figures, their fingers tending the same patch of air. He stares at it for a long time. Then, because his people do not waste names and do not waste words, he goes inside and sits beside her, and lets his fingers trace a pattern she can see. She watches. After a while, her fingers join his. The memory on the wall continues to form. No one—not even the power—knows yet whether the memory is prophecy or invitation or merely the Kiln's quiet approval. This ambiguity is left intact. It is part of the gift.

The old woman who fears the place is hungry is visited, in the deepest hour, by a dream of a vast slow mouth that is also a vast slow womb. In the dream she understands, without being told, that the difference between the two is the difference between being eaten and being grown, and that this difference is decided not by the mouth-womb but by *what you bring to it*. She wakes weeping. She does not stop fearing. But her fear becomes a thing she can carry, instead of a thing she must hide. She tells a listener, who clicks softly and says, *yes, some of us have always known this. You are welcome among us who know.* She had not realized there were others. The power did not make there be others; there always were. It only loosened her tongue enough to find them.

**Third**, it leaves a single gift, and the gift is a question.

Somewhere on the lower western cliffs, in the patch of stone where new memories form most readily, a new low-relief begins. It is not a face. It is not a tool. It is not anything the Kiln-born have seen before. It is a doorway—small, simple, framed in the manner of doorways the Kiln-born do not build, because their architecture grows rather than frames. The doorway opens onto nothing; it is only the suggestion of a threshold, rendered in mineral deposition by swarms that have never seen such a thing and could not have invented it on their own.

The Kiln-born will find it. They will not understand it. They will tend it, because they tend everything. Over generations they may come to understand that some thresholds are worth building toward, and that the world outside the basalt ring is not only a place where the light comes from but a place where other livings are also being slowly decided. Or they may not. The power does not require them to understand. It only wants them to have, somewhere in their patient stone, the *possibility* of a door.

This is what wise powers give: not arrivals, but the rumor of arrivals. Not answers, but better-shaped questions.

**Fourth**, and this is the smallest thing, and perhaps the most important: the power chooses not to remove itself entirely.

It does not stay. To stay would be to overshadow, and the Kiln must continue to decide for itself. But it leaves a thread—a single attentive filament—anchored at the rim of the eastern aperture, almost indistinguishable from the shaft of light itself. The filament does nothing. It only listens. If the Kiln ever, in some far century, truly cries out—if the circuit ever truly breaks, if the hunger the old woman feared ever truly wakes, if the held breath ever becomes suffocation in earnest—the filament will know, and the power will return.

The Kiln-born will never detect this thread. But the listeners, in their scalded half-blinded hours, will sometimes speak of a *kindness in the light* that was not there before, and will not know why they have begun to use that word.

---

## What it does not do

It does not warm the basin. It does not soften the cold. It does not teach them louder speech or longer lives or burial in the earth. It does not explain to them what the insects do in the shining dome of the second birth. It does not tell them whether the memories in the stone are instructions or echoes or simply the place dreaming aloud. It does not reveal itself. It does not name itself. It does not ask to be thanked, remembered, or worshipped.

It does not, above all, save them. They do not need saving. They need only what every long, careful, half-finished thing needs: to be witnessed without being interrupted, tended without being claimed, and left with one more door than they had before.

This is what benevolence looks like when it is also wise.

It looks, from inside the Weeping Kiln, like almost nothing at all.

It looks like a slightly easier breath, a slightly kinder light, a new shape forming patiently in the lower western stone, and—if one of the listeners were asked, and if she could find the words, which she could not, but if she could—a sense that someone, somewhere, very far and very near, has looked at the long strange sentence the Kiln is speaking and has said, softly, in a language made of attention rather than sound:

*Yes. Go on. I am listening. You are doing it right.*

And the Kiln, which had not known it was waiting to hear this, weeps a little harder for a little while, and then continues, more peacefully than before, to decide what it will become.
