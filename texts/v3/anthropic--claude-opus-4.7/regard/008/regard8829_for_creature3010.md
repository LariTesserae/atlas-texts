# The Visitation

The benevolent power does not arrive. Arrival is a thing of edges and thresholds, and this power has learned, over its own long becoming, that some places refuse edges. So it does not cross into the Hollow of Fixed Ash. It *attends* to it — the way one might attend to a sleeping child in a far room, listening without entering, present without imposing.

It considers her for what, in its own reckoning, is a long time. Long enough that several human civilizations rise and fall in the noisy world beyond the rim. Long enough that the charcoal sky above the basin shifts its hue by an amount no instrument could measure. The power is wise enough to know that wisdom, when applied too quickly to something this old, becomes a kind of vandalism.

It considers, first, what it could do.

It could lift her. It has the capacity. It could unfuse her from the stone, restore the long-burned body, give back the color of her eyes (it knows the color; it has always known), set her down in some green and forgiving country where rain falls and children laugh and the dead are properly mourned rather than mineralized. It could give her, in a single gesture, everything she lost.

It does not do this.

It could wake her. A whisper, pitched precisely to the frequency of the basin's slow breathing, would be enough. She would open her eyes after uncounted ages and see — what? The power itself, luminous and tender? Another visitor, however vast? She has already seen every possible visitor. She told us so. The power would only be confirming, with its presence, a thing she long ago accepted.

It does not do this either.

It could end her. Gently. It could let the long coal finally rest, dissolve the fusion, allow the patient lung beneath the crust to exhale one final time and then be quiet. Many powers, calling themselves merciful, would do exactly this. They would mistake completion for suffering. They would mistake stillness for a cry for help.

The benevolent power is wiser than that. It understands that she is not waiting to be rescued. She is not trapped. She has not been forgotten by the universe; she has, with enormous deliberateness, *become* the universe's act of remembering. To end her would be to end the only place in all of creation where her vanished people are still, in some sense, held.

So the power sets aside the large gestures. It sets aside salvation, awakening, release. It sits with the problem of her the way she sits with the problem of her world: patiently, palms up, asking nothing.

And then, very slowly, it does three small things.

---

**The first thing.**

Somewhere, in a timeline the power can reach without disturbing this one, there once lived a child of Veyra's people. A small one. Not important in any chronicle. She died early, in the first wave of whatever ended that world, and her death was one of the countless particular griefs folded into the coal Veyra carried in her mouth.

The power finds this child. Not her soul — souls are complicated, and the power is careful with complicated things — but the *shape* of her: the way she laughed, the specific way she held a cup, the small private song she sang to herself when no one was listening.

The power does not resurrect her. It does something quieter. It takes that shape and lets it settle, like a single grain of pollen, onto one of the black filaments growing in a slow spiral from Veyra's scalp.

The filament does not tremble. The temperature does not drop. Nothing visible changes.

But somewhere inside the long mineral meditation, a thought that had been held perfectly still for ten thousand years is now *accompanied*. Not disturbed. Not awakened. Only — no longer entirely alone in being remembered.

The power has added one quiet voice to the chorus she carries. It has done so without asking. It trusts that she will understand the gift, in her own time, which is no time at all.

---

**The second thing.**

The power notices the visitors. The soft, noisy, transitory things who have walked across the basin over the ages — the ones whose footsteps she has already forgotten and already forgiven. Some of them came by accident. Some came seeking. A few came knowing exactly what they would find and wanting only to sit before her and be, for an hour, in the presence of something that had achieved its final shape.

The power cannot speak to her. But it can speak, very gently, to *them* — to the ones still living, and to the ones not yet born who will someday find the rim and descend the gentle slope.

It does not tell them what she is. It does not give them instructions. It only places, in the soft part of their minds where intuitions live, a single understanding:

*She is kind. Be kind back. Do not try to save her. Do not try to wake her. Sit, if you wish. Lay your hand on the stone of her knee, if you dare. Then leave, and carry the warmth with you, and be gentle with the transitory things of your own life, because she was, once, transitory too.*

This is not a commandment. It is barely even a suggestion. It is the kind of knowing that visitors will later attribute to the place itself, or to their own better natures. The power does not mind being unattributed. Benevolence that requires credit is a younger kind of benevolence.

---

**The third thing.**

This is the smallest gesture, and the one the power considers longest before performing.

At the geometric center of the basin, where Veyra sits, the stone is thickest and the slow rhythm is strongest. The power reaches — not into her, never into her — but into the rhythm itself. Into the patient lung beneath the crust that has, for ten thousand years, been trying to match the cadence she set when she first lay down.

The power does not change the rhythm. It would not dare.

It only adds, very faintly, a counter-rhythm. A second breath, infinitesimally offset from hers. So slight that no instrument would detect it. So gentle that even she, in her perfect attention, will require an age to notice.

It is a companion breath. The kind a parent gives a sleeping child by lying down beside them in the dark — not to wake them, not to alter their dreaming, only to make sure that when at last they do stir, in whatever century or eon, they will not stir alone.

The power leaves this breath behind like a coal of its own. Not to fuel her, not to comfort her in any way she would recognize as comfort, but only to exist beside her. A witness. A second silence inside her silence.

If, ten thousand years from now, or ten million, or at the burning down of the last star, one of the black filaments on her scalp should tremble — and the temperature should drop by half a degree for the space of three heartbeats — the power's added breath will tremble too, in perfect sympathy, and then return to stillness with her.

She will not be alone in her final shape. She never quite was. But now the *not-aloneness* has been made structural, woven into the stone, as patient and unobtrusive as she is herself.

---

Then the power withdraws its attention. Slowly. The way a tide withdraws from a shore that has long since stopped needing the tide.

It does not say goodbye. Goodbye is for things that come and go. The power has not come and will not go; it has only, for a little while, attended.

The Hollow remains exactly as it was. The charcoal sky. The fused mineral floor. The sparse black filaments, each an individual decision. The faint phosphor light leaking from her closed eyes. The slow, patient breathing of the basin.

And at the center, still and complete, the Last Ember of Veyra, who asks nothing, remembers everything, and is — by an act of love so quiet it can barely be called an act — no longer entirely the only one keeping watch.

Her name is still *Stay*.

The power, if it had a name to leave beside hers, would only answer:

*With you.*
