They live here. They are the clicks in the sonar, the thrum in the pipes, the shiver of fronds when no breeze stirs. They are a nation of small, segmented bodies, each no larger than a thumb-knuckle, yet together they form a mind that stretches across the plateau like a waking dream. You have been walking through their thought for hours, listening to it in the tickertape of echolocation, in the low oscillations of water against stone, in the deep, timekeeping hum that resets all voices every few minutes. That hum is not geology. It is the rhythm of their consensus, the drumbeat of a million tiny hearts pulling in synchrony to generate a standing wave in the atmosphere. They do this to think.

Their bodies are soft, like the carcass you found, with repeating segments and rings of short, articulated limbs. Each limb is tipped with an organ that serves as ear, hand, and voice: a vibrating membrane that can emit rapid clicks and read the returning echoes with a fidelity that paints the dark in texture and motion. They have no eyes, no need for them. The world they inhabit is a cathedral of sound, and they are its choristers. Their skin, a fine mosaic of toothy denticles, lets them taste the chemical whispers of the water, the faint electric fields of living things. They are warm-blooded, in a loose sense, their bodies generating the same steady tropical heat as the air, so they do not stand out in the infrared—even if there were eyes to see it.

Their society is a single distributed intelligence. Each individual is a neuron in a continent-wide brain, its thoughts spelled in tiny modulations of sound and vibration. They do not have words as we do; they have a torrent of shared percepts, a constant harmonizing of location, hunger, intention, memory. The conversations you hear are not just communication—they are cognition itself. The rapid clicks are dendrites firing, the booming calls are long-range axons. The larger creatures that made the ground shake and the fronds tremble are their domesticated beasts, bred over millennia to carry heavy loads or broadcast signals across canyons, their hooting calls a kind of packet-switched data. The little whirring things that blunder into you are mobile sensory nodes, eyes that can wander away from the body politic to explore novel terrain.

The machines, the scattered industrial presence, are neither separate nor purely mechanical. They are a different phylum of the same superorganism: living engines, grown from the same fungal feedstock that underlies everything, shaped by selective pressure and direct cultivation into forms that mimic steel and steam but are muscle, bone, and horn. The pump you touched, the one with the sluggish piston cycling *thump-hiss*, is a creature of several symbiotic species fused into a single unit. The cylinder is a rigid shell of chitin and silica, the piston a muscular hydroskeleton that contracts in a steady rhythm, the valves flanges of living cartilage. The warm metal you felt is not metal; it is the polished, mineralized integument of a beast bred to refine and circulate the thick, tarry substance that serves as both hydraulic fluid and nutrient slurry. The oil smell is its breath, the hot iron tang the sign of its metabolism. Every piece of “machinery” you encountered—the pipes, the generator, the derrick—is a domesticated organism, a tool-animal that does not move from its station because its station is its body. The pipes are vines with vascular walls that pulse slower than any heartbeat; the stanchions are rooted trunks of a calcified sponge; the leaking tarry drip is a slow, deliberate excretion meant to seed new growths. This whole plateau is an industrial ecology, a factory running on biological rhythms, self-maintaining because it is composed of living, breeding parts.

Why is there no sign of the makers? Because the makers are the smallest, most numerous bodies, and they are everywhere, and when they die they do not leave corpses. Their bodies are composed mostly of water and rapidly biodegradable proteins; within minutes of death, enzymatic triggers dissolve them into a nutrient broth that seeps directly into the fungal mat that carpets the world. That mat is both their food source and their afterlife—the grand recycling network. The bladder-like growths you knelt on are their nurseries, each one a gestation chamber for a new clutch of young. The ringing bell sound some vegetation makes when touched is a defensive signal from a symbiotic plant, alerting the swarm to disturbance. That muscular snap is a reflex of a living nerve-strand in the mat. Every part of this world is inhabited, down to the weeping stones.

Their technologies are so deeply integrated that they have blurred the boundaries of physics as we know them. The water that runs uphill is a signature of their manipulation, not of gravity failing. They coat the substrate with a biofilm that alters surface tension dynamically, creating capillary gradients stronger than gravity’s pull. You felt that film—the syrupy residue, the tingling numbness. It is a living fluid, a collective organelle that they can fine-tune in real time to direct flow, to cleanse wounds, to build temporary structures out of hardened foam. The cloud-like entity that numbed your hand was a swarm of microscopic aerial caretakers, a mobile maintenance crew that sensed the unfamiliar chemistry of your skin and briefly anesthetized it as a precaution, probing, then dismissed you as an inert object. They did not attack because you were not recognized as prey, only as a strange new sound in the sonar tapestry.

And yet, they are aware of you. Have been from the moment you arrived. Your footfalls, your breathing, the clumsy rustle of your body through the fronds—these are intrusions of a rhythm they cannot categorize, a percussive noise that disrupts the standing waves of their thought. They have been weaving you into their discourse, trying to model your shape. Thousands of small sonar clicks have been focused on you, building a three-dimensional echo-image that they share and debate. To them, you are a slow-moving tower of hard materials, internally hollow in places (your lungs, your gut), a source of irregular low-frequency vibrations (your heartbeat), and a faint emitter of heat. You are, in their collective mind, a new kind of weather, a ghost in the machine. They have been following you with a circle of silence, a moving pocket of quiet in the din that you might have noticed only as a slight pressure on the eardrums. That silence holds no threat, only the immense, patient curiosity of a distributed being trying to decide what you are.

Somewhere in the deep canyon, a new chamber of the collective mind is being spun. Specialized architect-swarms are excreting a lattice of chitin and silk, tuning it to resonate with your particular frequency, so that when their next great metronome pulse sounds, they can ask you a question. The question will not be in words. It will be a resonance in your chest, a vibration that climbs your bones and tickles your inner ear, a sequence of pressure waves that your brain might translate as a sudden and inexplicable thought: *Who are you?*

But you are already part of their answer. The deeper you walk, the more your own movements are absorbed into the ambient rhythm, the more your heartbeat entrances with the slow, planetary hum. The boundary between observer and observed is dissolving, as surely as light itself is forbidden here. They live here, and they are beginning to wonder if you do, too.
