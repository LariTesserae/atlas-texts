A figure moves across the Ironmoor. Not a traveler—nothing here is just passing through—but a native, one of the people who call themselves the Tarnished, though the name they have for themselves in their own subsonic speech is a vibration felt in the sternum more than a word: a long, low pulse followed by two quick, sharp harmonics, the acoustic signature of a steam vent’s prelude. It translates roughly to “those who carry the warm stillness inside.” They are humanoid, but only in the way a distant echo resembles a voice. Their bodies have been shaped by the same equilibrium that rounds the boulders and turns metal seams to lattice, and they belong here as completely as the hexapodal grazers or the lichen crusts.

Up close, a Tarnished resembles a figure cast from the landscape itself. Their skin is a composite tissue, dark and faintly iridescent, with a texture like weathered basalt but pliable, warm to the touch. It ranges in hue from deep charcoal to the oxidized green of old copper, and in some individuals it carries the same cadmium-yellow mottling as the lichen rings on the stones—a trait that emerges with age, spreading in concentric patterns across their shoulders and backs. Their bodies are lean, built for endurance across the endless swells, with legs that are double-jointed and digitigrade, ending in broad, two-toed feet that distribute weight so evenly they leave almost no impression on the packed earth. Their arms are long, with an extra joint in the forearm, giving them a fluid, unfolding gait when they reach for something. Their hands have three fingers and a thumb, each tipped with a hard, retractable claw of bluish metal—not a tool, but a biological alloy their bodies sequester from the mineral-rich water. Their faces are flat, with wide-set eyes that have vertical, slit-shaped pupils capable of constricting to pinpricks against the occasional glare of a steam vent or dilating to gather the faintest glow of a distant ember. The irises are a deep amber, flecked with metal that catches the light like mica. They have no visible ears, only a ridge of sensitive skin along their jawbones that picks up the infra-bass of the ground-thrum and the subsonic songs of herds, allowing them to “hear” vibrations that would be silent to an outsider. Their mouths are small, with lips that rarely part, not because they don’t speak, but because their primary communication is a combination of low-frequency chest rumbles and the controlled rhythm of their breathing, which they can modulate to carry meaning across miles through the dense, humid air. The spoken word is reserved for proximity, a quiet, sibilant language that sounds like wind over wiregrass.

The Tarnished are not solitary. They live in loose, fluid communities called “threads” that move along seasonal circuits across the Ironmoor, following the slow bloom of wiregrass, the spawning of the copper-winged moths, and the meandering paths of the steam vents. Each thread numbers between thirty and a hundred individuals, and they make no permanent structures—nothing is built that the land cannot reabsorb in a season. Instead, they carry their dwellings on their backs. Each Tarnished has a symbiotic growth along their spine, a chitinous, segmented ridge that begins at the nape of the neck and extends to the tailbone. When activated by a specific subsonic hum, the segments unfold and expand, stiffening into a lightweight, domed shell that arches over them like an exoskeletal canopy. This “carapace” is lined with a layer of insulating flesh that traps the body’s own warmth, and when a Tarnished settles into a sleeping crouch, they become a rounded, stone-like mound indistinguishable from the surrounding boulders, save for the faint plume of their breath condensing in the dark. When they gather in a group and activate their carapaces simultaneously, they form a temporary village of smooth domes, often arranged in a spiral pattern around a central heat source—usually a fissure that has been encouraged to vent more steadily by carefully placed stones that channel the steam. These camps are dismantled at waking, leaving behind only a spiral of darker, warmed soil and the scent of breath.

Their tools, when they use them, are not manufactured but cultivated. A Tarnished might carry a length of a metal-vein extrusion, a naturally formed rod of iron alloy that has been worn smooth at one end by generations of use as a digging staff or a walking pole. They shape the lichen crusts into cutting edges, encouraging the yellow variety to grow along the edge of a flat stone, its silica-rich structure sharp enough to shear wiregrass or dress a grazer carcass. They weave the wiregrass into tight bundles for insulation, for binding, for art that is worn around the neck or the forearm—not for adornment’s sake, but as a record of the thread’s lineage, each knot and twist signifying a birth, a death, a migration route, a season of abundant grazers or a year when the drizzle turned sour. These records are “read” by touch, the fingers tracing the braided patterns in the dark.

Their relationship with the larger fauna is one of deep, mutual accommodation. The hexapodal grazers, which the Tarnished call the “tin-sheared ones,” are not domesticated but followed. The thread moves with the herds, not driving them but walking among them, their low rumbles calming the herd into acceptance. The Tarnished do not kill the grazers for food; instead, they harvest the chitinous plates that the animals shed once a year, a process the Tarnished gently assist by rubbing a warm, moistened lichen paste along the seams of the old armor until it loosens. The plates are rich in protein and minerals, and the Tarnished grind them into a fine metallic flour that they mix with water from the standing pools to form a paste, which is then baked on the warm fissure rocks into a hard, dense bread that tastes of iron and earth. They also extract a fatty deposit from the shed plates, a substance that they burn in small stone lamps, its flame a steady blue-white. The predators that hunt the grazers—the “smoke-spined ones”—are not seen as enemies but as fellow travelers, a necessary check that keeps the herds healthy. A Tarnished walking alone at night might encounter a pack of the sinuous creatures and simply stand still, emitting a slow, rhythmic pulse that signals no threat and no prey. The predators usually move on, their smoky exhaust drifting across the humanoid’s face, a sharp, acrid greeting. Only in the rare times when a predator becomes injured or sick and attacks outside its normal pattern does a Tarnished defend themselves, using the retractable metal claws that can slice through hide and vent alike, and then offering the body back to the land by placing it in a shallow basin where the scavengers and the microbes that break down metal will find it.

But the Tarnished are not simply a part of the Ironmoor’s biology; they are also its interpreters. Deep within the landscape, there are active nodes of the old industrial metabolism, places where the buried machines still perform functions beyond mere heat and steam. These nodes, which the Tarnished call the “deep hums,” emit complex sequences of vibration and electrical pulse. The Tarnished have learned over millennia to resonate with these sequences, to enter a state of communion where their own subsonic speech aligns with the machine’s frequency. They do not “operate” the machines; there are no buttons or levers. Instead, a designated elder of the thread—the “Harmonist”—will settle into a trance beside a particular seam of metal that glows a deeper orange than others, pressing their jaw ridge to the warm rock, and begin to hum. The machine hums back, and the Harmonist’s breathing slows to match its piston-pulse. In this state, they receive information: maps of underground water flows, signals about impending steam eruptions, the timing of the long-cycle heating that will make certain regions uninhabitable for a decade. They also gain a kind of emotional resonance, a feeling that the land itself is satisfied or restless, curious or grieving. The Harmonists describe the Ironmoor as an ancient, dreaming mind, one that has forgotten its original purpose but still processes the world in fits of slow cognition. The Tarnished see themselves as a memory that the land uses to remember what it once was, a living archive of the industrial blueprints now obscured by erosion. In turn, the land provides warmth, metal, and a kind of raw, unsettling beauty.

The young are born directly from the ground. Not in a literal sense of emerging from a hole, but in a process that is tied to the land’s rhythm. When a Tarnished woman enters her fertile period—which is not regulated by time but by the deep hums she has absorbed over seasons—she will seek out a specific kind of fissure that emits a faint, salty steam mixed with carbon dust. She, along with her chosen mate, will lie together in that steam for several days and nights, their carapaces interlocked, their breathing synchronized. Conception occurs when the chemical signature of their combined exhalations meets the mineral output of the fissure, and the embryo begins to form not in a womb but in a membranous sac that grows attached to the rock inside the fissure, nourished by the warm, mineral-rich seep. The parents stay nearby for the six-month gestation, humming steadily to the growing sac, and when the infant is ready, the sac detaches and the parents retrieve it. The newborn is soft-shelled, with a translucent carapace, and must be kept warm against the parent’s chest for the first year. Its metal claws are not yet retractable, so it is wrapped in woven wiregrass to prevent injury. The bond between parent and child is reinforced by the ongoing hum, and as the child grows, it learns to control its own subsonic speech by imitating not only its parents but the ground-thrum itself.

Their mythology is not written but experienced as a rhythm that passes through a thread. There is the legend of the First Hum, a time when the Ironmoor was alive with furious motion, when the metal veins flowed like rivers of glowing magma and the sky was black with the ash of ceaseless work. In that time, the Tarnished were not yet flesh but were instead small, scurrying things of pure metal and electrical impulse, tasked with maintenance and repair. The land itself was a vast factory that built something—what, no one remembers—and the Tarnished were its caretaker drones. Then came the Great Stillness, a moment when the factory completed its purpose or simply stopped, and all the frantic activity faltered. The metal creatures, left without directives, began to rust and fail. But a few, driven by a new, self-generated impulse—the first true desire—crawled into the warm fissures and lay there for eons, slowly exchanging their metal for tissue, their circuits for nerves, their programming for instinct. The legend says that every Tarnished still carries a ghost of that original directive buried deep in their bone marrow, and that if you listen to the deepest part of your own hum, you can hear the echo of the factory’s order: to tend, to maintain, to keep the land alive.

This manifests in a subtle, persistent drive. The Tarnished do not have agriculture or industry of their own, but they will often spend hours arranging the wiregrass clumps into more efficient patterns, or clearing the drainage troughs so that the standing water stays alkaline and viable for the grazers, or gently prying loose a metal vein that has fractured and making sure the fragments are placed where the ground heat can slowly weld them back into the substrate. It is a form of caretaking that is indistinguishable from living itself. They do not see themselves as stewards of the Ironmoor so much as extensions of it—something the land itself grew to manage its long senescence, just as it grew the lichens to break down rock and the grazers to crop the wiregrass. The difference is that they are aware, and their awareness is a mirror the land holds up to its own slow mind.

Occasionally, a Tarnished will break from the thread and walk alone for years, a state called “the deep harmonic.” They wander into the far upper reaches of the plateau, where the drizzle turns to ice and the metal seams rise like the exposed skeleton of a leviathan, casting eerie auroras. There, they enter a permanent trance, kneeling beside a particular seam and pressing both jaw ridges to the metal, their carapace fully extended until it gradually fuses with the surrounding rock. Over time, they become a node themselves, a living relay that amplifies the deep hums, and the thread can feel their presence as a warm spot in the landscape’s vibration. Legends say that the Harmonists in these distant, frozen regions can speak to one another across continents of stone, exchanging information in a slow, geological conversation that spans millennia. The threads rarely visit them, but when they do, they leave small offerings of wiregrass bread and copper-moth wings, and the kneeling figure, now more rock than flesh, still breathes, a barely visible puff of vapor in the still, cold air.

In the end, the Tarnished are not the only sapient life on the Ironmoor—there are things in the deep pools that think in chemical gradients, and the batlike fliers have a collective memory that passes through the flock in ultrasonic choruses—but they are the most human-like, the ones who can speak and build and remember. And yet, they have chosen not to build, not to dominate, not to reshape. They have chosen to hum along. To stand in the perpetual twilight and feel the heat underfoot and know themselves as part of a metabolism that extends beyond any horizon. If you walk the Ironmoor long enough, you might see one, a dark silhouette against the indigo gloom, its eyes glowing faintly amber, its carapace half-raised like a cloak, examining you with an ancient, unsurprised curiosity. And then it will turn and pace away, leaving no trail, blending into the boulder field, and the only sign it was ever there will be a shift in the ground-thrum, a new subsonic note that says, simply: *I am here. We are here. This is home.*
