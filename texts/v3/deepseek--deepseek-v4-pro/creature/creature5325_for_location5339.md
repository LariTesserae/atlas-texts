They are small people, as fits a place where every horizontal space is contested by roots. Compact, with the kind of dense muscle that comes from a lifetime of gripping and climbing and pulling against the spring of lianas. Their feet are wide and prehensile-toed, the skin of their soles cross-hatched with callus from the rough braid of walkways. They walk with a rolling, careful placement, feeling the tension of each strand before they commit weight, and they can stand motionless on a single swaying rail for the time it takes to strip a palm frond of its leaflets. Their hands are the same: short, powerful fingers, nails kept short and thick for peeling back bark, and an almost unsettling independence of motion—they can weave a basket while hanging by the other hand, or feel the pulse of a hummingbird's throat while steadying a bloom for its beak.

Their skin, beneath the constant patina of moisture and green dust, is a deep copper-brown, the color of wet bark in shadow, with an undertone like the bronze sheen on a beetle's wing when sunlight breaks through. Dark hair, universally thick and lank with mist, is worn cropped close or bound back with twists of fiber, because loose hair catches in the runner-vines that quest constantly through the air. The faces: broad, with high cheekbones that catch the diffuse light, eyes the color of the dark pool-water below, set deep and watchful. They squint often, not from any lack of light, but from the habit of scanning the green lattice for movement—a snake's head, a dislodged handhold, a child darting along a branch that has grown too thin since yesterday. Their expressions rest in easy, open alertness, the face of someone who is always half-listening to the creak of wood, the change in frog-song that heralds a storm.

Clothing is barkcloth, beaten from the inner skin of a tree that grows only in the cloud-wetted heights, soft as chamois and faintly fragrant with the antiseptic resin that preserves it. It's cut into simple tunics and loinwraps, sometimes layered with aprons of woven grass for carrying things. Many wear capes of monkey fur, felted into a spongy mat that sheds water and mimics the mottled gray-brown of the canopy shadow. The children, until they can walk the ropes with confidence, wear a harness of twisted vine: a continuous loop that their mother keeps hitched to the main walkway, so they can crawl and tumble without vanishing into the understory. They are tethered to life, for the first few years, with the same material that tethers the bridges.

There are perhaps forty of them, no more than the bowl can hold without the living walls being eaten back faster than they can regrow. They know every face, every voice in the constant murmur, and they know the exact sound of each other's footsteps on the palm-planks—a repertoire of creaks and bounces as individual as a name. They don't have names as we do, nothing fixed. Instead, they carry a shifting set of call-sounds: a particular two-note whistle for a child still nursing, a clicked tongue and hum for the man who tends the cistern-bromeliads, a low, breathy *huh-huh* for the old woman who remembers the storm that felled the grandfather fig. New sounds accrue: after a feat of saving a dropped infant, someone might be given a phrase that mimics the little gasp of relief, and it will stick as long as it amuses them. There's no formal naming ceremony; language here is as alive and untended as the walls, creeping into meaning.

The matriarch at the lookout perch is the closest thing to a central figure. She is the oldest, not in a way they mark, but she has lost the last of her birth-sounds and is simply called by the sound of the wind through the topmost leaves: a quiet, long *shhhhh*. She sits at the highest point, where the canopy opens to a single, jagged view of the next ridge, and she combs wool. The wool is from a creature they tend: a small, long-furred marsupial that dozes in the bromeliad cisterns and feeds on the algae skimming the water. It is docile, slow-breeding, and its fur, when combed out, is finer than any plant fiber, warm and nearly waterproof. She sits cross-legged on a platform of woven aerial roots, a wooden comb in one hand—carved from the dense heart-palm, its teeth worn to smooth pegs—and she draws the fur into a cloudy pile on a barkcloth mat. The animals come to her, clambering up the trunk with their gripping tails, and they lie in her lap while she works, their breathing slow as the drip from the moss. She is the calm center of the turbulence, the one who sees the weather coming before it breaks, who feels the shift in the root-tension that means a branch is failing. When she speaks, it's with the sound of the place itself: a soft, susurrating rhythm that the children imitate when they play at being old.

Daily life moves in a rhythm that matches the light—not sunrise and sunset, exactly, because the canopy diffuses both into a gradual shift from dim green to bright green and back again. There are no hours. Instead, there are tasks keyed to the moisture cycle: early, when the dewfall is thickest and the bromeliad cisterns brim, they empty the night's catch into skin bags. Later, when the upper leaves begin to dry and the hummingbirds are most frantic, they pollinate the food-plants by hand, using a fine brush of monkey fur to transfer the pale, sticky grains between blossoms, a job that requires stillness and patience and an eye for the tiny flowers hidden in the moss. The staple food is palm pith, harvested from a grove of slender, fast-growing palms at the basin's edge. The pith is white and crisp, tasting of clean water and a hint of sweet nut. They mash it into a porridge on communal hearths—flat stones heated by smoldering peat dug from a particular wet hollow, the smoke low and acrid and mostly dispersed through the open weave of the walls. The hearths are the only permanent fixtures, and even they shift year by year as the root-mass buckles and resettles.

They eat together, but not in a group. Food is shared freely, passed hand-to-hand along the walkways, a constant distribution: a woven packet of porridge left hanging at a junction for whoever passes; a skewer of roasted grub—thumb-sized, fat from feeding on the same palm pith—handed to a child who then scampers up to the lookout platform to share with the old woman. There's no hoarding because the world hoards nothing; every plant gives and takes without account. The concept of a meal is a loose, all-day grazing, punctuated by moments when someone brings back a particularly rich find—a cluster of ripe tree-fruit that has to be eaten now before the monkeys get it, a hatch of ant larvae that must be scooped from the nest and roasted immediately. Then there's a brief, excited convergence, a dozen people clustered on a wide platform, talking over each other, the sound rising above the baseline din before dispersing again.

Their tools are never far from their hands, but they don't cherish them in a way that mourns loss. If a bone needle slips into the black water below, the maker simply takes another shard of tapir fibula from the basket, grinds it against a rough stone, and threads it anew. There is an art to it, certainly—some are better at flaking the edge of a copper adze, some have a feel for the exact tension of a twisted vine that will hold a walkway for years—but the art is held in the body, not in the object. You watch an old man shape a new bowl from a knot of wood, using a curved blade and the pressure of his thumb, and you realize he has done this a thousand times, and the bowl will be indistinguishable from the one he made as a boy, and both will eventually rot back into the floor and feed the next knot of wood. It's not futility; it's just continuity, the same way the frogsong continues through the night.

The noise, always the noise. They don't try to quiet it; they speak into it, their voices pitched to cut through the chorus. By the time a child can walk the ropes, she has learned to pick out her mother's voice from the cacophony, to distinguish the alarm call of a monkey from the identical sound made by a teasing cousin. They don't have songs, exactly, but they have a kind of work-chant: a series of rhythmic grunts and hums that match the motion of weaving, the pushing and pulling of roots into new shapes. When a group repairs a storm-damaged bridge, they fall into it automatically, the tone rising and falling like the bridge itself, and it's said the plants respond—that the roots grow faster where the chant vibrates through the wood. It's not magic; it's just that the chant times the work, and rhythmic, regular tension trains the young roots more effectively. They know that. They know it as deeply as they know the taste of palm pith.

The children learn by doing, by being small bodies in the web. A toddler tethered at the hearth watches her mother's hands twist fiber, and her own hands twitch in mimicry long before she can form the words to ask. A boy of six follows his uncle along the high branches, watching how he tests each handhold—not pulling straight down, but tugging sideways to see if the root is merely resting or truly fused—and by the time the boy is ten, his own body reads the stresses without a thought. There's no formal teaching, but there are stories: short, dense narratives told in the evening, when the mist thickens and the birds quiet and the hearth-glows flicker orange through the green. They tell of the time before the basin was shaped, when the first people wandered up from the lowlands and found the bowl a tangle of unchecked growth, and how they began to guide it, generation by generation, into a shape that could hold them. The stories are not heroic; they are practical, describing which vines could be bent, which trees would fuse if bound, which insects could be lured to pollinate the food-palms. The past is stored as technique, not as glory.

They don't bury their dead; there is no ground to dig. When someone dies—usually of age, sometimes from a fall, occasionally from a fever that sweeps through the damp—they wrap the body in a fresh layer of barkcloth, carry it to a corner of the basin where the moss is thickest, and lay it down. They cover it with a lattice of living branches, quickly woven to discourage the monkeys, and they leave it. The moss grows over it within weeks; the roots absorb it; the body becomes part of the continuous, spongy floor. The place where they laid it becomes just another hollow, another pool of black water reflecting the canopy. No marker. And yet they remember. The old woman at the perch still makes a particular clicking sound when she passes a certain loop of root where her husband's body was laid, and the children know that sound means "the one who carved the first rain-catcher." The dead are not gone; they are just relocated into the fabric of the walkways, the walls, the cisterns. In a sense, the settlement is built of ancestors, each root and palm plank infused with the substance of the people who shaped it. That's not a metaphor to them; it's simply what happens.

So they live, these forty people, in a room with living walls and a floor of water and a ceiling of wings. They are wet and warm and never alone, never silent, never still. They are a part of the surging din, not above it. They edit the endless novel of growth, and they do it with bone and tooth and the patience of a thousand years of practice, and when they sleep at night in their hanging cocoons, the frogsong pulses through the vines and rocks them gently, and the old woman combs her wool and listens to the roots ticking as they grow, and somewhere a new shoot pierces a mat and begins its journey toward the light, and by morning someone will have guided it sideways, and someone will have added a new whistle to their name, and the world will be just as it was, and entirely new.
