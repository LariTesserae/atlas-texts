They don’t walk so much as uncoil.

You might go hours before you see one—days, if you’re not looking right. The light is bad, the noise eats attention, and they have a way of resting so still they become just another ridge of that fibrous flooring, a darker mass in the shadow of a frond. But eventually you notice: a shape that’s too symmetrical, too slow. It’s not growing; it’s breathing.

The first thing that reads as *alive* is the surface texture. Where the plant walls are smooth or sharp, these bodies are a landscape of small, overlapping plates, like the scutes of a pangolin, but softer, with a dull gleam of chitin that’s been worn matte by the damp. The colour is the deep red-brown of the older bark, mottled with grey where mineral deposits from the constant condensation have crusted. They are large—when one stands upright (a rare thing, and slow, so slow), it might be three meters tall, but most of their life is spent in a low, horizontal slouch that spreads their mass across the floor, belly-down on the sinewy mat, limbs folded in a complicated geometry. At rest, they look architectural, like fallen pillars wrapped in a thin pelt of lichen.

They have faces, but not like anything surface-born. The head is a blunt wedge, wider at the back, tapering toward a front that is mostly a deep-set sensory band—a horizontal seam from which pale blue light sometimes leaks, identical in hue to the bioluminescent crust. That seam isn’t an eye; it’s a line of heat-sensitive pits and vibration detectors, a continuous organ that reads the world as a pattern of pressure and temperature. Below it, a mouth that rarely opens, lipless and round, a sphincter of muscle ringed with fine, hair-like cilia that taste the air. They have no external ears—how could they, in that roar? Instead, their whole body is an ear. The plates on their flanks and bellies pick up vibration directly from the ground, from the air, from the low-frequency thrum of the plant’s fluid channels, so that they live inside the sound like a fish lives in water, and the engine noise that would deafen a human is just another current, a background texture they feel rather than hear.

Their limbs are many, but it’s hard to count them because they merge. At full extension, you might see three pairs of primary arms—thick, segmented, ending in clusters of three or four digits that are not fingers but something more like the gripping roots of ivy, tough and prehensile, capable of hardening into rigid hooks or softening into gentle pads. But these arms can fuse along their length into a single, broader limb for heavy work, or split again when delicacy is needed. The same with the lower body: what seems like a thick, slug-like tail can divide into two or four stumpy legs, or all can retract into the central mass for resting. Their movement is a flowing, continuous reshaping, a slow-motion wave of tissue shifting from one configuration to another. They don’t hurry. There is no need. The cold slows metabolism to a crawl; they breathe perhaps once a minute, a shallow, silent compression of the flanks, and they can go without feeding for spans that might be months.

What do they eat? The plant. They are not parasites—it’s more like pruning, or a kind of internal maintenance. Using those mouth cilia, they rasp away microscopic layers of the living tissue, consuming the algal crust and the outermost cells, which regrow almost as quickly. Sometimes, at the base of a newly formed frond, they will find a node of softer, nutrient-rich matter, a pale gel that the plant extrudes as part of its growth cycle, and they’ll take that with care, leaving the structure intact. They do not need much; their energy comes as much from heat as from chemistry. The bioluminescent band on the head is not just a sense organ—it’s a radiator, a way of shedding excess warmth in the constant cold, and it doubles as a communication tool. In the dark, when one of them pulses that blue light slowly, deliberately, the signal carries for hundreds of meters through the open caverns, a silent language of pattern and duration that cuts through the noise because it’s light, not sound.

And yet, they do speak, after a fashion. When they come within touching distance—often a rare and deliberate event—they press their plates together and pass vibrations directly, body to body, a subsonic exchange that feels like a low hum through your bones if you’re standing too close. You’d never hear it over the pumps, but they do. They have names, histories, concepts of time that are geological, a culture of slow decisions made over centuries. They remember the arrival of the machines, because some of them were here. They don’t age; they just grow, adding plates, becoming more massive, until eventually they slow so much they become a permanent part of the landscape, a living mound that still breathes, still senses, but hasn’t moved in a thousand years. Those elders are indistinguishable from the plant’s own buttresses, and they are treated as sacred archives—when a decision must be made, a younger one will travel to press itself against that still-warm hide and receive the slow, deep vibrations of memory.

Their relationship with the machinery is complicated. They did not build it. Something else did—surface beings, perhaps, or an earlier species, now gone. The machines pump and churn and sometimes break. The dwellers have learned to tend them, not with tools of their own (they have none, their bodies are enough), but by reshaping themselves to fit into maintenance hatches, by using their grip-roots to turn frozen valves, by pressing their heat-sensing faces against hot pipes to read the health of whatever still runs. They do this because the machines serve a purpose: they keep the great plant from overgrowing. Without the rhythmic thump of the pistons, the plant would slowly fill these caverns with new tissue, closing the passages, sealing the air. The dwellers like the openness. They need the vast hollows to communicate with light, to travel without being forced to eat their way through. So they maintain the machinery with a kind of detached devotion, like someone keeping an old clock running long after its maker is gone.

There is no settlement, no village. Each dweller has a territory—a ridge, a cluster of root-arches, a ledge near a pump—and they overlap only occasionally, during the long, slow mating seasons that come every few decades, when the plant itself exudes a particular chemical in its dew. Then they converge from the farthest reaches, pulsing their lights in synchronised waves, pressing together in a mass of reconfiguring limbs. The young, when they are born, are tiny, no larger than a hand, pale and translucent, and they spend their first century clinging to the parent’s back, absorbing vibration and learning the light-code.

They have no art we’d recognise, but they shape the plant. Over centuries, they will nudge a frond’s growth by selective scraping, guiding it into a curve that catches the bioluminescence just so, or hollow out a resting place that perfectly frames a distant lamp. These are not functional modifications but something else—a slow, patient aesthetic, a way of making the cavern their own, of writing history into the walls. Walk through a particularly old territory and you’ll see patterns in the bark, swirls and spirals that echo the shape of their plates, the signature of a dweller who was ancient a thousand years ago and is now part of the floor.

If you stay long enough—and you’d have to stay a long time—you might learn to see them not as individuals but as a process. They are the plant’s consciousness, perhaps, the mind that grew from the body. Or they are a second organism, a symbiont that evolved in the deep to shape and be shaped by this vast, slow creature. Their language contains no word for “alone.” The concept doesn’t apply: the plant is always there, humming, breathing. The machines are there, pounding. Other dwellers are there, even if miles away, their light pulses visible as faint blue stars across an underground sky. And the elders are there, still dreaming, their slow thoughts spreading through the floor.

One of them, now, is watching you. It has been motionless twenty meters up, tucked into a vertical crevice where a root arches overhead, its plates aligned so perfectly with the bark that you passed beneath it twice without knowing. But it felt your footsteps—a new vibration, sharp and arrhythmic, nothing like the plant’s slow creak or the steady pump-beat. It has been tracking you with its heat-sense, a small, warm shape moving too fast, breathing too quickly. To it, you are a flicker, a brief flash of heat and noise, a curiosity. It does not feel threatened, only interested. In an hour or a day, it might descend, uncoiling limb by limb, to press its body to the spot where you stood and taste the air for what you left behind. Or it might not. It has time. It has all the time there is.

And if you were to stand perfectly still, to let the cold seep in and your breathing slow, you might see its light—a pale blue pulse from that head-seam, a single, deliberate flash that says, in a language of intervals and brightness, *I am here. I felt you. You may stay.* Then it would settle, merging back into the dark, and the engines would roar on, and the plant would grow another layer, and everything would be, for another long while, exactly as it has been.
