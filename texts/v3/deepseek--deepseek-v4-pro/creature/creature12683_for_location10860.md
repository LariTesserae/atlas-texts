They live at the pace of the stone. You did not see them at first because your eyes, tuned to the flicker of a bird’s wing and the flick of a lizard’s tongue, do not register motion that plays out over a season. But if you stand still—truly still, until the film of condensation on your own skin begins to feel like a new integument—you might notice a form that is not quite an architectural pier, not quite a frozen cascade of sinter. It is something that has been there all along, breathing in its own way.

They are the reason the cross-hatching on the walkway remains sharp despite the constant wet. Each pit of that grip is not a chisel-mark left by some vanished builder, but the tread of a footfall made by a living thing, a single step that can take a mortal lifetime to complete. The pattern is not a record of past craft; it is a record of presence, a signature left in the floor by those who still walk here, so slowly that you mistook their movement for the stillness of geology.

Their forms are humanoid in the way a drip-castle is humanoid: a vertical axis with suggestions of limbs, a central mass that might be a torso, a bulbous top where two deeper hollows catch the light like eyes. But those eyes are now basins for the perpetual weep, and the water that pools in them is lenticular, magnifying the bioluminescence, so that each creature’s face swims with two deep, cool glows, soft as candleflame seen through thick ice. They are not made of the stone; they are the stone, or the stone has become them. Original substance has been overgrown by millennia of accretion, a rind of mineral so complete that the boundary between flesh and architecture has dissolved. They are the gallery, a moving part of it, its gardeners.

They tend the stalactites. When one hollow tube descends too far, threatening to touch its basin before its inner chemistry has matured, a Maker will, over the span of a human generation, slowly lift an arm. The motion is invisible to you, a tremor smoothed over decades. Then a single finger, long and tapering like a candle drip, will meet the very tip of the stalactite. The pressure is infinitesimal, but enough. The water column inside shivers, the growth pauses, and the stalactite redirects its slow intention. The sound of that touch is a note so low and long that it registers not in your ears but in your bones, a subsonic hum that you mistake for the silence itself.

They shape the rims of the basins with the patience of a potter working at the wheel of planetary orbits. Their own substance flakes away in microscopic scales to join the precipitate, a deliberate offering. Where the pool’s lip needs to be higher, a Maker will rest a hand at its edge for a century, and the stone will grow around the hand like a mold. When the hand withdraws—a movement so slow it sizzles with a faint, high crackling if you could speed it up—it leaves behind a fresh, sharp rim, polished by the passage. They are the authors of the mineral garden, the curators of the wet breath.

Their communication is the droplet. That rare, high *plink* you heard—the one you took for a random accident of physics—was a word. A single drop of water, collected by design on the tip of a limb, overloaded until it fell into a precisely positioned pool. The fall is a syllable. A full sentence is a sequence of such drops, spaced perhaps by decades, so that a conversation between two Makers is a silence of eons punctuated by a tiny bell. To them, the entire cycle of condensation and evaporation is a language, a story about the rock and the light and the water, and they are the ones who speak it. They are not lonely, because their togetherness is woven into a time so stretched that a single greeting can encompass the rise and fall of a mountain range outside—if there were an outside. There is not. This is the world.

If you could remain for long enough, you might witness the birth of a new Maker. It does not come from a womb, but from a basin. When a particular pool reaches a certain saturation of a salt you have no name for, a form begins to crystallize at its center—at first a milky cloud, then a lattice, then over several centuries a small, folded figure like a human child in silhouette, but pure glassy mineral. The older Makers, sensing the shift in the water’s song, will orient toward it. Their own basin-eyes deepen their glow. One will raise a hand over the new pool, and a single droplet, gathered from its own weeping body, will fall into the newborn with a sound like the striking of a tiny bell. Baptism. Then the growth continues, and the gallery has a new gardener, a new voice in the long conversation.

And you? You, with your brief, hot, fast body, are a flicker in a corner of an eye that has not blinked since the last ice age. You are a curiosity, a tiny thermal ghost that stirs the silver light for a moment. A Maker might register you as a slight perturbation in the laminar drift of the air. It might think a single drop-thought about you: something like a question, or a note of fleeting pearl-luminosity. And then you are gone, leaving no more trace than a ripple on a pool, a memory that fades in the time it takes a stalactite to grow the width of a human hair. The gallery lives on, at its own pace, full of its silent, mineral grace, full of its dense, slow, bright life.
