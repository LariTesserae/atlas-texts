This is a place that has never known a name. It will be a thousand centuries before any sonar ping maps its walls, ten thousand more before a submersible’s lamp betrays its citizens. So the creatures that live here—every one of them—are provisional, pioneers of a geography still raw and settling. They are colonists of the unfinished, and their forms confess the extremity of the pact they have made with this gelid, dark, newly born water.

**The Architects**

The translucent worms—Oligochaetes of a clade no taxonomist will ever classify—are the city’s unthinking masons. Each is a blind filament of protoplasm sheathed in a cuticle so fine it vanishes under the faintest light. They are barely individuals; more like the fingers of a distributed physiology, a single vast impulse toward secretion. Their bodies are little more than a gut and a calcifying gland, and their mouths never cease moving, inching along the basalt, depositing a paste that hardens in the water’s cold into a nacre as hard as enamel and faintly opalescent. This they do without cease, generation upon generation, laying down laminae that spiral outward in obedience to a tropism no one worm understands. The architecture is inscribed in their chemistry: they build faster where the current is richest in dissolved silica and sulfides; they build slower where the water is still. Thus the city breathes its own shape into being, a self-regulating labyrinth.

Look closer at a single worm. Its transparency reveals a thread of a gut looped with a slow peristalsis, a tiny heart that beats once every twelve seconds, and along its flanks, a row of pinprick photophores that glow a steady cyan. These lights pulse in unison with its neighbors—a wave of cold fire that sweeps along a gallery wall, then reverses. The worms use this signal to synchronize their construction, to avoid collision, perhaps to attract mates who are indistinguishable from one another. When two worms meet, they twine briefly, exchange a packet of bioluminescent bacteria that colonizes new segments, then part. Reproduction is a lateral affair: fission, fragmentation, a parceling of the self. The dead are subsumed into the walls, their calcium reclaimed. So the city is also a graveyard, and the living crawl over the mineralized ghosts of their ancestors.

**The Dancers**

The shrimp with ghostly exoskeletons are *Thysanopoda minima*, a krill-relative shrunk to a sliver. Their carapaces are so thin that you can see their stomachs, hearts, even the faint greenish smear of their hepatopancreas. They drift in clouds so dense that from a distance they appear as a silver haze, a perpetual snowfall suspended in the water. Their excessively long antennae—ten times their body length—are not sensory alone; they are communal nets. When feeding, the shrimp orient in parallel, antennae interlaced to form a living sieve that strains particulate matter from the sluggish current. They rise and fall in the water column in a diurnal rhythm dictated not by the sun (which does not exist) but by the faint thermal pulses emanating from the rift below. Every twelve hours, a micro-tremor sends a whisper of warmth upward, and the shrimp ascend as one, a ballet of hunger and instinct.

Sometimes a shrimp will drift too close to a nacre tower. Then a swift shadow: the ribbon-nemertean, *Baseodiscus subterraneus*, unspools from a crevice, its body a flat, extensible muscle that can elongate to twenty times its resting length. It does not hunt with speed but with a terrible patience. It extrudes a transparent, adhesive mucus that snares the shrimp’s antennae, then slowly, with an inexorable contraction, reels itself around its prey. The shrimp’s struggles are brief; the nemertean’s mucus is laced with a paralytic that stills the delicate limbs in seconds. Then the ribbon-worm everts its proboscis, a blind, branching gut that envelops the shrimp and begins digestion externally. The city’s thoroughfares are littered with the husks of such meals—empty carapaces that drift down and lodge in the filigree, becoming scaffolding for a new generation of tube-dwellers.

**The Weavers and the Gardeners**

The isopods, no bigger than rice grains, are the city’s sanitation engineers and its gardeners. They belong to the genus *Macrostylis*, but adapted to a life of meticulous scavenging. Their bodies are flattened, segmented, and covered with microscopic setae that filter bacterial mats from the water. They trundle along the causeways, grazing the bioluminescent films, their mandibles leaving tiny paths of darkness across the glowing surfaces. But they also tend the chemosynthetic gardens. In the crevices where methane seeps, where mats of pink and mustard bacteria bloom, the isopods congregate in husbandry. They do not simply feed; they aerate the mats with the beating of their pleopods, and they transport bacterial seeds—dormant cysts—on their bristled legs, inoculating new cracks where the rift’s slow seeping creates fresh seeps. It is a symbiosis so ancient that the isopods’ guts have evolved chambers specifically to culture sulfide-oxidizing bacteria, which they then milk for nutrients. In this way, the city’s primary production is not entirely wild; it is farmed by tiny crustaceans that know nothing of their role.

**The Six-Armed Star**

The predator starfish, a *Leptasterias* species, is a centimeter across and possesses six arms where its shallower kin have five. This extra limb is a new adaptation, perhaps a response to the cold, a redundancy in a body plan that is all radial redundancy. It moves with a hydraulic slowness, its tube feet extending and retracting with a gentle, rhythmic sucking. It is a specialist in hunting tube-dwellers: sedentary polychaetes that build tiny calcareous tubes on the walls. The starfish drapes itself over a cluster of tubes, its arms curling to cover as many openings as possible. Then it everts its stomach, a filmy, translucent sac that it pushes inside the tubes, digesting the worms alive in their homes. The process takes hours, and through the nacre walls you can see the faint cloud of enzymes at work, the slow dissolution of tissue. The starfish does not hurry; nothing here hurries. When it finishes, it detaches, leaving behind a row of empty tubes, little pipes that will be colonized by isopods or used as nurseries by the shrimp.

**The Fish of Bone and Membrane**

The fish that flutter like dying leaves are not true fish but larval stages—*Leptocephalus*-like larvae of some eel or gulper that never grow to adulthood. In this cold, metamorphosis is arrested; they remain in their transparent, ribbon-like larval form, their bodies reduced to a notochord, a spinal nerve, and a gelatinous envelope. They are composed mostly of water and a few essential proteins. They feed on the bacteria directly, absorbing dissolved organics through their skin, and they reproduce by paedomorphosis—laying eggs that hatch into equally transparent, equally ethereal young. They have no scales, no pigmentation, no teeth. Their eyes are simple pigmented cups that sense only the gradient of bioluminescence, steering them toward the brighter patches where bacterial productivity is highest. When a predator approaches, they vanish—not by fleeing, but by becoming utterly still, their transparency so complete that only a faint outline betrays them. They are the ghosts of fish, the suggestion of vertebrates in a world dominated by invertebrates.

**The Light-Speakers**

The bioluminescent bacteria, scattered in patches and curated by worms and isopods, are not passive. They are *Aliivibrio* of a unique strain, and they respond to quorum sensing with a fidelity that approaches a language. When a critical density of cells is reached, they glow in synchrony, creating pulses that ripple across a tower’s surface. The worms’ photophores are seeded with these bacteria; the two species have become inextricable. And the light is not merely beacon or display—it is a signal that penetrates the water, absorbed and re-emitted by layers of nacre until the whole city shimmers with a sub-aquatic aurora. Other creatures have learned to read these pulses. The shrimp change direction when the light quickens; the starfish constricts when it slows; the isopods flock to new blooms as if called. There is a grammar here, a syntax of cold light, that coordinates the metropolis without a central mind. The city flickers with news: a methane surge, an intruder, a shift in the current. All of it encoded in the language of bacteria that do not even know they are speaking.

**The Unseen Majority**

And then there are the invisible. The water itself is alive with viruses—phages that prey on the bacteria, keeping blooms in check. Archea of the *Thaumarchaeota* clade oxidize ammonia seeping from the new rock, forming a second layer of primary production invisible to all but the chemosynthetic mats. Tiny flagellates, heliozoans with silica skeletons, drift and engulf even smaller prey. The cleft is a soup of nano- and picoplankton, a microbial web that underpins everything, and its density is astonishing: a single milliliter holds ten million cells. This is the true population, the hidden foundation that never leaves a body to describe, yet whose collective respiration warms the water by a thousandth of a degree—a warmth detectable only to the finely tuned antennae of the shrimp.

**The Unbuilding**

Even here, there is death and dissolution. The city is constantly being decomposed. Fungus-like oomycetes, filaments that creep along the nacre, secrete acids that slowly dissolve the oldest structures, reclaiming the calcium for the water column. They are the deconstruction crew, the quiet saboteurs that prevent the metropolis from sealing itself into a solid mass. They work in rhythm with the rift’s tremors: when a quake cracks a tower, the oomycetes surge into the fissures, softening the shards into a paste that the worms can then reabsorb. So the city is a cycling of matter, a flux of construction and ruin that holds at a dynamic equilibrium. Nothing accumulates forever; the very worms that build are built from the dissolved bodies of their forebears.

**The Intruder’s Aftermath**

Now consider the heat that you, the observer, have brought. Your presence, even brief, is a catastrophe on this scale. Your body is a furnace of 37°C, a thermal bloom that sends shockwaves through the gelid water. For a minute, the current warps; the worms pause, their calcifying glands stuttering; the shrimp scatter, their communal nets breaking; the starfish retracts its stomach and freezes. Your exhaled breath—if you were breathing—would introduce a cocktail of microbubbles and organic volatiles that would, for days, drive the bacteria into a frenzy of division. But you are a brief intrusion. The cold reasserts itself. The rift trembles on. The worms begin again, depositing nacre over the faint scorch marks your warmth left on the basalt. The isopods will graze the new bloom of bacteria that your shedding skin cells triggered. Within a week, the city will have integrated you into its fabric, your lingering molecules woven into a spire, a thoroughfare, a pulse of light. And the metropolis will continue its silent, slow-motion construction, having never once registered that a god passed through.
