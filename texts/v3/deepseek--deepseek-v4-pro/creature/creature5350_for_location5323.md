At first, you mistake him for a piece of the architecture—just another fold in the bark, a darker knot in the undulating green. Then the knot shifts, and you realize you are looking at a back, a shoulder blade, the slow articulation of a spine uncurling from a crouch. He has been there all along, tucked into a hollow of the great stalk, and now he turns, and you see what lives here.

His name, if you could ask and he could answer in a way you’d understand, is something like the sound of a pebble dropped into still water from a great height—a single percussive note, a ripple, a fading. Call him Kith, a syllable small enough to fit in a human mouth, though it does him no justice. He is old, though age works strangely here. His body has been growing for what you’d call sixty years, maybe seventy, but his eyes—and you will notice the eyes soon—hold a patience that feels geological.

He is humanoid, broadly, in the way a deep-sea anglerfish and a horse are both vertebrates. The plan is there, but it has been revised so many times by the demands of this place that the original blueprint is a rumor at best. His limbs are long—too long, with an extra joint in the forearm that lets him fold and unfold like a fern frond. His hands are four-fingered, each digit extending into a delicate, retractable claw of what looks like polished obsidian but is actually hardened keratin, black and glassy, used for scoring the living wood of the stalks during harvest. The webbing between his fingers is thin and translucent, veined with the same faint amber that pulses through the canopy. When he spreads his hand, it’s a leaf catching light; when he closes it, it’s a tool.

His skin is the color of the rock walls after millennia of water-wear: a mottled gray-green that shifts as you watch, chromatophores beneath the surface adjusting to the dapple. This is not camouflage, not exactly—it’s conversation. His skin broadcasts his mood, his health, his attention. Right now it’s showing faint ripples of a deeper green, which you will learn means curiosity edged with caution. A paler, silvery bloom along his collarbones and down the ridge of his spine—these are the places his body most wants to be seen—signals openness, a slow welcome. When he is frightened or angry, the chromatophores will pull tight and drain to the color of old bone, and you will know to keep your distance.

His face is where the strangeness settles most intimately. The skull is high and slightly compressed at the temples, as if shaped by the constant, gentle pressure of the water. His mouth is small, lipless, designed for eating soft, drifting things—the jelly-bodies of waterborne larvae, the tender inner flesh of the stalk-fruit—and it does not smile. Expression lives elsewhere: in the angle of the head, the flare of the breathing-slits that run in three vertical lines down each side of his throat, and most of all in the eyes.

The eyes are large, dark, and placed slightly forward but with an articulation that lets each one move independently. They have no whites, only a deep, glossy iris the color of the water at its deepest, ringed with a faint copper halo that catches the light. The pupil is not round but a horizontal crescent, which gives him a perpetual look of serene, half-lidded contemplation. When he focuses both eyes on you, the crescents widen into circles, and the effect is of a sudden aperture opening, a camera adjusting to let in more light, more *you*. It is startling. It feels like being seen for the first time.

He does not speak with his mouth. Above the breathing-slits, on either side of his larynx, are two tympanic membranes that vibrate when he pushes water through a specialized chamber in his throat. The sound he produces is a series of clicks, whistles, and long, pure tones that carry through the water like a bell struck far away. His language is musical, tonal, and deeply tied to the acoustics of the chamber. A sentence might begin with a low, thrumming hum that resonates in the stalk behind him, turning the entire dwelling into a speaker, and end with a rapid series of clicks that bounce off the distant rock wall and return as a faint echo. He is never silent; even at rest, his body hums faintly with the subsonic frequency of the chamber’s own resonance, a note he has learned to harmonize with over a lifetime.

He wears very little. A belt of woven sinew circles his waist, from which hang a few tools: a hooked knife of obsidian, a hollowed bone needle, a small mesh bag containing what look like seeds, or eggs, or both. Across his chest, a harness of braided vine supports a living creature—a small, soft-bodied polyp shaped like a flattened star, its translucent body pulsing gently with bioluminescent light. This is his companion, his lamp, his familiar. It feeds on the oils of his skin and the drifting microbes in the water, and in return it glows when he commands, silently, through a chemical whisper you cannot detect. When he descends into the darker hollows of the stalk-dwellings, the polyp casts a circle of soft, blue-white light, and the shadows of his long fingers dance on the walls.

His dwelling reflects him. It is carved into the living heart of one of the great stalks, a vertical chamber that spirals upward for perhaps four meters, its walls still alive, still pulsing with the slow sap-tide. The entrance is a narrow oval, and inside, ledges of softer, spongy tissue serve as resting places, storage nooks, a sleep-hollow lined with shedded membrane-skins that have been softened over time to the texture of felt. Tools are tucked into the fleshy seams of the walls. A collection of polished stones—smooth, black, volcanic—sits in a niche, arranged in a pattern that means something to him. Perhaps an ancestor. Perhaps a calendar. Perhaps a poem.

He is not alone here, but solitude is his baseline. The hundred or so other inhabitants of this hamlet are scattered through the water column, each in their own stalk-dwelling or frond-woven pod, and they communicate over distances with the long, resonant tones that carry through the liquid. Their social structure is loose, familial but not tribal, more like a pod of whales than a village of humans. They gather when the canopy blooms—a rare event, once every few dozen seasons—and then they sing together, a chorus that lasts for days and sets the entire chamber humming like a plucked string. Kith remembers the last bloom. He was young. His skin was a brighter green then, and the song, he will tell you, if you can learn to listen, was the most beautiful thing he has ever felt.

His life is slow and patterned, but not empty. He tends the frond-platforms that grow from his stalk, coaxing them into useful shapes with careful scoring and gentle weighting. He harvests the soft, sweet lining of certain seed-pods, which he ferments in sealed hollows to make a food that lasts through the dimmer seasons. He swims—not with kicks or frantic strokes, but with an undulating, full-body pulse that ripples from his spine to his fingertips, a motion that looks more like a plant swaying in current than a creature propelling itself. When he hunts, he does not chase but waits, still as the rock, until a school of fingerlings passes close enough for his hand to snap out and close. He has never known hunger.

He has known death. The bones of his predecessors are incorporated into the detritus of the crater floor, and sometimes, when he swims low to collect a stray tool or seek a particular stone, he sees them—long limbs folded, skulls resting in the silt, eye sockets dark and peaceful. His people do not bury their dead; they release them to the bottom, where the slow processes of the water and the microbial life return them to the system. He has taken the obsidian knife from a set of bones he believes belonged to his mother’s mother. The blade still holds its edge. He thinks of her when he uses it.

And he thinks. His mind is not like yours. It is not a monologue but a flow, a current of images and sensations and tonal memories that drift and eddy and repeat. He does not separate himself from the water the way you separate yourself from air. The water is his interior, his breath, his thought-medium. When he is sad—a mood his skin shows as a faint, bruised purple along his flanks—he does more than feel the sadness in his chest; he tastes it in the water around him, a slight change in the dissolved chemistry that others can sense from ten meters away. His emotions are public things, shared in the liquid medium, and so he has learned, over a long life, to shape them carefully. Not to hide them—that would be like holding your breath—but to let them flow through him without resistance, a wave that rises and passes, leaving the water clear again.

If you stayed here long enough, if you let the water fill your lungs and the slow time seep into your bones, you might learn to understand him. You might learn that the pattern of polished stones in his niche is a song rendered physical, a map of the blooming cycle, a prayer. You might learn the name his mother gave him—the pebble-drop, the ripple, the fading tone. You might learn that he is not lonely, exactly, but that he sometimes dreams of the world above, the thin air and the bright, fast sun, and wonders if he could ever breathe something so insubstantial.

But you won't stay here long enough. The canopy will open—you will feel the bellows of the dome begin to exhale, a slow pressure-change, a current drawing you upward—and you will go, back into the mountain air, back into your world of edges and dryness and quick, nervous speech. And Kith will remain, a shape in the green-lit liquid, one hand lifted in what you might, after all, recognize as a wave goodbye. His skin will bloom silver for a moment, the color of brief connection, and then he will turn, and fold himself back into the bark, and become almost impossible to see again.
