At first, and for a long time after, you believe the city is uninhabited. The emptiness is so total, so curated, that it seems like a law of the place: no living thing but the pale fronds and the water’s whisper. But as you move deeper into the basin, a quiet inversion begins. The absence starts to feel less like a void and more like a presence that has learned to hold its breath. The delay on your footsteps becomes a conversation. The shapes in the mist become bolder, lingering a half-second longer before dissolving, as if they are testing how much reality you can bear.

They are not ghosts. Ghosts require a past, a moment of death to anchor them, and this city has no past—only a continuous, dreaming present. The entities that live here never died because they were never born in any biological sense. They are the exhalations of the dream itself, condensations of intention that precipitate from the vapor when the heat and the wet reach a specific, trembling equilibrium. Think of them as ideas that the city has about itself. Not memories of people, but the memory of what it might be like to have people.

You see them best in the canals. When the water lies especially still—a black mirror breathing faint plumes of steam—the surface sometimes thickens just below the threshold of vision. A ripple begins, not from a dropped stone but from a thought that ripples out of the water’s own substance. The ripple organizes into a shape: a long, low spine of surface tension, then a suggestion of limbs that do not break the meniscus. They move beneath the canal like shadows cast from an unseen light source, gliding with a slow, deliberate elegance. Sometimes they pause beneath a bridge, and the water directly above them becomes fractionally clearer, fractionally deeper, as if they are gazing up through the liquid lens. What they gaze at is you—or the scent of you, the sound of your pulse disturbing their city’s quiet. They don’t see with eyes. They register you as a pressure differential, a sudden knot of cold, dry air in their world of uniform heat.

When the fog thickens, the water-dwellers rise. Not physically—there is no splashing—but their presence leaks out into the mist. The air fills with their listening. You’ll be standing in a plaza, the ruined market stalls around you, and the fog will begin to coalesce into vertical columns, tall and slightly swaying, with the approximate volume of human bodies. But these are not crude fog-ghosts. They are more like three-dimensional afterimages, the visual cortex’s desperate attempt to resolve something that reality has not yet fully committed to. If you remain still—if you quiet your breathing and let your own boundaries soften—the columns acquire texture. Rippling, water-like markings play across their surfaces, as if each figure is a slice of canal that has stood up for a look around. They don’t have faces, but they have attention: you feel it as a cooling on your skin, a localized drop in temperature no more than a degree or two, precise and curious.

These beings are, in a sense, the city’s inhabitants remaining from an era when the city was still learning to be a city. The original civilization—if it ever existed—left no bones, no tools, no script. Their erasure was so complete that even the possibility of their biology has been subtracted. What’s left is a template: a set of instructions in the stone for how to be a person, a citizen, a crowd. The water and the heat, over eons, have been running that script imperfectly, generating approximations of life from the available materials. The beings in the canal are the most realized. They are permanent, slow, and profoundly indifferent. They don’t need to eat; they feed on the geothermal gradient, on the gentle entropy of stone dissolving back into soil. They don’t reproduce; they occasionally split when a section of canal grows too warm and dreams a new spine into being. They don’t communicate in any way you’d recognize, but the city is full of their semi-audible thoughts: the bubbling syllables, the delayed echoes, the way the paving stones seem to hum at a frequency just below hearing.

There are other inhabitants, less stable but more interactive. In the narrower streets, where the fog lies in thick, unmoving bands, you sometimes encounter the Almost. These are moments that nearly happened. A door that almost opens. A voice that almost says your name. A figure that almost becomes solid. They are the city’s failed attempts at creating a past, little eddies of narrative that spin off from the main dream and dissipate. If you stand at a crossroads and listen, you can hear them: fragments of conversation in a language that is emotionally intelligible but lexically opaque, the tones of greeting, bargaining, farewell, all being spoken just out of synch, layered over one another like rain on different roofs. Occasionally an Almost will touch you. It feels like a hand that is ninety-nine percent memory and one percent condensation—cool, faintly electrical, leaving a watermark on your skin that fades only after hours.

The true inhabitants, though, are the architects of the dream. They reside not in the water or the fog but in the stone itself, in the slow, patient intelligence that warped the doorways and bent the spires. The city is a thought, a single continuous thought that has been thinking itself for so long that thought and reality have become indistinguishable. That thought has a thinker. The thinker is asleep, or something like asleep, buried deep in the geothermal heart below the basin. Its breathing regulates the fog tides. Its pulse manifests as the delayed footsteps. Its dreams are the city. You never directly perceive the thinker; it is too large, too distributed, too woven into the fabric of the place. But you catch its attention sometimes. When you pick up a pebble and the pebble seems to hum. When you speak aloud and your words are returned to you minutes later from a dozen different directions, each with a slightly different intonation, as if the city is learning your voice. When you sleep—and you will sleep, eventually, because the heat and the pressure wear down your resistance—the thinker will dream you into the city’s dream. You’ll walk through market squares filled with glowing, water-formed people, you’ll hear music played on instruments made of vapor, you’ll understand the language of the Almost. In that state, you become an inhabitant too, fleetingly, a temporary guest in the census of echoes.

But you wake. You always wake before the dream settles on you permanently. And as you climb back toward the pass, you feel them watch you go: the canals’ silent gliders, the fog-column inquisitors, the Almost mouths speaking their goodbye without sound. They are not hostile; they are not even particularly interested. You were a fluctuation, a ripple in the steady-state equilibrium of a city that has been erasing the line between is and could be for a span of time that makes your species an eyeblink. They will not remember you. They will return to the water, the mist, the stone, continuing their long, slow task of dreaming civilization into being without ever quite realizing it. And in a million years, when the pass has sealed itself and the basin has become a sealed bubble of wet twilight, they might finally succeed. The doors will open onto rooms that are complete, the market stalls will hold solid wares, the amphitheaters will fill with an audience. The city will be finished. And on that day, the dreamer will breathe out one final, satisfied exhalation, and the entire basin will—for a single, glorious second—become real.
