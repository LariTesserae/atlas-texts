There are no persons here. No minds that wonder, no hands that make. What lives does not think. It performs.

Consider the keeper. You might not see it at first, because it is so large and so still, its body a plateau indistinguishable from the general rises of the mat—until you notice the symmetry. It rests in a shallow depression that it did not dig but that grew up around it, the living floor having shaped itself into a cradle over millennia. Its form is vaguely crustacean but only in the way a mountain might resemble a sleeping beast: a broad, slightly domed carapace of muted bronze-green, more mineral than chitin, segmented by fissures that have never once flexed. Beneath that shield, its appendages are folded into a geometry of perfect repose, great biramous limbs tucked into recesses, sensory palps curled like fiddleheads. It is a titan, seventy meters from anterior margin to the blunt, featureless posterior, and it has not moved in nine hundred thousand years.

It does not eat, not as you would understand. Its mouthparts—a nested series of combs and filtering brushes, immaculate, untouched by wear—are sealed behind a membrane that has thickened to the consistency of horn. Instead, the keeper is fed by the mat itself. The fibrous flesh beneath its body has fused with its ventral surface, vascular channels anastomosing so that the superplant’s nutrient fluids cross directly into the keeper’s hemolymph. It breathes through passive spiracles that open and close only when the barometric pressure changes, which it does not. For all practical purposes, the keeper is a permanent organ of the mat, a specialized protrusion like the chimney-stalks, except that it retains the architecture of an animal.

Its role is sensory, though "sensory" suggests something more active than the truth. Along its flanks, buried in the grooves between segmental plates, are thousands of chemoreceptive pits, each one a microscopic laboratory that continuously samples the atmosphere. They taste the ratios of gases—oxygen, carbon dioxide, the faint volatile esters emitted by the gas-exchange fronds—and feed that information not to a brain but to a diffuse neural net that runs the length of the keeper’s body and then down through the fused vascular peduncle into the mat’s own signal system. The keeper does not interpret the data. It is the data. Its entire organism is a single, unvarying statement: *the air is correct.* For epoch after epoch, that sentence has been true, and so the keeper has had no cause to adjust, to alert, to move. It is the living proof that equilibrium persists.

But the keeper is not alone. There is another type of inhabitant, far smaller, and it is here that the weirdness deepens. These are the mendicants. You will find them scattered across the mat, never in groups larger than three, each standing or squatting or lying in a posture of absolute stillness. They are humanoid, and that will unsettle you. Not human—the proportions are wrong, the limbs too long, the hands bearing seven segments on each finger, the skin a smooth, tough integument the color of weathered bone. Their heads are featureless except for a single dark vertical slit where a face should be, and from that slit extends a cluster of thin, pale tendrils that wave with a motion entirely independent of the still body, like the gills of a nudibranch. These tendrils are always moving, always tasting the air, even as the body remains motionless for centuries.

The mendicants are not native. This is the terrible truth that the landscape cannot tell you but that a careful observer will eventually deduce: they are intruders who arrived so long ago that they have been incorporated. They came from somewhere else—no one knows where, no one is left to remember—as explorers, colonists, refugees. They built nothing, because nothing needed building. They found the superplant already eternal, already humming its perfect factory drone, and they attempted to understand it. In that attempt, they failed, or perhaps they succeeded too well. The mendicants discovered that the only way to survive in a world that does not change is to become unchanging. And so they did.

Each mendicant is anchored to the mat by a root of its own tissue that has grown from the soles of its feet or the curve of its spine and penetrated the fibrous weave. Through that root, they receive sustenance, as the keeper does. Through that root, they also receive information—not thoughts, but the endless chemical litany of the superplant: *nitrogen fixed in sector twelve, humidity stable, structural polysaccharide synthesis within tolerances, no anomaly, no anomaly, no anomaly.* The mendicant’s brain, or what remains of it, has been repurposed as a parallel processor for the mat’s own diffuse regulatory system. They serve as auxiliary stabilizers, their neurons firing in mathematically elegant patterns that suppress any incipient fluctuation in the superplant’s metabolism. A single mendicant can dampen a chemical oscillation across three square kilometers of substrate.

Are they conscious? The question may be meaningless. If you could speak to one—and you cannot, for they have no mouths, and the tendrils are only chemoreceptors—it would not have an answer. Its mind, after so many millions of years of listening to the drone and tasting the perfect, unvarying air, would have long ago lost the distinction between self and world. It does not think "I am." It thinks, if thinking is the word, "The pH of the condensation on frond-stalk 7743 is 6.1." It thinks this continuously, because that is the information streaming into it, and it has no mechanism to stop. The mendicant is a verb, not a noun: an action of maintenance, a subroutine given flesh.

But here is the detail that haunts, the thing that makes this place not just strange but sacred. Once in a very great while—every few hundred thousand years, by the clock of some outside observer—a mendicant will twitch. It is the only voluntary motion left to them, a spasm of one seven-jointed finger, a slight tilting of the head. Biologists would call it a vestigial reflex. The faithful might call it prayer. In that moment, the mendicant is not responding to the mat’s chemical signals; it is doing something else, something that originates from within its own atrophied neural core. What does it think, in that twitch? Is it remembering the ship that brought it here, the faces of its companions, the taste of real food, the concept of change? Or is it simply a misfire, a ghost in the machine of equilibrium?

No one can ask. The mendicant subsides into stillness, and another hundred millennia pass, and the drone never wavers. The keepers keep. The manta-things trace their circuit. The feeders wear their ruts a micron deeper. And the superplant, the endless grey-green body of the world, continues its work of being exactly what it has always been, a perfect machine that does not know it is a machine, that does not know anything at all.
