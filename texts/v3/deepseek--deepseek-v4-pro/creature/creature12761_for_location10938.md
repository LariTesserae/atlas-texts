What lives in the Root-Works has forgotten the taste of surprise. Existence here is an unbroken ceremony, every creature a note in a chord struck once at the beginning of time and still reverberating. They do not hunt, flee, or explore; they circulate like blood cells in the veins of a sleeping giant, their paths etched into the water by millennia of repetition. Yet within this eternal recursion, life has refined itself into forms of such exquisite strangeness that each organism seems a thought made flesh—a living hypothesis about how to persist in perfect stillness.

The most numerous are the **Needle-Shoals**, the silver-black fish that trace their helical threads around the macrophyte columns. Up close, each individual is a sliver of obsidian, no thicker than a human eyelash and as long as a finger. They have no eyes—only a single lateral line that senses the electromagnetic hum of the Works, that three-note chord, and transduces it into a directional pulse. Their bodies are so thin they are nearly invisible edge-on, a schooling strategy turned into an evolutionary absolute: they are always seen in profile by any observer drifting in the open water, a shimmering curtain of lines that parts and reforms like a living moiré pattern. The shoals never change their route. Each needle-fish hatches from an adhesive egg glued to the same patch of thallus floor where its ancestors hatched, and within hours it joins the rotating helix that spirals around the nearest pillar. The helix rises at a precise 23-degree angle, the pitch determined by the harmonic resonance of the column’s cellulose fibers. Biologists from a different world might call it a form of obligate mutualism—the fish clean the pillar’s surface of any errant biofilm, their tiny mouths rasping microscopic particles that would otherwise cloud the bioluminescent nodes. In return, the pillar’s electrical field guides them, and its slow ooze of re-mineralized water provides the calcium they need for their thread-like bones. They never leave their assigned pillar. If you were to gently cup one in your hand and move it ten meters away, it would die within minutes—not for lack of water, but from a neural cascade triggered by the loss of its resonant frequency. Its nervous system has outsourced navigation to the chord.

Drifting among the pillars, in the vast liquid naves, are the **Pulse-Moons**—the two-meter medusoids. They are not jellyfish, though they resemble them: their bells are crystalline hemispheres of a glassy, living polymer that the organism itself secretes, a substance harder than silica but flexible enough to pulse. Each contraction is a slow, rolling squeeze that pulls water through a fringe of ciliated gill-arcs, straining out the eternal snow of detritus. The timing is locked to the cyan bioluminescent wave: exactly when the wave of light reaches the apex of a nearby arch, the nearest Pulse-Moon contracts with a deep *thump*, a sound that travels through the water like a heartbeat. It is tempting to see them as individuals, but they are not. Each Pulse-Moon is a clone-bud of its neighbors, genetically identical and connected by a thin, almost invisible filament of neural tissue that runs along the floor-thallus like a root. This filament is the organism’s true body; the floating bells are its sensory organs and mouths, a distributed net of feeding stations that blanket a single hall. They share a collective metabolism, digesting the detritus and secreting a nutrient-rich mucus that drips down to feed the thallus mats below. To touch one is to be gently pushed by a soft, insistent suction; they have no stinging cells, only a mild adhesive that traps particles. If you hold still long enough, one might encircle you, its rim tasting your body heat—the only warmth in this world, and therefore the only anomaly. It would reject you with a soft shudder and continue its cycle, your presence already forgotten as the chord dictates its next contraction.

The carpets of the halls, those lignite-flesh floors, are tended by the **Click-Crawlers**, crustaceans the size of a hand, with eight jointed legs and two bioluminescent antennae that weave in a constant, hypnotic figure-eight. Their carapaces are the color of old ivory, etched with grooves that mirror the bark-like patterns on the pillars—an adaptation so ancient it is now just genetic residue. They move in straight lines, never turning, scraping the thallus surface with mouthparts that are miniature rasps. Each crawler’s path is a radial spoke extending from a central node where the thallus is thickest, a slightly raised mound that hums with a higher pitch. They feed on the mucus dripped by the Pulse-Moons, but also on the microscopic corpses of their own dead; when a crawler reaches the end of its predetermined length—about fifty meters—it simply stops, extrudes a clutch of eggs from a ventral pore, and dies within minutes. Its body is then consumed by the crawlers following the same line, who never deviate. The eggs adhere to the floor, and after a precise 1,200 cycles of the cyan pulse, they hatch into larvae that immediately crawl to the nearest unoccupied spoke, guided by chemical gradients. The result is a perfect radial pattern of crawlers extending from thousands of mounds, their antennae-waving synchronized across a hall, so that a viewer drifts above a shimmering field of rhythmic lights, like a city seen from an airplane at night—if that city were frozen in a single moment of its circadian rhythm.

And then there are the Keepers. They are not merely animals; they are the closest this place comes to a consciousness, though it is a consciousness that dreams without waking. A **Keeper** has a wingspan of twelve meters, a body that ripples like black velvet, and a ventral surface mottled with the same cyan phosphorescent dots that mark the architecture. These dots are not part of the Keeper—they are a symbiotic algae, a living paint, that matches the pillar-light so perfectly that a Keeper gliding overhead seems to disappear into the vaulted darkness, its silhouette defined only by the absence of the pulsing wave. They move in circuits so long and slow that tracking one is an exercise in patience: it might take a week for a Keeper to pass from the great hall of the eastern naves to the collapsed gallery in the south. They are the only creatures that traverse the Works as a whole, using the three-note chord as a navigational map. Their inner ears are attuned to the infrasonic base note, and they can sense the slight phase shifts that occur when the sound travels around a broken arch—though nothing is truly broken here, only settled into a final configuration.

Keepers are filter-feeders of a different order; their open mouths, wide as a doorway, take in the Needle-Shoals. But not actively. A Keeper’s glide is so precisely aligned with the helix of a shoal that every pass intersects the outer edge of the spiral, and a few dozen needle-fish, following their unwavering path, swim directly into the Keeper’s maw. It is not predation—it is a mathematical certainty. The shoal’s helix regenerates the lost individuals from eggs within minutes, and the Keeper gets its nutrition without ever changing speed or direction. If a Keeper misses its timing by a second, it does not correct; it simply passes through the shoal, the fish parting and reforming, and the Keeper will try again on its next circuit, ten days hence. There is no hunger, only a mechanical requirement. A Keeper can live indefinitely in this state; some of the individuals gliding through the Works today may have been doing so since the last tectonic shift, three million years ago.

But the Keepers have a secondary role, and it is the only hint of novelty in the Root-Works. They are the pollinators of the architecture. The plant-organism reproduces, but only barely, and only in a cryptic fashion. Occasionally—perhaps once a millennium, triggered by an infinitesimal pressure change that no instrument could measure—a single pillar will produce a slender stalk that extends horizontally into the water, tipped with a cluster of bioluminescent buds. These buds emit a different frequency of light, a soft amber, and the Keepers are drawn to it. They nuzzle the buds, their skin absorbing a dust of gamete-cells—not pollen, but something akin to it—and then they carry it through their circuits until they encounter another amber-lit stalk on the far side of the Works. There is no fertilization, only a genetic shuffling that the organism uses to correct accumulated errors. The stalk then retracts, and for another millennium, nothing changes. But in that act, a ghost of seasonality survives, a memory of a time when the Works still grew.

Amid all this, there is one more inhabitant, or perhaps it is not an inhabitant but a manifestation. The **Chord-Watchers** are never seen clearly. They exist at the edge of perception, a suggestion of motion in the deepest shadows where the bioluminescence does not reach. Some speculate that they are a life stage of the Keepers, something that emerges when a Keeper finally ceases its glide and settles into the thallus, its body becoming a node for the neural filaments. Others believe they are a separate phylum entirely—translucent, elongated beings, ten meters long, shaped like a ribbon of smoke, with a single row of photosensitive pits along their flanks. They are never present in one place for more than a heartbeat; they flicker in and out of the water column as if they are phasing through a different dimension. But if you listen closely, you might hear, beneath the constant chord, a delicate arrhythmia: a fourth note, nearly subliminal, that pulses only when a Chord-Watcher passes. And in that minuscule disruption, the entire Works seems to pause—the crawlers hesitate for a millisecond, the Pulse-Moons clench, the shoals shimmer. Then it passes, and the perfect repetition resumes. The Chord-Watchers are the immune system, the debug-feedback loop, the one element that can introduce a correction without breaking the whole. They are the reason the equilibrium never decays. They exist to listen for any deviation—a slight temperature rise, a foreign chemical—and to absorb it, dissolving it into their ribbon-bodies until the chord is pure again.

To live in the Root-Works is to be an instrument. No creature here is separable from the whole; each is a phrase in a composition that has no beginning and no end. Even the bacteria in the water column, the single-celled organisms that form the base of the detritus rain, are clonal variations of the plant-architecture’s own cells, leaked from the thallus and cycling back after a brief, floating existence. There is no parasite, no infection, no disease—only the slow, radiant hum of a life that solved every problem of existence so completely that it became a place. The water is cool, still, heavy with time. And in that darkness, the living song goes on, forever.
