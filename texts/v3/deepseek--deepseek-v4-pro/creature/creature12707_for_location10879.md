The fig tree is the eldest resident, though not by the measure of the moss that coats the stones in velvet centuries. The tree arrived as a seed dropped from above—perhaps excreted by some bird that dared a momentary perch on the crumbling lip, or carried on a gust that spiraled down into the dim. It germinated in a crack where water collected and stone had softened to a mineral soil, and it has never known full sun. Its growth is a slow, silent endurance, more patience than vigor. It lives in a state of attentive waiting: roots fingering deeper into the limestone, seeking the permanent moisture below; leaves angled to catch the grey light, each one a dark, waxy plate that funnels condensation to the trunk. The tree does not fruit, but it does flower—tiny, hidden inflorescences inside what would become figs in brighter worlds—and these are visited by a single species of wasp that completes its entire life cycle within the cistern. The wasp, too, is diminished, pale, nearly translucent, a shadow of its sunlit kin. It arrives on the fig’s internal flowers, pollinates, lays eggs, and dies within the enclosed syconium, never seeing the sky. The tree and the wasp have co-evolved into a miniature, light-starved version of their outside relationship, a loop so tight they are more one creature than two.

The frogs—emerald tree frogs, their skin the color of wet jade veined with faint gold—are the presiding spirits of the walls. By day they cling motionless to vertical stone, tucked into the hollows the water has carved, each one a perfect stillness. Their toes, tipped with discs of impossible grip, hold them fast against the slickness. At dusk, one begins. A single clear note, a chime that hangs in the thick air, and then a long pause. Another answers from the far side, and then a third, until the space holds a constellation of spaced calls, each one a location marker, a declaration. They are not singing to the dark; they are singing to each other, mapping territory and desire. The males descend, slowly, toward the black pool at the cistern’s bottom when the season turns, and there, in water that never sees the moon, they release a gelatinous raft of eggs that hatch into tadpoles. These tadpoles are pale, almost eyeless in the first days, feeding on the soft algal scum that grows on submerged stone. Many are eaten—by their own kind, by the probing mouths of the rat-creatures, by the great diving beetles that patrol the pool. Those that survive climb the walls on new-formed limbs, miniature jade sculptures, and the cycle begins again. They are generations deep now, and they have never heard true rain, only the constant drip that mimics it.

The geckos are the ghosts. Their skin, once likely patterned with browns and tans, has become a translucent white, flushed with the palest pink where blood moves close to the surface. They are smaller than their above-ground relatives, and their eyes are huge, dark orbs that gather every available photon. They hunt by stillness and then a darting tongue, taking the beetles, the small crickets, the moths that occasionally blunder down from the canopy. They communicate with subtle body language—a tilt of the head, a slow wave of the tail—and at night, if you sit very still, you can hear their soft chirps, a sound more felt than heard, a vibration in the stone. They lay their eggs deep in the cracks, two perfect spheres the size of peppercorns, and those eggs are sometimes eaten by the centipedes, long and rust-red and many-legged, that are the cistern’s most fearsome predators. The geckos have learned to lay in places the centipedes avoid—near the calcite curtains, where the stone is sharp and new. Thus even the geology shapes the lives of the inhabitants, a slow dance of adaptation.

The rat-creatures deserve a closer look, for they are not quite rats anymore. Their ancestors, Norway rats perhaps, fell or climbed down generations ago, finding a world without light, without open runs, without the quick, fearful existence of the streets. Here, in the deep pocket of warmth and water, they have grown sleek and dark, their fur short and almost oily to the touch, their whiskers long and constantly in motion. Their eyes are not blind, as one might expect—there is still some light—but they are reduced, less fearful of it. They move by scent and by the map of vibrations that travel through the wet stone. They have become aquatic to a degree, diving into the pool to hunt tadpoles and beetles, their tails flattening slightly, their toes spreading for better purchase on slick rock. They den in the deepest recesses, where the old cistern floor slopes and the stone is honeycombed with the work of centuries. There, they build nests of the softest moss and shed fern leaves, and they rear young that are born pink and helpless but quickly grow into the silent, shadowy existence. They do not fight often; territory is limited, and violence would disrupt the delicate balance. Instead, they have developed a complex system of scent demarcation, an olfactory language that designates paths, food sources, and breeding rights. Their metabolism has slowed; they eat less, live longer, move with a deliberate, almost reptilian economy. Sometimes, in the absolute stillness, you might see one emerge at the pool’s edge, a sleek, dark head breaking the black water with barely a ripple, and it will look up, toward the distant light, a question without urgency.

The mycelial network is a resident too, a diffuse, silent intelligence threading through the floor. It is pale, a ghostly white lacework visible in patches when the leaf litter is pushed aside, and it connects the roots of the fig to the ferns, the moss, the rotting wood of ancient seeds that never sprouted. It digests the fallen leaves that drift down in slow spirals, and in doing so it feeds the whole community. The fungi fruit rarely, but when they do, they push up delicate, translucent mushrooms—tiny parasols that glow faintly with a phosphorescent light for a few hours before dissolving into the wet air. These bloom in darkness, and the insects come to them, and the frogs eat the insects, and the frogs are eaten by the geckos or the rats, and the wastes of all these creatures feed the mycelium. It is a circulation, a breath. The network has a memory of sorts: it responds to the seasons of leaf-fall, to the subtle temperature shifts when the city above bakes or cools, and it pulses with chemical signals when a root is damaged or a new source of nutrients appears. Some have claimed that such networks can communicate, and in this closed space, where all life is pressed together, the fungal threads are the connective tissue, the nervous system of the cistern’s body.

The insects are legion. Beetles with iridescent shells, black and green and sometimes a deep violet, crawling deliberately over the wet stone. Small crickets that chirrup in a slow, patient rhythm, not the frantic urgency of the surface but a steady, measured pulse, like a metronome set to the tempo of decay. Springtails leap from the moss when disturbed, a tiny explosion of life. The water pool has its own fauna: diving beetles that carry a bubble of air under their wings, tiny freshwater shrimp nearly transparent, a single species of snail with a paper-thin shell that spirals leftward, unique to this place. Dragonflies sometimes find their way down as nymphs, crawling from the seepage, and they mature into adults that never leave—they flit in the dimness, catching the smaller flies, their wings a blur of gossamer, their bodies turning from brown to a muted blue. They cannot escape easily; the opening is a tunnel of leaves, and they circle below it, a living ceiling fan of wings.

And then there are the stones themselves, the slowest of the living. The limestone is not inert; it is a colony of microscopic life—bacteria and archaea that film the surface, that eat the minerals and excrete the calcite, building the tiny rippling curtains. The droplets that fall from the ceiling are not pure; they carry the dissolved breath of the city above, the carbon dioxide exhaled by the ferns and the animals, and as they drip, they release their load, molecule by molecule, growing stone. In a thousand years, if the city is gone and the cistern remains, the opening may seal over with a new roof of flowstone, and the whole pocket will become a sealed bubble, a cave within a cave. For now, the stones are in flux, dissolving and reforming, a slow breathing that takes place at the edge of perception.

There is one more creature, perhaps, that lives here—a presence only felt. The cistern has a memory of its human making, a residue of intention in the cut stone, the hand-hewn edges, the ancient masons' marks now blurred beyond reading. But that memory is being overwritten. The frogs and the rats and the mycelium do not know it was a cistern; they know it as their world, complete. And so something new is forming, a collective entity, not a mind but a state of being: a small, steady, self-governing metabolism, a warm breath in the dark. If you sit there at dusk, with the frogs beginning their call and the last grey light filtering down, you can feel it—a sense that the cistern is alive, not as a single creature, but as an assembly, a community that has become so intertwined it is hard to say where one life ends and another begins. The fig tree feeds the wasp, the wasp feeds the fig, the frogs eat the beetles that eat the fungi that eat the leaves of the fig, and the rats eat everything, and they all breathe the same thick air, drink the same weeping stone. They are the life of the place, each one a note in a quiet, sustained chord.

So at dusk, climb down if you can, and be still. You will not be alone. You will be a visitor in a room where the residents have never seen a human, where your presence is a momentary anomaly. The gecko on the wall will watch you with its dark, ancient eyes; the rat will pause in its slow patrol, whiskers twitching; the fig tree will continue its patient suspension, and the frogs will sing their spaced-out calls, each one a tiny act of faith that another day will come, another drip, another breath. They are the living answer to the question of what a place becomes when it is forgotten. They are the forest inside the cistern, and they are enough.
