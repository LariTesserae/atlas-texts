You see the boy first—or the thing like a boy—because he is the one who notices you.

He stands at the edge of a worklight’s jaundiced pool, bare feet splayed on the springy root-weave, and he does not startle. His head cocks, slow, amphibious, and the gills at his neck flutter once, tasting the air you have disturbed. They are not slits but soft, three-fingered fronds, the tissue so thin you can see the faint glow of his blood moving through them, a bioluminescent trace of copper-green that matches the mold-light overhead. His skin is the color of damp limestone, pale and faintly granular, and it glistens with the same permanent film of water that coats everything here. He has no hair; instead, a ridge of fine, flexible tendrils runs from his brow to the nape of his neck, each one twitching independently, sampling the warmth, the vibrations, the chemical news of the grove. His eyes are large, dark, and wet, set wide in a face that is otherwise small-featured, almost delicate—a face made for close work in dimness. He wears no clothes, but his body is not naked: a harness of woven root-fiber and salvaged copper wire crosses his chest, holding pouches and tools, and around his waist is a belt of linked metal scales that chime faintly when he shifts. In one hand he holds a hooked rod of pitted iron, its tip glowing a sullen orange from some internal heat. In the other, he loosely grips a leash made of braided sinew, and at the leash’s end shambles the creature he herds.

It is a beast of burden, a multi-legged crawler the size of a small pony, its segmented back plated with chitin the color of old rust. Each plate is drilled and riveted with metal brackets, onto which are strapped bundles of scrap: curled shavings of copper, coils of corroded wire, the fractured teeth of some larger machine. The creature’s head is low, eyeless, a shovel-shaped mass of sensory bristles that sweep the ground before it, and from its sides protrude dozens of soft, jointed legs, each tipped with a delicate pincer that grips and tests the woven floor. It moves with a sound like rain on metal, a gentle, multitudinous clicking. The boy whistles—a note that comes not from lips but from the gills themselves, a fluting, reedy sound that cuts through the grinding hum—and the crawler stops, lowers its head, and begins methodically licking the condensation from a nearby pipe with a long, rasp-like tongue.

This is a Lungen, one of the undercroft people. They are the inheritors of this place, though “inheritor” suggests a one-time gift; it would be truer to say they are its symbionts, as much a part of the grove’s body as the braided trunks or the chuffing pistons. They have been here longer than the metal, longer than the engines, though the engines have shaped them in turn. Their history is written in the fused architecture around you.

As your eyes adjust, you see more of them. They are not numerous—a hamlet’s worth, perhaps three dozen—but they fill the space with a quiet, constant motion. High above, on a walkway woven from living vines and salvaged grating, a pair of adults are tending to one of the bioluminescent panels. They are long-limbed, their bodies lean and tensile, with the same pale skin and gill-fronds, but their tendrils are longer, some braided with copper filaments that arc with faint static when they move. They work without words that you can hear, passing a tool back and forth—a thing of bone and glass that emits a precise, high-frequency whine, coaxing the mold to spread in a new direction, away from a frayed cable that spits dangerously. One of them leans into the glow, and you see the tattoos that cover their back: a map of the grove’s rootways, the lines inked with a phosphorescent ink that shifts as they breathe, a living chart of their domain.

Down on the floor, near the base of a great column, a child younger than the herdboy squats beside a small pool of standing water that has gathered in a depression of the root-mat. The pool is not accidental; it is a cistern, ringed with stones worn smooth, and the child is singing to it. The song is low, thrumming, a vibration that travels through the water and makes the surface shiver, and in response, the water answers: a thick, gelatinous mass rises from the depths, a cultivated culture of nutritive sludge, flecked with bioluminescent motes. The child scoops a handful into a waiting gourd and drinks, the gills at their neck flaring with pleasure. This is the staple food: a protein-rich symbiosis of bacteria, algae, and the grove’s own exudates, grown in the dark warmth and harvested with song and patience. The children learn the frequencies before they learn to walk the root-ways.

Not all the inhabitants are Lungen, though the Lungen are the core. There are other beings here, integrated into the settlement in ways that seem both ancient and negotiated. At the far end of the chamber, where the ceiling dips low and the trunks crowd closer, you glimpse a figure that is human, or once was. He is old—ancient, even—his skin a weathered leather, his eyes clouded with cataracts. He wears a patched coat of many fabrics, and his hands are gloved in rubber that has fused to his flesh. He tends the largest of the engines, a massive iron heart half-swallowed by a column of braided wood. He is a Mechanic, one of a line that came down here generations ago when the world above was still a memory, and he speaks to the machine in a language of taps and oils, his own body scarred with burns and grafts. The Lungen move around him with a deference that is not worship but partnership; he keeps the heart beating, and they keep the grove alive that houses it. There are a few others like him—humans or near-humans, skulking in the edges, their lungs scarred from the wet heat, their eyes narrowed against the dimness. They have their own hierarchy, their own rituals, a chain of tools passed down.

And then there are the feral things, the fauna that the Lungen do not herd but live alongside. The air is thick with the blimp-like floaters; they are called Veil-seeds, and the Lungen harvest their trailing frills for fiber. Children sometimes leap from the walkways to catch them, drifting down on wings of stretched membrane. The chittering rivulets of crawlers on the walls are called Rust-mites, and they are a nuisance, a food source, and a vital recycler all at once; you see a Lungen woman scraping them into a bucket with the back of a machete, her movements practiced and unhurried. The great many-legged hauler herded by the boy is a Sulk, one of a dozen that the hamlet keeps for heavy labor. They are docile, scent-led, and they purr when groomed.

The community is silent in words but loud in other languages. The Lungen speak primarily through the gills—a complex range of whistles, tones, and breath-patterns that carry over the industrial hum. You hear it now: a constant, interwoven melody that is the settlement’s true voice, a music of coordination and safety. When they do use vocal sounds, it is for emphasis, or for speaking with the Mechanics, who cannot hear the gill-speech. The children are learning both. You catch a fragment of it as the herdboy turns to another child—a girl, who has slipped up beside him with a skin of water. She offers it, and he takes it, and the sound that passes between them is soft and guttural: *“Keth-tan. The new one is still standing there.”* So they have noticed you all along. They have simply been too polite, or too cautious, to approach.

Look now at the space itself, at the way the inhabitants have shaped it. Between the massive trunks, the root-weave floor is carved with shallow channels that guide the water to the cisterns. Hammocks of woven fiber are slung between columns, high up, where the warmest air gathers; you can see small forms curled in them, sleeping despite the noise, their gills moving in slow synchrony. Clay pots are nested in the crooks of branches, growing edible fungi that fruit in spirals of pale blue. A workshop is tucked into a hollowed trunk: a Lungen elder sits there, her tendrils twined with copper wire, using a pedal-powered lathe to shape a gear from solid bone. The lathe is driven by a Sulk in harness, walking its endless circle. Sparks fly from the cutting edge, each one a brief sun in the grove’s perpetual dusk.

The strangeness runs deep, but it is a lived-in strangeness, a place where the edges of species and categories have worn soft. The Lungen are not merely caretakers; they are an organ of the grove, its hands and its voice. They prune the ever-growing trunks so that the chambers remain open, they nurture the mold-lights, they sing the sludge to life, and they repair the machines that the human Mechanics cannot. In return, the grove gives them air, water, food, warmth, and a darkness that is not hostile but sheltering. Their lives are measured in cycles of moisture, in the slow growth of a copper deposit on a pipe, in the generations it takes for a Sulk to learn a new route. Days do not exist here, only the eternal, humid now.

And as you stand there, heavy in the weight of it all, the herdboy finally turns fully toward you. He takes a step, his bare foot sinking slightly into the mat, and his gills spread wide—a greeting, perhaps, or a question. His eyes hold no fear, only a reflective, patient intelligence. The Sulk behind him belches steam and settles to the ground with a clatter of scrap. The boy waits. He will wait for a long time, if necessary. All around, the hamlet continues its rhythm: the chuff of the piston, the drip every twelve seconds, the hum of a million wings, the low fluting of gill-speech. This is who lives here. This is what lives.
