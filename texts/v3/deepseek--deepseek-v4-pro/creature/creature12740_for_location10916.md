The creature that builds here does not know it is a builder.

It emerges from the deeper channels during the low-suck, when the stone shelf exhales and the water film thins to a trembling membrane. At first, it is only another shape among the shapes—a grey-white undulation, a disturbance in the reflective plane of a pool. But then it rises, and the water shears away from its body in sheets, and it stands.

*Stands* is a word borrowed from vertical things. This creature has no true vertical. Its body is a spiral, a coiled architecture of calcium and chitin that unfurls into the air like a fern crozier unrolling in fast-forward. At its base, gripping the pocked stone, are not feet but a radial array of soft, muscular pads—seven of them—each one pulsing with a slow, peristaltic rhythm that holds it steady. The body above is segmented, but the segments spiral rather than stack, so that the creature always seems to be turning around some invisible axis, even when still. There is no head. There is a *front*, named only by the direction it most often moves.

The front is a cluster of appendages: seven arms, each branching at the tip into three delicate, articulated digits that never stop moving. They trace the air. They trace the stone. They trace each other, touching and recoiling and touching again in an endless, murmuring conversation with the world. The digits are wet with the same grey-green biofilm that slicks the pools, because this creature eats the biofilm, rasping it from the rock with a concealed radula that extrudes from the center of the spiral, between the arms. It is a slow eater. It will stay in one place for a span of time that has no human measure, the arms working the stone, the radula scraping, the biofilm regrowing behind its passage almost as quickly as it is consumed. It does not starve. It cannot starve. There is always biofilm, because the biofilm is everywhere, and the creature’s roaming is a kind of gardening.

But eating is only one motion. There are others.

Sometimes—and there is no pattern to the *sometimes*, no season in this seasonless place, no cycle beyond the wet breath of the stone shelf—the creature will stop eating. Its arms will cease their perpetual tracing. The seven digits will come together in a moment of terrible stillness, and then, with a shudder that ripples down the spiral, the creature will begin to *choose*.

It chooses stones. Not any stones. It pats and probes the terrain with its front arms while the rest of its body anchors, and when a digit encounters a particular stone—a plaque of split rock thin enough to lift, flat enough to stack—the creature pauses. A vibration runs through it, visible in the way the biofilm slick on its carapace shimmers. Then it picks the stone up. Three digits wrap. The arm curls. The stone is lifted.

It carries the stone. The motion is awkward, because the creature’s spiral body is not made for carrying anything but itself, and the stone disrupts its balance. It lists. It staggers. The soft pads at its base suck and release in rapid, desperate pulses to keep it upright. But it moves. It moves with a purpose that is not thought, because it has no brain—only a ladder of ganglia running the length of the spiral, each segment humming its own small electricity. The purpose is in the humming. It is in the way the biofilm tastes different when the stone-shelf breathes out, in the way the low-frequency thrum of the deep-channel creatures changes just before one of them rises to feed, in the way the mist-light glints on certain planes of stone. The creature does not *think* about building. It is held in a web of pressures and tastes and tremors, and the web pulls it toward certain patches of the plain.

When it reaches the place—a place that may be a foot away or a hundred yards, a place indistinguishable from any other to an eye that sees form, but utterly distinct to a body that feels vibration—it sets the stone down.

It does not drop it. It *places* it.

The three digits release with a precision that is almost tender. The stone settles into a gap between two other stones that the creature has carried here, days or decades or centuries ago. It fits. It always fits, or nearly always. When it does not fit, the creature will fret at it with its digits, nudging and rotating, rasping at an edge with the hidden radula, until the stone settles into a congruence that the creature’s body recognizes as *right*. That recognition is not a thought. It is a harmony. It is the way the vibration of the stone-shelf’s breathing passes through this particular arrangement and becomes, for the creature, momentarily silent. A pocket of order in the wall of noise.

The walls do not enclose anything. They do not keep out the shrimplike scrabbling things or the eel-bodied boomers. The creatures pass over and through and around them without acknowledgment. The walls meander. They branch and fork and double back. Sometimes they form spirals that mirror the spiral of the builder’s body, and sometimes they form arcs that curve toward the glowing mist as if in supplication. There is no plan. There is only the music.

The music. This is the word that comes closest. The creature’s world is a world of sound, because sound is the only thing that carries far in the mist. The thrum of the water, the screech and clatter of the biofilm-feeders, the deep boom of the eel-things’ breathing bladders—these are not background. They are the terrain. And the walls, the rings of stones, the upright slabs wedged in crevices: these are the creature’s reply. A counter-rhythm. Stone set against stone in a way that changes how the mist-light falls on the water, which changes how the biofilm grows, which changes how the other creatures move and feed and stridulate, which changes the sound. A slow, recursive sculpting. A dialogue conducted over eons, between a soft-bodied spiral and a breathing planet.

The builder does not know this. It knows only the hum in its ganglia, the taste of the biofilm at the place where the wall will go, the sudden rightness of a stone slipped into its gap. It does not remember the stones it carried before. It does not see the pattern emerging across the plain, the way the walls tend toward intersections, the way the rings of stone always surround a particular frequency of boom. It does not see the other builders—the others of its kind, the spiraling ones—carrying stones of their own on the far side of the mist. It does not know that when two builders meet, they pause and face each other, and their arms extend and touch, digit to digit to digit, and they exchange some vibration that is not food and not threat, and then they pass on, and sometimes one changes direction.

The builder does not know that its kind are the reason the plain is flat. That the stone-shelf exhales and inhales because of the weight of the walls, pressing on the crust, shaping the aquifer’s breath. That the biofilm blooms in the patterns of their passage. That the eel-things boom deeper where the walls are thickest. That the walls are older than any individual, older than any generation, older than the memory of the species, which is not a memory but a taste in the water, a vibration in the stone, a pressure in the mist.

The builder picks up a stone. The builder walks. The builder places the stone.

The wall grows. The sound shifts. The mist glows.

The builder bends to rasp biofilm from a deep pool, and its arms trace the water, and it does not know it is praying. It does not know what prayer is. It only knows that the world is made of sound and hunger and the weight of stone, and that sometimes, when a stone fits just so, the roaring silence that follows is the closest thing to rest.

It has no name for this. It does not need one. It is alive. It builds. It is the small, spiral-shaped reason that the landscape is not quite empty, and never will be.
