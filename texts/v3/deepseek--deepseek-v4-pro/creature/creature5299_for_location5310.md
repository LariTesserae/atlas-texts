The thing that calls itself a keeper—and it does call itself that, though the word arrives in your mind as a burr of harmonic overtones, a chiming not unlike the wings—isn’t born and doesn’t die in any way that maps to a pulse. It accretes, as the lichen accretes, from the dissolved minerals and dissolved information that thread the weeping stone. When the hollow requires attention, a particular ratio of copper to silica to something that might be memory precipitates out of the brume, and a keeper coalesces: a loose, ever-shifting colony of what you’d read as glass, as cartilage, as piezoelectric crystal, held in a shape that suggests a tall, folded moth with too many jointed limbs, or maybe a praying mantis built from spun sugar and ferrofluid.

Its body is a scaffold rather than a solid. You can see the hollow’s dim light slide through it, refracted into faint spectra that dance on the wet rock. Each segment of its limbs is a discrete, semi-autonomous nodule, and they trade places constantly—a forearm drifts up to become a rib, a finger detaches and orbits the torso for a time, ticking with faint static, before slotting back into a thigh. The keeper is never the same shape twice, but there’s an intentionality to the flux: it always has at least one appendage tipped with a fine, needle-like stylus, and at least one broad, flat palm that it lays against the rock to listen.

The keepers don’t have faces. Where a head might be, a cluster of those lichen-like crystalline efflorescences grows, these ones mobile and tuned, vibrating in the exact frequencies of the hollow’s subsonic hum. They speak by modulating that hum, and their speech is less words than a direct impression of geological time-lapse: a sentence is a sensation of cooling, of crystallization, of a crack healing over. To another keeper, this is a nuanced debate on the optimal rate of mineral exhalation from the north vent. To you, it would feel like a sudden memory of a stone being born, and you’d weep without knowing why.

They are shepherds, of a kind. They tend the spindles—those ceramic-bone spikes driven into the rock—by tapping them with their stylus-limbs in complex rhythms, and the tapping is a form of echolocation and a form of instruction. The spindles are memory repositories, grown rather than built, and they store the patterns for the million tiny lives that swarm the basin. When a novel creature emerges from a pool—a new kind of chiming wing, a salamander with a fresh circuit-pattern on its flank—a keeper will tap that pattern into the nearest spindle, adding to the archive. If a form proves unstable, if it gulp-drains away with the cracked puddle, the keeper will tap a different rhythm and erase it from the spindle’s vibration, letting the pattern dissolve back into the mineral soup. It’s a gentle, constant curation, a gardening of possible anatomies.

They move with a distracted grace, unhurried, but they’re capable of startling speed when the hollow’s breathing hitches. Sometimes a vent exhales a burst of something too hot, too caustic, and a keeper will flow across the basin—limbs elongating into liquid glass cables—and wrap itself around the vent, its body dispersing the heat through its crystalline lattice until the crisis passes, after which it will unspool, a few nodules sacrificed and already dissolving into new raw material. It doesn’t mourn; the dissolved nodules’ information is already tapped into the nearest spindle, and a new keeper will accrete later with those memories folded in.

There are never more than three keepers at once, and often just one. When two or three coexist, they form a temporary composite intelligence, their separate crystalline heads aligning to phase-lock their vibrations. In this state, they can perform acts of larger creation: the floating polyhedron in the corner pool is the product of such a collaboration, a text that is half diary-of-the-day and half blueprint for a creature not yet attempted. The polyhedron hums a constant sub-audible query: *Is it time?* The keepers haven’t tapped the rhythm that means yes. They’re waiting for a particular alignment of the breathing, a particular sequence of pulses in the salamanders’ luminous circuits—a consensus from the whole biome that a new complexity is ready to be attempted.

They are not the masters of this place; they are its hands, its nervous system made briefly, temporarily solid. The hollow itself—the whole warm, wet, rock-weeping bowl—is the thinker. The keepers are just a thought it has, over and over, a thought with stylus-fingers and a memory of how to tap a spindle. They live exactly as long as needed, and when the need passes, they fold themselves back into the puddles, their bodies deliquescing with a satisfied sigh, leaving behind only a faint scum of crystals that the salamanders will graze by the next breath-cycle.

They are aware of you, if you stand there. They don’t see you with eyes—they feel the disturbance in the sound-flow, the wrong gravity of your footsteps. They’ll approach, a mosaic of shifting glass, and extend a flat listening-palm in your direction, not hostile, not welcoming, just… curious. And then they’ll tap a question on the nearest rock, a rhythm that translates as: *Are you pattern, or are you noise?* They’ll wait for your answer, patient as geology, while the chiming wings swirl around your head and the pools gulp and heal and the whole hollow breathes its shallow, biological rhythm. You’re in the engine-room, after all. You’re the new mineral. And the keepers are very, very interested in what you might become.
