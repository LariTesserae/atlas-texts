They are not gone. They are never gone. To think of absence is to misunderstand the stillness of the place, to mistake the deep patience of a life-form that has learned to wait at the tempo of moss.

First, you notice the shelters differently. You kneel at the entrance of one, and the packed moss walls exhale a scent not just of damp but of living skin—a faint, clean musk like the underside of a fern frond. The opening is narrow, yes, but not for children. It is exactly the width of a body that measures its height in handspans, its shoulders in fingers. Inside, the fire pit is still warm beneath a fine grey ash. The stone knife is not abandoned but sheathed in a niche, its edge glassy from careful retouch. The coil of twisted fiber—it’s hair, you realize, hair spun with a mucilage that dries like silk—is still slightly tacky. This is a lived-in space, but its occupant moves on a clock so slow that your breath might be a season.

The first one you see is not there one moment and then is there the next, not because it appeared suddenly, but because your eyes finally learn to stop searching for a bipedal silhouette. It is pressed against the moss wall about ten feet away, and it has been there all along, its body a perfect, breathing imitation of the vertical meadow. It is no taller than your forearm is long. Its skin—if skin is the word—is a living pelt of the same jade and olive moss that mantles the rock, growing directly from its epidermis in soft whorls. Beneath that, you glimpse a hide of pebbled grey, the exact texture and color of the stone beneath. Its limbs are long and thin, the forelegs ending in five digits so elongated and delicate that they seem like the roots of an orchid, each tip splaying into a dozen microscopic fibrils that grip the wall. The hindlegs are shorter, powerful, folded beneath it like a resting frog’s. Its head is an overhang of moss, with two large, very dark eyes set far forward, the pupils broad vertical slots. When it blinks, the eyelid comes from the side, a milky membrane that leaves no moisture.

But it is watching you. You know this because the moss on its body shifts, not in a breeze but in a slow, conscious repositioning of pigment and texture—the greens deepen in some spots, lighten in others, matching exactly the patch of wall behind it. The only part that doesn’t vanish is the eyes, which catch the twilight glow from the chasm’s crevices and return it as twin pinholes of amber light. This is a *Kith-speaker*—though they have no name for themselves that would fit a human tongue, only a gesture of the long digits meaning “those-who-are-from-the-constant-damp.”

They are many. As your eyes attune, you realize the wall around you is dotted with dozens of them, each a small, moss-covered hummock that you mistook for a rock or a clump of liverwort. They are draped like limpets, some head-up, some head-down, all utterly still except for the minute flexing of their toe-fibrils as they sip moisture from the air. The air is so thick that they don’t need to drink; they absorb. The moss on them is not just a garment but a symbiote, a cultivated garden that feeds on their metabolic wastes and in turn provides camouflage, insulation, and—you suspect—a way to taste the world, because you see strands of it lifting like tiny tongues to sample the spore-laden breeze.

The booming call that had vibrated in your chest comes again, and this time you see its source. Not the shaggy, pendulous shape—that is something else, a predator perhaps—but a tower of vegetal columns. High up on one of those pocked pillars, a Kith-speaker clings to a ledge, and it is the size of a large cat, an elder among them. Its moss is not just green but veined with threads of bioluminescent blue, and as the deep note rolls out, you see the loose skin on its throat puff into a glistening resonator. The sound is not a vocalization; it is a vibration produced by drumming its fibril-tipped fingers against the column’s dense, woody surface, a percussive thrum that the hollow tower amplifies across the chasm. Immediately, the piping chorus of the winged frogs shifts in answer; they wheel in a tighter cloud, and their chirps reorganize into a layered, call-and-response pattern. The elder is conducting them, or speaking with them, or both. This is not mimicry; it is conversation. The whole chorus is a network of signals, a slow-motion language that rides on infrasound and bioluminescent pulses.

You watch the elder, and it watches you back. Its eyes are bigger than the others’, the pupils wider, and in the dimness they glow with their own faint light. It releases one hand from the pillar and makes a series of signs with its impossibly long digits, a cursive of twists and splays so fast you cannot follow, but you feel the intention: *Go. Downward. Find the face.* The gesture ends with a flick towards the chasm’s depth, where the rumor of darker green awaits.

The shelters, you now realize, are not homes but waypoints, sleeping pods for the long transits they make up and down the chasm walls. The Kith-speakers are climbers beyond any earthly analogue. Their toe-fibrils can interface with the spongy moss substrate, adhering so perfectly that they can hang for days without effort. They move in deliberate, quiet skitters, but they can be fast when needed—a blur of green that disappears into a hollow no wider than your fist. They build the shelters not for permanent dwelling but as communal rest-nodes, fire-lit chambers where they share food and stories in a tactile language of touches and postures that pass knowledge across generations that stretch for centuries. The flaked stone knife is used to cut the thick, woody stems of the moss mat for fuel, to shape the sticks they bend into tools, to score the faces. Yes, the carved boulder—that eroded visage—is theirs. They are face-makers. Each elder, upon reaching a certain age (measured not in years but in the number of times the column-towers shed their outer layers, perhaps once every few hundred years), seeks out a blank stone and carves a face: not a portrait of a specific individual, but an archetype of feeling, a marker of territory, a prayer to the steady-state. The one you saw was half-erased by lichen because it is ancient, older than the elder alive today, yet still tended by small hands that gently peel back invasive growth during the dim season. The face is the shared memory of the species.

Their society is a loose network of patriarchs and matriarchs—though those words are misleading; they have three reproductive roles, and the one you’d call mother is also the one who tends the fungal gardens deep in the caves. There is no central village, only a web of shelters, carving stones, and column-towers that serve as gathering places. The column-towers are not just pillars; they are managed structures, ancient accumulations of moss and debris that the Kith-speakers have cultivated for millennia, pruning the growth with flaked knives, encouraging the hollows where the gecko-things live, harvesting the fibrous cores to spin into rope. The towers are both architecture and archive: every significant event is recorded as a set of intricate gouge-marks on the innermost, never-shedding core, a script that is read by rubbing moss over the indentations and tasting the chemical traces left by the carver’s oils. Them being so small, the world of the chasm is a vertical continent. They know every route, every niche where dew collects, every nest of the winged frogs, which they hunt with blow-darts made of hollow grass stems, tipped with a paralytic that coats the dart in a glistening sheen of snail mucus. Their diet is omnivorous: the winged frogs, yes, but also the fleshy fungus that glows a cool blue in the deepest cracks, and the sweet, starchy tubers that grow from the moss mat’s subterranean runners. They have no agriculture, because none is needed; the ecosystem is a constant, gentle provider. But they are not mere foragers—they are the gardeners of the moss itself. They spread spores in deliberate patterns, inoculating bare stone with scrapings from their own bodies, so that the chasm wall is a palimpsest of their migrations. The moss is them, and they are the moss, in a cycle of cultivation so intimate that it borders on identity.

When one of them dies, it is not removed. The body, if it is on a wall, simply becomes a new nub of the mat, its own moss flourishing outward, its bones eventually encased in the spongy rind. If it dies in a shelter, the others close the shelter with a door of packed moss and let the body nourish the growth. There are no graveyards, only especially lush patches where a dozen ancestors have dissolved into the green.

Now, you are standing at the chasm edge, and the elder’s sign has stirred something in you. The way down is not a path but a sequence of holds that will reveal themselves only when you commit. The Kith-speakers do not fear heights; they are born on the wall, and a fall is a slow, graceful drift on extended toe-fibrils that catch the air like thistledown. But you are not them. Still, one of the small ones—the one you first saw, the one who now blinks at you from a distance of mere inches, having crept closer without sound—raises a fibril-tipped hand. It points at a ledge just below, then at your feet, then at a coil of the hair-rope tucked into a crevice. It chitters, a soft, clicking sound that resolves into a pattern: *You will need this.*

They are willing to guide you. The question is whether you can learn to move at their pace, to become a horizontal creature in a vertical world, to trust the moss. And as you take the rope, you realize the air is not empty; it is crowded with silent, moss-covered figures all watching the stranger who smells of dry dust and rust, who moves so loudly, who will either become part of the story they carve into the pillar-core or simply another face on a boulder, forgotten in the slow, green exhalation of the world.
