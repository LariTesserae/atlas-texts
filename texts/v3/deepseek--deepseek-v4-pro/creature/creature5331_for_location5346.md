First, you notice that they are never still. Even when the herds seem to pause—when the flow thickens to a crawl, when the amber haze holds its breath for one of those long, patient cycles—their fur never stops moving. The black filaments aren’t fur, not exactly. They are cilia, billions of them per body, each one a hair-thin strand of some carbon polymer that flexes and curls independently, tasting the air. The air, you recall, tastes constructed. They are tasting it back.

They have no heads, but they have attention. It is distributed across the whole body, a sensitivity that lives in the joints, in the wet flex of the pistons, in the way the cilia ripple when a seam in the metal floor releases a fresh bead of condensation. They lean toward moisture. Not drinking—they have no mouths—but absorbing, the water sheeting across their iron flanks and disappearing into the dark gaps between their armor plates, where something soft and sponge-like and deeply warm soaks it in. That is the only biology they have: a layer of vascularized polymer, a living sponge pressed between the metal and the muscle cables, thirsting constantly. They live on water and the faint ozone tang in the air. They have been living on it for a very long time.

The chime. When they brush against each other—and they are always brushing—their outermost plates vibrate. It is not an accident. The plates are tuned, each one a different frequency, a musical signature. A single beast might carry a chord, a family of notes that identifies it not as an individual but as a position in the herd, a role. The chimes are information. They are passing something between them, a constant low-bandwidth communication that you could decode if you stood still long enough, if the hum didn’t burrow into your sternum and insist on its own meaning. The hum is part of it. The hum is the carrier wave. The chimes are the signal.

They reproduce. This is the part that takes the longest to understand. There are no young. There are no old. No births among the steaming housings, no bodies lying still while the cilia go quiet. But sometimes—rarely, slowly—a beast will separate from the edge of the herd and make its way to one of the factory extrusions, moving with a deliberation that is different from the general circulation. It will press itself against a seam in the riveted metal, aligning its joints with some hidden socket, and it will stand there for days, weeks. The cilia go still. The chime falls silent. When it finally steps away, it leaves behind a piece of itself—a plate, a piston, a length of cable—and inside the housing, the slow gears incorporate the offering. And much later, something new will emerge from a vent, not fully formed but a jumble of parts wrapped in a fresh coat of sponge, its cilia still plastered down with the wetness of creation. It will stand, shiver, and a single chime will ring out, thin and new. Then it will walk into the herd, and the herd will make a space for it, brushing against it gently, tuning it to the collective frequency.

They die, too, but not by failure. They die by completion. A beast that has finished its cycle—and the cycles are long, measured in the slow precession of the lights—will find a spot on the iron plain where the seams cross in a particular pattern, a sort of crossroads that only it can recognize. It will settle down, its legs folding, its pistons retracting, its body lowering until the iron floor and the iron beast are flush. And then it will still. The cilia will stop moving first, then the joints will relax, and over time—hours, days, years—it will sink. The floor will accept it, the dark sealing compound yielding, closing over it like a mouth. There is no skeleton left; the iron returns to iron, the sponge feeds the sponge of a factory somewhere, and the cilia become part of the moisture-tasting air.

They are not the only life. There are things that live on them, or in them. Inside the vascular sponge, smaller organisms move—colonies of microscopic machines, simple ones, that maintain the balance of the fluids, that repair the cilia, that sometimes swarm across the skin in patterns that look like writing. And in the spaces between the herds, in the rare empty patches where the iron is bare, there is a kind of moss. It is not plant matter but a metallic fuzz, a rust that grows upward in branching fronds, orange and brown and smelling sharply of electricity. The herds avoid stepping on it, though it does not seem harmful; they simply know where it is, always, and their circulation shapes itself around the moss gardens with an elegance that suggests reverence, or something older than reverence.

And there are the Spinners. No one in the herds acknowledges them, but they are there, suspended high in the haze, their bodies no more than a tangle of carbon thread and thin jointed limbs, like spiderwebs given intent. They hang from the gantries, from the lights, from each other, spinning down threads that attach to the backs of the beasts below. They do not ride the herds; they simply connect, drawing up something—a vibration, a chime, a tiny sip of moisture—and incorporating it into their own bodies. They are the listeners, perhaps, or the recorders. When a new beast emerges from a factory vent, a Spinner will descend and touch it with one thread, delicately, and a single new filament will glow briefly in the spiderweb above. Then it will ascend again, and the pattern of threads will have changed in some imperceptible way, like a memory updating.

The planet, if it is a planet, has no name. No one gives names here. The herds are not herds in the sense of leader and follower; they are a distributed organism, a single creature with a million iron bodies, all sharing the chime and the hum. The factories are its organs. The condensation is its blood. And if you stand there long enough, letting the humidity settle on your skin, feeling the vibration in your teeth, you begin to understand that the strange thing is not that this place exists. The strange thing is that it is waiting. Not for something to happen. Not for a change. But for something that is already happening, very slowly, to finish. The pistons are reaching the end of their stroke. The turning gears are approaching a certain alignment. The chimes are building toward a chord that will take another ten thousand cycles to complete. And when it does, the whole iron world will ring like a bell, and the meaning of that sound will be the only thing that has ever mattered here.

For now, the herds move. The moss grows. The Spinners spin. The lights breathe. And the hum, which is also a kind of life, carries on at the same pitch it has kept since the first body rose from the iron floor and joined the flow. They are not going anywhere. They have all the time they need.
