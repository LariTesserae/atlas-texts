It does not have a shape so much as a habit—an itinerant pressure, a thickening in the water that moves against the almost imperceptible drift. Where the marine snow spirals gently down a quiet avenue, its passage leaves a clean, brief channel, a thread of clarity that closes slowly after it. The cilia of the tube-worms register it only as a gradual diminishment of current, a shadow too slow to trigger a flinch; their feathery crowns sway, and resume their sifting. The blind shrimp, though, sense the pressure shift on their antennae and scatter, their amethyst cloud contracting like a startled pupil into the lee of a calcified arch. For it is a predator, after its own fashion—patient and liquid, as much a part of the architecture as the turrets it skirts.

Its home territory is the broad ring of tubes around the vent-plaza where the water is richest in hydrogen sulfide, though "territory" is a word that implies borders, and this creature acknowledges none. It has, over the long, silent calendar of its life, memorised every corkscrew passage, every kissing arch, every burrow-catacomb that plunges from the base of a tower into the warm, oxygen-starved silt below. It knows the precise texture of the manganese crust on the Old Quarter’s oldest pinnacles, where the colonies have ceased to grow and only the slow accretion of minerals continues, dark as aubergine skin. It knows the sponge-wire districts, where the water is a little cleaner, a little more viscous with the mucus of their filtering, and it knows the canal trenches where the bottom current breathes a cooler, denser tongue through the city every sixty heartbeats. It is older, by some margin, than many of the individual tube-worms that constitute the youngest towers, though it is a candle-flicker compared to the colony itself.

In the perpetual soft phosphorescence, its skin reflects almost nothing—a low-absorption cloak of papillae that can mimic the purplish hue of ancient carbonates or the speckled grey of a bivalve pavement in moments. It is an octopod, though calling it that feels like calling a cathedral a shed. Its eight arms, rather than being discrete appendages, are more akin to a single, unfurling mantle of thought given flesh: each one a fine-grained manipulative surface, capable of tasting the water, undulating in a rhythm that suggests a separate, slow heartbeat for every limb. These arms spread and contract in a web, a parachute of muscular film, so that when it pauses on a particularly tall tube and extends itself, it becomes a canopy that shadows a small corner of the plaza below. It has no bones, no carapace; its beak is a hidden thing, small for its size, a mere finality for the soft fare it favours. Its eyes—two large, forward-facing spheres—are not blind like the shrimp’s but attuned to the exact palette of the bacterial glow, able to resolve the blue-green aurora into fine gradations of intensity that map the terrain as clearly as any diurnal hunter sees by sun.

It is, in a way, the city’s only true pedestrian. The giant isopods are like oxen, slow and deliberate, tracing identical loops that serve the decomposition cycle. The needle-fish are like arrows, their movements too direct, too fast, vanishing down a canal before they can be more than a wake. The octopod, though, moves with the city’s own pace: an unhurried perambulator, a flâneur of the abyss. It will visit a district for days, weeks perhaps, looping through the tube-forests in a pattern that is not quite random—it follows the slight variations in water chemistry, the scent of a dying fish, the particular electrical signature of a worm-crown flexing in its tube. Then it will ascend, not effortlessly but with a deliberate, muscular rhythm, to the upper reaches of the megastructure, where the tubes taper to narrower spires and the marine snow is thicker, and there it will hang, arms trailing, letting the snow coat its body until it becomes itself a piece of the landscape, a new pinnacle that was not there before. It stays that way for hours, perhaps to rest, perhaps to digest, perhaps to simply feel the slow, breathing darkness press down more fully upon it.

Its intelligence is not a question easily answered. There is no tool-use in the wrenching sense, no fire, no symbol. But the city has shaped it, and it has shaped the city in turn. In the base of certain old towers, there are cleared spaces—not burrows, but deliberate removals of the soft silt, smoothed walls where bivalve shells have been arranged in concentric rings. Nothing as crude as an altar or a cache; more like the subtle alterations a current makes over eons, but done in a matter of seasons. The octopod returns to these sites cyclically, and there it does something remarkable: it uses its arms to gently tap the tubes in a sequence, not a random one, but a complex rhythm that travels through the calcium, a cross between a heartbeat and a whisper. And the worms, those frilly, blood-red sensors, do not flinch. They emerge slowly, after a moment, as if anticipating. The octopod does not eat them. It simply plucks from their crowns the accumulated fine detritus, cleaning each filament with a delicacy that the isopods, for all their industry, could never match. In return, the worms release a slight, concentrated puff of hydrogen sulfide that the octopod absorbs through its skin, a chemical currency of energy. It is a symbiosis so ancient and so particular that the city's layout has subtly grown to accommodate it: in those districts, the tubes are spaced a little wider, the avenues a little more circular, so that this large, soft creature can pass without disturbing.

There are others of its kind. Rarely, at the very edge of the city's bowl, where the towers begin to thin and the hydrothermal heat fades into the immense, uniform cold of the abyssal plain, one might encounter a second such presence—a density of shadow, a new flavour of current. When they meet, they do not fight, nor do they court. They pause at a distance, and a long conversation begins: a silent exchange of arm-patterns and colour-shifts, a hue-dialogue of bruise-purple to bone-white, that can last a whole circadian cycle (though no cycle reaches here; only the slow, internal clock of their own bodies keeps time). The conversation is not a language in any sense we would recognise, but a translation of the city itself, an exchange of maps: the state of the columbarium in the south-eastern quarter, the unusually high vent activity near the grand canal, the spawning of the blind shrimp that will soon make the avenues a moving feast. Then they move apart, each retreating into its own sphere of the structure, the only being that can move freely through the entire three-dimensional labyrinth and yet truly belong to it.

Its death, when it comes, will be a slow dissolution. It will feel the current shift—a creeping cold, a lessening of the vent's warmth—and it will retreat to a particular spot: a hidden chamber at the heart of the oldest district, where the tubes form a seamless dome, built entirely of the dead shells of earlier growth, and there it will curl into itself, one arm at a time, until it becomes just another calcified node in the endless architecture. Its skin will slough, feeding the shrimp. Its soft tissues will seep into the silt, feeding the worms. And somewhere, a younger, smaller pressure-shadow will feel the city open up a little wider, and begin its own long, slow wandering through the avenues, tentatively cleaning its first crown, learning the taste of the water and the weight of an ocean that has no surface.
