The Hearth-Keeper moves through the moss like a slow thought, its passage leaving no print, no bent stem, no displaced water. It is old in the way that stone is old—not by counting years but by having outlasted the need to count them. Its form is variable, settling only when stillness is required, and then it becomes something between a toad and a cairn: low-slung, wide-based, its back a mound of the same moss that carpets the plateau, living fur that blurs the boundary between creature and ground. When it rests, it is indistinguishable from the hillocks around it. Only the faint heat of it gives it away, a warmth that rises from the moss on its spine in a constant whisper of vapor, joining the general exhalation of the place without comment.

It has no name but the one the mountain gives it, which is not a word but a sensation: the particular resonance of the deep ground-hum when it passes through a body that has heard it for millennia. If you were to press your ear to the Hearth-Keeper's flank while it slept, you would hear that same note, the cataract's distant thrum, amplified and slowed, as if the creature's very bones were tuned to the gorge below. Perhaps they are. Perhaps it grew around that frequency the way a stalactite grows around a drip.

Its face, when it shows itself, is not a face as you would recognize it. There are eyes—two, or four, or sometimes only the suggestion of eyes, dark patches in the moss that glisten with a slow, interior light, like the surface of the footprint-pools catching a sky that isn't there. These patches orient toward you when you enter its awareness, and the awareness is old and patient and so utterly without threat that it takes you some time to realize you are being watched. The Hearth-Keeper does not hide, but it does not reveal. It simply remains, and whether you see it depends on whether you have learned to see the way the mountain sees: by patience, by texture, by the subtle difference between a stone that is only stone and a stone that breathes.

It eats nothing you would recognize as food. Its nourishment is the place itself—the steady seep of mineral-rich water through its underside, absorbed through a thousand small channels in its skin, the same skin that hosts the moss and the tiny, translucent worms that live among the moss's rootlets. It feeds on the exhalations of the saturated ground, on the fungal sweetness of rot, on the rare shafts of sun that strike its back and set off slow, deep processes of conversion. It is a living distillation of the plateau, a node where the diffuse vitality of the wet, breathing landscape collects and concentrates. It does not hunt, it does not forage. It is the hearth at which the mountain warms its slow hands.

Movement, when it moves, is a thing you do not witness but only infer. A stone that was here yesterday is there today. A patch of bare stone, smooth and concave, has gained a covering of moss between one visit and the next. The Hearth-Keeper relocates itself the way a shadow relocates—imperceptibly, continuously, a shift across hours that your eyes cannot track but your memory can. It follows something, or avoids something, or simply drifts on a circuit so vast that a human lifetime is too short to perceive its shape. There are those who have come to this plateau across generations, families who make the climb once in a grandfather's lifetime, and they say the Hearth-Keeper has been in the same spot for two hundred years. They say also that it has moved three times in the last decade, and no one knows what this portends. The Hearth-Keeper itself gives no sign of agitation. Its pace is the pace of the manganese dendrites spreading on the chamber wall.

It communicates, if that is the word, through the water. The small pools that fill your footprints, the skin of water in the stone chamber, the droplets that bead on fern-fronds—these are its medium. When the Hearth-Keeper wishes to know you, it does not approach. It sends a ripple through the shallow pool at your feet, a ring that expands and then contracts, defying physics, drawing inward to the center where a single bubble rises and breaks. In that breaking, there is a question. Not in words, not in images, but in a direct, wordless inquiry that bypasses your mind and settles in your chest: *What are you made of?* The question comes with a sense of the creature's own answer, offered in exchange—a slow, deep, mineral patience, a warmth that is not fire but the steady exothermal song of decomposition, a belonging to the mountain so complete that the boundary between self and stone is a matter of polite convention rather than fact.

It has a purpose, though purpose is a human word and fits poorly. The Hearth-Keeper maintains the equilibrium of the plateau. Not consciously, not with intent—it is the equilibrium, or a part of it, the way a keystone is part of an arch. Its presence regulates something. When it rests in one place too long, the moss grows thick and the water pools deepen and the musclewood trees begin to lean inward, as if listening. When it moves, the water redistributes, new channels open, old ones seal, and the whole breathing apparatus of the plateau adjusts itself around the Hearth-Keeper's new location. It is the stone that the rockslide pauses around. It is the note that the ground-hum tunes itself to. Without it, the guides say, the plateau would fall out of key. The moss would grow rank or die back. The water would forget its pathways. The chamber between the megaliths would flood or dry, and the dendrites would stop their slow branching.

The guides say other things about the Hearth-Keeper, too—things they will not tell outsiders, things they only whisper among themselves when the mist is thick and the ground-hum is loud and the small plink of the unseen creature echoes off the stones. They say the Hearth-Keeper remembers every footfall that has ever pressed the moss of this plateau. They say it holds the impressions of every visitor, every animal track, every scatter of bleached bones, stored in some interior archive of pressure and release, a memory written in the language of compression. They say that if you sit with it long enough—days, weeks, the span of a whole wet season—it will begin to show you the ones who came before. Not as ghosts, not as visions, but as subtle indentations in the moss beside you, the impression of a body that sat where you are sitting, a hundred years ago, two hundred, a thousand. The Hearth-Keeper releases these memories the way the chamber stone releases its skin of water when you break it: a disturbance, a ripple, a brief revelation, and then stillness again.

No one has ever seen the Hearth-Keeper breed, or birth, or die. It is possible there is only one. It is possible there have always been many, scattered across the range's high, wet places, each one tuned to its own cataract's note, each one a hearth for its own small, breathing world. The guides say that when a Hearth-Keeper finally reaches the end of its slow purpose—when the mineral deposits in its body have thickened past the point of permeability, when the moss on its back no longer drinks from the air but only crusts and crumbles—it makes its way to a place where the ground-hum is loudest, a ledge above the hidden cataract, and there it settles for the last time. Over centuries, water sheathes it in stone. Over millennia, the stone becomes part of the cliff. The guides say that certain formations in the gorge—bulbous, moss-covered protrusions above the spray line—are ancient Hearth-Keepers, still tuned, still humming, their slow song now indistinguishable from the mountain's own.

But these are stories. What you can know, what you can see, is this: a mound of moss that watches you with eyes like shallow pools, that breathes in rhythm with the drip and seep of the weeping stone, that answers your presence not with acknowledgment but with a slow, old, patient waiting for you to understand what it already knows. You are a brief disturbance in a long equilibrium. Your footprints will fill with water and then erase. Your bones, if you were to leave them here, would be tucked into the crotch of a musclewood root, bleached and fragile, no different from the others. The Hearth-Keeper will move over them eventually, and the moss will grow, and the water will seep, and the plink of the unseen creature will echo off the stones long after your name has been forgotten by everyone who ever spoke it.

It is not hostile. It is not indifferent, exactly. It is simply old enough that hospitality looks different. The Hearth-Keeper offers what it has always offered: the chamber between the stones, the dry stillness, the dendritic patterns that might be a message if you had a thousand years to read them. It offers the warmth of its slow, decomposing body, a heat that takes the edge off the high-altitude damp if you are willing to curl against a flank of living moss. It offers, if you are very still and very patient, the memory of everyone who has ever sat with it, a genealogy of pressure in the green pelt, your own weight added to a ledger that goes back to the first stone that fell and the first moss that grew and the first creature that understood that some places are meant to be kept rather than used.

When you leave—and you will leave; everyone does, eventually—the Hearth-Keeper will not watch you go. Its eyes will have already settled back into the texture of the moss, its attention returned to the slow hydraulics of water and root and heat. The ground-hum will fill the space your body occupied. The moss will rise, slowly, slowly, erasing the hollow of your weight. And in the chamber between the stones, the water-skin will settle back to perfect stillness, waiting for the next foot to break it.
