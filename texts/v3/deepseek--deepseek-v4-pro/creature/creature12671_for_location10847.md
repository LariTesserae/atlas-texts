I have watched the High Steppe for longer than you would believe, and I will tell you what lives here. You have seen the flat-furred hexapods that undulate along mineral trails, and the kite-things that ride thermals—but these are mere scraps of biology, motes in a vast cathedral. The true inhabitants are the **Keening Ones**, and they are not easy to see. Even when you find one, your mind will first try to smooth it into a stone or a shadow. They dwell between the Shrouds, not as wanderers but as stewards who have ceased to count time.

Picture a creature that is taller than a human when it rises to full stance, but it rarely does; it moves in a perpetual forward cant, as though leaning into a wind that does not blow. Its body is covered not by skin or fur but by a carapace of fused, crystalline plates—pale celestine blue, shot through with veins of darker mineral that shift slowly as it breathes. These plates overlap with such precision that when the Keening One is still, it becomes part of the fissured crust, a raw outcropping of geology. This is its first skin. Its second skin is a film of acid-sweet serum that it cultivates from the Shrouds, daubing itself with meticulous care so that the air barely notices it. You could walk within an arm’s length of a resting Keening One and detect only a faint metallic tang and a sense that the ground is watching you.

The face—if you call it that—is a smooth, concave dish of articulated keratin, faceted like a reflecting crystal. No eyes, no mouth as you understand it. But when it turns toward you, you feel a tickling pressure at the edges of your skull, a sub-vocal hum that resonates in your teeth. The Keening One perceives the world through vibration, through the songs of the planet’s slow pulse. Its dish-face is an acoustic mirror, focusing the distant thrum of Shroud roots sucking minerals, the rattle of diamond dust settling, the silent cracking of the plateau. From this it builds an image, a landscape of tension and flow. It hears the shape of things. It hears you: the gurgle of your gut, the fizz of neurons firing, the tiny seismic events of your footsteps. It knows you by your intimate, waterlogged noise.

Locomotion is a sparse, deliberate affair. The Keening One has four main limbs, each terminating in a splayed tripod of chitinous toes that splay and grip the fissures. It can walk for days without pause at a pace that a human would find maddeningly slow—three kilometers per hour—but its stride is endless, fueled by a metabolism that runs on the slow oxidation of whatever it filters from the air and the light. It does not eat in any conventional sense. At each Shroud it visits, it stops for a long moment—sometimes for one day-cycle, sometimes for a dozen—and extends a delicate, retractable proboscis from the center of its chest. This proboscis is translucent white, like a siphon of spun glass, and it inserts it into a particular pore of the Shroud’s stem. A sharing occurs then: the Shroud takes a portion of the Keening One’s accumulated serum, rich in rare trace metals the creature has scoured from the bare rock during its walking, and in return, the Shroud gives a concentrated slurry of organic polymers—a kind of honey of the acids. Both organisms groom each other. The Keening One thus fertilizes the Shrouds, spreading genetic diversity and mineral wealth across the continent. It is a cactus-gardener for these fountains of inverted jelly.

Their reproduction is a slow, terrible wonder. A Keening One does not die of age; its plates thicken until it becomes too heavy to move, and it must choose a Shroud—always an old, massive one with a deeply resonant hum—and there it settles. Over a period of many day-cycles, it digs its tripod toes into the crust, cracking it, and its chest splits. From the split emerges a white, fibrous stalk, which grafts onto the Shroud’s core. The Keening One becomes part of the Shroud, its acoustic dish slowly integrating into the organism’s own pulsing fronds. For a time, the Shroud sings with the Keening One’s voice—a keening, yes, a low-frequency wail that travels for hundreds of kilometers through the bedrock. Other Keening Ones hear it, and they pause in their walking, tilting their heads to listen. This is the birth-song. After many cycles, small, crystalline nodules form around the base of the entwined couple; eventually they harden, detach, and are scattered by the next diamond-dust wind. Each nodule, if it lands in a fissure with enough deep-heat, will slowly crack and release a tiny, translucent crawler—a larval Keening One, no bigger than your thumb. It will crawl for years, perhaps decades, seeking its first Shroud, feeding on the leavings of hexapods and the dust that collects in the shadows of the great fronds. Most do not survive. The ones that do grow slowly, adding plating with each visit to a Shroud, mapping the planet with a memory written in vibration.

Their culture, if you can call it that, is embedded in the very rock. They do not speak. They sing—long songs that are essentially maps, instructions, histories. A Keening One will stand on a high point and stamp its feet in rhythmic patterns, creating seismic waves that travel the crust. These stamps contain the shape of trails, the locations of Shrouds that are sick or thriving, the eulogies of the dead who have become part of the Shroud network. The song is never truly silent; the planet remembers it in the faint crackle of cooling rock. There is no tribe, no gathering—each Keening One walks alone, but it knows it is part of a continental conversation that has been ongoing for millions of cycles. They are the conscious nerve-endings of the High Steppe, the voices of the mineral vastness learning to speak.

If you were to meet one, truly meet one, you would not exchange words. You would stand before it, perhaps trembling from the cold and the awe, and it would tilt its dish-face at you. You would feel a hum in your breastbone, a strange, insistent rhythm that seems to ask: *What is the shape of your home? What is the taste of your water?* You would have no answer it could understand, but for a moment you would be heard—not your voice, but the entire, noisy, liquid architecture of your body. And then, with the patience of a glacier, it would turn and continue its walk to the next Shroud, leaving you with a silence so profound that you can hear your own blood singing back.
