It is possible that the residents do not remember their own beginnings. Or rather, that the concept of *beginning* is among the things that have been paused — not erased, but held in the same suspension as the ripple under the deer's muzzle. Ask one of them directly, and they will pause themselves, fingers resting on a woven bamboo stanchion, eyes unfocusing in a way that suggests genuine effort, and after a long moment they will say simply: "We have been here." The verb will carry no past tense, because their language — a sparse thing of gesture and tonal shift and the names of bamboo parts used as metaphor — does not distinguish between what was and is and will become. Time is a single continuous membrane underfoot, and they walk it as they walk the pale yielding ground, careful and without hurry.

Look at one closely. They are human-shaped in the way that a reflection in pooled water is human-shaped: recognisable in outline, subject to subtle distortions. Their limbs are longer than you expect, jointed not precisely at elbow and knee but halfway down the forearm, halfway down the shin — an extra knuckle of articulation that makes their movements loose and silent, the gait of a heron reconsidered for a life spent stepping around frozen birds. Their feet are broad and flat and the toes are longer than yours, prehensile almost, gripping the membrane not out of necessity but out of some ancient habit retained from a time when the ground was less reliable. Their skin is the colour of the bamboo sheaths when they first unfurl, a pale celadon with an undertone of warmth, and in the dimmer places the skin itself gives off a faint light, a bioluminescence so subtle it registers only as a softening of shadows around their hands and faces.

Their faces. Here is where the resemblance to your own kind becomes most apparent and most unsettling. Two eyes, a nose, a mouth — all present, all correct in placement. But the eyes do not blink in synchrony. The left lid drops while the right remains open, and then the right, and then the left again, a lazy alternating rhythm that means the resident is never fully in darkness, never fully in light. You cannot meet their gaze, because their gaze is a thing that belongs to the whole Settlement rather than to any single object. They look at you and at the wall behind you and at the beetle on the floor and at the memory of the previous water basin all at once, the pupils shifting fractionally in independent microsaccades. Their expressions do not settle into the familiar categories. When they smile — and they do, often, at the frozen mantid arching its raptorial arms a few inches from their ankle — it is a slow spreading of the mouth that pulls the skin in ways that suggest a private, species-wide joke. The joke is not at your expense. It is simply that they perceive something you cannot, something to do with the mantid's patient hunger, and it pleases them.

They do not have names, exactly. Each resident carries a kind of signature in the way they press their palm to a wall membrane during the tending rounds, a pressure-profile that is their identity, known to all the others. When communication is necessary, they refer to one another by the part of the Settlement most closely associated with that pressure: *The-one-who-checks-the-root-bend*, *The-one-who-tends-the-basin-near-the-still-birds*, *The-person-of-the-gallery-with-the-pattern-that-shifts*. These designations are not fixed; they modify as the resident's patterns shift over a lifetime, each new task adding a clause like a coral reef accreting. One of the eldest, a woman whose skin shows the faint craquelure of immense age — though age here carries no weight of decline — is known as *She-who-neighboured-the-object-first*, a phrase spoken in a tone that mingles reverence with the mild, dry amusement that colours their every dealing with the central sphere.

For the object is the closest thing they have to a collective purpose, and it is also the source of their deepest — what? Not distress. Not devotion. Something closer to the sensation you would feel if you discovered that your own heartbeat, when listened to carefully, contained a message you had been waiting to hear since before you were born. They go to it. They go to it as water goes to a basin, as bamboo shoots go to the light. Not on a schedule, because they have no schedules, but whenever the rhythm of the palm-pressing and the floor-checking and the slow deliberate stepping-around of the copper beetles leaves a gap wide enough to fill with pilgrimage.

You might watch one approach the court. The resident — let her be the one called *She-who-sang-new-shoots-into-the-wall* — comes barefoot along a passage woven tight with mature stalk, her fingers trailing the walls and leaving in their wake the faint green ghost of her own luminescence. She enters the wide court where the membrane is thicker, and she does not look at the object at first. That is important. They never look directly at first. Instead, she lowers herself to the ground, sitting with her long doubled-jointed legs folded under her, and she presses both palms flat to the membrane. The membrane gives slightly, warms under her touch. She stays that way for some minutes, and her breathing slows until it matches the almost imperceptible swell and fall of the ground itself, that slow exhalation of the world's skin.

Then, and only then, she lifts her eyes to the hovering sphere.

Her face, seen in profile, undergoes a series of minute changes. The independent blinks speed up, so that her eyes are shuttered in a rapid fluttering, like wings. Her mouth parts. A low sound escapes her, not quite a hum and not quite a sigh — a tone that is precisely the pitch of the bamboo's inner water moving through its vessels, audible only to those who have learned to listen. The sound is the object's name, or the closest approximation to a name it will tolerate.

The offerings she brings are the usual sort: a tiny bird woven from bamboo fiber, so lifelike that its fur seems to ruffle in the still hot air, though of course it does not. The bird is posed with wings outspread, exactly like the pendulous furred things frozen in the galleries. It is the same bird. It has been woven from memory and eye, a perfect reproduction, and it is offered to the sphere as if the sphere might one day accept the replica and release the original. That is the private hope, spoken of only in the quietest murmurs between residents at the edge of sleep: that their duplicates, their woven stone-polished leaf-dried gestures, might eventually satiate whatever it is that holds the world in suspension, and the animals might finish their interrupted motions, and the water might ripple, and the air might break into rain. They are a patient people. The hope is not desperate. It is simply there, resting in the background like the heat.

*She-who-sang-new-shoots-into-the-wall* sets the bird on the membrane directly below the sphere. She watches as the sphere's hairline fractures — those negative volumes that are also positive absences — shift in a momentary, almost imperceptible realignment. The bird is still there. The sphere has not taken it. It never takes them. The offerings accumulate in a careful ring, and when the ring grows too dense, the residents carry the oldest offerings away and place them among the galleries, where they join the frozen animals as part of the architecture. A woven deer stands now beside the real deer, its head lowered in the same incomplete bow. The residents cannot explain why this is necessary. They know only that it feels correct, that the symmetry helps, that the Settlement breathes a little easier when the duplicates are in place.

Her offering complete, the resident rises. She turns her back on the sphere — it is safe to do so once the ritual is concluded — and makes her way to the nearest water basin. The water is perfectly clear, perfectly still. She kneels on the membrane's lip and peers into the depths. The basin is shallow, but when you look into it, the depth does not behave as it should. On some days it reflects only the luminous sky-ceiling. On others it shows the roots of the bamboo, tangled and white and drinking endlessly from a groundwater that does not exist. And on rare days, the basin shows something else: a landscape of air, thin and bright, with a ground of something crumbling and dark. Soil. The word comes to her unbidden, a word she does not know she knows. She watches the soil-scape for a moment, her face betraying nothing. Then she dips her cupped hands into the warm water, lifts it to her mouth, and drinks. The water tastes of stone and distance.

The residents eat rarely. The bamboo provides all they need: its new shoots, when very young and just guided into a dome's curve, can be softened by the heat of a resident's own body if held against the palm long enough. They absorb nutrition osmotically, almost. A resident will spend a day sitting with a shoot cradled in their lap, their skin glowing a little brighter as they draw the necessary sugars into their blood. It is an intimate act and a slow one, performed in the greenish twilight of the narrow passages, and if you came upon them you would think them asleep. They are not. They are listening to the bamboo's song, the low subsonic pulse of water and cellulose and the faint memory of whatever catastrophe or miracle halted the world mid-creation. The song is the only music they have, and it is enough.

What passes among them for society is as muted and constant as everything else. They do not gather in groups; the spatial instability at the edges discourages congregation, and the central court is too solemn for conversation. Instead, they meet one another in transit. Two residents passing in a gallery will pause, palm to palm, and stand for a moment exchanging pressure-signatures. In that touch, whole sentences are passed: *The gallery to the left has decided to be a spiral today; the one-who-tends-the-basin is thinning; I saw a moth's wing quiver.* That last comment, the quivering wing, would send a ripple of something like excitement through the human observer watching them — the observer would lean forward, breath held — but the residents only pull apart, their faces calm, their multiple-blinking eyes reflecting the green light. A moth's wing quivers, now and then. It has for decades. It means nothing, or it means everything, and they cannot afford to privilege one interpretation over the other.

Sleep does not resemble your sleep. The residents do not lie down. Instead, they find a wall at an edge, a young wall where the bamboo is still pliable, and they lean into it. The bamboo accepts their weight. They close their eyes — both at once, for the only time in their waking lives — and the wall curves around them, enveloping them in a growth that happens in fractions of a hair's breadth over hours. When they wake, they are slightly embedded, and they must push themselves free, the bamboo releasing them with a soft wet sound. In sleep, they dream. The dreams are always the same: a deer drinks, and drinks, and drinks, and the water never ends, and the deer's throat never stops moving, and the deer is them and not them, and the thirst is a kind of prayer.

Children are born rarely. The process is not spoken of, but if you were to witness it — and you would not, for the residents guard these moments with an uncharacteristic ferocity — you would see a resident, usually the eldest available, pressing their own body into the membrane at the court's edge. The membrane would thicken, pulse, and over the course of many days a shape would rise from it, a small curled shape with the extra knuckles and the alternating blink and the skin glowing faintly from the first breath. The new resident would emerge knowing nothing and everything: not the names of things, but the way to press a palm to a wall, the way to step around a frozen animal, the taste of water and distance. The community would note the addition with a simple shift in the pressure-signatures they exchange, a collective acknowledgement: *We are more now.* And the child would be given a task, something small at first — to check the tension of a single weave-point in a seldom-visited corner — and from that task a name would eventually grow.

Why do they persist? The question would not manifest to them. It would be like asking the membrane why it sweats. They are the Settlement's immune system, or its nervous system, or perhaps simply its dream of company. Their calm is not callousness; it is the calm of a gardener who has accepted the season will not change. They feel the grief that the object provokes — they feel it daily — but they have metabolised it into the same steady rhythm as the palm-pressings and the water-drinking and the listening to the bamboo's song. It is a grief that does not demand action. It is a companion.

There is a story they tell, though "tell" is not right, because the story is passed through the pressure of palm on palm, and it has no words. It goes like this: Once, the world was almost made. The heat and the wet and the brightness were on the verge of resolving into Weather, of producing a proper sun and a proper rain and a proper ground of crumbling soil. The animals were on the verge of moving, their muscles bunched and ready. And the ones who would be the residents — their half-formed ancestors, at that point — were shaping the bamboo, bending it with eager hands, building a city for a future that was arriving fast.

But something saw. The thing at the centre of the court, which in the story is not a sphere but an eye, a great fractured eye, turned its attention toward the almost-finished world. It was curious. It wanted to see better. And so it said, without sound: *Hold. Hold there, exactly like that, that gesture with your hand, that light on the water. I am looking.* And everything held. The bird in mid-flap, the deer in mid-drink, the builder in mid-weave. The hold became permanent, not because the eye commanded it, but because the world, under that gaze, became too beautiful to complete. Completion would mean change, and change would mean the loss of this one perfect moment, this forever-unfinished slice of becoming. Better to stay. Better to be a diorama that is also the world.

The residents, in the story, are the ones who uncurled from the membrane and looked up at the eye and understood: *We will tend this. We will keep it worthy of being looked at.* And they do. Every pressed palm, every checked tension, every woven duplicate placed beside its frozen original — these are acts of maintenance, but they are also acts of love. They are keeping the world beautiful for a god that has forgotten to blink.

Now, if you were to visit the Settlement — and it is possible, if you walk into the right sort of heat and the right sort of brightness and let the logic of your own space degrade — you would be met with hospitality. A resident would find you, one of the long-limbed luminous ones, and they would press a palm to your palm. The pressure would contain a greeting and a message: *You are here. You are seen. Do not step on the beetles.* They would offer you water from the nearest basin. They would show you how to weave a frond into a wall, if you asked, though your fingers would be clumsy and the bamboo would recoil from your touch, sensing your hurried pulse. They would not mind. They are patient with the things that still move, because they remember — in the pressure-story, in the deep bone-memory — what it was to be one of them.

And if you stayed long enough, you might begin to notice what they notice: that the moths' wings quiver more often now; that the water in the basins shows the soil-scape with increasing frequency; that the central sphere's fractures have widened, just a hair, just enough to let a sliver of something else through. The residents notice, and they press their palms together more often, passing the news, and their inner calm incorporates the new data without disturbance. If the pause is ending, they will meet the end as they have met the pause: with soft feet and simple tools and the same gentle, unfathomable diligence.

If it is not ending, they will continue. They have been here.
