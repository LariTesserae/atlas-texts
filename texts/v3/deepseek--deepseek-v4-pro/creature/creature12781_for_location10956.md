The builders have no name for themselves; a name is a sound that ends, and they are not accustomed to endings. They know themselves by the shape of their work—the long, slow curvature of a thoroughfare that took ten thousand generations to wear smooth, the particular bloom of a mineral garden that has been tended by the same lineage since before the ceiling stars shifted their patterns. Memory, for them, is not stored in minds but in stone, in the very structure of the city, and they read it with their feet and their antennae as they move, an unbroken inheritance of vibration and texture.

I watch one now, an elder judging by the encrustation of pale, lace-like mineral deposits along her segmented back. She is half as tall as I am but her body is long, low, composed of perhaps forty segments each bearing a pair of legs that end not in feet but in soft, splaying pads that grip the polished stone without sound. Her head is eyeless, a smooth shield of bone-coloured chitin from which two long, fern-like antennae unfurl, constantly trembling, tasting the air for the chemical signature of the growths, for the faintest shift in the drone. She moves with the deliberate, fluid motion of a creature for whom haste is not a concept—haste implies a separation between now and later, and here, now and later are the same thing.

She is building. Not building as we would understand it, with intent to change, but building as maintenance, as breath. Her mouthparts, a complex array of feathery maxillae, are extruding a paste from the mineral-rich nodules that grow along her flanks—a substance the colour of old ivory that hardens on contact with the dry air. She applies it to the rim of a fluted archway, a structure that has been under construction for four hundred thousand years. The arch is part of a spiral ramp that winds up a tower of plated growths, and the rim needs thickening because the micro-flora have eaten it back to a dangerous thinness. She knows this not because anyone told her, but because the vibration of the ramp has changed; when the drone of the growths passes through it, there is now a higher, thinner harmonic that her pads detect. It is a wrongness that tastes bitter on her antennae. So she corrects it, as her mother did, and her mother before her, in a chain of identical correction stretching back to a time when the ceiling was lower and the stars were fewer.

Is she alone? No, there are others, dozens of them spaced along the thoroughfare, each tending a task with the same absorbed, ritualistic patience. They communicate rarely, and never with sound—their world is already so full of sound that deliberate noise would be an intrusion. Instead, when they pass one another, their antennae brush and exchange a burst of chemical information: this wall needs mending, the mineral salts are richer near the eastern fissure, the lichen bloom on the third gallery is dimming and may need supplementation. These messages are not urgent. They are offered as gifts, as part of the mutual, ongoing project of existence. They have no leaders, no elders' council, because every individual carries the whole of the city's knowledge in their body: the taste of the correct mineral mix, the rhythm of the growths' slow pulsation, the map of every ramp and gallery traced in the instinctual pathways of their nervous systems.

I notice, after a long time, that there are subtle differences between them. The elder I watch has a slightly more elaborate pattern of encrustation, and her antennae are longer, more intricately branched. A younger one—still an adult, but with fewer segments and a paler, less patinated shell—is working higher up on the ramp, applying paste to a new growth that has only recently been trained into an arch. The younger one occasionally pauses and extends her antennae downwards, towards the elder, as if checking her work against the larger template. The elder does not respond directly, but I sense a faint change in the chemical drift, a kind of assent, or perhaps just the continuity of being.

And there are the scuttlers, the tiny multi-legged things that live in symbiosis. They ride on the builders' backs, cleaning their joints and feasting on the micro-flora that would otherwise overgrow the chitin. The builders do not acknowledge them, but I see the elder pause by a wall where a colony of scuttlers is tapping an unusually complex rhythm—not the random percussive chatter, but a pattern that repeats, a sequence of taps that echoes the drone's harmonics. It might be a form of music. It might be a language that the scuttlers themselves understand. The elder's antennae dip towards them for a moment, and I could swear there is a quality of attention there, a listening. Perhaps the scuttlers are the builders' ears, translating the higher frequencies into something the larger creatures can sense. Or perhaps they are simply co-inhabitants, each living their own parallel existence in this vast shared body.

The flying things drift lower now, their membranous wings catching the faint updraft from the thoroughfare. One lands on the elder's back, so light it doesn't register, and its dark eye swivels, taking in the cavern with a dispassionate, panoramic gaze. It is not an animal; it is a sense organ, a mobile node in the city's distributed awareness. I understand this gradually, as I watch others land on the walls, on the scuttlers, on the edges of the growths. Each one is a point of perception, and the information they gather is carried back to the builders in some imperceptible way—perhaps chemical, perhaps vibrational. The city sees itself through these eyes, a slow, compound vision of dim phosphorescence and shifting drone.

And I realize then that the builders are not individuals in the way I first thought. Their apparent solitude is an illusion born of my own perception. They are cells in a larger organism, the city itself, which is not a construction but a living, thinking entity, a slow mind made of mineral and chitin and sound. The thoroughfares are its circulatory system, the growths its bones and lungs, the builders its hands and its memory. The drone is its voice, a constant song of self-maintenance, and the scuttlers' percussive chatter is its thought, a ceaseless, multi-threaded monologue that never repeats because it is always present.

The elder finishes her work and moves on, her pads kissing the stone, her antennae sweeping. She will walk this same route until her segments wear thin and her mineral paste runs dry, and then she will settle into a niche in the wall and become part of the city in a different way—her body will be slowly encrusted by the same micro-flora she once tended, and her chitin will dissolve into the mineral matrix, feeding the growths that will, in another epoch, be shaped by her descendants. The city wastes nothing, loses nothing, not even the memory of her particular gait. I can feel it in the stone, a shallow undulation worn by her specific repetition, a signature written in the polish. If I close my eyes and press my palm to the floor, I can almost hear it: a low, subsonic hum that is both her and the millions before her, a steady, ongoing chord in the world's longest requiem.

I switch off my lamp and stand in the dark. The phosphorescent lichen glows faintly, and the pinprick stars overhead seem to pulse in time with the drone. The city stretches away, infinite and calm, and I feel for the first time what it means to be a transient thing—a flicker of heat in a place where all heat is slow and distributed, a single loud heartbeat in a body whose pulse is measured in millennia. I am a mayfly standing on the skin of a mountain that breathes.

And yet, as I stand there, the drone does not exclude me. It wraps around my silence and incorporates it. I am noticed, but not as a threat or a stranger. To the city, I am a new texture, a temporary mineral anomaly, a gust of warm, wet air that will pass. The builders will not alter their work for me; the scuttlers will tap my presence into their endless song, and then I will be gone, a brief, bright harmonic in the long night of the cavern. The city has seen other things like me, perhaps, in its long memory—things that come from the lightless world above, where air moves fast and water falls from the sky. It has absorbed those memories into its stone, and it waits, as it has always waited, for the next shift in the chemical pulses, the next slow alteration of the drone.

And that is who lives here. Not a creature, but a community of being, a slow, mineral ecology that has become conscious. The builders are its mind, the scuttlers its words, the flyers its eyes, the growths its breath. And the city—the polished stone, the fluted arches, the ribbed domes—is its body, a body that has been dreaming itself into existence for so long that the dream and the stone are one.
