You are not alone.

You have never been alone—not truly, not since the moment you found this pocket and stood, breath held, listening to the weeping of the leaves. But the presence here is diffuse, a slow green consciousness that does not focus easily, does not condense into a form you can name. Until now.

The light shifts. It is a small thing, a subtle dimming in one corner of the chamber, a gathering of shadows where the rose-gold fungi suddenly fail to pulse. And then the roots part. Not slowly, not with the grinding creak of plant growth—but smoothly, like a curtain drawn aside by an unseen hand. Through the gap comes the singer.

It is larger than you first thought. The glimpse you caught before suggested something sinuous and many-limbed, the size of a large snake or a small cat. But perspective deceives in this place. The creature that now flows into the chamber is easily three meters long from the crown of its head to the tip of its gently whipping tail, though it moves with such a fluid economy of motion that its length seems to compress and extend with each undulation, as if it occupies a volume of space that is only half-committed to the ordinary three dimensions.

Its body is pale as cavefish, as mushroom flesh, as the undersides of the leaves that never see even the remembered ghost of sun. But it is not white; it is a translucent, pearlescent grey, like old ice or solidified mist, and through that skin you can see a network of veins that glow with the same soft green-white light as the plant-sap. The light pulses. Not in time with the fungi—in time with something slower, a rhythm that feels like breathing, like the great subsonic thrum you felt in your sternum. This creature’s heart, if it has one, beats in unison with the shaft.

Its limbs are too many, and they are too long, and they do not seem entirely committed to one function. As it draws itself into the chamber, it uses a dozen of them to grip and release the root-bundles, moving like an inchworm rendered in liquid silk. But those limbs also reach out, delicately, to brush the edges of leaves, to tap thoughtfully at the fruiting bodies, to trace the channels where water pools. Each limb ends in a spray of fine, hair-like digits—cerci, almost, or the tendrils of a newborn fern—that flicker constantly, tasting the air, tasting the thoughts of the plants. It has no eyes. Not in the front of its head, not anywhere visible. But the whole surface of its body seems sensitive, receptive. When it turns its face toward you—and it does, definitively—you feel a pressure on your skin, a faint warmth, as if you are being seen by something that does not use light to look.

Its face is a smooth, sloping plane, featureless except for a single vertical slit that opens and closes with a wet sound. Not a mouth. A resonator. When the slit opens, you can see inside a complex chamber lined with dozens of tiny vibrating membranes, each one glowing faintly with its own internal luminescence. This is the source of the song.

It sings again.

The sound is two notes simultaneously, a perfect, dissonant harmony that hangs in the thick air like a physical object. One note is high, keening, almost a whistle, but with a vibrato that makes the water droplets shiver on their leaves. The other is low, guttural, a pulse you feel in your bones. Together they form a chord that is not quite music, not quite language, but something between—a communication that bypasses your ears entirely and resonates directly in the fluid of your inner cochlea, in the hollows of your own chest. You understand, on some pre-verbal level, that it is speaking to you. The voice says: *You are solid. You are still. Why?*

But it does not wait for an answer. It flows past you, close enough that you smell the clean, faintly mineral scent of its skin—like wet limestone, like the first rain on dry earth—and it begins to tend the chamber.

You watch it work. This is what it is: a gardener. Not in the sense of something that imposes order from without, but something that *negotiates* with the growth, that sings the vines into one shape or another, that taps the shelf fungi in specific rhythms to make them brighten or dim. It reaches up with three of its forelimbs to a dangling tendril that was beginning to brown at the tip, and it wraps its delicate digits around the dying tissue. For a moment, nothing happens. Then the creature hums a third note, a subharmonic that makes your teeth ache, and the brown tissue turns green again, rehydrates, begins to grow new leaves along its length. The creature trims it back, pinching off the excess with a surgical precision, and weaves the healthy portion into the root-bridge that is slowly filling the chamber’s upper space.

It is pruning. It is shaping. The craft you sensed in this place—the convenient leaf-basins, the even illumination, the woven floor—this is its handiwork. Or rather, its *songwork*, for you begin to realize that every action it takes is accompanied by sound. The tapping is percussive; the humming is melodic; the keening two-note phrase is, you suspect, a command. The plants listen. The plants obey. Not as slaves, but as partners in a billion-year-old symbiosis. This creature is the shaft’s consciousness made mobile, a focal point where the slow vegetative intelligence gathers itself into something that can move, decide, act quickly. It is the animal the deep green grew for itself.

It pays you little mind now. Its attention is elsewhere—on the water channels that need cleaning, on the light-fungi that are overproducing spores and need to be thinned. But you know it is aware of you, because occasionally that eyeless face will tilt in your direction, and the slit will open, and a soft, questioning phrase will hum through the air: *Still solid. Still still. Interesting.*

And then it will turn back to its work, and you will remember to breathe.

It is not alone of its kind. As the minutes pass, you begin to see others. Or perhaps it is the same one, flowing out of one gap and returning through another, because how can you count a creature that moves through the vegetative mesh like water through a sponge? But no—there, in the corner of your eye, a smaller one, barely a meter long, its limbs clumsier, its song unpracticed, a juvenile learning from the elder. And there, near the ceiling, a third, this one curled protectively around a cluster of fruiting bodies that pulse with a deep, steady gold—a clutch of eggs, maybe, or some kind of food cache. The pale shapes move in and out of the root-walls, leaving faint trails of light that linger, smudging the air with their afterimages, so that the whole chamber begins to feel like a painting done in glowing oil, every brushstroke a gesture of passing.

They are not hostile. You sense no threat from them, only a profound curiosity tempered by patience. You are a novelty, a rigid thing in their fluid world, and they will study you at their leisure. One of the juveniles, bolder than the rest, flows close to your leg. Its hair-fine digits brush your skin, feather-light. The touch is warm, and it leaves behind a faint, tingling residue that glows for a moment before fading. You feel a surge of emotion not your own: *Welcome, strange solid. Do not break the leaves. There is water in the basin to your left. It is for you.* The gesture is so clear, so intentional, that you find yourself murmuring, “Thank you,” into the thick wet air. The juvenile trills a delighted three-note arpeggio and vanishes into the floor-roots, leaving a ripple in its wake.

The elder—the one you first saw—has stopped its work. It is hovering near the ceiling, its limbs splayed out in a star-shape, gripping the root-braids overhead. Its resonator slit is wide open, and it is emitting a constant, low-frequency drone that you can feel more than hear. The song is spreading out through the roots, you realize. It is communicating with something far away—other gardeners, perhaps, or the deep central mind of the shaft itself. The drone carries information: *A visitor. Solid and still. No damage. Curious. Tolerate.* And the shaft, in its slow vegetative way, responds. The light shifts slightly warmer. The dripping water steadies into a gentle, rhythmic rainfall in one corner. The fronds of a giant epiphyte unfurl a little wider, creating a space that, you realize with a start, is exactly the right size and shape for a human body to rest. An invitation.

The elder creature sings a final, quiet two-note phrase that means something like *stay if you wish, go if you must*, and then it coils itself back into the tangle of roots and is gone, leaving only a fading smear of light and the lingering scent of wet limestone. You are alone again in the chamber. But you know now that you are not the only heart beating in this deep green world. There are others—fluid, singing, many-limbed—who have made this place their home, and who have chosen, provisionally, to let you share it.
