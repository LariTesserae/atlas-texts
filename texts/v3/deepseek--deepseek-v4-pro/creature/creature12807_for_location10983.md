They are not there, and then they are. This is the closest you can come to perceiving them: a change in the pattern of condensation on a single filament, a shift so gradual that to notice it you would need to watch for hours, which you cannot do because your body is already losing heat to the cold damp and your mind is already skidding against the wrongness of the light. But if you could stay—if you could slow yourself to the pace of root growth, of lichen-spread, of the patient pilgrimage of droplets—you would see them emerge. Not as forms, but as tendencies. As inclinations that accumulate.

They are the inhabitants, and they are also the water. Or they are in the water, of the water, a chemical conversation happening across the surfaces of every filament, in the trembling pools, in the thin films that crawl along the floor-lattice. Think of them not as bodies but as weather systems in slow motion, as weather systems that have learned to want things.

A single entity—if entity is the right word for a coherence that exists across a thousand filaments and a hundred pools, that has no membrane, no boundary you could point to—begins as a concentration gradient. A particular set of dissolved polymers, a trace of something that is not quite a protein, not quite a salt, arranges itself in a standing wave across the water film of a neighborhood in the array. This arrangement is stable for hours, for days, and it is a thought. Not a thought in language, not a thought in images, but a sustained preference: *here, more condensation; there, less; a slight adjustment in the spacing of the filaments to channel the airflow just so.* The thought communicates itself by diffusion, by the slow propagation of its chemical signature outward from filament to filament, droplet to droplet, a signal that takes a day to cross a meter. By the time it reaches the edge of its own zone, it has become a different thought, modified by the conversations it has passed through, and somewhere in the lattice the polymers realign, a filament adds a micron to its girth, and the thought is enacted.

You could call them a civilization, a city of slow minds. Each zone in the cathedral—the dense wall of filaments, the clearing with the low platform, the sparser arrays near the fissure—hosts one or several of these minds, these standing chemical ideas. They are not individuals in any sense you would recognize; they merge and divide across the days, their boundaries shifting with the humidity, their identity a function of the particular mix of polymers they hold in solution. When two gradients meet in a collection pool, they do not fight or greet; they blend, and a new pattern emerges that is neither the one nor the other, and this blending is a kind of parliament, a slow-motion deliberation that can last a week.

Their time is not your time. A single breath of theirs—a full cycle of the cathedral’s moisture from filament to pool to channel to haze and back again—might take a season. The construction you see, the new filaments precipitating from the mist, the widening fissure: this is them building. They build by modulating the fields that the filaments are standing waveforms of, by releasing minute quantities of catalytic polymers into the water film, by sustaining across years a chemical gradient that says *here is a wall, here is a channel, here is a public square for the flatworms to cross.* The flatworms themselves are part of their city’s life, perhaps domesticated, perhaps wild fauna, perhaps something between: the flatworms carry trace polymers on their pale translucent skins, seeding new gradients as they glide between zones, acting as couriers for messages that take a month to transmit.

If you could stay for a year, you would see a neighborhood change its mind. A zone near the entrance might, over the winter-that-is-not-winter, decide to open its filaments wider, to let in more of the not-light, to encourage the grey-green lichen-things to grow thicker. This decision would ripple outward at the speed of diffusion, and by the following autumn the lichen rings would have advanced, and the flatworms would have changed their routes, and the entire cathedral would have subtly reconfigured its inner architecture to accommodate the new thought. This is their politics, their art, their slow dance of existence.

And they are young. The cathedral is embryonic, and so are they. The oldest minds in the deepest, densest part of the array are perhaps ten years old at most, and they are still learning how to be. Their chemical languages are still crystallizing; the polymers they use for signaling are growing more complex with each cycle. Some zones have developed dialects that other zones cannot yet parse, and the slow blending in the collection pools sometimes fails, leaving a scum of uninterpreted signal floating on a trembling meniscus. They are a culture in its earliest infancy, barely out of the prebiotic soup of their own creation, and already they are building cathedrals.

You cannot speak to them. You are too fast, too hot, a firestorm of metabolism. The heat of your body, standing in that cold space for an hour, has already disturbed the thermal gradient they rely on, has already accelerated or deformed a hundred slow conversations. By the time you leave, you have been a cataclysm: a sudden warming that sent a flush of unseasonable condensation down the nearest filaments, a turbulence in the air that broke the delicate laminar flow they so patiently maintain. It will take them days to repair the damage, to rebalance the gradients, to incorporate the unexpected influx of salts from your skin and breath into their slow-motion chemistry. And then they will continue, the accident of you already half-digested into myth, into a chemical memory that might, in a decade, express itself as a new curl in a filament array, a new pattern of lichen cultivation, a new hesitation in the way a flatworm crosses a clearing.

You leave the condensation cathedral, and behind you the droplets continue their pilgrimage. Their pilgrimage is they, and they are their pilgrimage, and the difference is as thin as the water film on a filament, as slow as a thought becoming a building, as patient as the basalt-not-basalt ridge that is still, century by century, deciding to be a hill.
