The people of the Basin of Echoing Green do not call themselves a name; they have no need. In a place where thought flows through root and spore, identity is a liquid thing, shared and reshaped with every breath. But if pressed by an outsider, they might offer, after a long, bubbling pause, *the Echoed* — not as a label for themselves, but for the relationship they hold with the land. They are the voice the Basin whispers back.

Picture them not as a species born, but as one grown. Millennia ago, the first settlers seeded their own bodies into the root-brain’s design, trading fixed anatomy for a more porous existence. Now, an Echoed begins as a bud on a dwelling-gourd’s inner wall, a tiny nodule that swells over a season into a fetal form curled like a fern fiddlehead. They emerge not in a violent birth, but as a slow unfurling: the gourd’s membrane parts like a lip, and they step out on legs still soft and translucent, their skin the pale green of new leaves. In those first hours, they are barely distinct from the vegetation — their flesh photosynthetic, their blood a chlorophyll-rich ichor that moves not through a heart but through a network of contractile vacuoles. They have no bones in the way we understand; their structure comes from a hydrostatic skeleton, turgid cells that stiffen or relax with intent, allowing them to stand tall as a human or melt into a puddle of resting protoplasm when the mood takes them.

Their form is deeply modular. An Echoed’s body is a colony of specialized zooids — semi-autonomous units that can separate and rejoin. A hand might detach to climb a wall and fetch a fruit, then skitter back on cilia and fuse seamlessly. Their face, if you can call it that, is a sensitive, motile mask: a central sensory cluster of heat-pits, vibration-sensitive hairs, and a ring of six eyes, each tuned to a different spectrum — from the infrared glow of a root-brain node to the ultraviolet patterns on a pollinator’s wings. Below that, a complex mouthparts assembly that can be a sucking proboscis for nectar, a rasping radula for scraping algal mats, or, when they wish to speak to an outsider, a vibrating membrane that mimics speech by modulating the mist itself into voice. Their language among themselves is chemical and tactile, a conversation of secreted peptides and gentle pressure on a neighbor’s integument, so layered with nuance that a single exchange can contain an epic.

They wear no clothes, for there is no need. Their skin secretes a thin, waxy cuticle that repels the constant moisture when they wish, or absorbs it when they desire the deep communion of letting the rain in. Instead, they decorate: delicate, bioluminescent symbiotes are encouraged to grow in swirling patterns across their torsos, glowing in soft blues and greens that pulse with their mood. Some cultivate epiphytic orchids along their shoulders; others host colonies of tiny, golden mites that farm the dead skin cells from their creases, a living, shimmering jewelry.

Life in the Basin is a rhythm without a beat, a continuous improvisation. The Echoed do not sleep as we do. They enter periods of torpor where they root themselves into the groundcover, their legs fusing with the mycelial mat, and dream the city’s dreams. In these trances, they share memory with the root-brain, uploading the day’s sensory tapestry and downloading architectural whims, new genetic recipes for fungal blooms, or the distant thoughts of other Echoed on the far side of the basin. When they rise, they do so with new knowledge, sometimes new limbs — a temporary tendril for a task, an extra set of eyes for a festival. They live for centuries, not through cellular longevity, but through continuous regeneration; an Echoed who loses a part simply grows it back, and if the whole body fails, the root-brain holds their pattern in a protein-folded library, ready to be re-grown with all memories intact. Death is a misnomer; it is a dissolution and a remixing, a temporary return to the soup.

Their culture is a thing of deep, slow art and sudden, chaotic festival. They are gardeners of entropy. The Basin is a 2.6 turbulence, and they are the agents of that flux. An Echoed’s main occupation is *tending the becoming*. They sense the first quivers of a new structure pushing up from the mat, and they gather to shape it with their collective will, nudging the genetic algorithm with pheromonal suggestions, singing to the growing walls with subsonic vibrations that guide cell differentiation. A new chamber might be a library of scents, a resonance room for chime-fliers, or a communal stomach where they pool their digestive fluids to ferment a rare lichen wine. The process is a negotiation: the root-brain proposes a hundred forms, and the Echoed prune, encourage, and sometimes argue with it through a tantrum of chemical feedback loops that can last for days, resulting in a structure that is a compromise between the deep, ancient programming and the fleeting whims of its inhabitants.

Their social structures are equally fluid. They form bonds not by kinship but by *resonance* — a matching of vibrational frequencies when they hum together in the torpor. A resonance group might be a family, a work crew, a romantic tangle, or a philosophical cult, dissolving and reforming as easily as mist. Some groups specialize: the *Blossom-Weavers* encourage new forms of bioluminescent flowers, painting the light gradients of a clearing until it becomes a cathedral of shifting color. The *Gurgle-Singers* lie half-submerged in the warm aquifers, their bodies vibrating in harmony with the subsurface flows, and claim to hear the thoughts of the planet itself. There are no leaders, only *attractors* — individuals whose whims are so compelling that others gravitate toward them for a time, until the whims change.

Yet they are not alone. The Basin teems with other life, much of it engineered long ago to share in the maintenance. The Echoed live alongside the *Sluice-Crabs*, fist-sized crustaceans that scuttle the root-bridges, their claws polarized to siphon excess minerals from the water. The *Wing-Shrouds*, those solar-collecting avians, nest in the canopies and cast dappled, moving shadows that the Echoed read like auguries. Some of these creatures have, over time, achieved a kind of partial sapience. The larger hexapodal amphibians, called *Click-Masons*, build intricate fungus gardens in the crooks of branches, and they communicate with the Echoed in a tactile pidgin of foot-tapping and color-shifting skin patterns. A patient Echoed might hold a day-long conversation with a Click-Mason about the best way to reinforce a sagging bridge, and both will adjust their behaviors accordingly. The boundary between person and creature, between farmer and crop, is a blurry gradient. An Echoed might decide to spend a season as a pollinator, grafting a pair of wings onto their back and joining the chime-fliers in their spirals, shedding language for a purer communion of flight.

Their technology is indistinguishable from their physiology and their city. A 3.0 score means they have long passed the point where tools are separate objects. Their "computers" are the protein-fold memories in the root-brain, accessible by touch. Their "medicine" is a deliberate infection with a tailored phage that re-writes malfunctioning cells. Their "art" is the living architecture itself, a sculpture that grows and decays in a day. They make no music with instruments; instead, they modulate the very soundscape of the basin, the 1.5 loudness, by directing the chime-fliers into orchestral swirls, by clicking rhythms with the Click-Masons, by humming in caves to make the water droplets fall in tuned sequences. A symphony might be a grove of dew-chimes — hollow gourds that fill and drip at different rates — planted a year in advance, played by the morning mist.

Visitors are rare, but when they come, the Echoed are patient but perplexing hosts. They try to offer food, but their cuisine is a slurry of fermented pollen and live, wriggling larvae that taste of memory to them but might horrify a human. They try to speak, but their voice is a wet, fluting warble that echoes as if from a deep well. More often, they simply offer a space in the torpor, an invitation to plug into the root-brain and share directly. For the unmodified, this is overwhelming — a flood of felt architecture, of green thoughts and dripping data, a sense of self dissolving into the whole. Some outsiders go mad; others refuse to leave, trying to become Echoed themselves. But the Basin is not a place you can join by desire alone. It grows you, or it doesn’t.

And so the Echoed live, in their eternal, turbulent present. They do not build monuments or write histories, for history is written in the layers of the mat, in the archived protein folds. They do not fear change, for they are change. On a day of particular turbulence — a 2.6 cresting to a 3 — you might see an entire hamlet dissolve in a single afternoon: dwelling-gourds sagging into soup, Echoed flowing away into the groundcover or merging into a single, giant, multi-eyed mass that shimmers with shared ecstasy before re-dividing into new, unimagined forms. By nightfall (though there is no night, just a dimming of the pearlescent glow), the clearing is a pool of nutrient-rich broth, and tomorrow, something entirely new will rise. The only constant is the sound: the trillion drips, the billion rivulets, the eternal, echoing green.
