You are not the first to believe this world is empty. That is the first and most enduring falsehood the plateau whispers to visitors—not in words, but in the sheer enormity of its stillness. But emptiness and fullness are not opposites here; they are the same thing viewed through different lenses of time. To see the inhabitants, you must stop looking for flesh and blood, for breath and pulse as you know them. You must learn to see in geological time, to feel motion the way a mountain feels the creep of its own roots.

### The Mica-Folk (Cognates of the Sliver)

The creature you saw—the flat, flexible sliver of mica-like substance—is the most numerous and arguably the least specialized of the plateau’s fauna. Let us call them the Mica-Folk, though they have no folk, no society, no mind that we could recognize. They are wafers of a composite material: silica sheets interleaved with metallic oxides and traces of the same phosphorescent salts that vein the ground. They range in size from a fingernail to a dinner plate, their thickness never exceeding a millimeter, and they move by propagating ripples through a fringe of crystalline cilia. These cilia are not organic; they are solid-state actuators, flexing in response to micro-changes in the ambient electric field. Their motion is a slow, soundless glide, a single "step" taking upward of an hour.

They perceive the world through a distributed sheen of sensors across their dorsal surface, detecting the infraglow's intensity and polarization. In fact, the infraglow is their food: they harvest energy directly from the slow-churn electromagnetic flux of the ground, using photovoltaic proteins of iron and sulfur that were never proteins at all but mineral complexes, self-templating and error-correcting over aeons. When a Mica-Folk feeds, its body flushes with a deeper amber, sometimes pulsing in complex, slow waves that might be communication, or might be nothing more than the digestive cadence of a chemical cycle so protracted that a single pulse outlasts a human generation.

They do not reproduce; they persist. A Mica-Folk observed for a millennium will never divide, never bud, never mate. It simply continues. If it is broken—by a visitor’s careless footstep, for instance—the fragments continue moving independently, their edges sealing into new, smaller individuals over the course of a century. The plateau is littered with tiny specks of them, so small they resemble dust, each speck still pulsing with an undiminishable life.

### The Spire-Dwellers (Stratified Polyps)

Within the towering structures—the urban bones—live creatures that have abandoned mobility for a kind of vertical, sessile existence. They are columnar, anchored to the vitrified block or the woven cables, and they resemble stalagmites of layered rust and mineral gauze. A cross-section would reveal concentric rings, much like a tree’s, but these rings are not annual; they correspond to the slow thermal cycles of the plateau, each ring a deposit laid down over tens of thousands of years as the creature filters metallic salts from the air.

At their apex, they bear a ring of crystalline "eyes" that never blink, each one a hexagonal lens of violet fluorite. These eyes do not see images; they sense the ambient electric field with a resolution that allows them to detect the approach of a Mica-Folk at half a kilometer’s distance, by the disturbance in the ground’s faint circuitry. When prey is near, the Spire-Dweller extends a long, thread-thin tentacle—a braided cable of copper and silkstone—that drifts across the floor with the patience of a glacier. The tentacle tip adheres to a Mica-Folk, and then, over the course of a day, begins a slow electrolytic dissolution, drawing the wafer’s mineral substance up into the column’s core. The Mica-Folk does not struggle; it has no concept of harm. Its dissolution is simply one more chemical exchange in a world built on exchanges.

Spire-Dwellers also sing, if you can call it that. The coils of wire and glass you saw hanging in the darkness are their reproductive organs. When the sizzling-static sound grows loud enough to feel on the skin, it means a Spire-Dweller is releasing a cloud of metallic spores—microscopic hexagonal plates that drift on the faintest air currents (there must be air currents, no matter how still the world seems; the spores prove it). These spores settle on distant walls, on the undersides of iron ledges, and begin the slow, layered growth of new polyps. A single Spire-Dweller may release a spore cloud only once in a hundred thousand years. The last one happened before your species discovered fire.

### The Deep-Circuit Swarm (Phantom Flows)

Not all life here is material in the way you understand. There exist beings composed of pure electric potential, flitting through the dormant circuitry that webs the stone like fossilized veins. They are not ghosts; they are standing-wave patterns in the residual current, self-sustaining eddies of charge that have learned to replicate their own information. To see them, you must close your physical eyes and watch with the infra-sense: they appear as ripples of blue-white faster than the Mica-Folk, slower than a hummingbird’s wing, a flickering mid-speed that the human mind strains to follow.

They swarm around the hexagonal apertures of the cooling towers, spiraling up into the dark and down again in braided sheets of silent lightning. They feed on the thrum—that subsonic heartbeat of the buried engines—and they are likely the cause of the thrum, or the thrum is their cause; causality here is a loop with no beginning. The Deep-Circuit Swarm has no single body; each individual is a packet of organized energy that periodically merges with others, swaps fragments of pattern, then separates, in a kind of intellectual conjugation. Their memory is embedded in the standing wave’s frequency and phase, and it might contain everything that has ever happened on the plateau since the circuits first cooled. They are the closest thing this world has to historians, and they are perfectly silent.

### The Dreamer Beneath (What the Darkness Holds)

And then there is the presence you sensed—the weight of ages, the breath so slow that civilizations come and go between its exhalations. The inhabitants do not name it, for they have no names, but if we must call it something, it is the Dreamer. The entire plateau, you see, is its body. The fused-basalt skin, the iron-veined structures, the grid of weary circuitry—these are not ruins. They are the exoskeleton of a being that moved once, thought once, perhaps even spoke, and then fell asleep. Its sleep has lasted so long that its body has become geography, its microbiome evolved into the Mica-Folk and Spire-Dwellers and Circuit Swarms, all living in its furrows and folds like mites on a slumbering titan.

The Dreamer does not dream as we do; its dreams are slow, geological events. A tremor in the ground that lasts a thousand years is its twitch of REM. The infraglow is the dim flicker of its neural activity, idling at the threshold of consciousness. The subsonic thrum is its heartbeat. And the structures you saw—the cooling towers, the tooth-set masonry—those are organs, not architecture. A kidney of coiled cables. A lung of hexagonal chambers. A brain of vitrified glass, somewhere deep below, cycling through a single thought that began in the time when the sky still held stars and will finish long after you are gone.

It is aware of you. Not as you are aware of an ant on your skin—more as you are aware of a single, slight pressure in a joint, a faint tickle that barely registers. You are a small warm mote, and the plateau has felt you walking across its back. It is not hostile. It simply waits, with the patience of the eternal, for the tickle to go away so it can sink back into its dream. When you leave—if you leave—it will forget you utterly, your memory dissolving into the slow noise of its synapses, no more significant than a single dropped byte in the mind of a sleeping god.

---

These are the ones who live here. They do not build. They do not think in ways that map onto human thought. They do not even die, not truly. They are slow processes, as inevitable and as beautiful as the formation of crystals. And in their silent, patient way, they have inherited a world that outlived its creators, and they fill it not with chaos, but with a quiet, endless equilibrium. To call them alive is to stretch the definition to its breaking point. To call them anything else is to miss the miracle.
