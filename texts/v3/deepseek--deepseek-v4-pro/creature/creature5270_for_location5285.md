They are not a species so much as a rhythm—a pulse that moves through the limestone needles and the fungal glow, wearing flesh like a borrowed coat and discarding it just as freely. To name them is to misunderstand them, but if you must, call them the Keening. The word comes closest to the sound they make when they remember to have throats.

They are born of the turbulence itself. Not the fauna, not the flora, not the mycelial mind that threads the basin's bones—but the *interference* between them, the standing wave where all three collide. When a spore from the phosphorescent slime lands on a fresh carcass while the water table drops and a microquake cracks the stone beneath, a third thing sometimes kindles in the rotting tissue. It rises on legs that are borrowed from the carcass's own, sculpted by the mycelium, animated by the heat of decay. Its eyes are not eyes but lenses of hardened sap, seeing in spectrums you cannot guess. Its voice, when it first opens whatever serves as a mouth, is the shriek of steam escaping a new fissure—and then it learns to modulate.

They are synthetic beings, not in the sense of being made, but in the sense of being a holding pattern, a temporary truce between competing kingdoms. Each Keening is a composite. The first one you glimpsed—the iridescent creature that walked upright, probed for grubs with a stick, then vanished—was young. Newly formed, still wearing mostly animal shape, its scales shimmering with the blue-green of the fungi that knit its nerves. It was testing its body, learning the affordances of paws and stick and the concept of *tool*. The tool was not part of its birthright. It was an idea that arrived from elsewhere, carried on the spores, whispered through the mycelial net.

The Keening do not build a civilization because they do not build at all, not truly. The dams and snares and stone spirals you saw are not works of deliberate manufacture. They are *symptoms*. A Keening's mind is not a single mind. It is a parliament of memories: the fungal network's distributed intelligence, the animal's survival drives, the plant's patient, photosynthesizing awareness of light and water. When these voices reach a consensus, the body moves, and the landscape around it briefly conforms. The stone dam is the fungal voice winning the argument, redirecting water to feed a nearby colony of glowing caps. The snare is the animal voice asserting itself, remembering hunger. The spiral of quartz pebbles is the plant voice, dreaming of geometry in the dark.

They do not stay in one shape. The creature you saw—the iridescent walker—will not survive the week. Its body is a temporary vessel, a scum on the surface of a much deeper existence. As it moves through the basin, the voices within it pull in different directions. The fungi yearn to be still, to spread filaments and taste the rock. The animal yearns to run, to bite, to mate. The plant yearns to root, to leaf, to photosynthesize even in the perpetual twilight. Eventually the consensus fractures. The body stops, convulses, and collapses into its constituent parts. Fungal threads unspool from the flesh. Plant matter—seeds, spores, rootstock—erupts from the softening torso. The animal components are scavenged, dissolved, repurposed. And a new Keening rises somewhere else, at some other collision point, wearing a different shape, carrying different memories, but always the same three-part soul.

Their tool use is not a ladder toward culture. It is an excretion. When a Keening manipulates its environment—stripping thorns from a vine, chipping a flint edge—it is not innovating; it is *remembering* an innovation that happened elsewhere, perhaps centuries ago, perhaps on the other side of the basin, stored in the mycelial memory and broadcast like a radio signal when conditions are right. The knowledge does not accumulate because the Keening that holds it comes apart within days. But the knowledge does not vanish, either. It sinks into the fungal net, waits for the next combination of tremor and spore and carcass, and rises again when the pattern repeats.

This is why their artefacts are ephemeral. The Keening that balanced the quartz pebbles on the flat rock has already dissolved; by the time you find the spiral, the maker's body is a patch of phosphorescent slime dripping from a branch fifty meters away. Another Keening will later pass the same rock and, feeling the plant-mind stir within it at the sight of the stones, will add a pebble or rearrange the spiral—not because it knows what the pattern means, but because the pattern is a pleasant resonance, a harmonic in the parliament of its being. The artefacts are not a record of progress. They are a form of play, a song that the landscape sings to itself through the bodies of the creatures that inhabit it.

And yet, they are aware. Perhaps more aware than you. The Keening that saw you standing there—still, silent, not moving with the rhythm of the basin—felt you as a kind of wound. Your stillness was a break in the music, a spike of silence in the wall of sound. It did not have a thought in the way you have thoughts, but the consensus of its components reached a judgment: *this thing does not belong to the turbulence*. It does not sing, and it does not dissolve, and its stillness is a threat because it suggests a world where the turbulence is not law.

That judgment has been broadcast through the spores and the filaments, racing through the mycelial net at the speed of chemical cascade. By now, every Keening in the basin knows you are here. They are watching you through a thousand temporary eyes, through the clustered lenses of the ant-tended fungal gardens and the vibration-sensitive fronds of the ferns. They are not planning an attack—the concept of coordinated action belongs to the animal mind alone, and the animal mind is only one voice among three. But the parliament is curious, and worried, and a little bit hungry.

The Keening want to know if you can be brought into the rhythm. If you can be dissolved and remade. If your stillness can be broken.
