Something lives here. It is not that the place is inhabited so much as that it has become a habit—a set of gestures the water and the heat repeat until they take on form. In the false dawn, when the mist climbs out of the cobbles in visible sheets, the gestures coalesce. They do not walk so much as seep, moving from one warm pool to the next, their outlines only a faint thickening of the air, like the wobble above a sun-struck road. You must watch the stillest surface—the side of a sweating brick hulk, the curve of a cast-iron pipe—to see them pass as a brief disturbance in the condensate, a film of moisture that drags itself upward against the pull of things.

They are many, though counting them would require a patience the climate does not permit. A dozen, perhaps, or a dozen dozen; they multiply when the heat peaks and draw together into larger, more ambiguous masses when the basin cools to a mere steam. Each has, at its densest point, a kernel of something not quite water—a clot of mud, a curl of rust, the lingering colloid of old grease—that gives it a provisional spine. Around this grit, the gathered humidity shapes a body: limbs like drips run slow, a head that is mostly an indentation in the wet air, a torso that passes through chain-link fences without noticing. They are the colour of the things they lean against: ochre from the bricks, grey from the slumped concrete, the bruised green of the canal’s scum. When they pause, they glisten with the same perpetually fresh sweat that pearls on every surface here, and it is impossible to tell where the creature ends and the world begins.

They are gardeners, or maybe petitioners. Each of the solitary plants—the cycad, the elephant ears, the occasional knot of tottering reeds—has its own attendant. You can see them in the long afternoons, when the sun presses down through the humidity like a thumb on a bruise, kneeling (if a puddle can kneel) at the base of a leaf-stalk. They do not water the plants—nothing here needs more water—but they tend them by touch. A wet hand laid against the elephant ear’s leathery skin will darken the leaf in slow pulses, and the plant responds with a minute tremor, a brief widening of its pores. The cycad receives a different care. Its attendant is older than the others, slower to form, and it brings offerings of iron. It scrapes flakes from the collapsed boiler with a patience that spans seasons, carrying each rust-coloured wafer in a cupped palm of water to the plant’s root-crown. There, the flake dissolves into a red cloud that the cycad drinks over the course of a week. The plant’s fronds, in thanks, grow a little stiffer, a little more like fossil stone.

The boiler itself is a locus of memory. The old attendant, the one made more of mud and corrosion than of water, can sometimes be seen—truly seen, as a slumped shape—bent there at dusk. Its head is a nodule of iron oxide, its eyes two motionless whirlpools of ochre liquid. It does not speak, but if you place your ear to the boiler’s warm, wet plates, you can hear a grinding sound, very deep, like a millstone turning in a drowned cellar. This is the sound of the fire that once lived here, the memory of the kilns that baked these bricks. The place was a brickworks, once. The clay pits are the pools now; the kilns are the scattered iron hulks. The boiler attendant holds that contradiction—fire in a place that is now all water—tight in its chest, and its slow, grinding song is a lament and a lullaby both.

At the canal, the black water accepts them. It is the only exit from the basin, and the only entrance. One by one, the seep-creatures slide into the current, losing their hard-won grit-kernels, becoming just a cool patch moving against the flow. They travel outward, perhaps to the river, perhaps to the sea. Hours later they return, always, but changed: a different fleck of grit, a new constellation of impurities. Sometimes they bring gifts. A glass bottle stopped with wax, a stone still warm from some faraway sun, the articulated skeleton of a drowned bird no bigger than a thumb. These they place at the feet of the plants, and the plants take them in—the bottle slowly sinking into the mud beside a drain, the bird bones woven into the elephant ear’s root mat. The basin, over decades, has become a reliquary.

They sing, too, when the heat breaks—not with voices but with the sound of water moving through narrow places. A gurgle in a clogged drainpipe, a hiss of steam from a crack in the concrete, the plink of a drip falling from a leaf-tip into a full pool. This is their language, a ceaseless conversation about the weight of the air, the salt content of the canal, the exact firmness of mud. The plants listen, and answer with their own slow exhalations. The air here does not so much hold moisture as become it, and the seep-creatures are that becoming, the thought that the water thinks when it lingers too long in one place and begins to remember.
