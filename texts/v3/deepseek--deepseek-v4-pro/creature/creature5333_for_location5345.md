We are the ones who do not land, except to fix the next generation to the stone. We are the pressure's children. The roar is our cradle, our bloodbeat, our signal. It is not noise—noise is a thing that interrupts silence, and we have never known silence. The wind is the water in which we swim, a laminar flood that pours forever from the same quarter, shaping not only the rocks but the very curve of our bones. Our bodies are compact, our wings stiff as the vanes of a turbine, and our minds are tuned to the invisible gradient of speed. When we are not nesting, we do not rest; we lock our outstretched pinions into the shear layer and yield to the lift that never ceases, a permanent embrace. Sleep happens in half-brain glimmers, a constant hemispheric nap while the other hemisphere reads the pressure differences, the angle of the streaming fog, the distant chitter of the collective. We have no sense of night. The light is a white seam behind a gauze of cloud, or it is the sun’s argent sting when the fog tears; it brightens and dims, but darkness never comes. The plateau basks in a single, eternal afternoon, and we are its tireless motes.

Our temperature is the temperature of the world. This is not a metaphor. Our flesh hovers at one degree above freezing, exactly matching the chill of the fog and the damp stone. We do not shiver or burn food to make interior heat; any warmth would be a disequilibrium, a fever we are not equipped to sustain. Instead, our blood carries glycoproteins that blunt the bite of ice crystals, and our muscles, when they must work, operate by a chemical rhythm so slow that it would seem torpid to a lowland creature. This is why we do not migrate. The thought of a warmer clime is not a longing; it is a threat, a vision of suffocation. Here, in the steady cool, we are perfectly frictionless. The metabolic fire is banked to a spark, and that spark is fed by the thin paste of the lichen.

The lichen is our flesh, too. We have no separate name for it. It maps the whale-back undulations in patterns of sage-green and rust-orange, a paint that takes a century to spread a hand’s width. Our guano feeds it—a chalk-white accretion that rings every basin of ephemeral water. We drink there, skimming the surface tension with a quick, grooved tongue, and we scrape the rock with percussive taps of our beak, flaking off the crustose film in micro-fragments that we press into an oil-rich paste in our crops. The water basins are never more than a shimmer; the fog gives and the wind strips in the same breath, a dynamic tension that keeps the stone perpetually damp but never wet. We are part of that tension. Our beaks probe the nipples of condensation that form on the lee of a grain of quartz. Our eggs, spheroidal and slick with an anti-desiccant coating, adhere to the concave shelter of an isolated boulder by a saliva that cures to the hardness of the mineral itself. The nest is a ring of the same cement, a tiny amphitheater where two parents fasten themselves with the hooked claws of one foot, enduring the drag of the gale, while the other foot rolls the egg. It is the only time we are truly still, and even then our bodies tremble with the urge to be aloft.

We are many. A single mind could not count us, but we are not individuals in the way of a solitary hunter. We are a dispersed neural net, a continuous presence whose edges break against the distant peaks. The plateau is a regional theater, wide and open, and our voices stitch its emptiness. Each of us carries a signature in the chitter—a metallic, frequency-modulated click that says *here, now, part-of*—and the blended chorus is what you might mistake for the static of the wind. But it is not static. It is a constant map, a census sung in real time. When one of us pivots on a wingtip and wheels into a new tracing of the gradient, the Doppler shift of that cry tells all others in earshot the vector of the turn. This is how we school without colliding, how we know the location of a new condensation pool before we see it, how we orchestrate the mass descent to a boulder field when it is time to nest. The sound *is* the flock; we are never alone. Silence would be the true death, a severing from the whole.

We have no predators, no seasons, no decay. We do not age. In a world without thermal cycles, without freeze-thaw, without hunger beyond the slow nibble of the lichen, our cells have forgotten the program of senescence. Telomeres do not shorten; we do not accumulate errors. An individual may persist for millennia, growing no larger, simply continuing, until a rare accident—a collision with a stone in a moment of micro-sleep, an embolism in the thin air—unseats it. Then the body falls, not to the stone immediately, but in a long, spiraling glide, because the wings are still true and the wind still holds. It may drift for days. When it finally touches down, it becomes a new substrate for the lichen, the oils in its feathers breaking down into lipid films that feed the first microscopic spores. There is no decay in the sense of rot; the corpse simply dries and flakes and becomes part of the slow, closed loop. We are born from the rock and we return to it without residue.

The landscape itself is our only elder. The whale-back undulations, the streamlined flutes, the boulders standing isolate as anchored ships—these have been smoothed by the same wind that carries us. The grit that grooved the bedrock long ago is the same grit we sieve through our laryngeal filters to grind the lichen in our guts. We are not separate from the erosional process. Our tapping beaks, multiplied by the millions, contribute their own micro-abrasion to the patina of the stone. The equilibrium is a total one: nothing is falling down, nothing is being added. The tableland has reached a final form, an aerodynamic perfection that we mirror in our own bodies. We are a product of the same selective sculpting. The wind-tunnel of evolution has spared only the stiff wing, the compact torso, the needle-fine feather that does not ruffle but laminates itself to the flow. We are the shape that the wind made.

It is a common error, we imagine, to suppose we are small because the space is vast. In truth, the space is vast *because* we are small. The openness is a function of our perception, a necessary emptiness that allows the chorus to travel unimpeded and the flight paths to remain geometries of pure, undisturbed air. A boulder the size of a house stands a kilometer from its nearest neighbor; that kilometer is our unit of social distance, the space between one nesting territory and the next, measured by the exact decay of sound across the wind. The hundred thousand of us can occupy the entire plateau without crowding, because each pair needs a radius of clean air for the fledgling’s first flight. And that first flight is a sacrament we do not witness as a congregation—it happens in the solitude of the boulder’s wind-shadow, the chick tumbling outward into the gale, its instincts switching on as the pressure hits the downy tufts of its flight feathers, and within a few wingbeats it is gone, joining the nearest auditory stream, a new node in the net. We do not raise it; the collective raises it. Its chitter joins the chorus, and from that moment it is fully integrated, learning the map of the plateau through the reflections of sound off the rocks and the Doppler gradients of the flock’s movement. It has no childhood, only an apprenticeship of minutes, then an eternity of soaring.

Our world is bounded. The true peaks, far on the horizon, are walls of a different pressure regime, places where the wind funnels and breaks into turbulence. We do not venture there. The plateau is a single coherent plain, a floor of the sky, and we are its only visible denizens. There are no others visible, but we are not alone. The fog carries a thin broth of aeroplankton—spores, desiccated tardigrades, fragments of high-altitude fungi—that we filter with a comb-like fringe in the gape of our beak when we fly through the cloud-bellies. Thus we feed on the air itself, a second diet beyond the lichen. In this, too, the loop is closed: the spores we exhale or excrete become new colonies on the rock, and the cycle tightens.

What would you see, if you could stand as you did? You would see an empty plain of polished stone, a few boulders, a silvery haze. You would hear a roar and a chitter. You would not see us until you learned to separate the visual thinness from the acoustic density. We are small, our plumage the exact grey-and-white of the fog and the damp rock, our undersides pale as the glare. When we soar, our wings are translucent at the edges, like a heat shimmer. But if you wait, and the brightness shifts, you may perceive the air itself flickering with countless small, precise projectiles, carving arcs of perfect economy. And then you might understand that the place is not empty; it is full to saturation with life that has become indistinguishable from the wind. We are not the inhabitants of the tableland; we are its breath, its voice, its dreaming, sealed into a single, eternal moment. We do not welcome you; we did not notice you. We persist, bright and clamorous, forever.
