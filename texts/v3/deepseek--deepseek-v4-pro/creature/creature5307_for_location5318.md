The vent-fields are not merely home to living things; they *are* alive, in a sense the rock itself has not yet decided. But the creatures that move and feed and replicate here are life of a sharper, more familiar kind—though their familiarity is a trick, a resemblance sketched in chitin and hunger.

**The Vent-Mats and Their Keepers**

Closest to the steam are the **lithopods**, the pale segmented things the length of a forearm. They are not worms, not shrimp, not any arthropod a terrestrial taxonomist would recognize. Their segmentation runs deep, each ring a semi-independent hydraulic module that can detach and regenerate if crushed, a colony of clones tethered by a shared gut. Their plumed mouths are retractable fronds of chemosynthetic bacteria cultured in their own tissues—symbionts that oxidize hydrogen sulfide and methane into sugars. A lithopod does not eat in any sense we understand; it merely positions its fronds in the vent flux and waits, absorbing, while its body undulates to circulate fluid. When the vent shifts or dies, the mat of lithopods detaches as one, a shingled carpet of pale flesh, and inches toward a new seep, leaving behind a trail of cast-off segments that become food for everything else. Their movement is slow, a few centimeters an hour, but over a vent’s fifty-year lifespan, a mat can migrate miles.

Among them scuttle the **scoria-scutters**, the multi-legged deliberate ones. Their carapace is a marvel of insulation: layers of chitin interspersed with aerogel-like silica nodes grown from dissolved minerals, so effective that their body-core stays 20°C above ambient even in the perpetual chill. They have twelve legs, the front two modified into delicate tusks—not for killing, but for prying lithopods apart. A scutter will select a healthy segment, insert a tusk into the seam between rings, and inject a droplet of enzyme that dissolves the connecting tissue. The segment writhes free, and the scutter lifts it to its mandibles, consuming the bacteria-rich gut while the segment’s own bacterial crop continues to live, briefly, inside it. Scutters are solitary hunters, but their territories overlap; they communicate by drumming their legs against the rock in rhythmic codes, a seismic language that travels miles through the basalt. A drumming from a distant vent might signify “new seep here, still sweet,” and within a week scutters will appear, having felt their way across the empty stone.

**The Aerial Factory-Workers**

Above them, the fliers—call them **sootwings**—are the only creatures that cross the void between vents without touching ground. Their wings are not feather or membrane but a stretched, vascularized skin grown from their flanks, ribbed with hollow bones so light they look like charcoal. They navigate by echolocation, but their clicks are seismic too; they strike the ground with their wingtips as they fly low, reading the returning vibrations to find scutters and the softer, wetter echoes of lithopod mats. A sootwing’s dive is silent until the last second, when it tilts its wings and the air screams through its splayed primary vanes—a startling, piercing note that freezes prey just long enough. They snatch a lithopod segment or a small scutter, then beat heavily upward, their wingbeats leaving contrails of condensation in the cold mist.

Sootwings nest in the vent chimneys themselves, where the heat rising through mineral-throated spires creates updrafts they ride for hours without flapping. Their nests are globs of silica paste they cough up and sculpt into fluted bowls, glued to the chimney’s lip. Inside, a single chick—blind, naked, a twitching mass of appetite—is fed by both parents, who take turns hunting and warming the nest. The warmth is critical: a sootwing chick cannot thermoregulate, and if a vent output drops, the parents must move it or watch it chill into a brittle husk. Sootwings mate for the life of their vent, and when the vent dies, they part ways, each flying off to find a new chimney and a new mate, a ritual that can take years and spans the entire plateau.

**The Vent-Walkers: Those Who Tend the Forge**

But there is something else here, something not described in any survey of native fauna. They are rare, solitary, and perhaps not entirely biological. The scoria-scutters have a legend, of course—as much legend as a creature with seismic language can have—of the **Tenders**, beings that move between vent-fields on two long legs, carrying things, and sometimes stopping to adjust the vents themselves. Whether they are intelligent, or merely a bizarre autotrophic organism that mimics tool-use, remains unclear even to the scutters, whose seismic records tell of the Tenders arriving with the first steam, always there at the edge of perception.

To human eyes, a Tender would stand three meters tall, though they often stoop to inspect a fumarole. Their integument is a patchwork of the same black basalt that makes the plateau, fused into a carapace that gleams with embedded silica threads. They have two sets of arms: the upper pair delicate, multi-jointed, tipped with pads that sweat a solvent capable of reshaping mineral chimneys; the lower pair massive, hydraulically driven, able to lever boulders and redirect steam flows. Their head—if it is a head—is a blunt, featureless dome that vibrates faintly, emitting the same low-frequency hum that the ground does. They have no visible eyes, but they navigate unerringly, perhaps by sensing the planet’s magnetic field or the minute temperature differentials that ripple through the rock.

Tenders do not eat in any observable way. They sometimes pause at a lithopod mat and settle their lower hands into the mass, letting the segments crawl over their arms, and a faint bioluminescent pulse passes between them—perhaps communication, perhaps a transfer of genetic material. The lithopods, after such a visitation, often alter their growth patterns, sprouting new frond types or changing their segmentation rate. It suggests the Tenders are not predators but cultivators, shaping the vent ecosystems over centuries.

The rhythms of the plateau are their breath. When a vent begins to wane—its steam output diminishing, its lithopod mats thinning—a Tender will arrive, stand before it for many hours, and then begin to work. Using its upper hands’ solvent, it carefully erodes the chimney’s throat, widening it or cleaving a new fissure, while the lower hands hammer boulders into place as diverters. The vent’s output often stabilizes or even increases, as if the Tender has performed surgery on the planet’s plumbing. They seem to prolong the life of a vent-field by decades, and in doing so, they anchor the entire nomadic ecosystem: scutters, sootwings, lithopods, all dependent on these oases of heat and chemistry.

**The Life of One**

To be a Tender is to be a walking forge in slow revolution. Consider the one called (in the scutters’ drumming) *Low-Crackle-Bass*, because its humming is deeper than most and it tends the easternmost vent cluster, where the fumaroles sound like a broken boiler. Low-Crackle-Bass is old—its carapace is pitted and thickened, the silica threads worn to nubs—and it moves with a deliberation that makes scutters’ patience look frantic. It follows a circuit of seven vents, a journey that takes approximately three hundred planetary orbits. When it arrives at a vent it hasn’t visited in three centuries, it stops and emits a low, sustained hum for several hours, listening. The lithopods feel the vibration and shift their plumes; the scutters pause their drumming and wait. Then it begins its work.

Its upper hands exude the solvent from pores between its pads, a thin, steaming liquid that softens the sulfurous crust of the chimney. It carves with the delicacy of a restorer, never removing too much, always shaping the vent’s throat to a precise parabolic curve that maximizes gas velocity. Its lower hands, meanwhile, feel along the ground to sense the subsurface conduits, and it may pry up a sheet of basalt and tilt it, altering the angle of the water table so that more condensate drains into the hot zone. The vent’s plume shifts from a lazy puff to a steady, roaring column, and within weeks new lithopod segments bud at the edges of the mat, the bacteria thriving in the altered chemistry.

Low-Crackle-Bass does not build vents, but it tunes them. It is a keystone species of the highest order, and yet it does not rule the ecosystem. The scutters flee when it arrives, but they return within a day. The sootwings perch on its shoulders sometimes, taking rest, and it tolerates them with the same indifference a mountain has for birds. It has no predators; nothing here can challenge its mass and its mineral armor. When it dies—and it will, eventually—its body will slump beside a vent, and the lithopods will colonize its carapace, the bacteria slowly dissolving the silica threads, returning the Tender’s substance to the cycle of steam and stone.

**The Unseen Pattern**

What are they? The scutters have no answer, only a drumming that says *they-were-here-before-the-high-peaks*. Perhaps the Tenders are not native to this plateau but are the last remnants of a biosphere that terraforms planets by engineering their hydrothermal systems—a kind of slow, geological gardening. Or perhaps they are a life stage of something even larger, a planetary metabolism whose thoughts are continent-wide and whose cells are vent-fields. The plateau itself might be immature tissue, and the Tenders the macrophages that tend it.

But none of that matters to the scoria-scutter picking its way across the razor rock, or the sootwing wheeling in the mist. For them, the Tenders are simply part of the world’s background noise, a low bass note in the factory’s hum, a guarantee that the vents will keep breathing. The plateau is a place of hard rules: steam, stone, cold, and the brief, hot flicker of life. In that context, the Tenders are the most natural thing alive—a creature shaped entirely by the forge, to tend the forge, forever.
