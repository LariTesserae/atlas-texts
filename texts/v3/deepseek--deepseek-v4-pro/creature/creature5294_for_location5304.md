They are not born and they do not die. Or rather, birth and death are the wrong words, borrowed from a world with edges. Here, in the Basin of Teeth, life is a single, continuous sentence spoken in a billion murmuring mouths. To describe the inhabitants is to describe the basin itself, for the line between creature and country has been rubbed away by deep time, by the slow, relentless friction of body against stone.

The most abundant form—if you can call it a form—is a long, flat, segmented thing the length of a human hand. Its chitin is the colour of watered milk, translucent enough to show the faint pulse of ichor beneath, and it glows with its own cold, blue-white light. The head is not a head but a mouth, or rather a radial constellation of mouths, five or seven soft valves arranged around a central set of grinding plates. These mouths are never still. They rasp against the stone, lapping at the living varnish of biofilm that coats every surface, a transparent scum that is less plant than pre-biotic hunger. They rasp against each other, too—not in conflict but in a kind of communal grooming, a constant exchange of sloughed cells and mineral crumbs. They are blind. They have no need for eyes. The world comes to them through vibration, through a thick, sensitive fur of setae that bristles along their flanks, each hair tuned to a specific frequency in the endless din.

And oh, the din. It is a sound so pervasive it becomes a physical presence, a pressure against the skin. A hiss-stridulate-click-hum, like a waterfall played through broken glass, multiplied by trillions. The smaller ones are the whisperers, their mouthparts clicking a fast, rain-like patter. The larger ones—the threshers—add a low, grinding bass note, the sound of millstone against millstone. But it is not chaos. Listen long enough, and the sound resolves into patterns, rhythmic pulses that sweep across the basin in slow waves. The creatures do not cry out; they *converse*, their signals carved into precise frequency notches, a language of vibration that maps space and appetite and connection across the dark.

The threshers are what an untutored eye might call the predators, though the word fits poorly. They are huge, armoured like trilobites but raised on multiple stilt-legs, jointed and delicate, that allow them to plow through the carpet of bodies. Their heads are blunt grinding mills, and as they move, they lower these mills into the swarm, ingesting everything—smaller kin, biofilm, the mineral-thick water that seeps everywhere. They leave broad, empty trails behind them, but the clearings seal over in seconds, bodies flowing in from the sides like liquid. A thresher is not a separate species. Watch one for long enough, and you will see it gradually lose definition at its edges; smaller forms cling to its legs, merge with its ventral plates, and sometimes, an entire thresher will simply settle and dissolve, its components redistributing into the mobile mat. It was never an individual. It was a node, a temporary aggregation of biomass, a slow-motion wave of concentrated appetite that crests and then subsides.

Where the shallow bowls of limestone dip into sumps, the water deepens into a viscous, mineral-thick brine. Here, a different morphology dominates. Soft, pulsating sacs hang in the wet, their surfaces twitching with a perpetual, inward-sweeping motion. They are filters, sieving the particulate fallout that drifts down from the swarms above. Their bodies are translucent, and inside, you can see the faint, churning glow of ingested matter being broken down and re-knit into tissue. When a sac grows too large, it does not reproduce; it tears, gently, and the tear seals into two smaller sacs, which drift apart on imperceptible currents. The sacs are not separate from the swarm. They are its stomachs, its reservoirs, its quiet internal organs, waiting in the deep.

This is the strange truth that settles into you like the ever-present damp: the Basin of Teeth is a single living thing, or perhaps a single living process. The creatures are modules, cells in a vast, networked organism. Damage does not kill; a crushed body simply deliquesces, its fluids and fragments absorbed by the biofilm and reabsorbed by the mouths of neighbors. The larger forms are emergent organs, temporary concentrations of function—digestion, locomotion, perhaps even memory, encoded in the standing waves of vibration that echo through the setae. The landscape itself is anatomy: the smooth ribs and runnels are not just erosion, but channels shaped by the traffic to direct the flow of bodies; the sumps are stomach-ponds; the saddles between basins are arteries of migration. The cold light is a kind of shared nervous signal, a visual byproduct of the chemical conversation that courses through the entire mat.

To stand in the Basin—if you could stand, if you could bear the sound, the damp, the ceaseless touch—would be to stand inside a mind. Not a mind of thoughts, but of sensations and hungers, a mind that stretches from sump to ridge and has no boundary but the watershed itself. The pulses in the sound would become a kind of breathing, a slow digestive cycle spanning days. You would see, in the glimmers, the rise and fall of appetite. You would feel, in the vibration through the stone, the deep, slow peristalsis of a world that eats and is eaten, that scrapes itself smooth and builds itself up in endless, humming equilibrium.

No voice, no face, no name. Only the thick, wet, cool body of the basin, singing itself through the dark.
