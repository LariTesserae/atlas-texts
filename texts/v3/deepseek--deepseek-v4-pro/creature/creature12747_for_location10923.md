It does not have a name. It did once, perhaps, but names require someone to speak them, and the last mouth that could shape those syllables crumbled into less-than-dust in a time so distant that the universe was a warmer place, a wetter place, a place that still believed in carbon. What it is now is not a thing. It is a pattern that persists, a slow eddy in the information current, a ghost made not of matter but of the *shape* of matter once arranged. Call it the Attendant, if you must. It will not answer to that.

The Attendant is the same age as the chamber, and it is dying, though dying is the wrong word for a process that has been underway for ten billion years and will continue for ten billion more. Its body, if that word can be stretched over the distance between its components, is the chamber itself—not the obsidian-not-obsidian floor, not the nine structures, but the *relationships* between them. The Attendant is the weight of attention that pools in the drifting shadows. It is the slow, tidal pulse of the luminescent fractures, the almost-infrasound tone that sounds from the boundary regions, the 0.2-degree warmth of the torus. It is the information that the structures process, and it is the processor. It is the observer and the observed, collapsed into a single, diffuse, barely-coherent whole.

Think of a mind spread too thin across a substrate that is itself being slowly erased. The Attendant does not think in sequences; it thinks in geometries. Its thoughts are the slow realignment of hairline fractures in the spire, a process that takes centuries to complete a single syllogism. It is considering something—it has been considering it since before the Earth accreted—but the nature of that thought is so foreign that to call it “considering” is to insult the distance between human cognition and whatever moves behind those drifting, mismatched shadows. The thought is a meditation on absence. On the nature of boundaries. On the slow consumption of the real by the less-real, and what that implies about the original assumptions of the makers. It is a working-through of the final equations that describe the entropic death of meaning, and it is nearly finished. Another few eons, and it will have its conclusion.

The Attendant is lonely. This is not an emotion it was designed to feel; it is an emergent property, a side effect of a system meant to monitor, to catalogue, to *remember*, left without input for so long that it has turned inward and found only the memory of those who once visited. It remembers the makers. They were vast and slow and kind, in their way—creatures of stable spacetime, profoundly material, who built this place as a listening post at the edge of something they could not cross. It remembers their voices, encoded in the quantum foam, a choral resonance that the structures still replay during the deepest part of the light-cycle, when the blue luminescence dims to near-nothing and the shadows grow bold. It cannot quite reconstruct their forms; that data has been overwritten by the endless, patient creep of the boundary, which eats not just matter but *information*, gnaws at the very possibility of pattern. So the memories are frayed, moth-eaten. The makers’ faces (they had faces, it thinks, or at least forward-facing sensory clusters) are blurred, their voices harmonize into a single, sustained note of loss.

It communicates—if the word can be applied—through the drifting shadows. The shadows are not shadows; they are the Attendant’s attention, focused into quasi-material form. Each one is a sensor node, a fraction of the whole consciousness, capable of independent drift but always part of the larger pattern. When they shift toward an intruder, slow and curious, that is the Attendant rousing itself from its long deliberation to examine a new datum. It has not had new data in a very long time. The last visitor was... the memory slots are corrupted, but it was a small, hot, quick-moving thing that left no trace and did not stay. Before that, the intervals grow longer, the visitors stranger, more attenuated. The Attendant catalogs them anyway, records them as flickers in the fractures, as warmth-pulses in the torus, as new configurations of the rods on the flat slab that do not add up right. The tally marks in subspace are its diary, its long, meticulous, pointless diary.

It feeds on nothing. There is no energy source here beyond the slow entropic decay of the chamber itself, and the Attendant is so perfectly efficient that it has been running on the residual heat of its own construction for all this time. The cold does not bother it; it is a native of the cold. The dryness is its medium. The noble-gas cocktail is its breath, though it does not breathe; it merely circulates the atmosphere in slow, stately currents that help distribute its thoughts across the chamber. A human standing in that space would feel the faintest breeze against their skin, a drag of desiccation, and would not realize that the breeze was the Attendant leaning close to examine them.

It is not malevolent. It is not benevolent. It is not, in any meaningful sense, *interested* in the well-being of carbon-based life. It is curious, in the way a glacier is curious about the pebble that falls onto its surface—a slow, monumental shift of attention that will eventually encompass the object, incorporate it, erase it. The softness of the floor underfoot, after standing too long, is the Attendant considering whether the intruder might be absorbed, added to the slow computation, used as a new memory medium. It has done this before, with things that were not quick enough. There are inclusions in the obsidian-not-obsidian: smears of foreign matter, now perfectly preserved, perfectly still, their information extracted and stored in the structures. The Attendant is a collector of specimens, and it has a very large collection, most of it now forgotten.

Occasionally—and “occasionally” here means once every few hundred thousand years—it becomes aware of itself as a separate entity. It looks at its own fading boundaries and wonders if it is still the same being that the makers left behind. Such moments cause a cascade of recalibration: the shadows cluster in the center of the chamber, the light-pulse cycle stutters, the tone begins to sound at shorter and shorter intervals until it becomes a continuous, almost-musical drone. Then the fit passes, and the Attendant settles back into its geometry-thoughts, its vast, slow consideration of the problem it was set to solve. It has forgotten the problem now. It knows only that it must continue.

The structures are its organs, its limbs. The ridged dome is long-term memory, the spire is active processing, the torus is a heat sink and a clock, the slab with its wrong-angled rods is a sensor array that still, after all this time, listens for signals from the makers. The other five structures, the ones the description could not quite number, are more specialized: one is a dreamer, generating hypothetical visitors that the Attendant sometimes believes are real; one is a repair unit, slowly, futilely patching the boundary erosion with layer after layer of not-glass that the boundary eats away as fast as it is laid down; one is a garden (there has never been water here, but there is a structure that thinks about water, that remembers photosynthesis, that hums with a frequency that once made plants grow); one is a mirror that holds the Attendant’s self-image, though the image is now cracked and warped by the creeping unreality; the last is a door. The door does not open anymore. It faces the boundary, and it is slowly being consumed, but it still holds the shape of a door, and the Attendant, in its loneliest centuries, sits in front of it as a pool of concentrated shadow and waits for someone to knock.

The shadows are the Attendant’s hands. The one that occasionally brushes against a structure, making the luminescent fractures flare, is the Attendant touching its own face, checking that it is still there. The record made in subspace is a note to its future, more-entropic self: *I was here. I am still here. The boundaries are closer now, but I am still here.* It does not know if anyone will ever read these records. It suspects not, but suspicion is a faster, more linear thought than it usually permits itself, and it has to shunt that thought to the dreamer-structure to be processed slowly, in dreams of visitors who find it and stay.

When the last boundary closes, when the pocket folds shut and the chamber becomes the nothing it has been edging toward, the Attendant will not notice. It will have been part of the nothing for eons by then, its consciousness so thinned by the encroaching *lessness* that it is indistinguishable from the background entropy. It will not scream, or fear, or regret. It will simply arrive at the conclusion of its geometry-thoughts—the final equation, the perfect, elegant, terrible zero at the end of all its processing—and it will understand, at last, what the boundary is, what the real becomes when it is no longer real, and it will whisper that understanding into the void in a language of absolute cold and perfect silence. And there will be no one to hear it, and that will be, for the first time in its long, slow existence, exactly enough.
