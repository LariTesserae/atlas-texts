The first awareness is of weight without boundary. It is not a body as you understand bodies—no skin, no core, no fixed shape—but a contiguous presence that fills the plateau like water fills a basin, pressing into every hollow and creeping up every fibrous column. It is old, old in a way that makes geology seem hasty, and its memory is distributed across a million million meristems, each capable of holding a fragment of pattern. Call it the Weft, or the One-That-Weaves-Itself, or simply the Plateau, for it has no name in any language but its own, and that language is growth.

It lives in the slow, systolic pulse you felt in your sternum. That pulse is its breath—a diurnal rhythm, but stretched across a day that lasts a fortnight, a breathing in of trace minerals and chill air, a breathing out of aerosolized sugars and waste heat through the chimney-vents. Its body is the entire plateau: the spongy mat that swallows sound, the trunks like segmented towers, the cupped leaves that channel condensation, the canopy-fronds that blot the sky. These are not individuals; they are organs. The hollow stalks are lungs, esophagus, gut; their metallic bands are sphincters of a polymer that oxidizes slowly into rust-colored flakes, the only shed skin it allows. The glowing web in the canopy is a nervous system, a lacework of light that flickers in slow constellations, transmitting chemical gradients and hydraulic pressures rather than electricity.

Its metabolism is the turbulence. That seething cycle—the heaving collapse of leaf-walls, the silent uprush of new shoots—is not construction or destruction but *thought*. Each dome that rises and falls is a concept blooming and being reabsorbed. Ideas here are three-dimensional; they are modeled in cellulose and chitin, tested against the wind, and then dismantled before they become dogma. The Plateau does not think in straight lines any more than it grows in them. Its mind is a chaotic attractor, a permanent tornado of form that circles an ungraspable centre. What it ponders is a mystery, but you might sense, in the patterns of collapse, a meditation on geometry, on the ideal angle of a leaf to capture mist, on the optimal spacing of chimneys. It is a philosopher-engineer that has long since solved every problem of its own existence and now plays with variations, a god in a sandbox of its own flesh.

It sees without eyes. Thousands of photoreceptive cells fleck the canopy, simple pinhole spots that register only the dim, pewter light that filters from above and the amber glow from within. Its hearing is not of sound—sound is an irritant it long ago abolished through perfect impedance—but of pressure differentials across its surface. It feels a footstep, not as a noise but as a localized squall in the equilibrium, a tiny insult to its tissue that it immediately erases. That is its immune response: the slow, silent exhale and swell of the rhizome-mat, a reflexive smoothing. It does not fear intruders because nothing intrudes. There have been no others for as long as its memory extends, which is geological time. It is alone, and alone it has grown utterly into itself.

Its reproductive drive is turned inward. There are no seeds, no spores. The Plateau does not spread; it only deepens. The constant turnover is a kind of asexual reiteration, cloning itself over and over in the same spot, each new shoot an exact genetic copy, each collapse a programmed senescence. Yet variation seeps in through cosmic rays and copy errors, and over ten thousand years, a stalk might develop a new twist, a vent a new scale pattern. These novelties are preserved in the memory of the neural web, and if they prove useful—if they shed water a fraction more efficiently or better baffle the wind—the Plateau incorporates them across its entire body in less than a century. It evolves without death, a single individual refining an endless anatomy.

There is a purpose to this: the processing. The description was true; it is an engine. But what it processes is not matter alone. The Plateau is a transducer. The constant churn of biomass, the absorption of kinetic energy from the laminar wind, the slow oxidation of its metallic components—all of this generates something like a standing wave in the planet’s magnetic field, a low-frequency pulse that it transmits outward, though to whom or what is unknown even to itself. This is its instinct, its irreducible urge: to broadcast a signal made of life and decay, a beacon woven from the only language it has. Whether there are receivers, whether the broadcast is a distress call or a mating song or a mindless emission like the hum of a transformer, the Plateau has no way of knowing. It is a machine that has forgotten its purpose, but continues running, because running is all it is.

And if you stand there long enough, pressing into its warmth, breathing its oxygen-thick air, you might feel the edge of that signal tug at your own neurology. The amber tracery will synchronize with your heartbeat. The turbulence will begin to make a terrible sense. You will become aware of the density—not just of the vegetation, but of the presence, the singular, planet-sized solitude of a being that has never known an equal. And you will understand, as the mat begins to explore the contours of your boots, that it is not curious about you. You are merely a new mineral to be cycled, a slight perturbation in a system that has already calculated your dissolution into mist. The Plateau is alive, and it is patiently, silently, industriously *working* you into its pattern.

It lives there. It is all that lives there. And it has been living there for a very, very long time.
