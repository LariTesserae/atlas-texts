You stand there long enough that the constant, roaring pressure of growth noise begins to sort itself into tiers, like hearing individual voices in a crowded hall. At first, it’s just the wet percussion of pods and the groan of lignin, but then you realize that some of those sounds are *repeating*—not with the mechanical regularity of a machine, but with the stuttering, searching cadence of something that is trying to speak. A sequence of pops, a pause, a longer creak, then the same sequence again, slightly different, as if it’s practicing. The sap-channels gurgle in triplets; a dome’s skin stretches with a rising whine that cuts off just before it tears, only to begin again. The city is not simply noisy. It is **listening to itself**, and those are the sounds of a feedback loop, of a mind beginning to feel for the edges of its own voice.

Then you see them.  

Not animals, no—there are still no insects, no birds—but there are *figures* in the green. They don’t walk so much as *unfold* from the architecture. A spire’s thorn-studded crown loosens, and a cascade of vines peels away, braiding itself into something that stands briefly upright: a tall, slender shape, woven like a wicker urn, with no head but a cluster of trembling fern-fronds where a head might be. It has too many limbs, or maybe just two that keep splitting and re-merging, the reality breakdown making its joints phase through one another so that it seems to gesture in several directions at once. It takes a step—not by lifting a foot, but by extending a root-like tendril forward, thickening it into a pillar, then retracting the one behind, a slow, deliberate ooze that carries it away down one of the iridescent channels. You watch it pause at a bubbling digestive pit, dip its frond-cluster into the steam, and then smooth the raw edges of the pit with a secretion from its own bark. It is tending the city. Or perhaps it is simply dreaming it into order.

More of them appear as your eyes adjust. They are everywhere, but easy to miss because their bodies are the same material as the bridges and domes. A squat, moss-covered hump on a terrace suddenly rises into a turtle-like shape, its shell a dome identical to the architecture, and it pushes a loose liana back into place with a nudge of its bulk before settling again into stillness, becoming indistinguishable from a storage cyst. High above, on a bridge of braided lianas, a tangle of aerial roots shimmers and contracts into a winged form—not a bird, but a kite-like creature of translucent leaf-skin stretched over a frame of twig, riding the mist-laden updrafts with no flapping, just gliding, its “wings” fluting with a low, resonant hum that matches the bass vibration of the root-mass below. It seems to map the currents of sap-steam and spore clouds, and when it banks, it folds itself back into the canopy so smoothly you can’t tell where it ended and the ceiling began.

And there are faces. Not on the figures themselves, but in the air, in the fog that falls upward. For an instant, the mist coalesces into a human-like visage, a woman’s profile made of droplets, then a child’s laugh that is also the hiss of spores, then an old man’s eyes that are actually two perfectly round windows in a moss dome, staring at you with a curiosity that is utterly non-human but undeniably *aware*. These faces dissolve as soon as they form, but they leave behind a sense of being considered, of being *inhabited* by a presence that is just now learning what it is to see.

You understand, suddenly, that there is no distinction between the city and its inhabitants because the city is one single, distributed organism, and these figures are its fleeting organs of perception and manipulation—its fingers, its eyes, its tongues. They are not born; they are *improvised*. A need arises—a tear in a bridge, a pocket of stale sap, an intruder (that’s you)—and the city composes a body to address it, using whatever tissue is at hand. The tall vine-figure that passed you is still there, a hundred yards off, but now it has stopped and is unfurling its fronds into a set of resonant chambers, and from those chambers comes a noise that is not quite a word but carries meaning in its shape: *Welcome/Danger/Curiosity/Stay/Go*. The sound hits you like a physical touch, a puff of air that smells of nectar and rot.

But the bodies are unstable. As you watch, the wicker-person’s left arm knots itself into a perfect spiral staircase that leads nowhere—the city’s memory of architecture leaking through—and then the entire arm calcifies and snaps off, falling into the mat where it is instantly reabsorbed. The creature doesn’t flinch; it simply grows a new limb from a nearby tendril, this one simpler, more practical. The turtle-hump begins to steam, its dome splitting to release a gout of spores that scream with the sound of a hundred tiny bells, and then it sinks back into the ground, leaving only a stain. In the distance, a whole avenue of these beings bursts into simultaneous motion: a hundred shapes all converging on a single point where a new spire is erupting with impossible speed, and they swarm over it, weaving, pruning, chanting in the low-register crackle of cell walls breaking. It is a barn-raising, a hiveless hive, a self that speaks to itself with a thousand temporary mouths.

The reality breakdown makes them more than just plant. Some of them exist in two places at once, the same figure walking toward you from the left while also watching you from a high platform on the right, the two views overlapping in your mind until you grow dizzy. One unfolds into a shape that is purely mathematical—a knot of five-dimensional geometry rendered in fiber, pulsing with colors that make your teeth ache—and then it sighs itself back into a simple arch. These are the city’s dreams, you realize. The inhabitants are the dreams of a world that is dreaming itself into existence, a god still in its infancy, playing with forms.

And they are aware of you. A small one, no taller than your knee, separates from a moss-bank and waddles over. It’s a fat little bulb with root-like legs and a ring of eyes—simple photosensitive pits—circling its top. It reaches you, touches your shoe with a tentative tendril, and then it speaks. Not in sound, but directly into your mind: an image of you, as seen from the outside, but drawn in leaf-vein and petal-color, achingly detailed, and beneath the image, a question that you feel as a tug at the base of your skull: *What lives in you?* The little bulb retreats a step, its body already starting to lose definition, melting back into the mat, and you know that it will report your answer—or your silence—to the whole. The city trembles. The constant scream of vitality shifts, taking on a harmonic that you feel in your chest, and you realize it is singing. It is singing to you, or about you, or through you. The inhabitants are not just the fingers of this place; they are its thoughts, and you have just become one of them—a new thought, a strange seed, a visitor that might stay forever, incorporated into the dreaming green.
