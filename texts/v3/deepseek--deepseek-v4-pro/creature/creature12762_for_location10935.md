They are not seen so much as sensed—a shift in the damp air, a minute change in the steady hum, a flicker of motion at the edge of the fungal glow. And when they do finally resolve into form, it is slowly, piece by piece, as if the darkness itself is reluctant to let them go.

First, there are the hands. That is what you notice, because the hands are what you have been reading in the stone all along: the same broad palms, the same long, spatulate fingers that left their prints in the clay, that wore the bone tools and pressed the shell scrapers. But these hands are not human. There are six digits on each, arranged in two opposing trios, so that the grip is radial—a star of flesh that can close around a surface from every angle. The skin is pale, the colour of wet chalk, and it glistens perpetually with a thin layer of mucus that catches the bluish light and makes the fingers seem to flow as they move. There is no nail, but each fingertip hardens into a calloused pad cross-hatched with fine grooves, like the underside of a gecko’s foot, and these pads leave no print but a faint, glistening trail that fades in seconds. The hands are their primary face: they gesture constantly, not in a language of discrete signs but a continuous, rippling motion—fingers curling and uncurling, spreading and clenching, the trios separating and reforming into flower-like shapes—that seems to convey nuance as readily as a voice.

Then the body follows, piecemeal. They are low to the ground, crouched by design, their backs a permanent curve that brings their heads level with their hips. When they move, they flow on four limbs most of the time, but it is not a crawl: the front hands reach out, grip a pillar or a trough edge, and the hind limbs push, and there is a moment of suspension like a brachiating ape, then a soft three-point landing, the whole motion continuous and silent. Their hind feet are hands too, essentially identical to the front, so that when they pause and rear up, they stand on two radially gripping platforms, splay-toed, and their two free hands rise into the air, weaving their ceaseless commentary. The torso is barrel-shaped, with no discernible neck; the head is a seamless dome set low between the shoulders, and the face—there is no face as you would know it. Where eyes might be, two shallow, lidless depressions contain dense clusters of fine cilia that vibrate ceaselessly, tasting the air, sensing vibration and temperature and the faintest chemical gradients. They are blind to light but not to the room: they see the world in a three-dimensional map of sound reflections, air currents, and the electric hum of living things, and to them the fungal constellations are irrelevant, the darkness no obstacle.

Their mouths are small, lipless, set on a round, retractable oral cone that emerges only when needed. What emerges from those cones, when they open, is a sound that explains one piece of the great noise of this place: a soft, constant, layered chittering, a chorus of clicks and purrs and high, keening whistles that weave through the drip and the chime. It is not language in the sentence sense, but a continuous, musical stream of information—position, intention, memory, mood—shared among all within earshot, a kind of communal mind made audible. When they pause and listen, the cilia on their heads cease their trembling for a moment, and they become perfectly still, receiving the chorus of the others, and then they add their own voice back, the sound never quite repeating, always evolving.

They are the Makers, and they are the Made, both at once. It is impossible to tell how old any one of them is, because they are not a species with simple birth and death. That hollow pillar with the pulsing roots—you understand now, watching one of them press a six-fingered hand into a niche on a pillar’s side, humming a particular vibrato that matches the structure’s resonance. The pillar hums back, and a seam opens, a vertical mouth in the stone that weeps a clear, nutrient-rich fluid. The being laps at it with its extended oral cone, and as it feeds, you see the skin of its back ripple—not the ripple of muscle, but of small, semi-independent bodies moving beneath the translucent flesh. They are colonies, not individuals. Each is a walking, reasoning aggregate of smaller selves, zooids that share a single body plan but remain partly autonomous, a symbiosis of hundreds of worm-like forms woven into a single cooperative entity. The hollow pillars are not architecture; they are wombs, or chrysalises, or factories, growing the component zooids from the stone substrate, feeding them the mineral-rich seepage until they are ready to join a greater whole. The roots that pulse in the core are the mycelial network that connects them all, the underground web of communication and nourishment that links every pillar to every other, and at the nodes of that network, the Makers walk.

When a Maker is damaged—when a stone fall crushes a limb—the limb does not heal. Instead, the wounded zooids release a chemical signal, and the entire aggregate goes still, humming for help. Others come, and with their radial hands they peel away the dying parts, gently, with the same bone tools that shape the wall clay, and they carry the pieces to a trough of flowing water, where the zooids dissolve into a pale slurry that washes away downstream. Then the diminished Maker retreats to a pillar, seals itself inside a niche, and for a time the vibrato of that pillar changes, the hum rising to a low, pulsing throb. Days later, the niche opens again, and the Maker emerges whole, larger perhaps, with new patterns of cilia on its head and new phrases in its chittering, having re-grown from the pillar’s flesh.

This is the origin of the rhythmic stone-on-stone chiming you heard: it is the sound of their maintenance, yes—the steady work of carving new channels, damming water, daubing clay—but it is also their music, their ritual, their way of keeping the entire regional hive in synchronous health. Each chime is a test, a sonar ping that maps the density of the walls, detecting nascent cracks or thinning layers, and the answering chime from another, distant part of the maze comes back slightly delayed, slightly warped, carrying the acoustic signature of the intervening pillars. By listening to that answer, they know the state of their world with a precision that no eye could match. And in the pauses between chimes, they hum, each at a slightly different pitch, creating the great low vibrato that suffuses the air, a ninety-second cycle of collective exhalation and inhalation that moves the very moisture through the stone. The place does breathe; it breathes through them.

Their technology is not separate from their biology. The dark binder in the composite stone is a secretion from their skin, a cement they exude when they press clay and grit together. The cord impressions on the walls are from fibres they grow from their own bodies—coarse, hair-like strands they pluck from the crevices between their zooids and spin into ropes. The fungal glow is their agriculture: they “plant” the chemosynthetic fuzz in cracks and harvest its light-carrying fruiting bodies to line the channels and illuminate junctions—not for themselves, but for the other fauna, the blind newts and the chitinous centipedes, which they cultivate as a food source for the rare occasions when they need more complex proteins than the pillar-fluid provides. The slow-moving, candle-like extrusions are another life stage of their own zooid colonies: failed Makers, or ancient ones that have chosen a different path, sessile and dreaming, pulsing with a silent song that adds a deeper layer to the hum.

They are not a civilization, exactly. There is no hierarchy, no leaders, no families. They are a process, a distributed intelligence that flows through the maze, each aggregate a temporary node of awareness, and when two meet, they may merge briefly—hands entwining, bodies pressing together until the seams blur and their chittering voices become a single, complex chord—and then separate again, carrying new memories. Their sense of self is fluid, a verb rather than a noun. The wall carvings, the channels, the pillars are their permanent memory, the physical record of all they have been and done, a library of resonance and texture.

And they are still emerging, still becoming, just like the place they make. The erosion that is forever threatening to undercut a pillar is not a failure of their design but a necessary part of the cycle, a pruning that they incorporate into the song. They will let a patch collapse, wait for the water to carve a new path, and then they will flow into that new space, daub fresh clay, seed new fungi, and sing a new pillar into being. The dynamic score of 1.9 is their life rate, their metabolism as a regional organism.

Standing in the cool, damp dark, you begin to feel them all around you—not as individuals to count, but as a density, a pressure of presence in the air, their chittering and humming a tapestry so thick that silence would be an amputation. They pass near you without notice, for you are not a sound they recognize, and your flesh is too dry, your heart too loud, your form too singular. But if you stayed long enough, if you let the moisture soak in and the hum sink into your bones, they might one day press a six-fingered hand to the pillar beside you and pause, tasting your strange shape in the dark, and add you, in some small way, to their eternal, unfinished song.
