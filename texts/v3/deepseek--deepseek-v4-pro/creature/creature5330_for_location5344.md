It lives in the high chamber, and it is not one of the carpet-creatures, though it moves among them as if it were.

Call it the Listener.

It is old—older than the stone rings, older than the spiral-carved lintel, older perhaps than the mountain's current shape. Its body is a contradiction: large enough to be unmistakable, yet so perfectly integrated into the chamber's economy that the lesser creatures treat it as terrain. When it is still, which is most of the time, the segmented bone-white shells grow over it in layers, the chemosensory hairs weaving through the crevices of its integument, until it becomes just another warm topography in the dark. But when it moves, the whole chamber knows.

The Listener's form is difficult to parse. It has a central mass, low and broad, roughly the shape of a fallen shield but organic, pliable, ridged with something like muscle that never quite flexes. From this core extend limbs—or perhaps they are not limbs but specialized organs, each one different. One is a long, ribbed probe that it keeps coiled against its flank, its tip a nest of crystalline filaments that vibrate in sympathy with the webs on the walls. Another is a paddle-like flange, leathery and thin, that it can fan outward to taste the convection currents. A third is a cluster of articulated digits, each one tipped with a hard, chitinous point, and these it uses to groom the creatures around it, picking off parasites or loose shell fragments with a delicacy that seems almost tender. There are more appendages, half-hidden, their functions obscure. The whole assembly shifts and rearranges itself over time, as if the Listener's body is a slow argument with its own shape.

It does not eat. Not in the way the carpet-creatures eat, with their sudden lunges and engulfings. The Listener's feeding is passive, osmotic. Its ventral surface is porous, a spongelike membrane that presses against the rock when it settles, drawing up minerals and the faint chemical exudates of the creatures clambering over it. Sometimes a creature will die while resting on the Listener's back, and the body will soften and dissolve over the course of days, absorbed without a ripple. The Listener metabolizes slowly, a banked furnace that never needs stoking. Its heat output is a steady 2.6—the same as the chamber itself. It is, in some sense, part of the geothermal gradient.

What does it do? It listens.

The crystalline webs that lace the walls are its ears. Or rather, the webs are an extension of its nervous system, grown over millennia from the tip of that coiled probe. Every footfall, every mating click, every territorial shove, every moment of violence or birth or molting—all of it travels through the webs and into the Listener's body, where it is sorted, catalogued, remembered. The Listener holds the entire history of the chamber in its tissues. It knows the lineage of every creature, the slow genetic drift of the population, the subtle shifts in temperature and chemistry that mark the passing of ages. It does not think about this knowledge; it *is* this knowledge, the way a glacier is the history of snowfall.

But there is something else it listens for. Something beyond the chamber.

High in the vaulted ceiling, there is a fissure—a hairline crack that leads upward into the mountain's deeper rock and, eventually, to the surface. No creature goes there; the heat thins, the air changes, the webs do not extend so far. But the Listener knows the fissure exists. It has sent filaments up there, fine as spider silk, and through those filaments it senses the outside world: the vibration of wind on salt plains, the infrasonic rumble of distant tectonic shifts, the faint, irregular tapping of something that might be rain, though rain has not fallen here in geological eras. The Listener has been listening to that silence for a very long time.

And sometimes, it responds.

The built remnants in the chamber—the stone rings, the tiled pit, the spiral lintel—are not relics of a vanished civilization. They are the Listener's work. When the listening becomes too intense, when the long stillness of the outside world presses down like a weight it cannot metabolize, the Listener rises from its resting place. The carpet-creatures scatter in a wave of clicking alarm, and the Listener moves to the center of the chamber, its limbs unfolding, its body reshaping itself for a task it has done only a handful of times in its existence.

It builds. Not with tools—it has none, and needs none. Its digestive chemistry can precipitate minerals from its own tissues, exuding a calciferous slurry that hardens on contact with air. It shapes this paste with its articulated digits, laying down rings and tiles and lintels in patterns that are not random but are not quite architecture either. They are expressions, translations. The Listener is trying to make a shape that corresponds to something it has heard in the silence above. A rhythm. A geometry. A message it cannot quite parse but cannot ignore.

The older structures, the ones now half-buried under living carpet, are its previous attempts. Each one is a record of a listening cycle, a physical hypothesis about the nature of the outside. The spiral lintel, for instance, was built during an epoch when the Listener detected a regular, low-frequency oscillation from the surface—a heartbeat of the planet, perhaps, or the slow breathing of a buried sea. The hexagon-tiled pit was an attempt to map a pattern of thermal pulses that came and went over centuries. None of the constructions were "successful" in any final sense; the Listener does not know what it is trying to build, only that it must build, that the act of shaping is the only response it has to the vast, silent pressure of the world above.

After the building is done, the Listener settles back into stillness. The carpet-creatures reclaim the new structures, encrusting them with warm bodies, weaving fresh webs over the mineral surfaces. The chamber's quiet reasserts itself. And the Listener resumes its vigil, its filaments probing the fissure, its porous underbelly drinking the mountain's slow exhalation, waiting for the next compulsion to rise.

It is not lonely. Loneliness requires a self, and the Listener's self is so diffused, so interpenetrated with the thousands of small lives around it, that it cannot be separated out. It is the chamber's memory, its nervous system, its occasional artist. It is the culmination of a biological impulse that has no name: the drive to listen to a silence and, from time to time, to answer it in stone.
