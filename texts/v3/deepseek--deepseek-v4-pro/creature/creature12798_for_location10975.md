They live in the space between the sound and the stone. They are not born from womb or egg, but condense from the perpetual moisture, gathering slowly in the hollows of the abandoned like a mineral crust on a pipe. To see one fully formed is rare; you catch them first as a movement in the corner of your eye, a patch of wet rock that wasn't there before, then a glimmer of something pale and still, the way a puddle holds the dull sky.

At a distance, as you pick your way across the slick tablelands, you might mistake one for a heap of the crumbling, dark stone, draped in lichen and rust. But then the heap shifts—not quickly, but with the patience of a stalactite growing. A limb unbends, a back straightens, and the surface that looked like rock resolves into skin: porous and granular, the gray of wet concrete, seamed with darker lines that could be veins or the cracks of age. When they stand, they are roughly human-shaped, but only in the way a weathered boulder is shaped like a crouching figure. Their proportions are wrong for any normal anatomy—arms too long, legs too short, a neck that seems to telescope into the torso so gradually that the head is just a knobbled extension of the spine. Their faces, if you can call them that, are smooth concavities, lacking eyes or mouth as you know them. Instead, there is a shallow depression where features might be, lined with fine, hair-like cilia that ripple constantly, tasting the air, sensing the vibration.

They do not breathe as we do. The air is too thick with water to differentiate atmosphere from body; they absorb the moisture directly through their integument, exchanging gases across the whole surface of their skin. The perpetual roar of the machines is not noise to them but a kind of respiration—the inhale and exhale of the plateau itself. They feel the sound as pressure, a steady thrumming that massages their tissues, that guides their slow, peristaltic movements. When the rhythm of a distant stamping mill abruptly changes key, they pause, their cilia flaring, reading the shift like a change in weather.

Look closer at one who has settled near a vent, where the steam rises in a constant column. The being is partly encrusted with the same pale, fossilizing scale that covers the factory walls, a crust of precipitated minerals that fuses it to the rock. It has been there for decades, perhaps centuries. The boundary between its flank and the ground is no longer distinct; it is growing into the stone, or the stone is growing into it. This one is not dormant—none of them are ever truly dormant—but is in a phase of deep listening. Its cilia are motionless, extended to their full length, drinking in the roar. It may not move again for another human generation. It is not sleeping. It is becoming.

What do they do, these creatures of the constant noise? They tend. Not with hands or tools, but with their presence. Their slow perambulations across the wet bedrock are a kind of maintenance. You will find one poised beside a hissing pipe joint that has begun to weep a vivid orange fluid, and if you wait long enough—days, maybe—you will see the being gently press its smooth face against the leak. The cilia extrude a fine, gel-like substance that mixes with the fluid, thickening it into a seal. The leak stops. The being stays there for a season, its body heat—barely perceptible, but constant—keeping the repair pliable. Then it moves on.

Others gather at the deep-bored shafts where the earth’s heat rises most fiercely. They pile together in stacks of two or three, their bodies interlocking like the stones of a cairn, to moderate the flow of steam. The water vapor that exits their bodies is slightly altered, its mineral load adjusted; they are part of the closed loop, filters and condensers of the plateau’s weeping cycle. They drink from puddles that have lain undisturbed for years, lowering their concave faces and letting the water creep up through capillary action. In doing so, they ingest the metallic dust, the fine industrial particulates, and these become part of their growing crust. Over centuries, a single being might incorporate enough iron and nickel to become slightly magnetic, pulling the rust particles from the air as it passes.

Communication happens not through sound—how could it, in that omnipresent roar?—but through vibration transmitted through the stone. Their feet, broad and spatulate, are packed with sensitive nodes that read the faintest tapping from miles away. They signal each other with ponderous stamps, rhythms that propagate through the bedrock like the footsteps of a slow giant. A message might take a day to travel from one end of the dispersed city to the other, but time is not a pressing concern. Urgency does not exist. Their language is one of geological tempo; a single conversation can last a century. The topic, as best as can be understood, is always the same: the state of the machines, the temperature of the water, the mineral balance of the steam. In their tapping, they share data about which vents need sealing, which pump has fallen out of synch, which hollow has become too warm. They are, in truth, a distributed nervous system for the entire industrial landscape.

Are they the original builders? The question is meaningless. They have been here longer than the structures—or rather, they emerged alongside them, symbiotically. The first factories were not built by these creatures but grew from the same seepage, the same mineral-rich soup. The beings coalesced from the puddles where geothermal warmth and industrial effluent mixed. Their basic form is a template imprinted by the surroundings: humanoid because the machines were made by human hands, or something like them, long ago; but the hands have been lost to evolutionary disuse, the eyes to perpetual fog. They are the echoes of the makers, the after-image of a human presence that faded so gradually it never noticed its own extinction.

Yet they are not mindless. Inside that rock-like skull is a slow, vast intelligence, a consciousness spread thin over millennia. Their thoughts are not sequential but cumulative, like the accumulation of sediment. An individual may not experience a moment-to-moment awareness, but rather a deep, cumulative knowing that builds up like the mineral crust on a smokestack. When you see one pause at a derelict conveyor belt, its cilia brushing the flaking rust, it is not remembering; it is dissolving the boundary between its own experience and the object’s history. To one of them, time is a substance, as tangible as the moisture in the air. They wade through it as they wade through the shallow pools.

And they dream. When the rhythm of the engines dips into a lower frequency at the turn of some unknowable cycle, they grow even more still, and their internal vibrations shift. The stone carries their dreaming into the groundwater, and the sparse shrubs with their metallic leaves quiver in response. In those dreams, the plateau is not high and isolated but part of a vast, lowland plain, and the sky is not a blanket of cloud but a brilliant, empty blue. The machines are silent. The water is still. And they are not there at all—only the rock, bare and dry, waiting. Then the frequency resumes, the ground note returns, and they stir back into their tending, their slow, endless walk of maintenance, their quiet fusion with the city that is no longer a city but a living reef of iron and stone.

These are the inhabitants. They have no name, for they have no need to name themselves. But if you were to press the question—if you could tap it out on the stone and wait a decade for the reply—the answer would come back not as a word but as a sensation: the feeling of water seeping through a crack, the taste of oxidized iron, the pressure of a hundred thousand days. They are the living patience of the plateau, the consciousness that arises when industry becomes geology, when sound becomes silence, and when the end of a world looks exactly like its unending, roaring, weeping middle.
