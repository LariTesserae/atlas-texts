The first thing it knows is the tick of water, the same small, bright sound that measures out the silence. It is not a thought so much as a rhythm, a pulse inside the cold that tells it when to move and when to still itself. Its body, if it can be called a body, is a gathering of the world’s own materials—a condensation of that white-green light, a membrane of the sap that beads on every strand, a skeleton of the same pale fibers that weave the root mat. It does not breathe; the air moves through it, carrying the scent of green things and wet stone, and the moisture that fogs on every surface fogs also inside it, a perpetual inward dew. To call it an animal would be to miss the point. It is a concentration, a place where the ongoing turbulence of this place knots for a time into something that can want and go.

Where it steps, the root mat gives slightly, the way a sponge gives under a weight it barely registers. It has no feet, only a broad, undulant contact-surface that spreads and contracts, tasting the sap-beads, the cold, the minute vibrations of distant growth-collapses. The light, that sourceless brightness, passes through its outermost layers with a faint refraction, so that it is almost invisible except as a shimmer, a distortion, a patch of air with the consistency of syrup. When it pauses—and it pauses often, for durations that would seem to a human observer like the stillness of the dead—it becomes indistinguishable from the glossy knots of resin that form where a root has cracked and wept. The only shadow it casts is a shadow that belongs to no single object, a diffuse darkening that might be taken for a trick of the unvarying glare.

It has memory, but memory here is not a private faculty. The mat remembers. Each strand, each cold-sap node, holds a chemical imprint of every footfall that ever pressed it, every stone that was laid by hands now gone, every frond that frayed and tumbled into the void. The being reads this memory the way a tongue reads salt, continuously and without effort. It knows the stone circles—not as artifacts, not as structures, but as an alteration in the taste of the surface, a slight alkalinity in the root-skin where something once held back the moisture. It knows the hand-axe, half-embedded and sinking, as a density-anomaly, a piece of the world that does not flex or seep. None of this knowledge carries what a human would call significance. It is simply a texture, a grain in the endless present. Those earlier lives, the ones who lit fires and twisted cordage and carved rootwood until it rang, are a fading spice in the weave. They are the ghosts of a warmer season that never existed, because there are no seasons here, only the slow storm and its occasional, mindless calms.

The being moves toward one of the stone circles, not out of curiosity—it has no curiosity, only an attraction to a particular pattern of moisture-retention that the disturbed fibers retain. The circle, a ring of stacked root-knots that have begun to lever apart under the pressure of a young shoot, houses a tiny pool of liquid water. The pool wears its own thin skin of ice, and beneath the ice, a filament of something not quite alive drifts in a torpid spiral. The being settles over the pool, its contact-surface dipping, not to drink but to merge the pool into itself, absorbing the water’s slow itinerary of dissolved minerals, adding its icy tension to the being’s own internal weather. As it does, the frond that has been prying at the stone ring twitches, grows a visible inch, and the ring shifts with a wet, grinding sound like a bone setting wrong. The being does not startle. It is, in a sense, the same process—a slower eddy in the same current.

It is not solitary, though it has no companion. Scattered across the leagues of root-matted emptiness, there are other condensations, other knots of light and sap, each one a little different in its inner proportions. They do not communicate by sound; the constant whispery rustle of the vegetation is already a language, a planet-wide conversation of stress and adjustment, of sap-surging and cell-death. The beings ride that language, their bodies subtly pulsing in rhythm with the growth-collapses and the tension-releases that travel for miles along a single ancient liana. Sometimes two of them will drift near each other, pulled by the same moisture cycle, and they will exchange materials—a droplet of particularly clear sap, a captured mote of that white-green light, a frozen bead of fluid from some deep fracture—and in that exchange, their internal patterns synchronize for a while, so that they share a sort of dream, a distributed awareness of the vast cavern’s state. Then they separate, carrying a piece of each other, thinning back into near-invisibility.

What they are is not a species in the Darwinian sense. They do not reproduce by fission or spore or seed. They precipitate, like the dew, when the conditions are right: when a particular mix of light, cold, and sap-borne message reaches a threshold, an area of the root mat will begin to bead more thickly, the drops will join, and over the course of several of the long, undifferentiated cycles (which a human would call days, though there is no night), a new concentration will achieve the minimal density required to move, and then to want. The want itself is the spark, the first true sign of being—it wants to seek a certain moisture, a certain sap-blend. And so it does, and so it is. And eventually, like everything else in this world whose youth is already dying, it will lose coherence. Its membranes will thin. Its light will scatter back into the general glow. The cold will slow its internal movement until it is simply a dew patch on the mat, indistinguishable from the million other dew patches, and its memory will bleed back into the weave with all the rest. This dissolution leaves no mark sharper than the hand-axes, no hearth to be swallowed. It is complete and unremarkable.

Yet in its brief knot of existence, it perceives something that a human might recognize as awe, if a human could share its senses. It perceives the scale—not as vertigo, but as a proper medium, the way a fish perceives pressure. It travels the catenary bridges between walls, moving not by muscle but by extending pseudopods of tensed sap that crawl along the roots and then contract, pulling the rest of its mass in a flowing surge. It crosses the great empty chasms where the light is brightest because nothing breaks it, and in those crossings, it spreads itself wide into a sail, a lens, catching the breeze that travels a mile or more, riding it to another filament. It senses the whole webwork as a single vibrating instrument, and for a time, it is a note in that instrument, a tone that sounds only once before fading back into the endless, whispering storm.

The stone circles will be gone, eventually. The hand-axes will sink fully into the mat, digested by the slow, mindless appetite of the roots. The tool-makers are no longer here, not even as these condensations, for the condensations are not their descendants—they are not descendants of anything. They are the world’s own dream, dreamed for a moment, forgotten, and dreamed again. And the only trace they leave is the tick of water, the creak of a stretching fiber, and, if you listen long enough, a faint, sweet resonance in the rootwood that rings when struck, a memory of craft without a rememberer.
