She is called Keth, and her name is a shape in the mouth, a press of tongue against the roof's ridge, a soft exhale that carries no sound. Among her people, names are not spoken aloud—they are signed into palms, tapped against sternums, breathed into hair during the long, dark ceremonies of binding. Keth received her name at the age of seven, when she first climbed alone to the high gallery where the weavers work, and she has carried it in the calluses of her fingertips ever since.

Her dwelling is a midden-shell, one of the hanging capsules formed from the hardened exudate of a sap-secreting node. It is shaped like a teardrop laid on its side, its walls translucent in a way that would matter if there were light. To Keth, it is known by texture: smooth on the interior where years of body heat have polished the resin, rough on the exterior where new layers accrete each season like tree rings. She shares it with no one, not from solitude but from function—she is a bone-reader, and the work requires stillness.

At this moment, which is not morning or evening because neither word applies here, Keth is crouched on the floor of her capsule, her knees pressed into the slight give of the living floor-membrane. Before her is a pile of knucklebones, each one carved with a sigil so fine her fingers must trace it several times to be sure. She is reading. The bones are not fortune-telling; they are history. Each sigil represents a household, a lineage, a debt, a birth. Keth's task is to arrange them in the correct order for the upcoming council, to tell the story of who owes whom and why it matters, so that the tokens placed on the mycelium disc will reflect the true shape of the settlement's obligations. She is thirty-one years old, and she has been doing this work since the old bone-reader, her aunt-by-smell, died and left her the pouch of knucklebones and a knot of hair waxed with the last of her glow.

Keth's face, were there light to see it, is narrow and still, the planes of her cheeks interrupted by the sealed ridges of her eyelids. The skin there is smooth, unbroken, the lashes grown inward and reabsorbed in infancy, as is the way. Her ears are large and set high, the cartilage soft and mobile, capable of swiveling slightly toward a sound of interest. Right now they are still, because she is deep in the bone-story, her lips moving silently as she recites the lineage chants her aunt taught her. Her hair, a dark, coarse mass, hangs in ropes past her shoulders, each rope treated with the larval wax. It glows now, very faintly, with the greenblue of concentration. The glow is patchy, a constellation of attention—brightest at the crown where her skull burns with memory, dimmer at the ends where the wax is older.

She reaches for a bone and her fingers know it immediately: the femur chip of the Sinker household, the one that lives lowest, where the water pools. The sigil is three parallel grooves. She places it in the sequence, then pauses. There is a wrongness. The bone feels lighter than it should, its weight off by some fraction. She lifts it to her nose, breathes in. The scent is the usual cold iron, but underneath: a thread of something else, something that smells the way a bruise looks. Decay. Not of the bone itself—bone does not decay here, in this cold—but of the story it carries. The Sinker household has been lying. They have claimed a debt paid, but the bone remembers otherwise. She sets it aside, carefully, and makes a mental note to request a council adjudication. The tokens will need to be remade.

Keth's body is a map of the settlement. Her feet, broad and tough-soled, know every vine-bridge by its weave. The high path to the weavers' gallery has a braided section where a young root pushed through and was incorporated into the walkway; her toes curl automatically when she reaches it, finding the familiar corrugation. The low path to the water cisterns is slick with condensation, and she moves there in a slight crouch, her center of gravity lowered, her arms out for balance. She wears a garment woven from the inner bark of a fibrous vine, beaten soft and stitched with gut. It wraps her torso and leaves her arms bare, because arms must read, must sense the shift of air that precedes a swaying bridge, a falling drip, a passing body. She is never cold, or rather, she is always cold in a way that has become neutral, a baseline. The chill is her skin, and she moves inside it without complaint.

Her diet is fungus and grub and the pale, eyeless fish that swim in the lowest pools. She eats with her fingers, grinding the soft flesh against her molars, and she drinks the constant drip, cupping her hands under the leaf-channels and feeling the water run over the scars on her palms. She is strong in a wiry, tensile way, her muscles built from climbing, from hanging, from the slow, sustained torque of turning the bow-lathe. Her shoulders crack when she rolls them, a sound she finds satisfying because it is one of the few sounds she can make that belongs only to her, that cannot be confused with the drip, the pop, the shudder of the pulse.

She has a tool, her own, made by her own hands. It is a rib from something large that died long ago, and she has worked it with flint until one edge is sharp enough to part sinew and the other is rounded, polished by the oil of her own skin. She uses it for everything: cutting wax, scoring sigils, prying open the shells of the nut-fungi that grow in the upper hollows. She keeps it in a sheath woven into her hair, the handle always within reach. When she is thinking, she pulls it out and taps it gently against her own collarbone, a rhythm only she can hear.

Keth is not lonely. Loneliness is a concept her people do not have a word for, because the settlement is always present in the soundscape, in the vibration of the bridges, in the shared pulse that rocks the capsules. But she is solitary by occupation, and her mind has a tendency to wander. She wonders, sometimes—a wonder she has never signed to anyone—about the world above. The oldest of the old, the ones whose hair has gone brittle and whose glow is a permanent, steady ember, speak of a place where the darkness is not total, where a thing called light exists and is so bright it hurts. Keth does not understand bright, but she understands hurt. She imagines light as a kind of pain, a pressure on the sealed lids, and she shudders and returns to her bones.

She has a secret, which is that she occasionally dreams in something like vision. It is not sight, not truly, but a kind of spatial surge, a volumetric bloom in her mind that paints the caverns in a language of distance and depth. She will wake with the phantom sense of something vast and open, something that terrifies and attracts her in equal measure. She has never told anyone, because there is no word for it, and because she suspects it is a sickness.

Now she finishes the sequence, sweeping the bones back into their pouch, which is made from the stomach-sac of a pale, silent predator. She ties the drawstring, which is sinew, and hangs the pouch around her neck. She stands, feeling the capsule sway slightly with the shift of her weight. Her ears swivel. There is a sound, a new sound: not the drip, not the scuttle of many-legged things, not the soft pop of predation. It is a rhythmic scuffling, far off, on one of the high bridges. Someone is running. Running is rare. Running means urgency.

Keth tilts her head, reading the air. The runner is coming from the weavers' gallery. She smells them now—a sharp tang of alarm-sweat, the oyster-scent of crushed larvae, the specific wax-mix of a body she knows. It is her cousin, the one whose name is signed as a flick against the chin. The cousin who watches the canopy gaps for changes in the draft. She is running because something has changed. Something is different. Something is wrong.

Keth straps the bone-pouch tight against her chest, takes her rib-tool from her hair, and steps out onto the vine-bridge. The dark receives her, and she moves into it, reading the vibration of the running feet, the shift of air, the sudden, subtle warmth of her own hair glowing brighter as the first flicker of something—fear, anticipation, the unnamed emotion—catches in the wax. The settlement waits, a hundred sealed eyes turned toward the high gallery where the draft has stopped. Drip. Drip. And then the pulse, coming early, a shudder that runs through the roots and sets every bridge swaying. Keth braces, and runs.
