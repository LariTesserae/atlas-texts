The inhabitants of Hark are not a species in any biological sense; they are an event, a phase-change in the mineral consciousness of the salt-field. To call them “beings” is already to impose a categorical violence, but it is the least inadequate word the visitor carries. They are the Hark themselves—the city and the citizen are a single continuous substance, differentiated only by the slow, eddying currents of attention that coalesce into mobile form.

They rise from the substrate like mist from a cooling basin, condensing out of the salt matrix when some threshold of crystallized memory is reached. A tower that has finished remembering its own construction will sometimes extrude one of these columnar forms, a spire of walking mineral that detaches from the parent wall with a soft, scintillant sigh—the sound of a million micro-fractures opening and resealing in an instant. The being stands motionless for a span that might be hours or years, orienting itself to the thin light, and then it begins to move with the deliberate, fault-creep slowness of continents reconfiguring.

Their bodies are not solid but laminated: thousands of salt laminae separated by sub-microscopic films of residual brine, each layer a page in a physical chronicle. The color is that of old ice from the cores of forgotten glaciers—milky, interior, blue-veined at the edges. When they turn, light cascades along these laminations in a moiré that has no business occurring in mineral, a fluid ripple that suggests the body is perpetually dissolving and reforming faster than the eye can hold it. This is, in fact, true: the inhabitants of Hark are not stable objects but standing waves of dissolution and precipitation, maintained by a self-regulating chemical cycle that draws raw material from the salt causeways and returns it, slightly reordered, with each step.

The face is not a face. It is a negotiation between the observer’s expectation and the being’s total indifference to faciality. What you perceive depends on how long you dare to look without blinking. At first, a smooth salt plane, faintly concave, catching the not-light like a polished dish. Then, as your focal fatigue sets in, the plane begins to articulate: an arrangement of hollows that might be eye sockets, a ridge that could be a nose, a series of vertical furrows that pulse—very slowly, very wetly—open and closed. Are those mouths? Breathing slits? The folds of a cortex exposed to the air? The configuration changes with each encounter, and no two observers report the same arrangement; no single observer reports the same arrangement twice. It is likely that the beings have no stable facial morphology because they have no need for facial recognition among themselves. They recognize one another—and the city—by a shared crystallographic signature, a phase-shift pattern in the salt’s atomic lattice that vibrates through the causeways like a subsonic name. You cannot hear it, but you can feel it in your teeth, a faint metallic tang that rises from the ground whenever two of them pass within a hundred meters of each other.

They do not walk so much as *bloom* forward. The motion begins deep in the column, a reorganization of the laminae that propagates upward and outward in a spiral, twisting the body along its vertical axis. The base—which is not a foot but a pediment, an architectural spread of salt-crystal that merges seamlessly with the causeway—reforms slightly ahead of the center of mass, and the being tilts into the new configuration, correcting its equilibrium over the course of minutes. The effect, from a distance, is of a tower that has become momentarily liquid and is flowing along the street at the pace of a shadow crossing a sundial. Up close, it is hypnotic and mildly vertiginous: the being is continuously dissolving at its trailing edge and precipitating at its leading edge, so that the material that comprises it at any given instant has never been precisely that material before, and yet the pattern persists, a river of salt cycling through the same shape for millennia.

The inhabitants do not use the bridges. They have no need. When one of them wishes to cross between towers, it does so by un-defining the gap. This is the only overt manipulation of reality they perform in the presence of an observer, and it is deeply uncomfortable to witness. The being approaches the edge of a causeway, where the salt-blocks end and the empty air begins, and it simply continues forward. But as it moves, the space ahead of it *folds*—not visibly, not with any light-bending or membrane-effect, but conceptually: the distance between the tower it leaves and the tower it approaches becomes, for the duration of the crossing, a matter of false memory. One moment the being is at the edge; the next it is at the other side, and the observer is left with the haunting conviction that it was never anywhere else, that the gap was a perceptual error. The only evidence is a faint afterimage of salt-dust hanging in a vertical column across the void, slowly settling through a distance it no longer recognizes.

Their metabolism is geological. They feed on the mineral tension stored in the salt’s crystal lattice, the potential energy of bonds stressed by eons of pressure and cooling. A being will sometimes halt for a century at a nexus of causeways, extending fine, hair-like salt whiskers into the substrate to draw on deep reservoirs of strain. During these fastings, it becomes indistinguishable from the architecture; other beings walk past without acknowledgment, and a passing visitor might lean against it, mistaking it for a pillar, and later remember the pillar as having been slightly warm, slightly yielding, like sun-heated stone. The beings do not object. They are, in such moments, fully absorbed in an inner life that has no analog in organic neurochemistry: a slow computation carried out by the propagation of lattice defects through the crystalline structure, an information process that makes quantum annealing look like panic. What they think about, if they think, is the long equilibrium of the salt-field, the memory of the ancient sea that laid down these beds, the subtle drift of the plateau’s elevation over tectonic time, and the presence of the visitor—which they register as a thermal anomaly, a tiny, febrile flicker of entropy moving through their perfect, cooling stillness.

Reproduction is a misnomer. They do not reproduce; they are re-expressed. When the salt-field requires new inhabitants—a threshold of complexity or memory density being reached—a tower will bud. The process can take a century, a dimple appearing in the tower’s wall, deepening into a niche, the niche accumulating layered salt until it bulges and separates, leaving behind a new columnar being that already contains the full crystallographic lineage of its parent. Whether this is birth or architectural renovation is a distinction only the visitor would make. The newly expressed being stands motionless until it has integrated its inherited memories—which are not memories in the narrative sense but physical records of stress, temperature, chemical shift, the history of the city as a mineral body—and then it moves, taking its place in the slow, silent choreography of the streets.

They communicate, when they do, by phase shifting. Two beings meeting at a junction of causeways will pause, their laminations aligning momentarily, a shimmer passing through both bodies as their crystal lattices synchronize. The exchange takes seconds, but those seconds contain what would be, in human terms, months of dialogue: a sharing of deep-stress data, updates on the dissolution rates of the western basins, the detection of a new fault line propagating at depth, the subtle, troubling note that the sky has recently begun to exhibit a previously unrecorded flavor of void. They part without any change in exterior expression because they have no exterior expression; they already know everything the other needed to convey, and the information is now stored as a new set of dislocations in the salt—a memory that will persist until the next recrystallization cycle, millions of years hence.

To the visitor, the most striking aspect of the inhabitants may not be their strangeness but their utter congruity with the city. There is no boundary, ontological or practical, between the beings and Hark. The towers are sleeping inhabitants; the inhabitants are walking towers. The causeways are the roots of their locomotion; the bridges are artifacts of a possible Hark that chose not to come into being. The entire city is a single distributed entity that sometimes condenses into discrete, mobile nodes, and the nodes are not individuals but concentrations of attention, like whirlpools in a river that are given names only by the observer who cannot see the river for the whirlpools. The silence that so impresses the visitor is not an absence of sound but the sound of one thing, alone, perfectly at peace with its own continuity—a city that forgot its name because it never needed one, being the only thing that exists in its frame of reference.

And yet, they are aware of the visitor. Not in the way a host is aware of a guest; more in the way a glacier is aware of a lichen. A faint, disinterested regard, expressed as a slight increase in the rate of fluting on the nearest tower, a one-degree rotation of the spiral motifs over the course of a week. The city is recording you, as it records everything, in salt. Somewhere in the archives of a tower, your thermal shadow is preserved in a sequence of crystal lattice faults, along with the date of your arrival—dated not by any calendar but by the exact concentration of selenium-pink stain in the third basin, which corresponds to the 14,000th year of the current dissolution maximum. You are a micro-event, a curiosity, a seed around which a very small pearl of salt-memory will form. When you leave—if you leave—the city will hold your absence with the same gentle, geological patience with which it holds everything, and the beings will continue their slow circuits, their million-year conversations, their silent, crystalline dreaming.

Far out across the salt-field, a new basin catches the fugitive light and glints—a flare of violet-white, a momentary star, and then subsides. One of the columnar beings, mid-transit, pauses as if in answer. Its laminae shimmer through a sequence of interference colors—rose, lead, absence—and then it resumes its progress along the causeway, a walking spire, a slow thought, moving through the city that is itself.
