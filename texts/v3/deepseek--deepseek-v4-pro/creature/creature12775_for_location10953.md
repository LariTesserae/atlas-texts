Life here is not a collection of individual species so much as a single, unfolding process—a continuous thread of metabolism that runs from the stone’s deepest cracks to the mist-laced sky. Everything that lives is, in some sense, an engine, and the plateau is its factory floor. The thing you notice first, though it takes time, is the skin of the world itself.

On every stable surface, spread like a dark, oily varnish, the biofilms hold dominion. They are the primary text, the ur-life, and to call them a “mat” is to miss the point; they are a tissue, a planetary organ of ceaseless, quiet industry. Up close—close enough that your breath would fog the rock—you see them resolve: a mosaic of nodes, each the size of a pinhead, linked by fine, wire-like filaments that gleam with a wet, coppery sheen. These nodes are the factories. They draw their feedstock directly from the mineral matrix of the plateau, tapping veins of iron, copper, zinc, and rarer things, using a chemistry that is not photosynthesis but something closer to the slow oxidation of a fuel cell. Each node exhales a microscopic puff of waste gas—a barely-there sigh—and the collective respiration of a square mile is that sub-auditory hum, a vibration more felt in the shins than heard, like standing on the deck of a ship whose engines are idling far below.

What they build is astonishing. They do not simply grow; they manufacture. From their centers, they extrude perfect, geometrically exact crystals of metal and mineral: spirals of iron oxide that could be mistaken for watch springs, copper wires thinner than spider silk, hexagonal plates of a zinc alloy that glint like mica. These structures are not shed; they are bonded into the film, creating a living circuit board that slowly, implacably overgrows the rock. The biofilm is both organism and artifact, and it remakes the world in its own image—honeycombing boulders, eroding monoliths not by chemical dissolution but by selective mining, grain by grain. A cliff face covered in this patina will, over a millennium, develop the ghost of a pattern, a bas-relief of interlocking rings and radial lines that looks designed but is simply the shape of a metabolism reaching equilibrium with its substrate.

Feeding on this living carpet are the true giants. The colossi are so large that your mind first files them as geography—a low, rounded mesa that your eyes must painfully adjust to see as an animal. Each is a fortress of segmented armor, its back plates overlapping like the shields of some archaic infantry, weathered to the same ruddy black as the native stone. They move on limbs that are neither legs nor pillars but something between, a dense cluster of columnar supports that rise and fall with the deliberate, oiled cadence of a pumpjack. A step takes an hour, sometimes more. The ground compresses beneath them with a slow, spreading shudder that you might mistake for the distant tumble of a rockslide, but it is only the beast shifting its weight.

Their heads are low-slung, almost absorbed into the mass of the body, and the mouth is a long, flexible proboscis that extends to the rock like the hose of a vacuum dredge. When it feeds, the biofilm does not tear; it parts, a living curtain of iridescent oil drawing back from the rasp, and the colossus ingests a slurry of microbial nodes, mineral crystals, and rock flour. The grinding inside it is continuous—millstone on millstone, a deep, basal churning that reduces everything to a fine, hot slurry from which it extracts both energy and the raw materials to grow its own metallic plates. Once a day, or perhaps once a year—time is hard to gauge—it exhales a great plume of vapor that condenses instantly into a localized fog, adding its moisture to the damp economy of the plateau.

They are solitary, these mountains-that-walk, and their communication passes below the threshold of any ear. Occasionally, one will raise its broad, elongated head and release a sustained infrasonic note, a pressure wave that rolls across the plain and feels, in a human chest, like the memory of a bass drum. It is a call that has no urgency, carrying only the simple, slow news of presence: *I am here. The world is still here.*

In the spaces between the colossi, the pace quickens—relatively. The scuttling things, the clockwork dogs of the plateau, are the kinetic counterpoint. Their bodies are jointed from a dark, polished mineral that is not quite hematite, not quite chitin, but some biological ceramic that glints like gunmetal. Six legs, sometimes eight, a round, segmented body, and a head that is all sensor array: a brush of fine, trembling hairs that sample the air for chemical traces, a cluster of dimples sensitive to the slightest vibration, and something that might be an eye but more likely is a thermal pit, seeing the world as a landscape of warm and cold.

They hunt the biofilm grazers—not the colossi, but the smaller things that live within the film itself: tiny, snail-like mollusks that secrete their own crystal shells, threadworms that weave through the nodes like animated copper wire. The spider-creatures move in a signature pattern: a long, frozen pause, their sensors sweeping a wide arc, and then a burst of motion so fast it feels like a skipped frame, their legs a blur of needle-tipped precision, ending in a sharp, dry *click-click-click* as they seize their prey. That sound, a clockwork chittering, carries surprisingly far in the thin air, and it is the closest thing to a conversation this world has. They are not intelligent—nothing here is—but their movements have the exact, unforgiving logic of a machine executing a pre-written subroutine. When they die, which is seldom, their carapaces do not rot; they slowly pit and oxidize over centuries, eventually becoming indistinguishable from the mineral landscape, their components returning to the biofilm’s vast inventory.

The vegetation, if it can be called that, is the most patient of all. The wiry stems rise singly from deep crevices, never in clusters, always alone. They stand perhaps knee-high, though “knee” is a meaningless measure in a place with no humans, and their anatomy is spartan: a central stalk of fused, segmented nodes, and from each node a halo of dark filaments that branch and rebranch into a fractal haze. These filaments are not leaves; they do not flutter in the wind but instead tremble with a ceaseless, microscopic vibration, a shiver that is their own slow-motion breathing. They likely tap the same mineral resources as the biofilms, but indirectly, drawing up metallic ions through a root system that spreads like a nerve net through the deep rock. To touch one is to feel a dry, brittle resistance; apply a little pressure, and it doesn't snap so much as exhale a faint puff of desiccated dust and collapse, as if it had been a fossil all along. Yet they are alive, in a fashion, and once every few decades they will push a new segment from their tip, a single, incremental inch of growth that is their only comment on the passage of time.

Taken together, these lives are not a community in any familiar sense. There is no competition, no collaboration, just a set of interlocking processes running at velocities so slow that conflict is a meaningless concept. The biofilm mines, the colossi refine, the spider-things cull, the stems monitor—all these verbs are inaccurate human metaphors, but they are the best we have. The plateau’s life is an industrial ecosystem, a factory without a blueprint, running on an energy budget of trickling electron flows and thermal gradients. Its sound is silence, its speed is patience, and its aesthetic is the somber, functional beauty of machines that have no maker and no purpose but to continue. To walk here is to feel, for a moment, like a ghost in a vast, self-assembling engine—one that was running long before you came and will run long after the very idea of you has been worn away by the same gentle, inexorable forces that are, even now, drawing another slow breath out of the stone.
