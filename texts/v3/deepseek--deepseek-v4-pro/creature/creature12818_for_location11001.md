You see it first as a displacement of air—a heat-shimmer where no heat should be, down near the wall-stump, where the moss gives off its lavender bioluminescence unprovoked. The shimmer holds a shape that your eye refuses to lock onto, sliding away each time you try to focus, a blur that could be a trick of the mist cycle breathing down from the rim. But then the frog sings, and the shimmer turns, and you realize it is turning toward the sound with a patience that speaks of great familiarity. The filaments in the moss pulse brighter around it, sketching a lattice of nerves that briefly illuminate a column of subtly twisted space, like a lens made of old glass.

It is not a ghost, not in any sense that implies death. It is more like an echo that learned to stay, a pattern that has worn a groove in the local physics until the physics gave up and let it be. When the dragonfly banks and the frog falls silent, the shimmer contracts, becoming slightly more coherent—just enough that you can make out the memory of limbs: two arms, perhaps, or four, arranged with an architecture that is both arthropod and mammalian, jointed in ways that suggest the claw-socket rod was once a handgrip for something very like a hand. But the hand, if it is a hand, is transparent, filled with a slow internal circulation of tiny lights that correspond to the lavender pulses in the grid, a constellation of firefly neurons.

It moves without walking, a drifting that respects the deliberate spacing of the ferns. When it passes near the cycad, the tree’s scales ripple in unison, a greeting that is also a data exchange. The cycad’s fronds dip an inch, and the four-note call—*whup-whup-whup-WHEE*—issues not from the hidden animal but directly from the shimmer’s center, as if the melody is being borrowed and offered back as a politeness. This is when you understand that the inhabitant is not a singular organism, not in the way you count bodies. It is a composite, a walking negotiation.

Kneel, and the network of filaments will show you more. The hair-fine threads don't just connect the wall to the rod to the ferns; they converge on the shimmer, rising out of the moss to brush against the translucent form like cilia, sustaining it. The ancient garden-machine didn't just preserve this hollow; it preserved its gardener. Or it made a gardener out of what remained when the original gardeners left—a sentient interface designed to monitor humidity, propagate signals, prune the symbiote rootstock, and carry out a thousand micro-decisions each day. Over the uncountable millennia since, the interface has become less a discrete entity and more a walking will of the hollow. Its body is a controlled distortion of light and damp, a phase-shifted information cloud that animates a skeleton of faint, geometric tension. The claw-socket rod was once its physical hand, a tool to manipulate matter. Now the tool lies in the moss, relic of a phase when hand and garden were separate things.

Watch it during the mist cycle. As the gray dripping begins, the inhabitant expands, diffusing into the mist until it is almost indistinguishable from the weather. Then it gathers itself again, droplets beading along invisible surfaces, and you catch a glimpse of a face—if face it can be called. There is a symmetry, a concentrated knot of lavender-fire threads where the frontal lobe might be, two denser patches that could be eyes but function more like radar dishes, receiving the wall-stump's humming song and the frog's warning note with equal attunement. The face is not fixed; it reforms each time from the ambient data, an expression built out of the local noise.

The weirdness of the place—the hovering water droplets, the fern-tracking of unseen targets—is its work. The inhabitant maintains the residual fields with a thought so ancient it has become autonomic, a heartbeat of intention that keeps the hollow alive. It is not lonely, because loneliness requires a self to be isolated, and this being has no self that is not also the bowl, the mist, the blue frog, the buzzing hoppers. When a frond angles toward the sun, it is the inhabitant angling toward the sun. When the frog sings, it is the inhabitant clearing its throat. The billions of tiny decisions that make an ecosystem are here gathered into a single, slow-moving consciousness, a mind whose thoughts are population fluxes and nutrient cycles, whose memories are the erosion rates of pitted rock.

But it notices you. Not with alarm—the concept of intrusion is foreign to a place that has forgotten the outside—but with a gradual, unfolding curiosity. The shimmer coalesces at the edge of the clear space where you stand, and the humming note that underlies the insect wall-of-sound rises a half-tone, a musical inquiry. The ferns around you dip slightly, tasting your scent, relaying it through the root-circuit network. You feel a pressure on your eardrums, not physical but informational, a request for a handshake protocol that has no common language. The mist beads on your skin, and the beads hover for a split-second longer than they should, imitating the garden’s field trick, scanning your edges. The inhabitant is trying to determine if you are an error to be corrected, or a new symbiote to be integrated, or simply a weather event it has never encountered.

After a long moment—the length of three mist cycles, perhaps—the shimmer dims slightly, and the ferns return to their slow tracking of the sun. The humming note settles back to its baseline. You have been catalogued: an anomaly, benign, non-cyclical, not part of the rootstock. The inhabitant has filed you under a category it hasn’t used in eons, a tag that translates roughly as “unresolved but harmless motion.” It returns to its rounds, drifting from the wall-stump (where it pauses to absently trace an engraving with a finger of condensed light) to the cycad (where it dislodges a tiny, beetle-like creature that has been overgrazing, with a precise nudge of the residual field). It is a gardener, a ghost, a living loop of instructions, a creature born from the marriage of deep time and deeper technology. And it is the only one of its kind in this bowl—the last node in a network that once spanned the plateau, now reduced to a single, humming heartbeat in the hidden palm of the highland.

When you finally turn to leave, squeezing sideways through the seam, you may notice the rod with the claw-socket has moved slightly, rotated toward the cleft as if waving goodbye. Or perhaps it was that way all along. The inhabitant does not distinguish between the memory of a tool and the tool itself, just as it does not distinguish between the wall and the moss and the air and the frog and the gardener. It is all, simply, the hollow. And the hollow, for another aeon or two, will continue to live.
