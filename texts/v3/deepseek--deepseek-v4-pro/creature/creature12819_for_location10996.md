The people of the hollow moved through their days as the mist moved—slow, pervasive, shaped by currents older than memory. They were not many; perhaps forty souls scattered among the platforms, and to an outsider they might have seemed a single family, so alike were they in their quietude and their wood-dark skin. That skin was smooth and poreless, almost glossy where the light caught it, and it served not merely as a surface but as a sense organ: they could feel the minute shifts in humidity that presaged a migration of the furred tides, and the faint electrostatic charge that built before the winged things cascaded from the canopy. Children learned to read the air on their own bodies before they learned to speak the few words their elders used—a vocabulary reduced to whispers, clicks, and gestures, because sound was a currency spent rarely, and never carelessly.

They lived in symbiosis with the fauna, not as tamers or masters, but as one species among many in the wet, breathing web. Each family kept a particular relationship with a particular creature or cluster of creatures. Old Imara, whose platform was the highest and most precarious, tended the membrane-winged sonar-beings; she could interpret their frequency prickles by sitting motionless for hours, her own body a resonator, and when she finally moved, she would signal to the watchers which direction the next silent cascade would take. Her hands were stained a permanent violet from crushing the bioluminescent midges into paste—a paste that, when smeared on skin, granted a few hours of invisibility to the serpentine predators, allowing her to harvest their shed scales for tools. Those scales, translucent and razor-edged, were prized for knapping obsidian, but gathering them required a patience that only Imara possessed in full.

Then there was the boy called Kel, who was not quite a boy any longer, though no one bothered with exact years. Kel had been born during a rare alignment of migrations, when the furred shadows flowed upward along the ladders and walkways for three days straight, and the midwife had to deliver him while standing on a stool because the floor was a river of small, questing bodies. He grew up with an affinity for those creatures—he could predict their pulses by the way the moss trembled, and sometimes, when he crouched on a lower platform and extended a hand, one of the many-eyed things would pause its ceaseless motion and touch his fingertip with its nose, a greeting that no one else in the hamlet had ever received. The elders watched him with a wariness bordering on reverence; they knew that such intimacies could tip either way—gift or curse.

The community’s rhythms were dictated entirely by the fauna’s cycles. Dawn, in any conventional sense, did not exist in the hollow, but the people recognized the start of a new period when the midges shifted their glow from cool blue to pale green, a color that signaled the approach of the serpentine things’ nesting hour. At that signal, the cooks would smother their fires with damp moss, and the children would retreat to the innermost rooms of the stilt-houses, where the walls were lined with woven mats that absorbed sound. For the next span—measured not in hours but in the time it took for a frond to uncurl a hand’s width—adults would move in absolute silence, harvesting the nests: delicate, papery structures glued to the underside of aerial roots. The eggs within were the size of pearls and tasted of mineral and salt, a staple of their diet, but the harvesters had to work blind, because the serpentine parents sensed movement through vibration, and eye-contact was felt as a threat. The people had learned to navigate this task by memory and touch, their fingers reading the root-textures like a language.

Every member of the hamlet bore a small scar behind the left ear, a deliberate incision made in infancy with an obsidian shard. It was not a ritual marking, though it had the weight of one; it was a practical adaptation. The incision, when healed, created a subtle cavity that amplified the infrasound hum of the hollow—that subsonic breath they all felt in their bones. With the scar, they could detect shifts in the hum’s pitch, which served as an early warning for the rarest and most dangerous event: a pool-sound. In living memory, that had happened only twice. Once, a child had dropped a pebble into the dark basin, and the resulting sound had rolled upward like a visible wave, causing the entire fauna to freeze for one heartbeat before erupting into a frenzy that lasted three cycles. The other time, an elder had deliberately struck the water with a stick to avert a catastrophe—a rockfall from the sheer walls that would have crushed the central platforms. That sound had summoned something from the pool that the hamlet still would not name, and the elder had died in the aftermath, his body found drained of moisture, as if the moss itself had drunk him.

So the pool remained an object of silent reverence and terror, and the drawing of water was a role assigned to a single person at a time, a duty passed on when the current Water-Tender felt the hum in their scar begin to shift toward a lower, more dangerous register. The current Tender was a woman named Miru, who had held the role for so many cycles that the young ones thought her as old as the mountain. She walked to the pool each day at the moment when the midges flickered into their brief, brilliant purple phase—a phase that lasted only as long as it took to fill a gourd. She never made a sound, and she never looked directly at the water’s surface, because she said it looked back with a hunger that could hook a soul. Instead, she watched the reflection of the canopy in her peripheral vision, and she sang internally, a song without words that her mother had taught her, a song that was said to be the inverse of the hum, canceling it out in her own body.

The hamlet had no name for itself, because names required a separation between the namer and the named, and they did not conceive of themselves as separate from the hollow. They were the ones who tended the platforms and kept the ladders mended, who read the fauna’s choreography and adjusted their own movements to match, who never took more than the web could replenish. Their tools broke down after use and were returned to the wet, feeding the moss and the ferns. Their dead were wrapped in layers of translucent petals and laid in the aerial root systems, where the serpentine things would slowly consume them over many cycles, incorporating the flesh into new eggs, new scales. It was not a funeral; it was a continuation. They had no stories of an afterlife, only an understanding that the hum went on, that the pool breathed, that the mist returned each day with the same slow, deliberate patience.

Sometimes, a child would ask why they never climbed the sheer walls to see what lay beyond the bowl. The elders would gesture at the mist, which never quite lifted above the crags, and at the rock faces too sharp to scale without metal, and then they would point inward, to the dance of life that sustained them. But the deeper answer, the one never spoken, was in the silence itself: they had been here so long, had adapted so completely, that the thought of a world without the perpetual seep, without the moss-pelt and the furred tides and the subsonic hum, felt like the thought of being deaf and blind. They were not trapped. They were chosen, or they had chosen themselves, so far back that the choice had become biology.

And so they persisted—wood-skinned, scarred, silent—moving through the dim, green-grey light like creatures evolved for a niche no outsider could ever truly see. Their eyes, if you had been close enough to look, held no whites; they were dark from edge to edge, adapted to the permanent twilight, and in them you could watch the reflection of the bioluminescent midges, a constellation of cold fire that mirrored the quiet, teeming sky of the hollow.
