They have no name for themselves, but if they did, it would be the same word they use for the sound of a drop falling from a root-tip into a slate channel. A soft, closed-mouth click of the tongue against the roof of the mouth. A *tluk*. One *tluk* among trillions. They are part of the Filter House's pulse, not apart from it.

You would not notice them at first. Your eye would skate over their still form, reading it as another knot of damp stone, another lump of mossy patina against a pillar. They are small, no taller than a Skimmer-shell is wide, with a body that mimics the architecture. Their skin is the exact grey of wet slate, grained and pitted, and they can hold so still that the water-carved channels begin to trace paths over their folded limbs, as if they were just another feature of the floor. When they move, it is with a torpor that matches the syrupy creep of the water, a deliberate, millimetric shifting of weight that takes minutes to complete. A single step, from one handhold of rough-hewn rock to the next, might take an hour. In the dim, blade-like light, they are a sculpture learning to breathe.

They are not the builders. The builders are a myth even to them, a story told in the placement of each stone, a ghost in the machine. They are the tenders, the inheritors. Their life cycle is woven into the Filter House's metabolism. They are born from the largest of the hanging roots, the ones thick as an arm. Once in a great while—when the condensation is perfect, when the Skimmer population crests and the Harvester webs grow heavy with captured moisture—a nodule on such a root will swell, pale and translucent, and from it will emerge a new tluk, slick and blind and immediately reaching for the stone. They grow slowly, absorbing minerals directly from the weeping walls, their flesh incorporating the same dark patina that coats everything. They eat nothing. They drink only the water that runs over their skin. They are, in a sense, a mobile extension of the root system, a fruiting body given agency.

Their purpose, if one can speak of purpose in a place so balanced, is maintenance. But it is a maintenance that looks like prayer. A tluk will spend days crouched beside a channel that has become clogged with a Skimmer's moulted shell. With one long, delicate finger—they have three on each hand, articulated with an impossible, joint-less fluidity—they will worry at the obstruction. The action is so slow, so gentle, that the water barely ripples. The shell flakes away, grain by grain, dissolving back into the mineral broth. It is not cleaning; it is a conversation, a coaxing of the blockage to rejoin the system.

They also tend the Fog-Harvesters. This is the only time you will see a tluk move with anything resembling speed, though "speed" is a relative term. When a Harvester becomes entangled in its own dew-silk—a rare but dangerous event that can snap a leg or tear a web—the tluk will ascend the pillar. They do not climb so much as flow upward, their skin adhering like a slug's foot to the vertical stone. Then, with the same infinite patience, they will use their three-fingered hands to carefully unweave the sticky strands, re-aligning them, even re-attaching them to the Harvester's spinnerets. The spider-creature does not protest. It simply waits, perfectly still, a glassy ornament trembling with the vibration of the great bass note. In the greenish light, the tluk and the Harvester form a single, chimeric silhouette: a small grey homunculus cradling an impossible jewel of liquid geometry.

Their relationship with the Skimmers is more one of rhythm. The Skimmers move in their slow, synchronized waves, and the tluks move among them, not disturbing the pattern, but becoming part of it. A tluk might lie on the floor for a full day, its body bridging two channels, creating a tiny dam that forces the water into a new, temporary delta. The Skimmers, sensing the altered flow, will adjust their grazing paths accordingly, funneling the biofilm into new configurations. The tluks seem to orchestrate this, not through command, but through a deep, bodily knowledge of the hydraulic symphony. They are conductors of water, and the Skimmers are their orchestra.

Communication among them, if it exists, is not audible. Perhaps it is chemical, carried in the water itself, a faint taste of change that spreads through the channels. Perhaps it is vibrational, a subtle modulation of the ever-present drone. When two tluks meet, they do not look at each other. They simply arrange their bodies in close proximity, their three-fingered hands occasionally brushing, and then they move apart, their tasks slightly altered. They are solitary, but not alone. They are all extensions of the same wet, living system.

There is a chamber deeper in, below the level of the main floor, where the filtered water collects in a black, still cistern. Only the oldest tluks go there. They are huge by then, their bodies thickened and their movements so slow as to be almost imperceptible. They wade into the water and never re-emerge. Their bodies dissolve over decades, returning to the mineral soup that feeds the roots that once birthed them. It is a closed cycle, a perfect ouroboros of damp and stone.

If you were to kneel in the Filter House, put your ear close to the slick floor, you might hear, beneath the dripping and the crackling of jawbones, a faint, regular tick. Not a drip. A *tluk*. It is the sound of their hearts, a single-chambered organ that beats once for every three drops that fall from the root-tips. And if you were to stay for a year, for a decade, you would notice that the pattern of the channels on the floor slowly, almost imperceptibly, shifts to follow the new paths they lay with their bodies. The Filter House is not a ruin. It is a garden. And the tluks are its silent, patient gardeners, as much a part of the machine as the stone itself. They are the mind that the system has grown, a slow thought made of water and time.
