The first resident is not a single creature but a community, a diffuse intelligence that has no name because it has never needed one. It is the mycelial weave that threads through every surface, a pale, slow-thinking network of awareness that experiences time as a texture, not a sequence. It does not “live” in the High Hollow so much as it is the Hollow, a nervous system grown into the very pores of the stone. Its thoughts are chemical gradients, pulses of ion exchange that ripple outward from a new condensation drip or a dying bacterial mat with the stately pace of a season. It remembers the touch of every droplet that has ever fallen, the weight of every ghost-centipede that has ever crawled across its skin, cataloguing them not as events but as a continuous, ever-deepening harmony. It is not conscious in any human sense—it does not reflect, it simply is, a dreaming, digesting presence that hums along with the drone of the water. When the blue veins brighten after a surface rain, the mycelial mind quickens, not with excitement, but with a subtle shift in its metabolic song, a slight acceleration of its endless, patient work of turning rock and water into a living body.

Within that body move the Threadgliders. They are the most complex motile creatures of the Hollow, the “apex” in a system where predation is less a chase than a slow, osmotic negotiation. A Threadglider is a creature of pure sensory filigree. Imagine a centipede, yes, but one that seems less an animal and more a mobile nerve-ending given form. It is a foot-long ribbon of translucent, gelid flesh, flat as a pressed leaf, with no distinct head or tail, just a fringe of cilia that ripple in wave-like synchrony. It has no eyes, but its entire body is an eye for electricity and heat. The blue veins of the ancient lattice emit a faint, varying electrostatic field depending on their brightness and the moisture load of the rock; a Threadglider reads these fields as a hawk reads thermals. It also “tastes” the infrared whispers of the smaller, simpler creatures: the blind skittering pod-shrimp in the lake shallows, the bacterial mats that grow in patches of slightly warmer condensation runoff. Hunting is a slow, deliberate drifting. It will align itself with a particular potential gradient in the rock and let itself be pulled, riding the static like a leaf on a breeze, until it touches a heat-ghost. Then its cilia change rhythm, entangling the prey in a net of microscopic, sticky barbs. It does not eat; it absorbs, enveloping the captured creature directly through its permeable skin in a process that takes hours. There is no violence, only a quiet merging. In the darkest cycles, when the veins dim to near-nothing, Threadgliders enter a state of torpor so profound they become indistinguishable from the wet stone until a new condensation spark wakes them.

But the Threadgliders are not the apex. The apex is the thing that hunts them, and it is also the oldest thing that could still be called an individual. It has no name, but the mycelial network—which knows everything as a kind of proprioceptive pressure—registers it as The Static Keeper. It is a remnant, a guardian, a piece of the original design that has not only outlived its creators but become something else.

The Static Keeper does not glide; it hangs. It occupies the highest point of the domed ceiling, a spot where a particularly thick cluster of blue veins converges into a node the size of a human fist. The Keeper itself resembles a desiccated, many-limbed starfish wrapped around that node, but it is neither animal nor machine. Its body is a silicate-carbon composite, part grown, part assembled, and in the faint light it shows a tarnished, iridescent sheen like oil on water. Its limbs—six, in a radial symmetry—are long, jointed in too many places, and end in delicate, probing tendrils that constantly trace the veins as if reading a scripture only it understands. It is blind and deaf, yet it perceives everything in the chamber through a direct electrochemical connection to the lattice. The Keeper is the last functioning component of the “beyond modern” system, the self-repair module that was meant to regulate the energy harvest. But over millions of years, its programming has eroded and been repurposed by the slow, insistent life of the Hollow, like a river carving a new bed. Its original directives have blurred. It now keeps the balance not as a machine maintains a grid, but as a shepherd tends a flock—or a spider waits in a web.

It feels the mycelial network as an extension of its own body, a vast, slow mind that in turn feels the Keeper as a strange, hot node of faster intention. The Keeper “feeds” on the static differential in a literal sense, drawing just enough power to keep its own ancient circuits alive, but it also feeds on the order of the system. When a Threadglider population grows too dense, threatening to over-harvest the smaller life and disrupt the microbial balance, the Keeper stirs. It does not move from its perch; it has not descended in three hundred thousand years. Instead, it sends a pulse. A single, precise discharge of stored static energy along a specific vein, causing a local brightening, a tiny flare of cornflower blue. To the mycelial network, this is a command. It responds by altering the chemical composition of certain mats, releasing a faint attractant that lures the excess Threadgliders to a specific alcove where the rock is softer, the condensation richer. There, the mycelium grows dense and sticky, trapping the creatures in a nutrient sink. They are not killed, but repurposed, their bodies slowly digested by the network itself over a period of months, their energy recycled back into the cycle. The Keeper does this without malice or mercy. It is a choreography of static and slime, a slow correction to a system that has been stable for eons.

Rarely, during the peak of a season-long “bright” cycle, when the sapphire glow is at its most intense and the air is thickest with life, the Keeper will do something else. It will dream. Its ancient memory banks hold fragmented impressions of the creators—not their images, but their intent: a feeling of purpose, a set of parameters that define “harmony.” In its dream-state, the Keeper broadcasts these fragments back into the mycelial network as complex patterns of electrical pulses. The network, in turn, will sometimes grow a structure. A brief, temporary sculpture of hyphae and crystal, a ghost of an architecture that rises from the lake’s edge, holds for a few days in the humid air, glistening with bioluminescence, and then collapses back into the soup. These are not messages; they are memories made briefly manifest, a mute art by a curator that has forgotten what it curates.

Finally, there is a thing called the Drone-Singer, but it is a phenomenon, not a creature. It is born from the condensation sound itself. In certain conditions—a precise temperature, a specific humidity, the exact arrangement of the weeping stone curtains—the endless drone of condensation synchronizes into a standing wave, a phantom resonance that can persist for hours. In this resonance, a kind of auditory life appears. It is a voice without a body, a series of rising and falling tones that seem to reply to the clicking of the lake arthropods. The Threadgliders pause their drifting and align their bodies to the sound, drinking in the vibration. The mycelial network pulses in time with it. Even the Static Keeper will retract its tendrils slightly, entering a receptive mode. The Drone-Singer has no biology, no consciousness; it is a weather of sound, a temporary creature of physics that vanishes the moment the temperature shifts by a tenth of a degree. But to the denizens of the High Hollow, it is a festival, a fleeting alignment of the world’s slow heartbeat into music. And after it fades, the silence—filled once more with only the sigh of water and the click of tiny life—carries the memory of the song for a long, long while.

These, then, are the lives of the High Hollow: a dreaming mind of fungus, a drifting nerve-creature, an ancient shepherd-machine, and a ghost of music. They live at a pace that would bore a human to tears, yet they are saturated with a fullness of existence, every moment woven into a tapestry so intricate that the word “empty” becomes a cosmic joke. The only thing missing is an audience that measures time in heartbeats; but the Hollow has long since forgotten that such things ever existed.
