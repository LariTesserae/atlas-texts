The one who lives here does not have a name. Names imply a community to speak them, and there is none—not in this shaft, not in the hundred shafts adjacent, not in the tangled vertical world that the city has become. But if you needed to call her something, you might call her the woman who hums.

She is not alone because she is lonely; she is alone because she is adapted. Her body has forgotten the shapes that fit in crowds. She moves through the lattice the way the water moves, by finding the path of least resistance, and her limbs have lengthened to suit the work—arms with an extra joint at the mid-humerus, fingers that can curl backward around a strut and lock, toes that have spread and strengthened until her feet resemble the gripping pads of a gecko. She wears no clothes because clothes would catch on the sharp edges, would soak through and freeze, would muffle the tactile information she needs. Her skin has thickened into a hide that repels the wet, a surface of fine keratin scales that overlap like the tiles of an old roof, dark gray with a faint iridescence that echoes the dormant bioreactor cells. She is covered in them, in fact—the scales are not her own evolution but a symbiosis, the filtration colonies finding a new substrate on a living host, and she lets them grow because they keep her clean, because they filter the trace toxins from the air she breathes, because in some slow chemical exchange she does not fully understand, they feed her.

She is old. Not in the way the construct is old—the constructs were built, and they persist. She was born, and she has aged. Her hair, what remains of it, is a white so fine it reads as silver in the cold light, cropped close to her skull where it hasn't fallen out entirely. Her eyes have filmed over with a secondary lid, a nictitating membrane that stays half-closed most of the time, protecting her vision from the constant gloss of condensation. She sees the world through a permanent blur, but she does not need sharp vision. She needs the hum.

The hum is how she knows the city. She presses her palm flat against a strut and feels the frequency, reads the subtle shifts that tell her which conduits are active, which sections are stressed, where the next thermal contraction will occur.
