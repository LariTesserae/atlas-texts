In the perpetual grey-green twilight of the plateau, something else moves with the same quiet, metabolic slowness as the machinery. He is not hidden, exactly, but he is easy to miss—a shape the colour of wet concrete and old rust, blending into the fretwork of pipes and dripping lianas. You might spot him first as a shift in the texture of the fog, a deeper patch of shadow that does not resolve until you are almost within arm’s reach.

He is human, or was human once, though the word has lost its contours here. Tall and gaunt, with a spine curved into a question mark from decades of stooping under low ductwork, he wears his environment on his body. His clothing is a patchwork of salvaged canvas, rubber sheeting, and woven mats of the same olive-black moss that smothers every wall—a living, breathable garment that insulates him against the constant twelve-degree chill and itself drips with condensation. Stitched into the fabric are pockets full of tools: a copper wrench, a handful of rusted hex keys, a pruning knife whose blade is sharpened to a whisper. A length of translucent tubing coils around his waist, patched in three places with something that might be tree-sap.

His skin is the white of a blind cave-fish, bloodless and smooth, and when he pushes back the hood of his anorak you see a face that is all altitude and erosion—high cheekbones, a blade of a nose, deep-set eyes that are too large for their sockets, the irises a muddy hazel that has learned to drink every last photon. His pupils are always dilated, even when he looks toward the shaft’s amber glow, and in the faintest light they reflect a greenish tapetum, the gift of some long-ago retrofit he barely remembers. His hair, what little remains, is a fringe of grey lichen, and it stirs in the breeze from the slow-turning fan as if it, too, is a kind of epiphyte.

He does not speak, not to visitors, anyway. You might hear him muttering to a seized bearing, or humming a tuneless, subsonic note that locks into the thirty-hertz throb of the tank district—a duet with the engines. His voice, when it emerges, is a rusty creak, unused to forming words, but the sounds he makes are not language; they are the soft, plosive noises of a man talking a machine into health, the way a farmer soothes a difficult horse.

He has a name, one he gave himself centuries ago, but it has worn smooth from disuse and now exists only as a faint glyph scratched onto the inner face of a heat exchanger where he sleeps. Call him the Keeper. He tends the plateau as a gardener tends a wild garden—not commanding, but nudging. He clears the worst of the liana-throttle from a catwalk joint so the alignment rails can still ratchet. He taps a pressure gauge with the back of one knobby finger, reads the tremor, and tightens a valve a quarter-turn. He harvests the pale, tuberous watercress from the weep-beds of the heat exchangers, and he eats it raw, the starch coating his tongue with a taste like wet cardboard and electricity. He drinks from the rivulets that run down the fluted columns of the vertical-farming towers, water that has passed through a hundred roots and a dozen charcoal filters, and it is always cool and faintly sweet with the exudate of ten thousand leaves.

He moves through the reshuffling geography of the tableland with the ease of a man who has become part of its algorithm. He knows, without counting, the fourteen-second cadence of the robotic arm, and he will sometimes pause to watch it, his own breathing synchronising to that soft pneumatic sigh. When a gangway ratchets to a new position, he is already stepping onto it, his weight compensated for, his path never blocked. He oils the ground-tracks of the migrating tanks, not because the tanks need him to—they have run for decades without—but because he likes the way the greased steel gleams for a day before the moss reclaims it. It is his small act of polish, his signature on the world.

He does not think of himself as lonely. The plateau is a society of many parts: the cylinders hum to each other, the conveyors shift their freight, the fan draws the mist down into the amber depths. He converses with them in the language of touch and listening. Sometimes, when the night cycle deepens the fog to a woolly black, he curls into a nest of warm piping in the crook of a heat exchanger, wraps his moss-coat tight, and watches the tiny, gloss-black shrew-things as they ripple through a sump. They are his companions, in a way. Once, he found a bat tangled in a net of spider-plant tendrils, and he cupped it in his hands until it stopped trembling, feeling its leathery wings tickle his palms. He reattached it to a beam near the outflow pipe and did not see it again, but he likes to imagine it remembers him.

His age is impossible. He has been here since the last of the engineers disappeared into the mist, leaving the machines to run their endless loops. He may have been one of them, or he may have been a child born in the shadow of the vat-fields, raised on nutrient-clouded water and the low, constant vibration in his bones. His body is a hybrid of adaptation and careful maintenance: his lungs have learned to filter the ever-damp air without complaint, his joints move on their own lubricant of thickened synovial fluid, and his heart beats at a steady fifty-two, locked to the deep, smooth hum that never ceases. The moss on his coat is slowly colonising his left shoulder, a bright green epaulette that he has decided to let be.

If you follow him, he will not acknowledge you directly. He might pause and turn his head, those enormous pupils drinking you in, and then he will return to his work. Eventually, after an hour or a day, he will lead you to the edge of the vast open shaft. He will stand there, leaning on the trembling railing, and he will watch the fog pour downward into the amber glow. He will raise one hand—long, pale fingers, nails as thick as horn—and gesture not at the fan, but at the whole interlocking tissue of leaf and pipe and strut and mist.

“It breathes,” he might say, the words hauled up from some deep memory of language. And then he will turn back into the green and the rust and the drip, disappearing as completely as a shrew into the leaves, leaving you alone with the hum and the cool, living machine that goes on forever in every direction.
