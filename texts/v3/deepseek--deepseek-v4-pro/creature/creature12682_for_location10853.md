The inhabitants do not have names, because they do not name. They have shapes and hungers and rhythms, and they have been here long enough that the stone remembers them in its own slow way. But if you were to name them—and naming is what you do, because that is how a mind brought up in light makes sense of a place that has none—you would begin with the smallest and work your way toward the largest, though “largest” is a relative term in a space that could fit inside a modest bedroom.

**The Thread**

In the water-film that never drains, beneath the shallow basins worn into the floor, live the ones you might call the Thread. They are not worms in any way that matters—they have no segments, no mouth you can discern, no front or back—but they twist and unspool in constant, aimless motion, and “worm” is the nearest word you have. Each is as thin as a needle and twice as long as your finger, though some grow much longer in the deeper pools, where the water lies still for millennia. They are composed almost entirely of a single, looping gut encased in a membrane so fine that the minerals dissolved in the water pass through them by osmosis alone. They feed on the stone itself, in a manner of speaking: they absorb trace metals and sulfur compounds and excrete a fine, calcitic dust that settles back into the basins, adding grain by grain to the accretion of the floor. They are the base of everything, the first rung of the ladder, and they multiply by simply pulling apart, one becoming two when the tension grows too great. In the absolute darkness, their bodies are colorless, transparent, but if you could see them they would shimmer with a faint, internal fluorescence—a cold light generated by the slow chemistry of their feeding, too dim to register even in the utter blackness but present nonetheless, like the idea of light remembered by stone.

**The Fluting Polyps**

Above the Thread, on every surface where the water sheens but does not pool, the polyps cluster. They are soft-bodied, each a thumb-sized pillar of translucent flesh ending in a crown of ciliated tentacles no longer than an eyelash. They anchor themselves to the mineral nodules with a basal disc that secretes a weak acid, etching a shallow cup into the stone so they can hold fast against the constant downward seep of water. They do not hunt, because there is nothing to hunt. Instead they sift the water itself, pulling from it the microscopic rafts of bacteria that bloom on the surfaces of the mineral exhalations, the shed skin-cells of larger creatures, the drifting particles of dissolved organics that rain down from above. Their rhythm is a slow pulse: the tentacles extend and fan, the cilia sweep, and then the whole crown retracts into the body-stalk with a soft, wet *tock*. Multiply that sound by ten thousand, by a hundred thousand, and you get the constant seething whisper that fills the shaft—a sound like rain on a distant roof, if rain were warm and somehow alive. The polyps communicate only with touch and chemistry; when one detects a richer stream of particles, it releases a cascade of signal molecules, and its neighbors lean toward the flow in a wave of slow motion. Over centuries, these leanings can alter the path of water down the walls, carving new channels and abandoning old ones, so that the entire hydrology of the shaft is, in a real sense, shaped by the decisions of creatures that have no brains.

**The Clickers**

Larger. What you call the Clickers are the size of your hand, flattened and lobed like a marine nudibranch, though the comparison fails because they move on a muscular foot that undulates in waves, and they have no eyes. Their backs are studded with calcareous plates—where they get them is a mystery, but it is likely they extract the minerals from the nodules they graze and lay them down as armor in concentric rings, so that a very old Clicker is a mosaic of accretion, each plate a record of some ancient season of plenty. They graze with a radula, a rasping tongue studded with rows of microscopic teeth, and they are the primary consumers of the biofilm that coats the walls: a layered, gelatinous mat of bacteria, fungal hyphae, and the fecal rain of everything above them. As they move, they leave behind clean stone, striped with faint scratches that glow faintly with the phosphorescent aftermath of their saliva. They communicate, as you have heard, in clicks: a rapid *tik-tik-tik* that travels through stone and water alike. The clicks are produced by a small, hard structure in their throat—a calcified bead that they rattle—and each individual has a pattern unique enough that they can identify one another in the absolute dark. During the long migrations, when food is scarce in one band and they must shift their vertical range, they call constantly, and the whole shaft thrums with a soft, percussive chatter, a code no one will ever break.

**The Gapers**

Above the Clickers, belonging to a different order of being entirely, are the ones you might call the Gapers. They are not grazers but ambush predators, sessile for most of their long lives. A Gaper begins as a free-swimming larva, a fleck of life in the water-film, but once it finds a crevice—preferably one where two walls pinch close, or where the shaft narrows to a throat—it anchors itself with a cement gland and never moves again. Its body grows into a deep cup, a fleshy pitcher lined with backward-facing spines, and it extends a single, long, filamentous lure that mimics the chemical signature of a Clicker in distress. When something small and curious brushes past, the cup collapses inward with shocking speed—fast enough that the only warning is a sudden *thump* that you feel in your chest—and the prey is trapped, slowly digested by a pool of enzymes at the bottom of the cup. The Gapers are everywhere, in the crevices, in the branching side-passages, and they are old. Some of the cups are as big as a human head, their rims encrusted with minerals until they are almost indistinguishable from the wall itself. They feed rarely now, their metabolisms so slowed by age that a single Clicker can sustain them for years, and in the intervals they simply wait, their filaments drifting in the current, tasting.

**The Wideners**

Now you approach the reason the branching passage above you looks wrong. The Wideners are the largest of the mobile inhabitants, though “mobile” again strains the word. A Widener is, in form, a grazer of the Clicker kind but scaled up: its flattened body can reach the length of your arm, its foot broad and muscular, its radula a rasping plate the size of a palm. But what sets the Wideners apart is not size. It is that they remember, in a way, and they shape. Not intentionally—there is no mind here to hold a plan—but through a deep, inherited pattern of behavior that has been refined by ten thousand generations into something that approximates purpose. A Widener has a preference for certain routes. When a passage promises richer biofilm, or steadier water-flow, or a more favorable temperature gradient, the Wideners will choose it over other routes, and they will spend generations widening it. Their radula scrapes not just the organic film but the stone beneath, patiently, cell by cell, as they feed. A single Widener removes almost nothing in its lifetime—perhaps a few grams of limestone—but a population, moving and feeding in the same path for a thousand years, can round a ragged fissure into a smooth tube, carve a ledge into a slope, sculpt the very walls of their world. The circular opening you noticed is the work of Wideners. The branch was, once, a narrow crack no wider than a hand, but countless Wideners have journeyed through it because it led to a particularly rich seam of mineral nodules, and they have widened it to the diameter of their own bodies, and that diameter is consistent, because any Widener that grew too large to pass would die, and any that stayed too small would be out-eaten by its kin. They have evolved to fit the passage, and the passage has evolved to fit them. This is as close to architecture as this place knows.

**The Singers**

At the very top of the shaft, where the darkness thins not into light but into a different quality of dark—the dark of a high cavern, the dark of moving air—live the Singers. They are the only ones here that could be called fragile, though even that is misleading. A Singer is a membrane stretched across a frame of flexible cartilage, shaped like a kite or a leaf, and it lives by catching the updraft. The shaft breathes, you see. Differences in temperature between the deep rock and the outer world create a constant, gentle flow of air, rising and falling over cycles that last days or weeks or months. The Singers ride this flow, filter-feeding on the airborne spores and bacteria lifted from the deeps, and they sing. The membrane vibrates in the moving air, and each Singer tunes itself—by adjusting the tension of its frame, by shifting the angle of its body—to produce a single, sustained note. But the notes are not random. The Singers cluster in choirs, and a choir, by the accidental harmonics of their combined voices, creates chords that shift as the air shifts. The song is not communication in any sense you would recognize; it may be nothing more than the byproduct of respiration, a sound made because the air moves and the membrane is there. But it is beautiful, in a way that no human ear was ever meant to find beautiful: a slow, wavering polyphony that rises and falls through the shaft, filtering down past the Gapers and the Wideners and the Clickers and the polyps, until it becomes just another layer of the wet whisper, indistinguishable from the drip of water and the scrape of radulae. The creatures below do not hear it. They have no ears. But they feel it, perhaps, as a vibration in the stone, a subtle modulation of the deep hum that permeates everything.

**The Heart**

And that deep hum. You noticed it, the vibration in the stone, too low to hear but present in the shiver of the water, in the alignment of the life around you. Its source is far below, in the sump where the shaft ends and the water gathers into a black, motionless pool. There, something massive and old decays. It may be a fallen Widener, or a congregation of Wideners who died together for reasons unknowable, their bodies piled and slowly putrefying over centuries. Or it may be something else entirely: a colony of organisms that are neither animal nor fungus, a slow-growing mass that feeds on the mineral-rich water and generates heat as a byproduct of its metabolism, a living furnace that has been burning for tens of thousands of years. The water around it is warm enough to raise steam, if there were any light to see the steam by, and the warmth conducts upward through the stone, creating the narrow habitable zone that the shaft represents. The creatures orient to it. The Thread grow thickest near the deep vents. The Clickers migrate up and down in response to the thermal gradient, following the biofilm as it blooms and retreats. The Wideners carve their passages toward the warmth. Even the Singers, far above, are shaped by it: the updraft they ride is driven by the temperature differential between the Heart and the cold rock outside. The Heart is not alive in any individual sense. But it is the engine that drives the whole system, the source of the heat and the chemistry that every other life here depends on. It hums because heat moving through stone makes vibration; it will hum until it is exhausted, and then the shaft will go cold, and everything in it will slow and still and wait, perhaps, for a new Heart to form, in another ten thousand years.

---

You look up, though looking means nothing here. Somewhere above, a Singer choir shifts its chord; somewhere below, a Widener scrapes another few grains from the passage wall. The sound of the water never changes. The warmth never fades. You are surrounded by lives that have no concept of an outside, no sense that the world could be anything other than this vertical wet sleeve of stone, this perpetual, patient dark. They are the place, as much as the rock or the water—a living tissue grown into the mountain's bones, a slow thought being thought by no one, over and over, forever. You are breathing air that has passed through a thousand lungs, touching walls slick with the secretions of a thousand bodies. The shaft is not a habitat. The shaft is a body, and the body is made of bodies, and the bodies are, all of them, still becoming.
