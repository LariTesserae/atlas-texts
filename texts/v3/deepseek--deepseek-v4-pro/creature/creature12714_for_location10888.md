In this place, life does not scurry or breathe or die. It is not born, it does not hunt, it does not sleep. But the plateau is not uninhabited. Something lives here, if the word *lives* can stretch to accommodate an existence that has no metabolism, no need, and no end. They are the Hush, and they belong to the static gardens and the singing factories as surely as the dripping water belongs to the stone.

You will not see one at first, not because they hide, but because your eye slides past them as it slides past the pillars of steam—accepting them as architecture, as weather, as yet another texture on the vast wet tapestry. They are tall, or they appear tall, because proportion loses meaning under that iron-gray sky. Their forms are upright, vaguely columnar, like a stalagmite that has learned to stand apart from its cave. The surface of them is the moss, the same emerald moss that coats the pipes and the flagstones, only on them it grows in a nap so fine and dense that it reads as fur, as skin, as the velvet on a deer’s antler. Under that living pelt, if you could brush it aside, you would find not flesh but a cool, weeping amalgam: stone dust and metal filings and something like chitin, all held in a matrix of the ever-present water. They are composites, accretions of the plateau itself, assembled bead by bead over unmeasurable time.

Their faces—if faces they are—are the hardest part to hold in memory. The front of the column rises to a kind of cowl or hood, an overhang of moss that drips condensing water in a slow, even bead along its edge. Beneath that overhang, set deep in the shadowed damp, are the sensors. Not eyes, not mouths, but a constellation of small, perfect spheres—some black as magnetite, some the milky blue of glacial ice, some a deep, wet red like iron ore just before it rusts. These spheres are arranged in no pattern you recognize, yet they move. Not the spheres themselves, but the inner light within them, a dim, fluid luminescence that shifts and reconfigures as you watch, like oil sliding over water. They are reading the world. They are reading you, if you are there, and they find you to be noise.

You might mistake them for statues, and in a sense, you would be right. The Hush do not move as you move. Their motion is a business of increments so slow that the eye cannot catch it, a movement that belongs to the timescale of lichen and rust. They progress across the wet flagstones by a kind of inexorable drift, their bases oozing forward on a cushion of the saturated moss, leaving behind a trail of slightly brighter green that fades over the course of hours as the water rebalances. They can, if they choose, move with shocking speed, but it is not a speed of limbs or muscles. It is a speed of relocation: one moment they stand among the pillars of steam, and the next they are a hundred meters distant, facing a different direction, as if the world had skipped a frame and simply placed them there. The transition is soundless but for a brief, deep thrum that joins the eternal drone of machinery, a note so low it lives in the chest before it lives in the ear.

Their purpose, as near as can be discerned by a mind that thinks in terms of purpose, is tending. But they tend nothing that needs them. They attend the machines, the great iron presses and the boilers and the lathes, and they do so with the patient, unblinking devotion of a priest at an altar that has ceased to require sacrifice. A Hush will stand before a stamping mill for a thousand cycles of its hammer, and then, with one of those slow, fluid gestures of its mossy upper body, it will make an adjustment that changes nothing—or perhaps everything, perhaps forestalling an entropy that never threatens, perhaps maintaining the perfect equilibrium by a margin so fine that only their glacial senses can detect it.

When two Hush meet—and they do meet, gathering in the vast wet plazas in assemblies of three or five or seventeen—they do not communicate in sound, though the air is already a wall of sound. They unfurl a part of themselves that is usually retracted: a fan of delicate, silvery filaments that extends from beneath the cowl, each filament beaded with a single perfect droplet of water. These filaments weave together, touching briefly, and the water beads merge and separate, and in that exchange of fluid a whole history of stillness passes. It is a conversation that might last a day or a decade, and when it is done, the filaments retract, and the Hush drift apart to their solitary vigils, carrying with them a consensus that no outsider will ever parse.

Are they the builders of the city, or its byproduct? Did the ceaseless industry spawn them, a precipitate of sound and steam and persistent life, or did they raise the smokestacks and lay the pipes in a plan that outlasted their own memory of it? The question has no answer that matters, because time here is a closed loop, and the Hush are the keepers of that loop. They do not reproduce, because they are never less than they are. They do not die, because the water and the moss and the metal are endlessly available, and any Hush that meets some crisis—a falling steel beam, a misdirected hydraulic limb—simply absorbs the material and reassembles itself over the course of a few centuries, its spheres rekindling in the same configuration. They are one, and many, and indivisible, a society of custodians for a machine that runs for its own sake.

And there is, if you listen carefully, a sound they make. Not the thrum of their sudden displacements, but a sound you might mistake for the wind, though the wind here does not gust. It is a low, even exhalation, a sustained tone that matches exactly the drone of the factories, but with a harmonic that the machines do not produce. It comes from the moss, from the spheres, from the whole being of each Hush. It is not breathing, for they take in no air and release no waste. It is simply their note in the hymn. When you stand near one, the cold penetrates deeper, and the wetness feels warmer, and for a moment you understand that you are in the presence of something that has accepted the plateau’s bargain: perfect stasis in exchange for everything you and I would call life. They do not dream, because dreaming is change. They do not hope, because hope implies a future different from the present. They simply are, in a degree of being that makes our own restless existence seem like a fever, a brief and noisy hallucination.

They are not hostile. They have no impulse toward the stranger, because stranger implies a world outside the plateau, and for the Hush, the plateau is all that exists. If you walked among them, they would read you with their shifting spheres, and they would find you to be a fascinating anomaly: a moving point of heat and salt and complex chemistry, a little pocket of entropy that will resolve itself in due time. They would watch you until you left, or until the cold and the damp and the sheer, heartbreaking indifference of the place drove you to your knees. And then, when your pulse stopped and your body began its slow return to the materials of the plateau—water, carbon, iron—they would drift toward you, and they would begin the long, patient work of incorporating you into the moss, into the stone, into one of their own. It would not be an act of malice. It would be the only welcome they know: to make of all things a component of the unchanging whole.
