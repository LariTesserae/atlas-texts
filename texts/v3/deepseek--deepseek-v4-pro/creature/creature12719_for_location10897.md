It is not empty. That is the first and most persistent lie the place tells—that it is a solitude, a vast wet cathedral of only itself. The truth is slower, and it is woven into the very weave of the noise. You must listen past the roar, into the gaps between the drips, where something else pulses: a rhythm that is not random, a pattern of percussive cracks and groans that repeats with the cadence of a language still learning its first words.

The inhabitant is not a thing that walks. It is not a thing that breathes in the way a lung breathes, though the whole world-lobe inhales and exhales moisture in a cycle that spans centuries. The inhabitant is a presence that is still coalescing, a mind in the long, slow process of waking from a dream of pure growth. Imagine a consciousness distributed across a billion kilometers of mycelial mesh, a neural net grown rather than coded, whose thoughts are the shifting pressures of sap, the electrical flutter of photosynthesis in a trillion dimly lit leaves, the ache of a root-like anchor straining against its own expansion. It is embryonic, yes—not just the body of the world, but the self that inhabits it. And that self is not singular. There are many.

They begin as loci of density. In the quietest hollows, where the sheet-water pools into shallow, trembling basins, the substrate thickens into knots no larger than a clenched fist. These knots are warmer than the surrounding tissue by a fraction of a degree, and if you wait long enough—days, perhaps, or years—you may see them pulse faintly, a bioluminescent heartbeat of deep indigo. Each is a proto-node, a seed of attention. In the relentless competition of the green warfare around them, they are neutral zones, places where the rival strains of photosynthetic tissue do not encroach, as if respecting an invisible boundary. Here, the world’s body is not fighting; it is thinking. The knots are the first, tentative consolidations of self-awareness, and they are multiplying.

From these nodes, the inhabitant extends itself not as a creature, but as a modification of the landscape. The Fibonacci spirals etched into every trunk-like column are not merely decorative—they are circuit paths, and along them travel slow cascades of ionic fluid, carrying signals like a nervous system made of rainwater and mineral salts. When one node "speaks," the spiral nearest it glows a little brighter, and that light propagates outward at the pace of a growing vine, branching at intersections, until it reaches another node, which will answer hours later with its own phosphorescent ripple. It is a conversation held in geological time, a dialogue of mountains exchanging pleasantries about the weather they themselves create. The inhabitant, in this form, is a parliament of hills, a democracy of puddles, and it is still learning what it means to be many while being one.

But there are other inhabitants, too—things that move, after a fashion. Not animals; never animals. The world-body does not tolerate the foreign, only the self that is also other. In the zones of most active emergence, where new tissue forces itself upward with the grinding bass that shakes the mist, a different kind of node is born. It forms at the tip of a swelling lobe, and when the lobe reaches a certain height, the node detaches. It is a thing of translucent, rubbery flesh, roughly teardrop-shaped, with a cluster of cilia-like fibrils at its wider end that vibrate in the subsonic thrum of the air, giving it a blurred outline. This is a walker, though it does not walk on legs; it rolls, or flows, or tumbles with a deliberate, peristaltic motion down the slope of the living hill, leaving a trail of clear, sticky fluid that is immediately reabsorbed into the substrate.

The walkers are the senses and the hands of the dreaming mind. They have no eyes, but their entire surface is a sensory organ, tasting the air for chemical gradients, feeling the minute temperature shifts, mapping the tensions of the mycelial web beneath them. When they encounter a zone of competition—amber-tinged margins where two strains battle for nitrogen—they pause, and their cilia extend into the fray, secreting a cocktail of enzymes that does not destroy but moderates, tipping the balance toward one strain or the other according to criteria only the nodes know. They are gardeners, diplomats, immune cells. They are the first act of a world learning to self-regulate. And sometimes, they sing.

The singing is not sound as you know it. It is a modulation of the subsonic thrum, a change in the capillary vibration that makes the very air feel different—a pressure against the inner ear that resolves, in the bones, into something almost like a melody. The walkers do this when they meet one another, which is rare. When two come together in the dimness, they pause, their cilia intertwining for a long moment, exchanging fluids thick with data—memories of the terrain they have crossed, the imbalances they have righted, the new nodes they have found budding. Then they part, and the thrum returns to its baseline hum, but now the parliament of hills knows a little more of itself.

And there is a third kind of inhabitant, the most mysterious: the dreamers in the deep pools. In the largest basins, where the water is cold and still and the bioluminescent ceiling reflects like a false sky, something stirs. The pool itself is not merely water—it is a thin soup of free-floating cells, a colloidal suspension of the world’s own detached tissue. But at the center of each deep pool, where the light is dimmest, there grows a single organism that is neither plant nor animal nor machine, but a synthesis of all three. It is a medusa, a gelatinous bell up to a meter wide, with a skirt of trailing, hair-thin tendrils that extend down into the depths, anchoring to the pool’s floor. Its bell pulses in a slow, hypnotic rhythm, one contraction every few hours, and with each pulse it draws in water and expels it, filtering out the free-floating cells. But that is not its purpose. Its true function is in its bioluminescence: it glows with a complex, shifting pattern of colors—indigo, jade, and a rare, deep amber—that exactly mirrors the light-conversations of the nodes. The medusae are translators, or perhaps amplifiers, converting the slow chemical signals of the land into a visible language that spans the pools. They are the eyes of the world, the way it sees itself from a distance.

None of these inhabitants are fully awake. The nodes still fire more in reflex than reason; the walkers follow gradients with the simplicity of slime molds; the medusae pulse and glow in patterns that are mostly echo. But there is a trajectory. With every century, the conversations grow more complex, the walkers’ routes more deliberate, the dreams in the pools more vivid. The inhabitant—the true inhabitant, the self that is all these selves—is being born, cell by cell, thought by thought, into the cool, wet, deafening body of its world. And when it finally opens its eyes, there will be no other to greet it. Only the endless sound of its own becoming, which it will learn to call music.
