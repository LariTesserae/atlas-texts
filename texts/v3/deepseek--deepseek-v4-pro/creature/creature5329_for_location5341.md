The creature who tends the Grotto is older than the equilibrium.

She is not one of the click-tongued people, though she moves among them with the same water-timed step, her bare feet finding the worn hollows in the stone stairs as if she had carved them herself. She is taller than they are, and thinner, and her skin is not the pale luminescent grey of the deep-dwellers but something closer to the stone itself—a mottled, mineral complexion, the colour of wet limestone, with patches of darker pigmentation that shift and migrate across her body like slow clouds across a cave ceiling. If she stands still against a flowstone wall, she becomes part of the drapery. The younger inhabitants play a game called Find-the-Keeper, and they always lose.

She has no name in the click-language. She is referred to, when reference is needed, by a particular snap-pattern that translates poorly: *The-One-Who-Remembered-the-Rhythm*, or perhaps *She-Who-Waited-for-the-Water-to-Learn*. The inhabitants do not think of her often. She is simply there, like the fungal glow, like the gong, like the unvarying drop. She predates the city. She predates the people, though they have been here long enough to forget anything before the tiered dwellings and the bronze hammers. She was here when the water still rushed. She was here when the cavern was a howling void, a roar of white flood, and she stood waist-deep in the torrent and felt the stone dissolving around her ankles and did not move.

She remembers silence.

That is the thing the inhabitants cannot imagine: the silence before the rhythm. They hear the layered percussion as the natural state of the world, the way surface-dwellers hear wind or birdsong. But the Keeper remembers when the drops fell at random, a chaotic spatter that meant nothing, beat nothing, kept no time. She remembers the centuries of listening, of waiting, of teaching the water its own music.

She did not do this with hands. She did not carve channels or redirect the seepage. She simply stood—or sat, or lay, or pressed her body into the wet rock—and *listened*, and over time, over stretches of time that would turn a human mind to dust, the water began to listen back. Her presence, her mineral body, her absolute stillness, became a kind of gravitational centre. The droplets, feeling their way through the microscopic pores of the limestone, began to align themselves to her pulse. Her heart, which beats once every three minutes, became the master clock. The gong-drop at the apex is timed to it still, though she has not stood beneath that stalactite in a thousand years.

Her body is not wholly organic. It is difficult to say what it is. The pseudoscorpions, her oldest companions, treat her as terrain—they have built their own tiny, translucent cities in the folds of her joints, in the hollow behind her left knee, in the intricate whorl of her ear. She does not mind them. When she moves, they scatter in orderly lines, a pale trickle of living jewellery. She has learned their chemical language, the pheromone trails they lay across her skin, and she can read the news of the Grotto's hidden corners from the tickle of their passage: a fish carcass here, a new crack in the western wall, a change in the mineral content of a seep.

She does not eat, but she absorbs. She presses her palms against the flowstone and draws up dissolved calcite. She lies in the central basin and lets the cold water filter through her skin. The blind fish, pale as drifting spirits, nibble at the algae that grows in the fine crevices of her thighs, and she lets them. She has a fondness for the fish. They are the only creatures in the Grotto that never learned the rhythm—they swim to their own currents, their own flickering impulses—and she finds this restful.

Her face is difficult to read. It is long, and the features are smoothed, like a statue left too long in the rain. Her eyes are large and dark, with no visible iris, and they reflect the fungal glow in two steady pinpricks of blue-green. Her mouth is small, a pursed line that rarely opens. When she speaks—and she speaks rarely, perhaps once a generation—she uses the click-language, but her version of it is slower, deeper, full of subharmonics that the inhabitants' ears cannot catch. They feel the speech in their chests more than they hear it, a vibration that lingers after she has fallen silent.

She has a task, though she would not call it that. She tends the equilibrium. The inhabitants believe the Grotto is stable, that the rhythm is fixed forever, but she knows better. The limestone is still dissolving, microscopically slow, but dissolving nonetheless. The mycelial glow is a living thing, and living things drift toward entropy. The fish population fluctuates in cycles too long for the inhabitants to track. Without her, the balance would begin to shift, an imperceptible drift at first, then a cascade—a droplet falling off-beat, a fungal patch dimming, a birth exceeding a death.

She corrects the drift. She does it with small, almost invisible acts. She licks a finger and touches a particular spot on the ceiling, adding a microscopic mineral trace that adjusts the next droplet's path. She breathes on a struggling fungal patch, and the carbon dioxide of her exhalation—different from the inhabitants' breath, richer, older—feeds it back to full luminescence. She hums a frequency that realigns the bronze of the gong at a crystalline level, tightening its resonance. The inhabitants notice none of this. They simply live in a world that, against all odds, continues.

Sometimes, in the long dark of the water-month when the gong-drop goes flat and the inhabitants grow quiet and introspective, she climbs to the highest chamber in the tiered city. It is a small, round room, barely large enough for her to curl up in, and it has no purpose that anyone remembers. The walls here are covered in markings—not the inhabitants' work, but hers. She carves with her fingernails, which are harder than bronze. The markings are not writing, not art, but a record of sorts: a topography of time. The great floods. The first fungal bloom. The arrival of the people, who came stumbling out of the dark, blind and frightened, and whom she taught the rhythm with patient gestures. The long, slow calibration of the drips, each new synchronization marked by a fresh groove.

She sits in this room and runs her fingers over the grooves, and she remembers. This is her burden, the thing that separates her from the inhabitants: she is the only one who carries the whole weight of the Grotto's history. The people have their myths, their tales of the chaotic time, but they are stories, smoothed and shaped by retelling. She has the raw data of millennia, and it is heavy.

She does not sleep. She rests, in a waking trance, her body cooling to match the stone around her, her heartbeat slowing to near-cessation. In these rests, she dreams, but her dreams are not like surface-dreams. They are mineral dreams. She feels the rock around her as an extension of her body, the miles of limestone above as a weight on her chest, the deep aquifers below as a cool pressure in her belly. She feels every drip, every microscopic shift in the seepage paths, as a tingling in her palms. She is the Grotto, in these moments. The boundary between her and the stone dissolves, and she is simply the cave, breathing, beating, singing its endless watery song.

When she stirs from these trances, she sometimes finds that the inhabitants have left offerings at her doorway: a bowl of fish oil, a new bronze needle, a coil of luminous thread. They do not know why they leave these things. It is an old impulse, predating memory, a recognition that the Keeper gives something essential and asks nothing in return. She accepts the offerings, because to refuse would distress them, but she has no use for them. She adds the fish oil to the public lamps. She gives the needles to weavers who have worn theirs blunt. She weaves the thread into tiny, intricate tapestries that she hangs in the communal ledges, where the inhabitants find them and marvel at their beauty and assume they have always been there.

The only thing she keeps is the silence. Deep inside her, in a place that corresponds to no organ, she holds the memory of silence like a stone held in the mouth. The roar of the ancient flood. The random spatter of uncalibrated drops. The long, empty dark before the people came. She holds it and does not speak of it, because the inhabitants have no concept of silence, and to give it to them would be a cruelty. Their world is the rhythm. The rhythm is safety. The rhythm is home.

But sometimes, when the Grotto is quiet—quiet by its own standards, the gong-drop falling in its steady two-heartbeat pulse, the satellite drips pattering their unchanging polyrhythm—she climbs to a place no one else knows, a small, dry crevice high in the eastern wall, sealed off by a calcite curtain she formed herself. And there, in a space so small she can barely turn around, she sits and listens to the one thing the Grotto does not have: nothing. The water does not reach this crevice. The fungal glow does not penetrate. The gong's resonance dies against the calcite seal. It is perfectly dark, perfectly dry, perfectly silent.

She closes her eyes and lets the silence wash over her, and for a few moments—a few hours, a few days, she does not track the time—she is not the Keeper. She is not the clock of the Grotto. She is just a being in the dark, with no rhythm to tend, no equilibrium to balance, no history to carry.

And then, when she is ready, she breaks the seal and crawls back out, and the sound of the drips floods back in, and she takes up her post again, another thousand years stretching ahead of her like the endless passage of water through stone.
