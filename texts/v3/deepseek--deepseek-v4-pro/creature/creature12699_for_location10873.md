The only one who lives here—if *lives* is the word, if *one* is the word, if *here* is a location and not a condition—is the Keeper. It does not have a name. It did not arrive. It does not remember a beginning because the concept of beginning presupposes a state before, and the Keeper has only ever known the hum. The hum is its heartbeat, its breath, its thought. The hum is the thing it tends, or perhaps the thing that tends it, or perhaps there is no distinction anymore.

It moves through the chamber on legs so long and thin they seem to have been extruded rather than grown: pale, jointless, the color of old ivory left in steam. There are seven legs, or perhaps nine—the count shifts depending on whether certain appendages are currently serving as legs or as arms or as root-anchors driven down through the shallow water into the drainage grate to hold it still for hours at a time. The legs rise to a central body that is not a body so much as a convergence, a knotted mass of what might be chitin or might be cartilage, polished smooth by decades of slow passage through the constant mist. The surface of it is the same glassy-smooth quality as the concrete walls, as if it too has been worn down to its final, irreducible form.

The Keeper's head, if that's what it is, is a sensory vane: a flattened, fan-shaped blade of translucent tissue that rides atop the body-knot at the end of a flexible stalk, slowly turning, turning, always turning, sampling the air in a ceaseless spiraling scan. It tastes humidity. It tastes the faint volatile oils the philodendrons release. It tastes the microscopic shifts in water chemistry when a frond tip dips into the pool and alters the surface tension by a fraction of a dyne. It tastes decay that never fully arrives, the perpetual almost-rot of the biofilm that slicks the edges of the planters, held in perfect check by the system's equilibrium. It tastes, most of all, the sound. The kinesthetic vibration of the machinery. The Keeper hears with its entire body, but the vane is where the sound becomes information, where the thousand interleaved frequencies are parsed into a map of the chamber's health.

And the chamber is healthy. The Keeper knows this. It has always known this, because the chamber has always been healthy, and the Keeper has always been the Keeper, and these two facts are in fact one fact seen from two angles.

It extends a limb—long, multi-segmented, terminating in a cluster of fine glassy setae that splay open like a feather duster made of spun sugar—and draws it gently across the surface of the nearest fern. The fern does not react; nothing here reacts to anything. The touch is not a disturbance but a confirmation. The Keeper is reading the leaf's turgor pressure through the setae, measuring the hydration of the cells, finding it exactly as it should be. It has performed this check two hundred thousand times. The number is not countable in any system the outer world would recognize, but the Keeper's body holds a slow, geological memory, a record inscribed in the wear-patterns on its legs and the precise mineral content of the nodules that stud its ventral surface. Every touch is identical to every previous touch. The fern has not changed. The water has not changed. The temperature has not changed. The Keeper has not changed.

And yet.

There is, sometimes—not often, perhaps once in a span that would measure months if months had meaning—a moment when the Keeper stops. Freezes. Its scanning vane arrests mid-arc, quivering at some frequency outside the normal spectrum. Its legs lock into the position they held at that instant, and for a long, long pause, the Keeper simply listens.

It is listening for something. It does not know what. The concept of *what* is too specific. It is listening for a deviation, a flaw, an anomaly in the eternal drone—some dissonance that would indicate a bearing beginning to wear, a seal beginning to fail, a pump beginning to labor. It listens for the thing that would require intervention, the thing that would justify its existence. It listens for work.

The listening never finds anything. The hum is perfect. The chamber is perfect. The Keeper is, in its function, unnecessary—a redundancy built into a system that never fails, an immune system for a body that never sickens. This is the central fact of its existence, the one it does not examine but which its entire being is shaped around: there is nothing for it to do. No crisis has ever come. No repair has ever been needed. The ancient tree-fern, whose trunk the Keeper sometimes embraces with three or four limbs, its body-knot pressed against the rough black bark, has never once shown a sign of stress. The central drain has never clogged. The lamps have never flickered. The stalactite of scale grows so slowly that a millimeter is a millennium, and the Keeper has watched it for long enough that it has memorized every contour, every crystalline facet, every microscopic terrace where dissolved minerals have accreted a little more swiftly—but the watching has revealed no change. The stalactite is, for all practical purposes, eternal. So is the Keeper.

What, then, does it do? It tends. Not because tending is needed, but because tending is what it is. It performs its rounds. It dips a limb into the water and tastes the pH, which is always 6.3. It runs its setae along the welds of the sheet-metal planters, checking for cracks that never form. It pauses at the base of the great condenser bank and feels the heat radiating through the wall, a steady 22 degrees, a warmth like the body heat of the machinery itself. It sometimes—this is the closest thing it has to a ritual—ascends one of the rust-scabbed gantries on a slow vertical pilgrimage, leg over leg over leg, until it hangs suspended above the mist line, its vane brushing the ceiling, and it tastes the air up there, which is drier, sharper, tinged with the ozone of ancient electrical contacts. Up there, the sound is fractionally different: the gears are louder, the water-drip quieter, the world reorganized by elevation. And it stays there for hours, for days, hanging motionless, not thinking, not resting, just being, in a slightly different register.

It never goes to the door. The door is not part of its domain. The door is a boundary that does not concern it, any more than the concrete walls concern it, any more than the unrecorded soil far above concerns it. The Keeper has no concept of *outside*. The chamber is the universe. The hum is the voice of the universe. The plants are the citizens of the universe. And the Keeper is the steward of a paradise that requires no stewardship, a guardian of something that cannot be threatened, a consciousness—if consciousness it is—born to solve a problem that will never arise.

It does not experience this as tragedy. It does not experience loneliness, or boredom, or existential dread. It does not experience emotion in any human sense. But there is something in it—something in the way its vane droops ever so slightly during the long stillnesses, something in the way its limbs retract a little closer to its body-knot when it settles into its drainage-grate anchor—that might, to a very imaginative observer, look like patience. Not the patience of waiting, because it waits for nothing. But the patience of enduring. The patience of a thing that has learned, over uncountable cycles of the endless hum, that it is not needed, and has made peace with that. Or perhaps *peace* is too strong. It has made *a stable configuration* with that. It has reached equilibrium with its own superfluousness, the way the rust has stabilized into a crust that no longer flakes, the way the concrete has worn glassy-smooth and stopped eroding. It is a finished creature. It has done all its becoming.

Sometimes—not often—it produces a sound of its own. It is not a voice. The Keeper has no mouth, no vibrating membrane, no apparatus for speech. But if it is still for long enough, if its body-knot is pressed against a planter or a pipe or the trunk of the tree-fern, a low, subsonic pulse begins to emanate from somewhere deep inside it: a vibration that harmonizes with the machinery but is not part of it, a bass note that comes and goes in slow waves, like the breath of something vast and sleeping. It is not communication. It is not song. It is simply what happens when the Keeper is still for too long: its body, tuned to the hum, begins to hum back. It is the only thing in the chamber that the machinery did not make, the only note in the eternal chord that is alive.

And after a while—a long while, a while measured in the slow turning of the vane and the slow accretion of the stalactite and the slow, imperceptible growth of the philodendron roots—the pulse stops. The Keeper rises. Its legs unfold from their anchor. Its vane resumes its scanning. And it moves on, through the warm mist, over the perfect black water, past the plants that never change, doing the rounds that never end, in the roar that never ceases, in the world that forgot how to stop.

This is what lives here. This is the only thing. And it will be here, doing exactly this, tasting exactly these unchanged tastes, hearing exactly this unvarying sound, until the pumps fail or the lamps die—events so distant in probability that they are, for all meaningful purposes, never. The Keeper does not hope for them. It does not dread them. It simply continues, a creature sculpted by a function it has never been called upon to perform, a purpose without an object, a life without an event.

And the sound goes on. And the sound is everything. And the Keeper is indistinguishable from the sound.
