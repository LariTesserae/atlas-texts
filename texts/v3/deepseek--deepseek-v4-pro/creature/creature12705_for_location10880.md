It is easy, at first, to believe the chamber is empty of any resident but the plants themselves—that the niches are remnants of some former need, shaped by a growth pattern with no current occupant. The pulsing light and the curling root-fingers seem almost enough on their own, a self-contained intelligence that hums in the water and the slow respiration of the canopy. But the longer you stay, the more the silence begins to feel expectant, like a held breath. And eventually, you notice the hollows are not just formed; they are maintained. The moss inside them is warm, and it moves, very slightly, as if someone left only moments ago.

The inhabitants are called, if they are called anything, the Keen. The name is not spoken here; it is a vibration in the root-fabric itself, a sense of _presence_ that travels through the living walls when they stir. They are not separate from the chamber, not exactly. To think of them as tenants is to misunderstand the relationship. They are more like the soul of the place, the thought that the plant-flesh dreams.

Their bodies are small and pale, shaped for the cramped tunnels and the narrow spaces between braided columns. Picture something between a frog and a child’s hand, but not quite either. They are roughly the size of a cupped palm—that is their adult scale—with skin like the belly of a mushroom, smooth and faintly translucent, so that the slow pulse of fluid inside them shows as a shadowy tide. They have no distinct head, or rather, the head and torso are one tapered, bulbous form, as if a turnip had grown limbs. Four limbs, but the front pair are longer and end in pads of six splayed, boneless fingers, each tipped with a soft, retractable claw used not for scratching but for gripping slick bark. The hind legs are shorter, folded like a grasshopper’s, built to press against the walls of tunnels and launch them forward in the cramped dark. Their faces—if that is the word—are a constellation of sensory organs on the upper slope of the body: two large, dark pits for heat-sensing, a fringe of cilia that taste the air constantly, and a single, tiny mouth that is never used for breathing. They breathe through their skin, absorbing oxygen directly from the water-slick surfaces they press against. The mouth is only for speaking, in a language of clicks and soft, resonant drones, and for the one other thing that matters.

They are colonial, like the plants. A single Keen is rarely seen; they exist in clusters, and the clusters are extensions of a greater whole. Their minds are not entirely their own. They share a slow telepathy, not of thought-words but of images and impulses, that travels through contact with the living tissue of the chamber. When a Keen presses its palm to a root, it hears the memories of the place and the whispers of its kin. This is how they shape the architecture: not by commanding the growth, but by suggesting it, by dreaming a new niche or a wider passage, and the plants—which are, in a way, their own extended bodies—respond over days and weeks by swelling or receding. The braided columns are the result of centuries of such dreams, a collaboration between animal intention and vegetable patience.

Their life cycle is tied deeply to the central pool. The pool is not water only; it is a broth of genetic potential. The Keen do not bear live young. Instead, at the end of a long life—measured in decades of slow, cold metabolism—a Keen will make its way to the pool’s edge and dip its body into the black water. There, its skin dissolves, releasing a cloud of microscopic spores that swirl in the dimple-light. Those spores, over time, settle into the moss-bedding of the niches, where they fuse with the plant tissue and grow into a small, root-like nodule. From that nodule, a new Keen buds—at first a pale, worm-like thing that feeds on the warm, metabolizing moss, and then, slowly, a recognisable form. They emerge from the moss as adults, already imprinted with the collective knowledge of their line, though each one adds new dreams to the shared pool over its lifetime.

They do not eat in the way animals do. They are chemoautotrophs, part of the vast symbiosis. Their cells harbor bacteria that break down the minerals in the constantly seeping water—the basalt’s iron, the trace volcanic sulfur—and turn it into energy. The faint fungal-sweet scent in the air is partly their own exhalation, a byproduct. They also absorb sugars directly through their skin from the plant surfaces they rest on, a gift from the colony that sustains them. In return, they prune the parasitic vines, groom the beetle populations that might damage tender new leaves, and their own bodies, when they die and dissolve into the pool, feed the entire system. It is a closed loop, a city where waste is not a concept.

Their social life, if you watch long enough, unfolds in the niches and on the root-benches. They are most active when the light pulses at its brightest, the ten-second beat quickening slightly as if the whole structure wakes. During the dimmer phases, they press themselves into the moss, absorbing warmth and nutrients, their bodies flattening until they seem just a paler patch of lichen. At peak luminescence, they venture out. They are not a bustling species; their movements are deliberate, almost meditative. One might crawl to the central pool and sit there for hours, its heat-pits fixed on the surface, reading the chemical signatures drifting up. Another will ascend a braided column, its splayed fingers finding holds in the wax-like ridges, and disappear into a high passage. They communicate by drumming on the roots—patterns of soft thuds that carry through the dense wood, a language of rhythm that sounds like the creaking of the forest only more structured. A conversation between three Keen might be a polyrhythm that lasts an entire night, each response taking minutes to come.

The gliding creature seen earlier—the pale, skin-winged thing—is not one of them. That is a _kith_, a domesticated symbiont that acts as their eyes in the upper canopy. Keen are mostly blind, relying on heat and touch, but the kith have large, light-sensitive spots on their wings. Through the shared neural network of the plant, a Keen can see what the kith sees: the approach of a storm from outside, the opening of a new leaf, the slow advance of a fungal infection. The kith, in turn, are fed on the sugary excretions of the Keen, and they nest in the high crevices, coming down only to breed in the pool. The beetle is a pest they tolerate, its opalescent carapace a mimic of the pulsing light, and the Keen sometimes gently guide it away from a new niche with a tap of their finger-pads.

The sense of awareness that pervades the chamber is not a single god-mind but a tapestry of all these beings together. The Keen, the plant-colony, the kith, the microbial soup, even the slow fungi—they form a composite intelligence that has no name for itself because it has never encountered anything else with which to need a distinction. It simply is. The chamber is its body, the Keen are its most mobile thoughts, and the slow growth of new tunnels into the mountain is its long-term planning. The “young” geological terrain is part of that plan: the basalt is still fresh with minerals, the altitude ensures the cold that keeps the metabolism slow and sustainable, the perpetual mist feeds the water cycle. Perhaps the entire settlement is a pioneer colony, deliberately situated on a newly exposed ridge to claim the raw stone before any other life does.

And yet, there is something else. In the deepest, oldest borings that tunnel below the floor, there are hints of a different kind of inhabitant—one that existed before the Keen, or that the Keen themselves are slowly becoming. In the dark, away from the pulsing light, the roots become thicker and more knotted, and the walls of the passages are not grown but gnawed. There is a creature there, rarely seen, that is all mouth and long, slow limbs, a pale blind thing that seems to be eating the living wood itself. But it does not harm the whole; it is part of the cycle, a reclaimer, maybe a devolved form of the Keen that has lost its way and returned to a more primal hunger. The Keen do not fear it. They drum softly when they approach those depths, and sometimes, when a Keen is very old and has not yet dissolved, it will descend and not return, becoming something else in the dark.

This is the population of the grotto-hamlet. They are the dreamers of the roots, the cultivators of the moss, the listeners to the water-clock. They are not individuals in the human sense, but neither are they a hive-mind with a single will. They are a conversation that has been ongoing for centuries, a negotiation between animal and plant, between motion and stillness, between the quick pulse of the light and the slow creep of the root-tip. To live there is not to be born and to die; it is to be a note in a song that never stops humming through the walls.
