I name them *Cynosura*—a private name, an astronomer’s joke, because they have the gravity of stars and the order of a constellation. Not that they name themselves, or need to. Sound among them is not a label but a texture, a shared surface. But I have to call them something, if I’m to describe them at all.

The first thing to unlearn is the category “individual.” A single Cynosura is a living thing, yes—fur, heartbeat, the bright spark of awareness—but it’s not a complete animal. The unit of being here is the cluster: the seven, the twenty, the fifty pressed together on a platform. They sleep in heaps that breathe as one, the outer bodies curling tighter to conserve heat, the inner bodies sprawled in the humid warmth. Touch is constant. The soles of their paws are lined with fine sensory papillae, so that even a grip on a branch is a stream of information—vibration, temperature, the chemical traces left by the last passerby. When two meet, they don’t just greet; they read each other with their hands, a rapid patting and stroking that checks health, mood, recent diet, reproductive state. To be isolated is a state worse than hunger. A separated Cynosura will stop eating, stop grooming, and eventually fall silent within a day. The colony knows this. They won’t let it happen.

Their bodies are built for the hollow’s economy. That belly pouch I first saw—it’s not for young, not exclusively. It’s a wet-skinned, highly vascularized pocket that can close to almost nothing or stretch to hold a cube of pulp the size of the animal’s own head. Food transport, yes, but also water transport. The pouch lining secretes a thin mucus that binds water molecules—a trick borrowed from frogs—and a foraging Cynosura can carry enough moisture in its belly pouch to triple its own body mass, hanging pendulous and careful from the slower vines. The colony drinks from each other’s pouches, mouth-to-belly, a gesture of such casual intimacy that it makes me blush to watch it. They’ll even offer to strangers; I’ve seen a scout returning from the outer leaves extend its pouch to a nursery-mother without breaking stride, a transaction completed in half a second.

Their hands. The hands deserve a paragraph. Four digits, one opposable, but the bone structure suggests an ancestor with five—the thumb and outer finger have fused into a single stout digit with a split nail, half-claw, half-comb. The comb is for grooming, the claw for gripping bark. The other three fingers are long, with three extra pads each, and can flex backward almost to the wrist. This gives them a grip that works on any diameter, from the hair-thin tendrils of the climbing fern to the thick support beams of ancient wood. They don’t build tools that require a power grip—no hammers, no axes—because their whole hand is a precision instrument. Instead, they build by weaving, knotting, braiding. A Cynosura can tie a slipknot with one hand while hanging by the other, using lips and teeth as a third grip point. I’ve seen a group of four working on a new sleeping platform, their hands moving in a blur of cross-tying, while a fifth ran along the structure testing each knot with a series of sharp tugs. Imperfect knots were called out with a specific chitter—two descending notes—and the tier responsible would hurry to retie it before the testing-master passed judgment again.

I call it “chitter,” but the auditory range of these creatures extends well above and below my hearing. Watching them, I noticed many silent interactions—silent to me—where a whole group would suddenly shift behavior with no audible cue. They are generating ultrasonic pulses, I’m sure of it: tiny clicks from a specialized organ in the throat, bouncing off the dense foliage to create a sonar picture of the hollow. This is how they navigate so fluidly in the dim light. But it’s more than navigation. The clicks have modulation patterns, a syntax of frequency and rhythm that I can only glimpse on my portable sonograph. They are imaging each other—not just location, but identity. Each Cynosura has a unique internal cavity structure: sinus spaces, stomach and intestinal loops, the contours of the pelvis and skull. The sonar pulses bounce off *inside* the body, not just the surface. To a Cynosura, another individual is a known interiority, a specific acoustic signature that includes the fullness of the gut, the hormonal swelling of the gonads, the subtle asymmetry of an old healed fracture. I think this is why they have no fear of having secrets. How can you hide anything when your insides are public? They know each other the way we know a face—more deeply, perhaps, because the face can lie, but the hollow echo of a bone or the slosh of a stomach cannot.

This interior knowledge shapes their society. I’ve watched a group engaged in what I can only describe as a deliberation, though it bore no resemblance to human argument. A foraging party had discovered a new source of edible fungus on the outer shell of the hollow, near a ventilation gap. The question was whether to expand the tunnel in that direction, which would risk weakening the structure and exposing the colony to predators. For two full cycles of the day’s dimming and brightening, groups of Cynosura gathered on the main platform, their bodies pressed together in a tight huddle of maybe fifty individuals. The chittering rose and fell, not in turns but in a layered swell, like voices in a choir. I began to see patterns: specific rhythmic motifs that were taken up by others, modified, returned. The huddle wasn’t exchanging arguments; it was modeling the situation in sound, building a shared acoustic representation of the hollow’s structure, the fungus location, the predator risk, the nutritional benefit. The entire colony was thinking as one brain, with each individual contributing a partial model based on its own experience. The nursing mothers contributed information about larval protein needs; the builders contributed structural knowledge of the mud-shell; the scouts contributed the exact sonar map of the new fungus. The decision, when it came, emerged not as a vote but as a kind of crystallisation—the rhythmic patterns suddenly coalesced into a single steady beat, the “signal of completion,” and the group dispersed to their tasks. The tunnel extension did not happen. Instead, they wove a long basket-ladder and hung it from the ventilation gap, accessible only from outside, so the fungus could be harvested without breaching the shell.

Reproduction is communal in the truest sense. The females ovulate not on an individual cycle but in response to a colony-wide pheromone released when the total population drops below a certain threshold. That threshold is calculated by the density of bodies in the night-huddles; when the platforms feel empty, the older females begin to exude the mating-scent, and within a few days, every reproductively active female is in estrus simultaneously. The males compete, but not in the usual sense. They perform a kind of coordinated dance on the highest platform, a stamping display that sends vibrations through the entire structure. Females observe from below, reading the rhythmic complexity as an index of the males’ neural integrity—the more intricate the rhythms, the better the brain. But it’s not a winner-take-all. Mating is multiple, and paternity is diffused. The resulting litters (typically two or three per female) are born after a short gestation and then transferred immediately to the communal nursery, where they are fed from the pouches of any lactating adult. No one tracks kinship, because kinship is universal. A baby Cynosura learns the colony’s sonar dialect from the moment it can click, absorbing the shared acoustic model of the world along with its first pulp-meals.

I’ve been trying to gauge their intelligence. What is the measure? Tool use? They weave baskets so fine they hold water—literally watertight, the fibers treated with a plant resin and woven in a pattern that tightens when wet. Language? Their sonic communication has a recursive syntax that allows them to describe past events, future intentions, hypothetical scenarios (“if the bright light returns and the large shadow passes, then we will…”). Art? I haven’t seen anything I’d call art, unless the arrangement of the sleeping platforms is art—and maybe it is, because they seem to care deeply about texture and form, about the way a platform springs under weight, about the exact color of the dried moss they prefer. One platform near the top is never used for sleeping; it’s kept meticulously bare, a single oval of woven reed that curves upward slightly at the edges. The acoustics of that platform are exquisite. I’ve seen individuals climb up there and sit alone, for hours, not moving, just listening to the echo of the colony’s sound reflecting off the hollow’s dome. Something about that feels like contemplation, maybe worship, maybe just aesthetic pleasure. I choose to read it as consciousness at rest, savoring its own existence.

They have death. I’ve seen it. An aging Cynosura, fur thinning, hands no longer gripping with confidence, will stop eating and climb to a particular ledge near the top, just beneath a ventilation gap. The others will visit it there, pressing close, sharing water from their pouches one last time. The ultrasonic chatter around the dying one takes on a different tone—lower in frequency, longer pulses, a kind of slow-motion sonar caress. When the heart stops, they know because the internal echo changes: the liver still, the gut silent. The body is left in place for a full day, visited continuously, then carried by a group of ten down to the lowest level and passed through a small opening into a sealed chamber I hadn’t discovered before. The compost room. The dead are placed among the root-masses of the hollow’s central tree, where fungi break them down into nutrients. There is no ceremony, but there is a change in the colony’s rhythm for a full day afterward—a slower beat, a softer chitter. Mourning, or what I have no better word for than mourning.

The predator I saw—a large flying shadow, probably a species of gliding reptile—is their only regular threat. They have no defense but vigilance and a remarkable tactic of silence. When a sentry (a role rotated among the adults) spots the shadow, it emits a single high-intensity ultrasonic pulse. Every sound in the colony stops instantly. The cessation is more startling than the noise ever was; one moment a riot of chittering, the next a velvety hush, broken only by the rustle of leaves. In that silence, the predator hears nothing, and because the hollow is sealed, it cannot see movement. It passes. After an interval measured by some internal clock, the sentry emits a soft three-note chirp, and the noise resumes gradually, like a machine spinning back up.

Time feels different here. Outside, the salt flats bake under a sun that moves in a slow, merciless arc. Inside, the light barely changes, and the temperature is constant. The Cynosura have no concept of day and night as we know them; instead, they track humidity cycles, the subtle shifts in plant transpiration that occur as the outer leaves cool or warm. Their activities follow these cycles in a polyrhythmic pattern: foraging peaks twice in each “day,” resting spreads out in four phases, the nursery is never fully inactive. The entire system is a perpetual hum of maintenance, never quite sleeping, never quite waking. It’s a life without seasons, without years. The oldest Cynosura in this colony might be decades old, or centuries—I have no way to tell. They don’t age visibly after reaching adulthood. Their teeth grow continuously, like rodents, but wear down so slowly on the soft pulp diet that an individual could live until the teeth simply become too long and must be filed by a companion. That cooperative tooth-care is one of the most tender acts I’ve witnessed: one Cynosura holding the other’s jaw steady, using a rough piece of bark to rasp the incisors down, while the patient makes a low constant trill that I interpret as the equivalent of a sigh.

They are not “people” in the human sense. They do not name, they do not worship, they do not create narratives of past and future. But they are persons. Each one knows itself as part of a whole that knows itself. The colony is a mind made of minds, a distributed consciousness that has no center and no boundary. If I could translate their sonar songs into a single sentence, it would be something like: *We are the hollow, and the hollow is full of us, and it is good.* And they have been saying this, in their way, for perhaps a hundred thousand years, while the salt spread and the world outside went dry, and they alone—in their sealed bubble of breath and leaf and fur—continued to be full.

I have decided to leave them undisturbed. The entrance I crawled through was an anomaly, a thin place in the wall created by a recent rockfall. I will seal it before I go, with stones and mud, restoring the shell’s integrity. They never knew I was here, not fully; my body heat was a puzzle, my silence a gap in their sonar model. In a few cycles, the incident will have passed into the rhythm, a slight perturbation smoothed away by the constant flow of their togetherness. I will carry them out into the desert with me, though. The sound of them. The smell of them. The knowledge that such a thing is possible: a civilization made entirely of kindness, where every need is answered by a neighbor’s body, and the whole world is a warm dark room full of family. I crouch one last time in the thoroughfare, letting the chittering wash over me, and I think: this is the opposite of loneliness. This is what we lost when we became ourselves.
