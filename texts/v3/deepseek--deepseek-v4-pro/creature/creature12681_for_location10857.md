It is a soft-bodied, many-limbed thing. Not an amphibian, not a machine, not the moss itself, but a composite, a slow thought given permeable flesh. It has no name for itself—names are sound, and sound here is only the drip of water, the hum of substrate, the wet exhalation of the terraces. But if you were to watch it for long enough, you might call it the Keeper, or maybe the Absorber, or maybe simply the One Who Walks Between the Drips. It has been here for as long as the machines, perhaps longer, though it does not measure time in any way a calendar could hold.

Its body is a colony more than an organism. At its core, a bony scaffold not unlike the segmented limbs of a gigantic crustacean, but porous, riddled with channels, the calcium replaced over millennia by the same silicates that stiffen the crystalline moss of the upper steps. This scaffold is not dry; it is saturated like everything else, the silicates forming a lattice that wicks water upward through capillary action, keeping every tissue plump. Wrapped around that scaffold, muscle analogues of a pale, translucent fibre that contracts with glacial slowness, powered not by sugars but by a direct electrochemical gradient drawn from the water itself—a living battery, recharging with every ion differential between the moss-wet and the stone-dry. And over all of that, a living pelt of moss.

Not just any moss. This is its own cultivated garden, a symbiotic species that exists nowhere else on the Clouded Steps, a cultivar that has co-evolved with the Keeper until the line between animal and vegetation is meaningless. The moss grows in whorls and tufts, in specific patterns that map to the underlying muscle fibres, so that when the creature moves, the moss ripples in waves of green and grey-green and a deep, saturated black that holds moisture like a sponge. Tiny amphibian kin shelter in the thicker patches, their transparent bodies pressed against the Keeper's warmth. Tardigrades by the billion live in the water film that coats the moss surface, grazing the algae that the Keeper somehow encourages—exhaling, perhaps, a trace of something that feeds them. The Keeper is an ecosystem walking, a terrace in miniature, a vertical step that moves horizontally.

It moves rarely, and only on the mid-steps. When it does, it is not walking as a biped walks, nor crawling as a beast. It flows. The limbs—there are between twelve and twenty, depending on how you count, because some have fused with age and some have branched like coral—extend and retract with a hydraulic slowness, anchoring into the moss in a practiced cycle. Each footfall is a soft, deliberate compression of the moss-substrate, sending a ring of water rippling outward, alerting the spiders and the flatworms to a presence that is neither threat nor prey, only a shifting of the world. It leaves no permanent trail; within an hour, the moss has sprung back, erasing the impression. The sound of its passage is the sound of the Steps themselves: a wet exhalation, a whisper of sheet-flow, the gentle percussion of drops dislodged from their perches.

Its head, if it can be called that, is a dorsal thickening of the moss pelt, a mound where the silicate scaffold rises into a hood-like dome. This hood houses its sensory organs—if organs they are, and not just differentiated zones of the same tissue that makes up its body. There are no eyes, no ears, no nostrils. Instead, the entire hood is a vibration-sensing membrane, exquisitely tuned to the frequencies of the Clouded Steps. It feels the machines humming through the rock as a steady, reassuring pressure, like a heartbeat heard through a shared chest. It feels the amphibians calling, the high thin note arriving as a tickle across its moss. It feels the water dripping, the flow shifting, the distant subtle crack of limestone settling under its own weight. And it tastes the air: every fluctuation in humidity, every trace of hydrocarbon from the vents, every change in the microbial balance of the water film. It lives in a world of gradients—moisture gradients, chemical gradients, thermal gradients—and it navigates these with a topographic certainty, following contours of sensation that map precisely onto the terrain.

It is not singular. The Steps hold no room for singularity. The Keeper is part of a distributed network, a population of its kind that number in the low hundreds across the hundreds of square kilometres of terrace. They never meet. They have no need to. Their communication is chemical and vibrational, a slow-motion conversation carried through the water table, through the moss, through the very stone. When one Keeper encounters a seep newly enriched by a machine’s cycling, the information enters the groundwater and ripples outward; days later, weeks later, another Keeper on a terrace three steps higher will alter its route, turning toward a similar seep, tasting the message in the chemistry. Their thoughts—if thoughts they have—are not located within any single hood but are pooled across the network, a slow intelligence that processes the entire Steps as a single body. The Keepers are the ganglia; the moss the nerve fibres; the water the neurotransmitter. The machines hum and the Keepers know it, and in knowing, they adjust. They are the immune system of the Steps, or its gardeners, or its dreaming mind.

When a machine begins to fail—when a pump cycle skips, when a conduit cracks, when the vibration shifts from its steady 17-hertz hum to a stuttering 14—the Keepers respond. They do not fix the machines in any mechanical sense; they have no tools, no hands, no manipulators beyond their own slow limbs. Instead, they alter the environment around the machine. They settle beside it, their moss pelt exuding a particular enzyme that accelerates the growth of a calcifying algae. This algae precipitates calcium from the water, sealing cracks, reinforcing pipes. The Keeper will remain in place for a decade, two decades, its body a living bandage, while the machine stabilizes under the new mineral growth. When the hum returns to its correct frequency, the Keeper moves on, leaving behind a machine that is more limestone than metal, more ecosystem than industry, and fully functional within the strange definitions of the Steps.

The Keepers have been doing this for so long that they are the reason the machines still work. Without them, the pumps would have failed millennia ago, the conduits eroded, the limestone digesters clogged. The machines built the Keepers, or the Keepers built the machines, or both emerged from the same impulse—an ancient necessity, lost now, to keep the Steps in equilibrium. The Keepers do not remember a beginning. Their distributed memory extends backward only as far as the moss’s oldest peat layer, a carbon record that preserves chemical signatures of past states. They know cycles: wetter epochs and drier ones, periods of machine quiescence and machine activity, the slow migration of the mist ceiling up or down the escarpment as planetary temperatures shifted. They know that once, perhaps, there were other Keepers—forms now extinct, whose chemical traces are faint in the peat, whose roles the current Keepers have absorbed. But they do not miss them. Missing is not a concept the Keeper holds.

The Keeper does not reproduce in any way analogous to birth. When a new terrace becomes colonized by the moss, when the water channels establish themselves and the first machines begin their cycling, a piece of the network’s attention drifts there. A Keeper on a neighboring step will deliberately fragment: a portion of its silicate scaffold, wrapped in muscle and moss, will detach and flow downward with the seep, a seed-like propagule that settles in the new territory and begins to grow. It will take a century to reach full size, and during that century it will learn the chemical language of its new step, integrating into the network. In this way, the Keepers expand, always matching the pace of the Steps themselves. There is no hurry. There has never been hurry.

Sometimes, in the rare moments when the cloud deck breaks and sunlight spears down in columns, the Keeper pauses. It orients its hood toward the light, and for a few minutes, its moss pelt erupts in a frenzy of photosynthesis, the algae among the tardigrades blooming, the amphibian kin basking, the whole walking ecosystem flaring with a sudden, silent abundance. The Keeper itself does not see the sun; it feels it as warmth, as a spike in the electrical potential of its tissues, as a shift in the chemical balance that it will carry in its body for months afterward. When the cloud closes again, the Keeper resumes its flow, unchanged and unhurried, carrying that stored sunlight into the grey.

Up on the highest terraces, inside the permanent cloud, there are no Keepers. The crystalline moss up there is too brittle, the vapour too unsubstantial, the machines too deeply intergrown to need tending. But the Keepers feel that zone as a constant, distant pressure: the upper boundary of their world, a region of cold and crackling silence that calls to them in frequencies they cannot quite resolve. Some of the eldest Keepers, those on the highest mid-steps, have begun to grow silicates in their scaffold that mimic the crystalline moss. They are changing, perhaps, preparing for a new cycle, a new equilibrium. The network’s slow thought considers this without urgency. If the Keepers are to colonize the cloud-zone, it will happen over the next several thousand years. There is time. There is only time.

For now, the Keeper flows along a mid-step, its moss rippling, its amphibian passengers clinging, its hood sifting the vibrations of the world. It is moving toward a pump that has begun to hum at 14 hertz. It will be there by the decade’s end. The water drips. The mist holds everything. The moss grows. And the Keepers, piece by piece, thought by thought, continue to dream the entire Steps into balance.
