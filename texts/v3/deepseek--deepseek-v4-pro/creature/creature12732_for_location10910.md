There is a moment—you almost miss it, because it happens at the edge of your attention, in the mist—when the hum of the great shaft-fan dips a half-tone, and something moves on the far catwalk.

It is not a machine. You have learned the machines by now: their smooth arcs, their repeating rhythms, their utter indifference. This movement is organic but not hurried, a slow bipedal amble that pauses, shifts weight, resumes. The figure is man-shaped in the way a shadow is man-shaped: the outline is right, but the mass is wrong, or the proportions stretch when you try to focus. It walks along a gantry thirty metres away, one hand trailing lightly along the wet railing, and where its fingers pass, the rust blooms briefly orange-bright before subsiding into the same stable vermilion crust. It does not seem to notice you. Or perhaps it does, and walks on anyway.

After a minute it stops by a coupling-box, a junction where three pipe-runs converge in a nest of pressure gauges and manual valves. It opens the box—the lid swings on hinges that have been greased recently, and by something that understood hinges—and it leans inside. You can see only its back now: broad shoulders under a heavy, draggling coat that might once have been canvas, or might be woven from the same tough fibres as the epiphyte mats, long strips of it hanging down like the fronds of the ferns. The coat drips. Everything drips. The figure's head is bare, but you cannot see the colour of its hair, because moss grows in a thick, close nap across its scalp—the same emerald moss that softens the walls, kept short and even, like a gardener's lawn tended over centuries. Tiny pale tuberous things, the mutated watercress from the heat-exchanger beds, root in the moss and trail small white flowers down the back of its neck.

It straightens, closes the box, and turns. Its face is aged in the way the concrete is aged: river-tumbled, the features blunted, the skin a weathered grey-brown that holds the low light without reflecting it. Its eyes are the colour of the mist—a luminous grey-white, with no pupil you can distinguish, as though the fog has colonised the sockets. Its expression is not hostile, not curious, not welcoming. It is the expression of someone who has been doing this work for a very long time and sees no reason to stop.

It raises one hand—the hand that touched the railing—and makes a small gesture, a rotation of the wrist that means, in a language you do not know, *come, if you are coming; stay, if you will stay; it matters little.* Then it turns and walks on, and you follow, because there is nowhere else to go but deeper into the tissue, and at least now you are following something that is following a purpose.

His name—you learn it later, or you infer it from the way he taps his chest once, a single percussive thump that sounds like a pressure-gauge resetting—is Cas. Or something that Cas is the closest your mouth can shape. He does not speak aloud, but he communicates in the way the place communicates: through vibration, through the low subsonic hum that you feel in your sternum, which now you realise is not just engine-throb but something like a carrier wave, a substrate on which meaning can be laid like moss on concrete. When Cas wants you to notice a thing, the hum at that frequency intensifies, and you find your attention drawn to it—a loose bolt on a catwalk joint, a tray of seedlings that wants watering, a pressure-valve that will need replacing in perhaps another decade. When he is amused, which is rarely, the hum modulates in a way that makes the ferns shiver. When he is remembering, which is often, the hum goes very quiet and very low, and you can feel the shape of the memory in your own bones without being able to access its content: a long, slow, cold century of watching the mist rise and fall, the tanks migrate, the robotic arm make its fourteen-second eternity.

Cas is the caretaker. That is the word that arrives when you ask the question, not in language but in a direct conceptual pulse that bypasses your ears: *caretaker*. Not an engineer, not an operator. The machines operate themselves. Cas is more like the one who notices. The one who walks the gantries and the catwalks and the slowly migrating floor-ways, and where a coupling is beginning to fatigue, he tightens it. Where a moss has grown too thick and begun to clog a drain-grate, he scrapes it back with a flat blade of sharpened brass that he wears at his belt, leaving just enough to keep the water spread even. Where a bat has died—it happens, the small dark things have their own small dark lifespans—he collects the body and carries it to one of the composting digesters, which accepts it with a soft wet gasp and returns it to the nutrient cycle within the hour. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is rushed.

He lives, insofar as he lives anywhere, in a corner of the vat-fields where three enormous cylindrical tanks have leaned together over the decades and formed a hollow between them, roofed by the sagging catwalks overhead. The space is dry, by the standards of the plateau: only a fine persistent drizzle, not a drip. A pallet of compressed fibre serves as a bed, covered with a blanket woven from the same glossy liana-vines. There is a heat-exchanger that has been diverted to keep the ambient temperature at perhaps 15 degrees. There is a collection of things that Cas has found and kept, not for utility but for some quality he does not explain: a glass pressure-gauge face, intact, its needle frozen at 2.7 bar; a single orchid that has mutated to a deep, impossible blue and that he tends in a repurposed inspection port; a small polished stone that is not from this place, a piece of granite worn river-smooth, which he turns over in his fingers sometimes while sitting and listening to the hum. That stone is the only evidence you ever see that anywhere else exists.

Cas eats—you were not sure at first if he would, but he does, and he offers you food when the day-cycle dims further and the mist takes on a faint amber tint from the shaft-lights below. The food is mostly the pale tuberous watercress, steamed over a vent-pipe, and a dense, bland cake made from the processed fronds of the epiphyte mats, rich in something like protein and something like carbohydrate and nothing at all like flavour. There is water, filtered through a bed of the moss, which gives it a clean, mineral taste. Cas eats slowly, with the same deliberate, maintenance-interval rhythm he applies to everything. He does not seem to need much. You suspect he eats as much to tend his own internal machinery as to sustain it.

He sleeps, too, or enters a state you interpret as sleep: sitting upright on his pallet, back against the warm flank of a tank, eyes open but the mist in them utterly still, the hum in his sternum dropping to a frequency so low it is almost silence. The moss on his scalp grows visibly while he sleeps—you watch it for an hour, and the new tips are a brighter green, like spring. When he wakes, a tiny dark shrew-like creature is nesting in the moss above his left ear. He reaches up, removes it with a gentleness that does not disturb its sleep, and sets it on a vine-tendril that will carry it to its own small den. The gesture is automatic, part of the maintenance.

He is not young. You cannot guess his age because his age is not measured in years, but in erosion-cycles. The patina on his skin, the blunting of his features, the way he moves through the space with the slow certainty of a conveyor belt—all of it speaks of deep, settled time. He has been here since the mist ceiling was lower. He has been here since the cylindrical tanks were in a different configuration, a different district entirely. He has been here so long that the machines consider him part of themselves: when he passes through the robotic arm's sector, the arm does not pause its fourteen-second cycle, but it does dip slightly, a nod of acknowledgment, and he dips back, a reciprocal gesture, and they both continue. They have been greeting each other this way for decades.

And yet he is not alone, or not entirely. There are others—you sense them before you see them, small interruptions in the hum, moving through distant sectors of the tissue. Cas does not seek them out, but he is aware of them the way he is aware of the pressure in the northern pipeline or the migration rate of the tanks: as variables in a vast, slow, interlocking system. Once, from a high catwalk, you see another figure far across a mist-filled chasm, carrying a coil of something over its shoulder, bare-headed, moss-pelted, moving with the same deliberate amble. It does not acknowledge you. Cas watches it for a moment, and the hum between them carries a brief, dense burst of information—*all clear in the eastern vat-fields, the third-stage compressor is due for bearing-wear in approximately eight hundred cycles, the watercress mutation in sector 14 has stabilised*—and then they turn away from each other and continue. That is all the society there is.

The weirdness of Cas is a mere 0.1. He belongs here so completely that after an hour in his company you stop noticing the moss on his head and the mist in his eyes. He becomes, like everything else, simply a presence in the tissue: a slow, quiet, biodynamic component, maintaining the equilibrium, eating his tuber-cakes, tightening the loose bolts, carrying the dead bats to the digester. Once, you ask him—shaping the question as best you can in the hum, the feelings, the gestural grammar he understands—whether he built this place, or whether he was built by it. He pauses in his walking, and the hum goes very quiet, and the memory that passes through your sternum is old and immense and wordless: a vast cold thing, a long silence, a slow warming, a first drip of condensation, a first spore of moss. He turns his mist-coloured eyes on you, and the expression on his river-tumbled face is not an answer. It is the same rotation of the wrist that means *come, stay, it matters little.*
