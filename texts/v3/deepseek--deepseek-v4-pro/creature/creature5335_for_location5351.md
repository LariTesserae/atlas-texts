The one who walks the moss before the fog knows no name. Among the people of the Rimeplate, names are a currency too precious to spend on a single soul; they are lent by the knot-historians, used for a season, then returned to the collective cord so another might wear them. This one is known, for now, as the Listener Who Keeps the Low Note—or simply, the Listener.

She is small, even by the measure of her people, a compact architecture of tendon and patience shaped by lifetimes of slow moving. Her moss-cloth mantle, layered and oiled with the rendered wax of water-buds, is the color of old lichen, and it sways as she walks with a sound like wind through dry seedpods. Her mask is not the everyday carved wood of the settlement-dwellers. It is a living mask, woven from the aerial roots of a ground-vine trained over decades to embrace a framework of polished rib-bone—bone from what creature, no knot-historian can say. The roots have grown into one another, plaiting a face of interlocking, rust-green strands, with only the faintest depression where her breath mists. Behind it, her eyes are grey as the fog itself, and just as unrevealing.

She moves not as one who walks but as one who is walked. Each footfall is received by the moss before she ever commits to it, the living carpet registering her weight with a micro-shift of moisture. She knows the land’s respiration—when it inhales fog, when it exhales the thin, cold clarity between—and she times her journeys to the rhythm of that breath. Today, it is mid-exhale. The air is glassy and still, the sky a pale, pearlescent dome that feels concave, as if the plateau lies at the bottom of a bowl. The succulents have tracked the unseen sun to their westerly maximum, their faceted leaves glinting like a scatter of abandoned gemstones. In the distance, one of the dead-bloom cairns stands in warning, a three-stone stack casting a shadow that points the wrong way.

The Listener is not alarmed. She knows the dead blooms as she knows the ache in her knuckles, the one that tightens when a reality-crack is near. They are not enemies. They are not even properly dead. They are mouths, of a sort, and mouths may be spoken to—or listened to.

She stops at a pool. Its surface is flat and unmoving, no skin of ice marring its stillness. She does not look into it; she never looks into the pools. Instead, she kneels at its rim, reaching not toward the water but toward the rim of moss that encircles it. The moss here is a deep emerald, velvet and ancient, its individual stems thick as the hairs on a human head. She presses her bare palms—bare in this bitter cold, skin grey-veined with the permanent chill—flat against the moss, and she feels.

Beneath the surface, the moss-hum vibrates. It is a sound below sound, a frequency that travels not through air but through the water-logged matrix of the ground, through the rhizomatic web that binds the Rimeplate into a single, continent-spanning organism—if organism is the word. It hums in long, slow waves, a note that has been sounding since before the mountains were ground to dust. The Listener feels it travel up the bones of her arms, into the hollow of her chest, where it resonates with a second, private hum: her own blood, her own breath, tuned by years of practice to a perfect fourth below the land’s fundamental.

This is her work. Where the knot-historians bind the people’s story, and the stone-layers bind the walls, she binds the pitch. The shamans teach that the Rimeplate is a song that is forgetting itself. The ancient erosion was not just of rock; it was a wearing-away of the chord that holds the world in the key of the real. With every grinding millennium, a note has been lost. The fog brings moisture, but it also brings silence—a muting of the world’s resonance. If the song ends, the plateau will not crumble into chaos. It will simply cease to be a place, and become a memory with no rememberer.

So the Listener listens. And she hums.

Her hum is no louder than breath, but it is pitched to a precise frequency, one that she has spent her life identifying through trial and slow, dangerous experimentation. She has lain for days in the moss, letting it absorb her until her boundaries blurred and her mind began to resonate with the plateau’s own deep time, a state in which she felt the slow tectonic shiver of the planet’s cooling crust, the weight of glaciers that no longer exist, the patient, vegetable hunger of a world without animals. In those states, she learned the missing notes—not as sound, but as a kind of aching absence, a vacuum in her breastbone that could only be filled by sound.

Today, the absence is a void at the edge of C-sharp, one that the moss-hum skates around but never touches. She has known this void for years. It grows. It corresponds, she believes, to a particular dead bloom a week’s walk west, one she has never visited but has felt in the hum-map she carries in her mind. She has sent word via a hermit-runner—a young one, fleet and slightly mad from too much solitude—to the nearest sister Listener, asking her to reinforce the east while she attends to this.

Now she kneels, and she hums the note that is not there.

The moss responds. Not visibly; it does not sway or change color. But the hum beneath her palms deepens, broadens, as if a missing harmonic has suddenly been restored. The air around the pool shivers, and for a moment, the sky above her develops a hairline crack—a thread of chromatic light that runs from horizon to zenith and then vanishes, leaking a sound like a distant glass bell. The pool’s surface ripples, though there is no wind. In that ripple, for the briefest instant, a reflection forms—not of the pale sky, but of a darker sky with two suns, one setting, one rising, and a silhouette that might be her own but taller, thinner, looking back. The Listener does not see this. Her eyes are closed. She is inside the hum, inside the note, inside the becoming-real of a world that is constantly forgetting itself.

When she opens her eyes, the pool is still again, and a skin of ice is forming across its surface—a good sign, a sign of normality restored, if only for a while. She rises, joints protesting, and wipes her hands on her mantle. The fog is due in an hour. She will need to reach the hermitage at the Three-Stones before it blankets the world, rendering distance unreliable. Already, she can feel the air growing heavier, the light thickening.

But before she goes, she does something that no one else in the Rimeplate would dare. She turns toward the dead bloom cairn, the one with the wrong-way shadow, and she listens to it. There are voices there, always, a susurrus that leaks from the small hole that will one day bloom if the bloom is not stabilized. They speak in a language that is not the yodeling tongue of her people, not any tongue of earth or air. She cannot understand the words—they are more like the shapes of words, the ghosts of meaning—but she can feel their intent. It is curious, that intent. Not malevolent. Not loving. Simply fascinated. Something(s) on the other side are peering through the thinning place, just as her people were once fascinated by the pools before they learned to fear them.

She does not fear them. She fears only the final silence, the day when all the notes have been lost and no Listener remains to hum them back. So she raises one hand toward the cairn, palm out, in the gesture of acknowledgment that her people use for the land itself, for the succulents and the moss and the fog—a recognition that everything here is alive, or at least aware. And she turns and walks slowly into the gathering mist, her form dissolving from solid to suggestion to memory, leaving only the hum, still settling into the moss, and the echo of a missing note, now found.
