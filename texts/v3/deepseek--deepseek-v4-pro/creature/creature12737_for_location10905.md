The first thing you notice about the Hinderers is that they are not quite here. Not in the way a stone is here, or a tree. They are smeared across time, their edges soft, as if their bodies are merely the visible portions of a much longer melody. One hangs before you in the luminous mist, tail coiled around a root-strut polished by generations of similar grips, its torso slowly rising and falling with the world’s breath. Its skin is the colour of aged ivory, slick with the same mucoid dew that sweats from the walls, and its face—a gathered, watchful thing—is dominated by eyes the size of cupped palms, black and depthless, pupils dilated to drink in the diffuse glow. Its mouth is a small, sphincter-like purse, perpetually trembling as if tasting the air’s constant vibration. When it howls, the sound does not so much reach you as inhabit you; it enters the sternum and buzzes there, a complex chord that your own bones translate into something almost verbal: a greeting, an identification, a line in an endless song.

This one is an adult, though “adult” here means only the phase that builds and tends. Its history is written on its skin—the fine, spiraling ridges where its juvenile coat was shed, the puckered scar at the base of its spine where it once pupated and from which its dwelling-bladder extruded like a thought made flesh. That bladder, one of many studding the wall like clusters of translucent fruit, is a part of it still; you can see the umbilicus-stem, thick as an arm, pulsing with a slow peristalsis as the Hinderer’s metabolic rhythm exchanges information and nutriment with the parent tissue. The creature’s hands—five-fingered, each digit tipped with a sucker-pad that glistens—are never still. They stroke the root-strut idly, picking at flakes of dried sap, coaxing a trickle of moisture that it absently licks away. Hinderers need no tools because the world is their extension; their very touch can direct the growth of the superorganism, urging a valve to widen, a ledge to extrude, a liana to thicken into a bridge.

Yet this adult was once a howler. Look to the walls, where the chaos is thickest, and you will see them: slender, six-limbed creatures with faces like bruised persimmons, all wet and overripe, their mouths permanently fixed in a yowling O. They are the adolescents, bodies still plastic, skeletons still experimenting with cartilaginous whims. Their extra pair of limbs—mid-torso appendages that will later absorb into the ribcage—flail as they leap and grapple in troupes of twenty or thirty, their howls a cascading, overlapped racket that never resolves. Each call triggers a dozen responses in different vibrational keys, and the sum is a kind of music that the Hinderer adults hear as a nursery rhyme, a raw and unformed version of the language they now speak. An adolescent is all appetite and escape, chasing fliers, fleeing Gulpers, shedding skin in pale, translucent sheets that drift into the floor mat to be digested. When its time comes, it will slow, find a quiet depression in the parent wall, and press its back into the liver-coloured flesh. The pupation is a duet: the Hinderer sings a particular, private frequency, and the wall answers by parting. A bladder buds outward, a new room extruded around the creature as it curls into a fetal ball, its six limbs fusing to five, its face reshaping, its mind flooding with the adult understanding of the world’s voice.

But what of the fliers that the howlers chase? They are everywhere and nowhere, swirling through the central void in murmuring clouds so dense they dim the pearlescent light. Each is a ripple of membrane stretched between elongated ribs, a creature less like a bird and more like a living sail, translucent enough that you can see the spore-soup churning in its tiny gut. Their wingbeats are a subsonic thrum that you feel in your teeth, and their collective movement shapes the air into slow, helical currents. They are the larval Gulpers, gorging on the atmospheric plankton until the moment their bodies tip past some inner threshold. Then they alight—always on a vertical surface, always in a crevice where the wall-flesh folds—and their wings wither, their rib-sails shrinking into nubby adhesive pads, their torsos bloating into pale, shapeless mounds. A larval Gulper becomes an ambush predator by the simple act of sitting still. It inflates its body until its skin is taut, until it is indistinguishable from the surrounding tissue, until the only sign of its existence is a faint, circular seam. Then, when something passes—a howler, a flier, even an unwary Hinderer—the seam gapes, the body collapses around the prey with a sound like a wet slap, and the air ripples. That ripple is not just sound; it is a wave of thickened reality, a transient warp in the local physics that makes nearby lianas sway and Hinderers pause and touch their tails to their throats in what might be a gesture of respect.

The Gulpers themselves are part of the Hinderers’ extended anatomy. The Hinderers do not hunt them, nor they the Hinderers, except when the cycles demand a culling. Every organism in The Gullet is a phase of a single, looping life-cycle, a braid of forms that reincarnate without ever leaving the chamber. The flier you watch spiraling upward may carry the recycled neural tissue of a Hinderer ancestor who fused with the wall centuries ago. The Gulper that just fed on a howler may, in its own eventual dissolution, release a cloud of spores that will settle on a Hinderer bladder and trigger the growth of a new nursery node. Death is not an ending but a molting. When an elder Hinderer’s voice begins to fray—when its howls grow tuneless and its movements slow—it climbs to a place of honor near the ceiling’s leaf-awnings and presses its entire body into the parent flesh. The wall accepts it. The Hinderer’s form softens, its bones decalcify, its skin flows like wax into the existing tissues. For a time, its face may remain as a bas-relief, a recognizable mask in the wall, its mouth still shaping soundless words. Then it becomes a new cluster of sensory nodes, or the cartilaginous frame of a new bladder, or a sweeping liana that will bear fruit for the next generation.

The sound never stops. It is the town square, the law, the history. Hinderers do not gather in councils; they howl, and the howls overlap and interfere, creating standing waves of meaning that hang in the humid air like written law. A dispute over dung-garden territory is resolved not by combat but by a duet, each party modulating its frequency until a harmony emerges that both can feel in their bones as a compromise. The world’s own voice—the deep, geological thrum of its metabolism—is the bass note against which all this is sung, and the Hinderers believe that if they ever stopped, if the chorus ever fell silent, the organism would forget its shape and dissolve, the walls would slump into undifferentiated meat, the light would gutter out. So they keep singing, even in their sleep, their bladders pulsing with a low, continuous hum.

Time is not a river here but a fog. The same drop of water you saw beginning its fall when you entered is still falling now, an hour later, a single globe of luminous liquid suspended in the air, growing fatter and brighter as it descends at the pace of a thought. The Hinderers’ own heartbeats are synchronized to the world-pulse, which takes a geological day to complete a single cycle. They live fast in some ways—the churn of the wall-fauna is a blur—and slow in others, an adult Hinderer spending what feels like an afternoon to ascend twenty feet, tasting the air for changes in spore composition, pausing to touch foreheads with a neighbor extruding from its bladder. Their society is urban in the truest sense: a dense, vertical settlement of individuals who are never alone, whose every excreted breath feeds the communal atmosphere, whose walls are not barriers but continuations of their own bodies.

If you stand in the central emptiness long enough, letting the warmth and damp soak into you, the sound will begin to separate into threads. You will hear the Hinderer mother’s lullaby—a soft, rhythmic popping of her sucker-pads against her bladder’s membrane, a percussive code that tells her unborn pup (still a knot of tissue in the wall) what shape to take. You will hear the warning tones of a Gulper’s inflation, the triumphant chord of a howler that has just escaped one. You will hear, beneath everything, the dreaming mind of the continent-sized organism, a slow, ruminative melody that the Hinderers call the Song of Songs, a music that does not describe the world but is the world. And if you stay longer still, if you let your own bones rattle in sympathy, you might find your mouth shaping a note you did not know you could make, a note perfectly in tune with the universe’s one, deafening note. Then a Hinderer will swing closer, its huge wet eyes meeting yours, and it will pause, and listen, and howl back an answer that vibrates in your chest like a second heartbeat. You have been recognized. The Gullet, for a moment, has included you in its census.
