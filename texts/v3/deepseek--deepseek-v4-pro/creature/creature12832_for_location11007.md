The creatures here have no names but their own, and those names are not sounds. The lantern-keepers—a term given by surface-dwellers who never saw them, only heard rumor of their glow—move through the blue light as if they were thoughts rather than bodies. To watch one for a span of time is to doubt its solidity. Their flesh is not quite opaque; it has a translucence like deep sea jellies, a milky internal radiance that might be a symbiosis with the same crystalline structure that sheathes the walls. In the quiet of the Reach, they are the quietest thing. They do not breathe in any visible cycle, though close observation reveals a faint pulse along the cilia-fan of the head, a slow flutter of sensory threads that taste the air for moisture and motion. Their limbs are jointed oddly, with an extra articulation that makes their gait a smooth, continuous flow rather than a sequence of steps. They leave no marks on the stone, even on the dust of the fungal mats. Perhaps they weigh less than they should; perhaps the crystal light buoys them in some subtle field. They are tall—taller than a person—but their bodies are narrow, almost flat in profile, like living bas-reliefs walking the ancient halls.

They hunt the moths, as you saw, but hunting is a word too urgent for what they do. A keeper will stand motionless for what might be days, the cilia-fan splayed wide to detect the approaching flutter of moth wings. When one drifts near enough, the tendril does not shoot or snap; it simply extends with the same deliberate, fluid motion as their walking, touches the moth, and retreats. The moth is then consumed not by a mouth—there is no mouth visible—but absorbed through the tendril's surface, dissolved into a pale fluid that travels back into the keeper's body in a slow, peristaltic wave. After feeding, the keeper's internal glow brightens slightly, as if it has stoked some internal fire, and it may stand still for another span of time, digesting with geological patience.

The moths themselves are a river of living light. They are not insects in any taxonomic sense, though the word fits their size and flight. Their bodies are flattened ovals, nearly transparent, with a faint network of veins that glows blue when the crystal light strikes them. Their wings are two pairs of thin, undulating membranes that move more like the fins of a cuttlefish than the chitinous flaps of a moth. They produce no sound—no buzz, no hum—because their motion is a laminar flow of air. They smell of nothing, taste of nothing, and when they die, they do not rot but slowly desiccate, their bodies crumbling into a fine, white dust that mingles with the fungal mat and becomes food for it. Their life cycle is bound to the moisture: after the rare times when the dripping water intensifies and pools spread briefly across the floor, the moths gather above these pools and release clouds of minute, drifting spores. Those spores settle on the wet stone and germinate into a new flush of fungal mat, and from that mat, after a slow gestation, new moths coalesce. They are not born; they are formed, crystallized out of the mycelial lace like frost on a window.

And the fungal mat, the silent tapestry beneath everything. It is not a single organism but a community, a sprawling network of hyphae that sense the crystal light and grow toward its most intense patches. In the zones of brightest phosphor, the mat becomes thick and fluffy, an inch deep, and it glows with its own faint, secondary luminescence, a pale green that contrasts with the blue of the walls. Where it grows, it slowly eats the stone, dissolving the calcium carbonate and leaving behind a delicate, sponge-like residue that the water then carries away drop by drop. This is the true engine of the Reach's turbulence: the mat is both architect and demolition crew, building up crusts in one place while reducing walls to sand in another. Keepers sometimes pause over a lush patch of mat and lower their cilia-fan into it, not to feed but perhaps to communicate, or to exchange something—minerals, information, a kind of slow chemical talk that would take hours to complete a single sentence.

There are other entities, less seen. In the pools that form in deep basins—pools that are never more than a hand's breadth deep but are ancient, having held water continuously for millennia—there dwell limpets the size of saucers, with shells of translucent calcite that spiral in perfect logarithmic curves. They graze on the edges of the pools, scraping the stone with a tongue studded with microscopic crystals, and their grazing leaves faint tracery lines that resemble writing. The keepers sometimes visit these pools and stand over them, cilia dipping into the water, as if reading. The limpets glow faintly, each one a tiny crescent of blue light under the water's surface, and they move with an imperceptible slowness that matches the drip, the wearing, the flow of centuries.

In the far distances, where the crystal fails and the cavern falls into absolute dark, there are things that the light does not reveal. They are known only by the reaction of the keepers, who will sometimes turn their fans in that direction and stand very still for a very long time. And then, after a while—an hour, a day—they will resume their walking, as if whatever had troubled them has passed. No sound comes from that dark, but occasionally the moths will all, in unison, shift their drifting paths away from it, as a school of fish avoids a predator. Perhaps there are larger keepers, or something altogether other, that dwell in the unlit deeps and feed on the crystal itself, or on the silence. But here, in the city's scattered neighborhoods of ruin and glow, the visible life continues its ancient cycle, untroubled by haste or waste or want.

To live here is to be a slow thought in a slow mind. The creatures do not adapt; they are the adaptation, a single, integrated metabolism that stretches from the fungal mat's delicate threads to the keeper's translucent skin. They were not created; they accreted, over eons, from the same drip and glow that built the crystal vaults. And if you sit long enough on a fallen block, the blue light seeping into your bones, you might begin to feel it: the tempo of this place, a rhythm so deep it makes your heartbeat sound like a frantic drum. The keepers will pass you without acknowledgment, not out of indifference but because you are a flicker too fast for their perception. By the time they have noticed you, you will have come and gone a hundred times. Only if you could slow yourself—slow your breathing, your thinking, your very chemistry—to match the pace of water on stone, would you become real to them. And then, perhaps, you would see them not as ghosts but as siblings in a shared, wide stillness.
