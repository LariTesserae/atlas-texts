She wakes in the steel shack bolted to the spire, her body curled around a heating pipe that hasn't been warm in a decade. The light through the salt-bleached porthole is the same tarnished silver, but it’s the timbre of the water that tells her the tide: high slack, with the current reversing in—she listens—twenty minutes. She can hear the shift in the groan of the turbine housing forty meters below, the change in pitch of the standing wave against the eastern ridge. This is her clock, more reliable than the cracked digital panel on the wall that blinks 04:16 for the thousandth day.

Her name is whatever the last maintenance log called her, but she’s forgotten the sound of it. The seals call her a series of clicks when they see her silhouette descending the rusted ladder into the entrance pool. She answers with a click of her tongue against the roof of her mouth, a habit she picked up from them. Her hair, once clipped short for hydrodynamics, now drifts around her face in a kelp-brown tangle, and she doesn’t bother to push it back. The cold has long since numbed the exposed skin of her cheeks and hands to a permanent, waxy chill; she wears fingerless gloves of neoprene and a vest stitched from old cable insulation.

There are others like her, scattered across the drowned province at distances that might as well be interstellar. Twice a year, when a rare conjunction of tidal height and current calm aligns, she can make the crossing to the old junction box reef where a man named something else tends a crop of mussels on a hanging rope grid. They trade supplies—a spool of copper wire for a jar of pickled sea lettuce, a salvaged battery for a dozen iron nails she’s hammered from the reinforcing rods of the mooring post. They speak in shouts over the roar, and sometimes, when the wind drops and the water goes glassy for an hour, they don’t speak at all, just sit on the barnacle-crusted concrete and watch the wheeling shearwaters. It is a full and adequate social life.

She dives every day, not out of necessity but because the water has become a second skin, and her small body, when she pulls on the weighted hood and the patched drysuit, feels more like a seal’s than a human’s. She no longer uses a tank; a surface-supplied air hose trails up to a small compressor on her platform, and she has learned to hyperventilate as a dolphin does, storing oxygen in her tissues for minutes at a stretch. She moves through the kelp forest with a kind of lazy grace, using the surge to propel her from holdfast to holdfast, checking the cable tensions on the turbine rotors. The blades turn slowly, even at slack water, harvesting the residual energy of the abyssal currents, and the hum they make is a familiar comfort, a lullaby that resonates through her boat and her bones.

Her dwelling is the repurposed tide-gauge station, a cone of rust-pocked steel and shattered glass that smells of diesel and drying bladderwrack. Inside, every surface is mapped with her small industries: a rack of drying fish fillets, a bench lathe she operates by foot pedal, a library of waterproof notebooks filled with her spidery, salt-blurred script—tables of water temperature, pH, cetacean pod movements, the slow corrosion rate of alloy strut B-7. She is, she supposes, the last custodian of a dataset that began before she was born, though there is no one left to archive it, and the notebooks will eventually dissolve into the food web like everything else. The recording instruments still blink and tick in their sealed housing, consuming the faint trickle of current from her patched solar array, and once a month she cleans the sensor heads as reverently as a priestess polishing an icon.

When night comes—a dimming from silver to deep steel—she lies on her bunk and listens to the true voice of the place. It is never silent: the hydraulic roar is a constant, but within it she can parse the individual threads. The squeal of the anemometer on the next spire, the pop of gas bubbles from a seep down in the chasm, the distant, echoing clang of a loose pipe knocking against rock. Sometimes, when the weather is quiet and the water is flat, the whole basin resonates like the body of a cello, and she hums along, a single low note that harmonizes with the hum of the turbine. She is not lonely, because loneliness requires the memory of an alternative. She is simply here, a soft, warm, thinking polyp in the reef of the world’s backbone, breathing in, breathing out, with the slow, tidal rhythm of a place that will still be heaving and grinding long after her notebooks have turned to compost and her bones have settled into the sediment, another layer of the accretion on the lost mountains.
