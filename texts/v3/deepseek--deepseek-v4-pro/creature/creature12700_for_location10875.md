There is a figure who moves through the Sumpworks with the same unvarying rhythm as the piston’s clank, though you will not see him if you stand still. He belongs to the perimeters, to the gangways where the mesh floor sings a low note under his weight, to the shadowed lee of the settlement tanks where the steam never lifts. Call him a keeper, if you must give him a label, though he keeps nothing in any sense you’d recognize: the pumps need no tending, the valves never seize, the sumps never clog or overflow. The place maintains itself, and he maintains himself within it, and the two agreements are indistinguishable.

He is old, but the kind of old that refuses chronology. His skin has the grey-brown patina of the riveted plate on the boiler house — not through filth but through a gradual exchange of substance, as if the oils and oxides of the iron have crept into his dermis over decades and replaced something softer. Where a sleeve rides up on his forearm, you can see the dark lines of his veins are not blue but a faint coppery green, like the patina on a steam pipe. His hair is long, matted into cords that hang against his back, each strand wrapped in a faint algal film, so that when he turns his head, the movement releases the scent of warm, living water. He wears no shirt, only a pair of trousers cut off at the knee, the fabric so saturated with metallic silt that it holds its shape when he peels them away at night — if you can call it night. The sodium glow never dims, so night is simply the interval when he stops walking and folds himself into a hollow beneath a cable tray, a space where the rats will gather against his flanks for the warmth of his body, and the birds will perch along his outstretched arm, picking at the salt crystals that bloom there from the perpetual sweat.

He was not always like this. There are moments, when a fish jumps in the sump and the splash echoes across the concrete, that something flickers across his face: a tightening around the eyes, a fractional parting of the lips. It is the ghost of a memory, the kind that belongs to a time before the Sumpworks, when the world still had a weather that changed. He does not chase these memories. They rise and fall like the midge columns, and he observes them with the same dispassionate courtesy he offers the monitor lizard when it basks, unblinking, across his path. He and the lizard share a recognition — not of kinship, but of co-tenancy. They are the two apex creatures of this small universe, and there is no rivalry, because their niches do not overlap. The lizard consumes the rats and the sick fish; he consumes something else entirely.

You might think he eats, because his body is lean and functional, the muscles of his legs and back standing out in hard cables. But you would never catch him at it. The birds have learned to bring him offerings — shreds of wire, bright chips of rust, the occasional beetle — and he accepts them gravely, holding each in his palm until his skin seems to absorb them. The rats, too, will nuzzle at his fingers and leave behind, in exchange for his steady warmth, tiny offerings of polished bone or nuggets of some black, brittle substance that crumbles to powder under his gaze. He does not chew or swallow in a way that human anatomy would recognize. Instead, he becomes what is offered to him; the substances disappear into his palm, into the pores of his skin, and the next morning the cable trays overhead are festooned with a fresh crop of those strange, wiry nests, or the sump water glitters with a new bloom of microscopic life that the fish rise to gulp in clouds. He is a conduit, a slow-moving nexus where the detritus of the Sumpworks is siphoned into new form. The equilibrium of the place is not just his doing; it is his breathing, his walking, the steady beat of his heart, which — if you press your ear to his chest during the hours of rest — resonates at precisely the low-frequency hum of the generators.

He has a voice, though he rarely uses it for speech. When he does, it emerges as a series of clicks and hums that perfectly mimic the valve-release cycles, the buzz of the transformer, the syncopated hiss. He speaks to the birds in their own chittering, and they answer him by tilting their heads and mirroring his cadence. To the rats, he produces a high, ultrasonic squeak that sends them scattering and reassembling in new patterns, and in this way he directs their movements through the pipe runs, ensuring no one colony overgrazes the flocculent bounty of a single settling tank. He can call the monitor lizard by tapping two fingers on a hollow stanchion — a sound exactly like the knock of a distant compressor — and the great reptile will come sliding through the rust-green water to lie across his feet, its serrated tail drawing slow patterns in the slime.

He does not sleep in the human sense. During his rest, he becomes a statue, his eyes open and fixed on a single point — a rivet, a gauge, the mirrored surface of a tank. The birds will cover him entirely, a cloak of grey-brown feathers and wiry nests, and the rats will form a moving carpet over his legs, and the midges will settle in a fine, trembling fur on his arms. In this state, he dreams the Sumpworks. He dreams the exact pressure of steam in each pipe, the voltage in each cable, the number of fish eggs clinging to the submerged roots of the pipework, the precise angle of every gravel berm. And when the piston gives a slightly louder clank — the one that marks the end of a cycle, though the cycle has no beginning and no end — he rises, the creatures scattering, and begins again his circuit of the gangways.

He does not think of himself as alive or not-alive. The Sumpworks is a living organ, and he is its slow, deliberate consciousness, the only part of it that can look upon the whole from the narrow vantage of a single set of moving feet. If you were to ask him his name, he would not remember any word that was once spoken to him in a world of weather and season. But if you stand very still on one of the open decks, in the dim orange light, and wait until the rhythm of your own breathing matches the generator hum, he might pause beside you. He will tilt his head, his clouded, garnet-flecked eyes reflecting the sodium lamps like two blind fish, and he will press something into your palm: a shred of wire, a nugget of black brittle substance, a single feather. And in that offering, you will understand everything you need to know about his place here, and yours, and the way a world can persist in a locked, unaging equilibrium simply by being given, and given, and given.
