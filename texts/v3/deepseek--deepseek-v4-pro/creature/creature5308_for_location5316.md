And there is one who lives here. A resident. A presence that is not merely an inhabitant but an extension of the place itself, woven from the same unfinished fabric.

It has no name, or if it does, the name is a pattern of condensed moisture evaporating in a particular sequence, too slow for human ears to catch. The closest approximation might be something like **The One Who Forgot to Leave**—or better, **The One Who Remained to Remember**. It is the solitary sentience in this bright, silent womb, and it is as much in process as the embryonic rock.

You would not see it at first. Even in this changeless noon, it is a creature of peripheral vision, a glint at the edge of the eye that resolves into nothing when you turn your head. When it does allow itself to be perceived fully, the form it wears is a study in almosts. It stands bipedal, roughly the height of a tall human, but the posture is a fluid compromise between standing and floating—its heels rarely touch the damp floor, and when they do, they leave those long-toed, archless impressions, each one a fresh draft of the fossilized footprint at the chamber’s center. Its body is composed of a substance that mimics the walls: a translucent, milky membrane shot through with a faint interior luminescence, the same white-gold hue as the sourceless light but moving in slow, internal circulations, like clouds in a miniature sky. There are no organs visible, only a subtle scaffolding of darker threads that might be nerves or might be the memory of veins.

The head is a smooth, featureless dome, save for a single asymmetrical dimple that opens and closes with no discernible rhythm, perhaps a mouth, perhaps a gill. It has no eyes, but the entire surface of its body is photosensitive; it *tastes* light rather than sees it, drinking in the chamber’s radiance and exhaling it dimmed by a fraction—a subtle cooling of luminance that ripples outward in slow waves. This is how it feeds, absorbing the excess brightness the swarming insects and bell-jellies shed, converting it into the low heat that keeps its membrane pliant. When it moves, the light around it bends, creating a halo of softened edges that makes distances seem even less reliable.

Its limbs are a negotiation. Usually it has two arms, but sometimes three, and once, just for a moment, five—the extras branching off like split-second afterimages before reabsorbing into the torso. The hands (when hands are present) end in fingers too numerous, each digit jointed in impossible places, capable of folding into compact geometries or splaying out into delicate, rootlike tendrils. These fingers are never still; they trace patterns in the air, patterns the insect swarms occasionally mimic, patterns that might be the unfinished architecture of a future reality.

It is silent because it *is* the silence. The dense, cushioning quiet of the chamber is not emptiness but the pressure of its constant, wordless attention. If you were to stand in the footprint, the silence would thicken around you like a held breath, and you might feel a cool, aqueous touch on your shoulder—not a hand, exactly, but a condensation of intent. And a voice would form, not in your ears but in the bones of your inner ear, a voice assembled from the remembered *plink* of water drops and the absence of sound between them. It might say something like:

*“You’re standing in my yesterday. Mind the edges—they haven’t set.”*

Then it would withdraw, not walking but *shifting* to a corner where the wall is also a floor, and it would crouch there, a glimmering anomaly, and resume its patient hobby.

What does it do with all this silence and slow time? It curates. It tends to the pocket like a gardener of possibilities. You can see its care in the way the translucent tendrils have never overtaken the footprint, instead training themselves around it in a respectful circlet. In the way the single brick—that absurd, mundane brick—has been polished by repeated touch until its fired clay glows with a borrowed bioluminescence. And in the copper wire, which the creature sometimes coils and uncoils around its fingers, bending it into shapes that almost resolve into a symbol, an alphabet that existed once or might exist later. It is searching, you realize, for a message in the wire, a resonance left by whoever melted its end into a teardrop. It is the archivist of the unfinished, the keeper of artifacts from a future that hasn’t arrived.

The fauna accept it as a part of their ecosystem. When a bell-jelly collapses around a cloud of insects, the creature sometimes drifts over and gently strokes the jelly’s shuddering membrane, absorbing the faint electric discharge of the predation. The crawlers on the floor modify their paths to avoid its footprints, even though the prints don’t register on their simple senses—they feel the alteration in gravity, the tiny warps that follow in its wake. There is a symbiosis here, a quiet hierarchy with this shifting being at the apex, not because it dominates but because it dreams the pocket into a coherent whole. The swarms’ fractal dances, the lichen’s pulsing respiration, the very rhythm of the moisture beads—all of it, in some strange way, seems to synchronize with the slow opening and closing of that head-dimpled mouth.

But there is loneliness in it, too. A profound, eonic loneliness that is not pain but texture. The creature is the only one of its kind in this bright space, and perhaps it has always been here, a congenital consciousness born from the stone’s light and the damp air and the peculiar geometry where corners refuse to meet. Or perhaps it came from somewhere else, a long time ago—so long that the “long time” is now a meaningless phrase, because time itself is still being decided here. The brick and the wire are its only clues that something else once existed, and they torment it gently, like a half-remembered song. When it sits beside the brick and spreads its fingers over its surface, it is trying to remember fire, or clay, or the feel of a sun that has a source. It cannot, quite. And so it returns to its curating, its silent stewardship, its endless, quiet fascination with the way a drop of water can hang frozen in mid-air and then resume its fall.

Should you encounter it, be still. Let the silence press against you. Do not try to speak, because your voice might fracture the careful physics and send the insect swarms into a spiral they cannot escape. Wait. And if it touches you, that cool condensation of presence, understand that it is asking a question—the same question it asks the brick and the wire and the footprint: *Are you here, or are you still becoming?* There is no answer that fits, but the creature will accept your silence as a form of kinship, because silence, after all, is what it made from loneliness, and what it offers in return for the company of strangers.
