The one who lives here is not a person you’d notice at first, even standing in plain sight. She is as much a feature of the courtyard as the tilted slab or the puddled iridescence, and the place has shaped her until she reads as a living extension of its textures. She is a woman grown into middle years, though that phrase feels wrong—she isn’t grown into anything, but out of it, like the alocasias pushing up from the cracks, deliberate and singular. Her name, if she ever had one, has long since ceased to matter; the sparrows don’t use it, the moss doesn’t ask, and there is no one else here to require it. She is, simply, the Keeper, a word she might have read once on a faded sign and decided fit well enough.

Her body is a catalogue of the place’s own materials. Her skin, exposed where tattered sleeves fall away, is sun-starved pale with undertones of brick dust, a patina of fine grey silt settled into the lines of her palms and the creases of her elbows. She wears layers that have arrived the way the moss arrived—piecemeal, by accretion, each garment found or left behind or simply manifesting from the lost-property bricolage of the city’s forgotten edges. A man’s overcoat, wool worn to cobweb, mottled with damp and stained the grey-green of lichen. Under it, a knitted jumper of indeterminate colour with sleeves unravelling in cords that drag behind her like trailing roots. Heavy canvas trousers cut off mid-shin, held up by a belt of woven electrical wire, the copper cores glinting green at the broken ends. Her feet are bare, the soles callused to a nubbed leather, and between her toes, tiny mats of emerald moss have taken hold, exactly the same moss that reigns across the diagonal on the high wall, as if she carried a grafting of it wherever she stood too long.

Her hair is a thicket of iron and salt, cropped short and uneven, with one lock above her left ear bleached bone-white by some old scar or quirk of metabolism. It sticks out in a spray, stiffened by the same residue that films the puddles, and nesting spiders have strung a few tender filaments of web between its strands, a mobile architecture she never disturbs. Her face is sharp-planed, the bones prominent, jaw and cheek like the broken edges of the concrete—not worn soft, but clean-fractured. Her eyes are the dark gloss of oil on water, iridescent in certain slants of the overcast light, and they hold the same patient, mildly unnerving intelligence as the cat that watches from the shadows. She blinks slowly, with the rhythm of a settling slab.

Where she lives is not a *where* so much as a *how*. She has no bed, no hearth, no hoarded corner. She inhabits the courtyard in its entirety, moving through it with the same logic as the ants following the crack-line. She knows which ledges warm first when the sun angles just so, which pipe holds a residual heat from the factory thudding beyond the wall, which hollow under the loading dock holds the coolest breath for sleeping when summer finally penetrates the chill. She rests curled in the cave of shadow beneath the dock’s raised lip, her back against a rusted bumper plate, a scrap of carpet padding her spine. There is a small cache there: a tin cup she fills from the conduit drip, a broken shard of mirror she never looks into, a collection of rat-gnawed books whose pages have become food for silverfish more than reading, a twist of wire holding a blue jay feather, a desiccated alocasia bloom (a rare event, and she treasures its fleshy, hooded form). She eats what the courtyard provides. There is a fig seedling that has muscled up through a drainage grate, its roots in some forgotten deep earth, and she eats its small, seedy fruits when they swell. She gathers the purslane and chickweed that volunteer in the gravel gaps, adding them to a thin stew she cooks in a can over a tiny fire of scrap lumber, the smoke spiralling up to mix with the damp air. The pigeons, unwary, have learned to fear her patient hands only a little, and occasionally, when the season turns and something must be repaid to the balance, one does not rise from her stone; she plucks it clean, and its bones join the scatter of concrete spalls.

Her days, if they can be called days, follow no schedule but the courtyard’s own. She syncs with the slow respiration of the damp. In the darkest hours, she walks the perimeter, bare feet silent on the moss, touching each alocasia leaf as if taking attendance, pressing a fingertip to the trembling louvre that still breathes with the building’s hybrid lung. She stands for long intervals at the center, where the puddles hold the sky’s weak reflection, and she seems to be listening to the sub-bass thud that travels through the ground, her head tilted, lips parted, as if the pump is delivering a message in a frequency just below language. She murmurs back sometimes, a low, rhythmic syllable that clicks in her throat like starling chatter, and the damselflies stitch their flight patterns around her stationary form.

She is not alone, not in the way that word usually means. The animals accept her as a peculiar, upright animal herself—predator when necessary, fellow tenant always. The rat grooms on its warm pipe unafraid of her passing; the sparrows drop to her feet when she scatters a few dry seeds from the fig’s bounty. The cat, that lean observer, sometimes rubs its flank along her shin with a rumbling purr, and she hunkers down to meet its gaze, and a silent understanding passes between them, a mutual acknowledgment of intelligence in a shared, bounded world. She has no language for it, but she knows the cat is as much a guardian of the courtyard’s weird logic as she is, only in a different shape.

The source of that weirdness is in her, too, a quiet current that lifts the small hairs on a visitor’s arm if they stand too close. She does nothing overt. She merely *persists*. But there is a sense, when you watch her move among the giant leaves, that the shadows don’t lie quite right on her, that the slow time of the courtyard is somehow more concentrated in her vicinity. A tendril of moss uncurls faster when she breathes on it. A puddle’s oil-slick iridescence seems to swirl into patterns that echo the shape of her eye. She is the place’s slow heart, its dreaming intelligence given a body of rags and silt. She didn’t arrive; she was assembled, piece by piece, from the same dampness and rust and green, the courtyard’s own need for a keeper made manifest.

If you were to ask her how long she has been here, she would not understand the question. Time is not a line but a cycle of wet and dry, of leaf-unfurl and leaf-collapse, of the moss’s incremental creep across the brick’s diagonal. She cannot count the winters because the chill never deepens enough to mark a year; the bumblebees visit the same blooms, the conduit drips the same tempo, the tilted slab has not yet fallen. She has a memory of the before—a vague, inorganic harshness of noise, a world that was all city without this slipped stitch—but it feels like a dream she woke from so long ago that the details have faded into the hum. She is now, and here, and she tends the place with the same unspoken intentionality that spaces the alocasias in their solitary majesty, that holds the starling spiral in its perfect form, that keeps the dampness from becoming flood. She is the courtyard’s own consciousness, dispersed and gathered, and when she dies—if such a being can die—the moss will cover her where she lies, and a new shoot will push from the crack of her ribcage, and the pulse will go on without a name.
