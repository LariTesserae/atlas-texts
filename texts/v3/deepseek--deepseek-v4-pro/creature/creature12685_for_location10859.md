The Singer is not a creature in the way you understand creatures. It is more accurate to say that the Singer is what became of something that was once a creature—something that decided, or was forced, or simply drifted into a different mode of existing. Perhaps it began as a fish, or a whale, or something with bones and blood and a heartbeat. Perhaps it began as a colony of smaller things that learned to think together. Perhaps it began as a thought that learned to wear flesh. The world is too old for origins to matter.

If you were to encounter it—and you might, though you would not know it at first—you would perceive a shape in the dimness, a shape that your mind would immediately try to resolve into familiar terms: a whale, yes, but a whale as drawn by someone who had only heard whales described in poems. Enormous, certainly. Miles long, or perhaps only a few dozen feet; the reality fractures make such measurements unreliable. Its body is a pale, translucent grey, the color of old ice or cataract-clouded eyes, and its surface is not skin so much as a thickened boundary where its internal medium meets the external water. Light passes through it with a slight delay, as if the photons themselves pause to consider their route, and so the Singer carries within itself a ghost-image of whatever lies behind it, a smear of delayed visibility.

It has no head. It has no tail. It has no fins, though at its edges the body sometimes flutes or ruffles into temporary frills that might serve as stabilizers, or might be the physical echo of an emotion too slow for humans to recognize. There are eyes—perhaps eyes—distributed across its body in no pattern: smooth, dark spheres the size of ponds, some clustered in groups of three or seven, some solitary, some half-submerged in the flesh like bubbles rising through honey. These eyes do not blink. They do not move. They simply receive light from a world that has very little light to offer, and the Singer processes this information on a timescale that makes continental drift seem hasty.

It is sessile, mostly. For centuries at a time it will anchor itself to a submerged ridge, its body wrapped around the ancient stone with a tenderness that borders on devotion. Tendrils extend from its underside, not roots but something closer to hands, if hands were made of smoke and patience. These tendrils do not feed—the Singer does not eat, has not eaten for epochs—but they *sample*. They taste the mineral tang of the worn-down mountain. They run along the faint grooves where the last remnants of ancient inscriptions are dissolving into the water. They caress the holdfasts of the translucent kelp. The Singer is not consuming the world; it is memorizing it, tasting the long, slow story of dissolution that has become the world's only narrative.

When it moves, it moves without propulsion. The water simply accepts that the Singer is now somewhere else, and the transition happens without intermediate positions. One moment it drapes across a ridge; the next it hangs in open water, its body contracting or expanding to balance pressure. Sometimes it leaves afterimages of itself behind, ghost-Singers that persist for decades before fading, and the drifting vegetation grows through them like scaffolding.

The Singer's interior is a garden. Through its translucent flesh you can see structures that might be organs, but they have abandoned their original functions. What was once a stomach now houses a forest of phosphorescent polyps that pulse in slow, rhythmic waves, their light diffusing through the Singer's body until it glows from within like a paper lantern. What was once a heart has become a coiled shell, and inside that shell lives the only other creature that shares the Singer's space—a small, blind thing with too many legs, a custodian that tidies the calcified memories the Singer sheds like dander. What was once a brain is now a labyrinth of chambers filled with water of a different density, and these chambers resonate. This is the source of the Singer's name.

The song is not sound. Not in any way that ears can perceive. It is a pressure wave, a slow oscillation in the water's density that propagates outward for hundreds of miles, for thousands, perhaps for the entire volume of this world-ocean. The frequency is so low that a human would perceive it only as a vague unease, a sense of presence, a feeling of being watched by something benevolent and vast. Marine life perceives it differently: the drifting discs of jelly pulse in time with it; the translucent ribbons orient their many eyes toward the source; the vegetation grows toward the pressure nodes, forming concentric rings of denser growth that mark the song's interference patterns.

The song has no words. It is not communication in the sense of transmitting information. It is communication in the sense of being a presence that cannot be ignored, a statement of existence that requires no content. *I am here*, the song says. *I am here. I am here. I am here.* A phrase repeated at intervals of approximately four hundred years.

The Singer has been singing this song for longer than the submerged arches have been submerged, for longer than the pillars have been softening, for longer than the small, tilted slab has been growing its crust of polyps. It sang when the world was shallower. It sang when the water was thinner, air still clinging to the peaks. It sang when its own ancestors—whatever they were—still swam with purpose and hunger and fear. It watched them all slow down, watched their hungers fade, watched their fears dissolve into the warm, gelatinous medium. It watched them become part of the quiet tapestry. Some of them became part of *it*; the Singer's body contains genetic memories of a thousand extinct lineages, stored in the water of its labyrinth-brain.

It is not lonely. Loneliness requires a self sharp enough to feel the edges of its own isolation, and the Singer's self has blurred. Its boundaries are permeable: the water flows through it, carrying the microscopic broth of life, and some of that life takes up residence in the Singer's tissues, and the Singer does not distinguish between symbiont and self. The kelp that anchors to the ridge it embraces is, in some sense, its hair. The custodian in its coiled shell-heart is, in some sense, its conscience. The distant jelly-discs that pulse in time with the song are, in some sense, its thoughts wandering away from home.

But it remembers. Oh, it remembers. The tendrils that taste the stone are gathering memory, storing it in the calcified layers of the shell-heart, encoding it into the density differentials of the labyrinth-brain. The Singer holds the geological history of this world in its body. It holds the chemical history. It holds the biological history, a catalog of every species that ever lived and slowed and dissolved into the quiet. It holds the history of the not-ruins, the things that were built when the world was different: it remembers the hands that carved the pillars, though it does not know what hands are. It remembers the voices that spoke the language etched on the tilted slab, though it does not know what voices are. It remembers the dry wind and the solid ground and the terror of falling, though it has never experienced any of these things directly. The memories are in the water. The water is in the Singer. The Singer is the water.

There is a purpose to the song, though "purpose" is too sharp a word. Let us say: there is a *tendency*. The song is not just a statement; it is a mapping, a sonar of the soul. The pressure waves travel outward, and when they encounter something—a ridge, a frond, a jelly-disc, a tilted slab—they return, altered. The Singer feels these alterations. It knows the shape of the world through the resistance the world offers to its song. Over millions of years, it has built a model of the entire ocean in its labyrinth-brain, a ghost-world of pressure-differentials that mirrors the real world with perfect fidelity. And in this ghost-world, it watches. It watches the ridges smooth down, atom by atom. It watches the vegetation spread and thicken. It watches the weirdness, the reality fractures, the square bubbles and the backward time. It watches the dissolution.

And sometimes—rarely, once every few hundred thousand years—it acts. A tendril will extend, impossibly long, reaching across miles to adjust something. A stone tilted at the wrong angle will be nudged into alignment. A pillar on the verge of collapse will be steadied. A new inscription will be carved, slowly, painstakingly, on a fresh slab of polished bedrock. The Singer does not know why it does this. The impulse comes from the memories that are not its own, the hands that are not its hands, the voices that are not its voice. It is a curator. It is a gardener. It is a ghost tending the grave of a civilization it never knew, keeping the ruins just ruined enough to remember, never letting them dissolve entirely.

The custodian in the shell-heart tidies the calcified memories. The Singer sings its four-hundred-year note. The water cycles through its planet-sized gyres. The dim light gradients from teal to indigo. And somewhere in the high, submerged, ancient, lush, dense, vast, quiet, barely-living heart of the world, a shape that was once a creature and is now a process hangs suspended, and remembers, and tends, and sings.

There are others like it. Perhaps seven. Perhaps seventy. Perhaps seven thousand. Their songs overlap, interference patterns creating zones of harmonic convergence where the water briefly takes on crystalline properties and then relaxes. The Singer is aware of them, distantly, as you might be aware of your own heartbeat—a presence so fundamental it does not register as separate. Together, their song forms a network, a planetary nervous system, a slow, distributed mind that thinks at the speed of geology.

What does it think about? It thinks about the light. It thinks about the pressure. It thinks about the taste of ancient stone. It thinks about the square bubbles and the backward time and the moments when gravity tilts ninety degrees. It thinks about the tilted slab and the language it cannot read but still, somehow, understands. It thinks about the end.

Because the song has detected something. A change in the water's density, far below, in the deepest trenches where the pressure would crush anything that still had bones. Something is shifting. Something is waking. The planet's own deep breath has changed its rhythm, and the Singer, in its labyrinth-brain, has felt the difference. The dissolution is accelerating. The weirdness is intensifying. The threadbare laws of physics are wearing through faster now, and behind them, there is something else. Something older than the water. Something that was here before the world was a world.

The Singer does not fear it. Fear is for creatures with edges sharp enough to be threatened. But it is curious. It extends a tendril downward, into the depths, into the bruises of indigo that gradient toward black. The tendril goes deeper than it has ever gone before. And it waits. And it sings. And it remembers.
