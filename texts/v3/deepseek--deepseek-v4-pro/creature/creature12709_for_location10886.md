The people of the mesa are of a piece with their world: compact, deliberate, spare as the phonolite that shapes their home. Generations of life in the green twilight and perpetual, clinging damp have sculpted them. They are small—adults rarely exceed five feet—with lean, sinewy bodies built for vertical movement. Their hands and feet are long, the toes almost prehensile, and their nails grow thick and horn-like from a lifetime of gripping stone and rough euphorbia bark. Skin is the color of aged parchment, with an undertone of grey-green lent not by pigment but by the dense, slow blood that moves beneath an epidermis threaded with photosynthetic symbionts—a lichenous partnership inherited from some ancient, deliberate exposure. They cannot live on light alone, but in the sun-starved forest, even a faint supplementary metabolism staves off hunger between meals. Their hair, if left uncut, falls in pale, wiry mats that trap moisture from the fog, and many braid agave fibers into it, creating stiff crests or hoods that channel condensation toward their mouths.

They move without hurry. A villager ascending a swaying rope-ladder does so with the unhurried rhythm of a climbing vine, each limb finding its purchase before weight shifts. On the ground—what little exposed stone remains—they walk softly, often on the outer edges of their feet, as if wary of crushing the bead-like leaves of the stonecrop. Silence is not a virtue but a habit; they speak infrequently, and when they do, it is in a murmured register that does not compete with the environment’s drone. Their language is a hybrid of half-voiced tones, breath-shapes, and a rich vocabulary of gestures made with the fingers against the palm or thigh. A conversation across a crowded room looks like a pair of weavers passing invisible threads. Children learn to listen before they learn to speak, and their first words are often mimicked phrases from the cricket chorus or the gecko’s chirp—onomatopoeic names for the beings that will be their food and companions.

Sustenance is drawn entirely from the fat plants. The foundation of every meal is a spongy bread made from the pith of mature agave hearts, slow-roasted in covered pits over the course of several days, then pounded into a sweet, fibrous paste and dried on the sun-warmed rocks during the rare hours of direct light. Eaten plain, it tastes faintly of artichoke and minerals. More commonly, it is soaked in the clear ichor tapped from the euphorbia trees—the villagers have perfected a method of incising the bark with stone adzes in such a way that the wound seals within hours, yielding just enough fluid to fill a gourd without harming the tree. The ichor, mildly saline and rich in calcium, forms a broth when combined with roasted crickets (their husks ground to a salty powder), the fleshy petals of night-blooming cereus that twine up the euphorbia trunks, and the occasional gecko, skewered and blackened over a smoldering agave-fiber fire. Fire is precious, fed by dried lichen and dead euphorbia limbs, and is never allowed to rage; cooking is a slow smolder that lasts for days in communal hearths carved into the stone basins.

Their material culture is a celebration of patience. A single basket, woven from the sun-cured fibers of agave leaves, may take a decade to complete, its patterns growing more intricate as the maker’s hands learn to speak the language of the weave. These baskets are not merely containers; they are maps, genealogies, and calendars, the dyed patterns (ochre from lichen, bone-white from ground phonolite dust, black from the soot of burnt euphorbia latex) recording rainfall that never fell, the lineages of planted euphorbias, the phases of a sun they rarely see. Bone needles are polished to a glassy finish and passed down for generations; a single copper blade, hammered from a nugget found in an ancient volcanic seam, is the closest they come to a crown, and it circulates among the households for the most delicate wood-carving tasks, its use governed by unspoken consensus.

Society is woven as tightly as a basket, but without hierarchy. There are no chiefs, no elders’ council—only a constant, low-level negotiation of need and resource that plays out in the placement of a ladder, the sharing of a hearth, the timing of a harvest. Households are fluid; a young adult might drift from one carved trunk-dwelling to another over the course of years, learning the particular rhythms of each tree. The only fixed points are the children, who belong to the mesa as a whole and are passed from lap to lap in the amphitheater during the dusk gathering. Knowledge is transmitted through doing, and a person’s identity is expressed through their craft: the basket-maker, the ichor-tapper, the cricket-singer (a specialist who imitates the drone so perfectly that they can summon a swarm out of the agave crowns), the fog-watcher whose ears detect the first faint hiss of condensation long before the mists roll over the rim.

That gathering at dusk is the axis on which all else turns. As the fog begins its daily ascent, the villagers converge on the natural bowl at the mesa’s heart, moving without signal or summons. They bring nothing but themselves, seating themselves on the polished rock in spots that have been worn smooth by centuries of backsides, each family group slotting into its accustomed place. The crickets are already sawing, the geckos chirping, and as the amber light thickens, a sound emerges from the human throats: a low, wavering hum that matches the drone’s frequency until the boundary between creature and human blurs. It is not a song; it is a tuning. Eyes half-closed, they breathe the resinous air and let the vibration pass through their bones, feeling the mesh of life around them—the slow suck of water from the fog, the minute grinding of lichens on stone, the heartbeat rhythm of the forest’s collective metabolism. There is no doctrine, no prayer, only the recognition that they are one instrument in the larger drone, and that their own thirst, like the plants’ thirst, is a thirst that has never known liquid and therefore has no name. In that hum, they become what they are: creatures of fog and slow time, who have not forgotten water because they never knew it, and who listen, endlessly, to the sound of a world content with its own dry, living pulse.
