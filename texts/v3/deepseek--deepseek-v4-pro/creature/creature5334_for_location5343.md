They call themselves the Tektites, after the little nodules of glassy stone that sometimes form in the wake of hydrothermal vein-collapses—fused, dark, full of trace metals, and shaped by forces far beneath their feet. They are a people of the seep and the steam, chalk-skinned and slim, with a quiet, economical grace that comes from living in a world where a misplaced step could send you into a churning carpet of sharp-chinned grazers or a fissure exhaling a breath of scalding mist. Their bodies have long since adapted to the thin, warm air: their chests are deep but narrow, their blood ripe with hemocyanin analogs that lend their un-oxygenated flesh a faint bluish tinge under the yellow light. That chalky pallor is not merely pallor—the skin secretes a fine, waxy film that condenses moisture from the atmosphere, a bodily trick to reclaim water in a land where it weeps from stone but never falls freely. In dry times, they can go for days by licking their own forearms.

Their faces are angular, with prominent cheekbones and a high-bridged nose that flares to sift the constant mineral dust. The eyes are large, dominated by dark, liquid irises; when the nictitating membrane flicks across, it leaves a momentary silvery sheen, like oil on water. This membrane is not just protection—it filters certain spectra of light, allowing a Tektite to read the heat gradients in a steam plume or detect the subtle UV patterns on a carapace-beast's back that indicate its mood or health. Their ears are small, cupped, and perpetually faintly vibrating to the dominant hums of the city; they can pick out the distress click of a molting grub from three tiers up, or sense a pressure-change in a conduit before a blowout.

Their dress is a pragmatic marriage of industry and the chitin bounty. They wear tight-woven tunics of steamed molt-fiber—flexible as silk, but tougher than boiled leather—layered over aprons or trousers of beaten brass-mesh for foundry work. For everyday life, many prefer cloaks made from the wing-cases of the great shimmer-beetles: translucent, rainbowed, and oil-shedding, they rustle with a sound like distant applause. Their feet are bare or wrapped in thin strips of tanned grazer-hide; the soles are heavily calloused and prehensile, able to grip the slick, warm stone as surely as a carapace-beast's footpad.

But to see a Tektite is nothing without seeing them in the context of the Great Tide. The carapace-beasts are not merely livestock; they are the breathing, scuttling foundation of existence. There are over sixteen recognized domestic species, each with its own guild of tenders, and perhaps hundreds of wild ones that fringe the city's margins. The smallest, the Knuckle-backs, are thumbnail-sized detritivores that swarm the channels eating shed bio-film; they are kept in check by larger, rat-sized Pincer-hares, which children sometimes keep as pets, their claws carefully blunted with caps of recycled brass. The most common beast of burden is the Hearth-sow, a bison-sized, six-legged creature with a domed carapace that radiates metabolic heat like a pot-bellied stove. Its back is a living forge; Tektite smiths will set up anvils directly onto specially-bred flat-shelled individuals, using the beast's own body warmth to keep metals at working temperature. The Hearth-sows' rhythmic panting, which fans their internal furnaces, has become the heartbeat-tempo of the city's ambient sound; work songs are built around it.

The Tektites do not simply use the creatures—they listen to them. The phenomenon of synchrony, the way beasts sometimes move as one mind, is called the *Concordance*. It is most evident during the great Bloom-years, when the bacterial mats flourish and the herbivore swarms explode. In those decades, entire herds of grazers will flow through the streets like a single organism, each turning, stopping, and surging at exactly the same moment, guided by no visible leader. The Tektites have learned to read the Concordance’s approach in the harmonics of the factories—for the city’s steam-whistles, gear-trains, and drop-hammers were deliberately tuned over millennia to resonate with the dominant frequencies of the carapace-beasts’ chittering. It is a form of communication both practical and spiritual. An engineer adjusting a pump’s piston cadence is, in a real sense, joining a conversation with the migrating herd three levels down. The “harmonies” that visitors find unnerving are, to the Tektites, the sound of the world working correctly.

Their society is organized into *Chancels*, which are part guild, part clan, part geological formation. Each Chancel claims a section of the city—a particular set of thermal springs, a series of mold-gardens, a factory tier—and their identity is bound entirely to the long cycles of that place. The Zephyr-Chancel, for example, manages the wind-funneled vent chimneys that power much of the hoisting gear; they are slight, agile people with especially pronounced ear-cupping, and they speak in rapid, high-pitched patois that carries over the roar of their charges. The Deepward-Chancel works the lowest inhabited galleries, where the water is scalding and the carapace-beasts grow bioluminescent sacs; their members are unfailingly pale, with eyes heavily shielded by overgrown membranes, and they move with an eerie silence, communicating through a system of taps on the resonant basalt walls. Marriage between Chancels is a complex transfer of thermal and mechanical obligations; a dowry might include rights to a particular corpse-fall (a spot where carapace-beasts reliably come to die and molt their last shell) or a breeding pair of thermosiphon-fed stills.

Their technology is Industrial, but it breathes. The brass gears are often shaped directly from the mandibles of great tunnel-borers; chitin-steel is not a simple composite but a chemical marriage performed in the acid-baths of specially-bred gastropods. They generate power not just from steam but from the movement of the beasts themselves—walkways are underlaid with piezoelectric-mimicking crystals that have been bioengineered into the city’s rock, and every scuttling leg contributes a trickle to the grid. The Tektites do not see a distinction between their machines and the organisms they serve alongside. Both are “the Living,” and a broken pump is mourned with a small ceremony of oil and rust-prayer just as a dead Hearth-sow is saluted for its heat.

Time moves strangely for them. They do not measure it in days or years but in *Seeps*—the cyclical waxing and waning of the hydrothermal flows. One full Seep is roughly seventy-three standard years, the average span between a bloom and a bust. A Tektite child learns the history of the previous Seep as living memory: “In the Seep of the Shifting Stream, when the Deepward galleries were still young and the mold was thin, the Chancel of the Rusted Chain…” This long view makes them patient and somewhat fatalistic. They do not resist the city’s slow erosion; they participate in it. A sculptor doesn’t carve a statue—she “helps the wind and water find the shape already inside,” and her work might be continued by her great-grandchildren. Buildings are designed not to resist wear but to wear beautifully, the initial facades cut from softer stone so that within a century they will have weathered into precisely the fluted, organic shapes desired.

Their belief system is a form of geothermal animism. They speak of the *Heart-Murmur*, a subsonic throb that one can feel only in the deepest shafts, which they say is the world dreaming. The thermochemical engine is not a machine to them; it is a slow god, and the city is its breathing apparatus. The original builders—whoever carved the ghost-tunnels aligned to extinct migrations—are called the *First-That-Carved*. No Tektite claims direct descent from them; rather, they believe that the First-That-Carved were themselves carapace-beasts of immense size and subtlety, who secreted acid to melt the rock and then retreated to the core to slumber. The tunnels they left are now used by the current fauna, and the Tektites have built their own homes on the ceiling of those ancient passageways, creating a palimpsest of occupation. Priests known as Listeners crouch at certain intersections where the echo of a drop-hammer perfectly mimics the chitter of a long-extinct species. They interpret these echoes as the thoughts of the Carvers, and from them derive the proper timing for new excavations, migrations, and even the seeding of new bacterial strains.

Daily life is a negotiation with the Tide. A typical morning in a middle-tier Chancel might see a tendress leading her string of Hearth-sows down to a mold-garden, their heavy feet making the bridges hum. She walks among them without fear, occasionally patting a carapace rim or plucking a parasitic nipper from a leg joint. The beasts respond to her low, guttural commands, which are more vibration than voice. Children scamper behind, collecting the nutrient-rich frass the sows drop, which will be pressed into fuel cakes. Above, a cableway rigger dangles on a harness, repairing a hoist with tools that were forged from his own ancestor’s reclaimed bone-steel—the Tektites bury their dead in the hydrothermal springs, and the minerals that lacing their skeletons are later mined. He whistles a work-tune that harmonizes with the whine of the pulley, and three streets over, a wild Pincer-hare pauses mid-scavenge, its feelers twitching to the tune, joining the Concordance for a fleeting moment.

In the deeper galleries, a Deepward Molder tends the chemo-synthetic beds, stirring the slime with a rake whose teeth are the actual mandibles of a giant filter-grazer. The bacteria glow a soft, sickly yellow, and the heat is enormous. The Molder’s mask—a beautiful piece of copper filigree and beetle-wing—filters the air and also lets her taste the mat’s health by the subtle changes in humidity. She moves with a slow, ritualized gait because the slightest noise can send a panicked wave through the grazing herd in the adjacent chamber, and a stampede into the hot channels would cause a city-wide famine. Her Chancel has been doing this for eleven Seeps, and she knows that three Seeps ago, an over-bloom caused a bust that starved the subsurface for a decade. She watches the bacterial hues carefully, and if she sees the faintest shift toward virulence, she will trigger a controlled die-off—a religious act as much as an agricultural one, requiring the Chancel to fast and sing the Hush-Laments for an entire season.

Art is functional but pervasive. The Tektites ornament their doorways with moiré patterns scratched into chitin, each line representing a historical seismic tremor. They compose music that is entirely percussive and steam-whistle-based, played on the city’s infrastructure itself; at night, when the factories idle, designated “song-pipes” release programmed bursts of steam that play eternal, evolving compositions—the same sequence might not repeat for a century. Their stories are not heroic but geological. A great epic might tell of a particular calcium-rich vein that took three Seeps to be fully excavated, and the protagonist is the vein itself, whose moods are expressed through the carapace-beasts that fed upon its leachate. In these tales, the Tektites themselves are supporting characters, mere facilitators of the true drama, which is the world’s slow metabolism.

They do not see themselves as masters of their environment. They are symbiotes, as much a part of the Plateau as the biofilm. When they carve a new dwelling, they thank the rock for releasing them. When they harvest a molt, they whisper a name—the beast’s own, as best they can determine it from its vibration-pattern—and they leave the creature a pressed cake of its own frass enriched with copper salts as a gift. This careful, reciprocal economy has maintained Carapace City for epochs beyond the Tektites’ own genetic memory. They know that if they ever broke the Concordance—if they ever forgot that the hum of the machine must match the hum of the living—the city would not fall. It would simply grow silent, and cold, and the beasts would drift away to the deep fissures, and the Tektites would become just another fossil in the weeping rock. So they listen. They always listen.
