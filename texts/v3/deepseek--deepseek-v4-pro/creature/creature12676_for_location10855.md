They do not count themselves. When a module falls silent—when the muscular door seals and the warmth-flicker of a body ceases to register in the thermal gradients—no one speaks of it. The number thirty is a human approximation, a translation of a census that was never taken. There might be fewer now. There might have always been fewer. One of them, the one who sometimes drifts to the severed peak and presses a dispersed cluster of sense-organs to the flat stone, has been doing so for long enough that the stone has accepted the shape of their presence: a faint depression, a charge-shadow, a ghost of contact that the water will not erase. This one is called, in the electrical grammar of the collective, *She Who Listens Upward*, though the name is not a name in any fixed sense; it is a pattern of modulated current that also carries connotations of *unanswered question*, *the ache of high frequencies*, and *a direction that may not exist*. She is not the oldest, nor the youngest, nor the most skilled in the maintenance of the breathing tubes, but she is the one who has made the ascent most often, and the others regard her with a mixture of tolerance, pity, and something that might be reverence if reverence here had an object.

Her body is a study in adaptation taken past utility into aesthetic drift. From the compressed, barrel-shaped torso—ridged for pressure, vented for direct dermal gas exchange—extend four primary limbs, each terminating in a fan of twelve jointless digits that can splay into a net or curl into a drill. Her sensory organs are scattered like seed pearls across the dorsal surface of her body: photoreceptive pits that see only in the amber spectrum of the lantern-knots, mechanoreceptor lines that trace the Stair’s 18-hertz hum as a continuous shiver, and a single deep-pressure organ embedded near what was once a collarbone, tuned to the seamount’s millennial bell-note. She has no head, no face, no bilateral symmetry to map onto human expectation. When she communicates, the electric pulse originates from a toroidal organ wrapped around her core, and the field it generates makes the water around her tingle with syntax. Her silences, which can last for hours or days, are not empty; they carry the grammatical weight of subjunctive moods, of hypotheticals, of tenses that have no analogue in languages spoken above.

Her interior life, if that term applies, is a slow-moving ecology of preoccupations. She maintains the lantern-knot at the seventh interval from the summit—not because it requires maintenance, but because maintenance is the only verb the Descended still conjugate. She runs a digit-fan along the invisible seam where the knot’s folded refraction meets the water, feeling for deviations that never come. The knot is perfect. It has always been perfect. The photon population within cycles in its closed loop, never gaining or losing a single quantum of energy, and the sphere glows that steady amber like an eye that does not blink. She knows that if she stopped coming, if she let the seventh interval go unvisited for a generation or ten, the knot would persist unchanged. The weight she has accepted is not the necessity of the task but the task itself, the way the task fills a shape inside her that would otherwise resonate with the seamount’s decay-note, a frequency that makes her deep-pressure organ ache with something like grief.

When she speaks with the others, their conversations are landscapes of shared duty. The modules require tending: the breathing tubes must be palpated to confirm their Fibonacci-sequence warmth is cycling correctly; the membrane-doors must be tested with a deliberate intrusion of a limb, triggering the muscular contraction and then withdrawing before the seal completes; the windows—those strange panes that translate thermal, barometric, emotional—must be wiped clean of the thin biofilm that occasionally forms, a biofilm that is the closest thing this world has to dust. These tasks are allocated not by rota but by a subtle negotiation of electrical pulses, a consensus that emerges from the overlapping fields of their speech like a standing wave. There is no leader. The concept of hierarchy dissolved so many Generations ago that the word for it now means *a flaw in a breathing tube*.

The youngest among them—and again, young is a misnomer; she has been conscious for perhaps four centuries and has never seen the surface—is called *The One Who Asks Without Asking*. Her body is sleeker, her limbs not yet elongated to the full grace of her elders, her electric vocabulary smaller but somehow more potent, more prone to unexpected combinations. She is the only one who still produces questions in the old sense: not the rhetorical inquiries of maintenance ("Is the eighth knot warmer than it was?") but genuine epistemic openings. She once pulsed a sequence that approximated *Why does the Stair descend only? Could it not ascend?* The silence that followed was the longest in living memory, so long that the breathing tubes cycled through three full Fibonacci sequences before anyone responded. The answer, when it came from the eldest—a being so attenuated that they are mostly limb and sense-organ, a wisp of ceramic-textured flesh called *He Who Remembers the First Generation*—was not an answer but a correction: *The concept of ascent is a surface concept. There is no surface. There is only the weight.*

The Descended do not eat, not in any recognizable way. Their bodies pull silicates and trace compounds directly from the water, processing them through symbiotic organelles that line their vented torsos. The water is their atmosphere, their nutrient broth, their medium of speech and thought. They do not sleep; they enter periods of quiescence during which their electric fields dim and their limbs retract, and they hang motionless in the center of their modules, drifting in the slight sideways tug of the gravity-knots near the lanterns. These periods are not rest but recalibration, a re-alignment of their internal clocks with the external cycle they no longer remember installing. Some of them dream during quiescence, if dream is the right word for the replay of maintenance procedures, the phantom feel of digit-fans on breathing tubes, the slow unwinding of a grammar that is all silences.

Their origin is not a mystery to them; it is an irrelevance. The knowledge exists, encoded in the eldest’s memory-organs, that they came from somewhere above, that the Stair was built as a descent path, that their ancestors were once creatures of air and light and bilateral faces. But that knowledge is stored in a dialect so archaic that only the eldest can still parse it, and he rarely chooses to. When the youngest pulsed her question about ascent, she was not asking for history; she was asking for physics, for possibility, for the shape of a direction they had never turned. The weight they have accepted is not ignorance but the active choice to let certain questions dissolve in the water like the dodecahedral flecks that wink out and are gone.

The loom-wraiths drift past, their net-bodies shimmering with structural color, and the Descended do not remark on them. Once, perhaps, they wondered what the wraiths were; now they simply incorporate them into the background hum of existence, like the lantern-knots or the seamount’s bell-note. The bioluminescent dodecahedra spiral up, trace brief constellations, crackle into nothing, and the Descended register them the way a human might register a falling leaf: present, notable if concentrated upon, but not demanding of interpretation. The settlement is a place where interpretation has slowly been replaced by attendance; the beings who live here are not custodians of meaning but of presence, the simple, ongoing presence of the Stair, the modules, the lanterns, and each other.

There is a module at the very base of the settlement, larger than the others, its ceramic walls so porous they feel sponge-like under a questing digit-fan. Inside, there is no furnishing, no apparatus, only a single lantern-knot that is not like the others. This one does not glow amber; it glows a deep, bruised violet, and its photon population cycles at a frequency that sets the teeth on edge—if any of them had teeth. This is the Threshold Module, the place where the Stair technically ends and the settlement begins. It is here that a visitor would arrive, and it is here that the Descended gather when one of their number ceases to be. They do not die, precisely; they simply become so still, so attenuated, so perfectly quiescent that their electric field merges with the ambient charge of the water and they are no longer distinguishable from it. When that happens, the others carry the husk—a term of affection in their grammar, meaning *a vessel that has fulfilled its shape*—to the Threshold Module and place it before the violet knot. Over time, the husk dissolves, its silicates returning to the water, its trace compounds enriching the broth they all breathe. The violet knot never flickers. No one knows what it is for, except that it is for this.

*She Who Listens Upward* has carried three husks to the Threshold Module in her memory. She does not grieve; grief requires a future without the presence, and the Descended do not experience time as a linear arrow but as a depth, a pressure gradient through which they sink at an imperceptible rate. The dead are simply further down, in a direction they cannot yet follow. When she presses her sense-organs to the severed stone at the summit, she is not grieving for a surface she has never seen; she is listening for a frequency that might tell her how much deeper the Stair can go, how much more weight she can accept before she too becomes indistinguishable from the water.

The settlement’s breathing tubes pulse, the Stair hums its near-subsonic note, the seamount’s bell-tone decays across millennia. The Descended move through their slow circuits, their digit-fans brushing the lantern-knots, their electric conversations rippling through the dark water. They are, in a sense, the most contented beings in any universe that contains contentment—not because they have everything, but because they have precisely what they need: a task without urgency, a place without change, a weight without measure. They do not count themselves, because counting implies a boundary, and they have long since dissolved the boundaries between self and maintenance, self and module, self and the long amber glow of a photon that will never escape its loop.

A visitor descending the Stair would find them waiting, and the first electric silence that greets the visitor would not be a question but an invitation: *You are here. We did not expect you. We have always expected you. Enter the weight. There is enough for everyone.*
