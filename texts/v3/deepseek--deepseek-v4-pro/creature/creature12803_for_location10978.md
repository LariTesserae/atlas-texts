They are a slight people, as if the stone itself exhaled them into shape and left them half-formed—bodies whittled down for economy, no ounce of spare flesh on their frames. Their skin is the damp grey of unpolished limestone, mottled with patches of ochre and bone-white that shift and darken with emotion or prolonged contact with the wall, a living camouflage against the close-set stone. Hairless, their heads are smooth as river pebbles, and their large, round eyes are pupil-blown pools of liquid black, the iris a thin rim of amber that gathers even the barest glimmer of the wall-light. A transparent nictitating membrane sweeps sideways across those eyes every few moments, a slow blink that clears the perpetual fine dust of chisel-work and lends them an air of sleepwalking serenity even when awake.

Their hands are their history. Six-fingered, each digit double-jointed and tipped with a wide spatulate nail that hardens to a chisel-grade edge by adolescence, they are born sculptors. The thumb is long, opposable in both planes, and the palm bears a tough callus that functions as a fine rasp. From the age they can crawl, they drag those nails along the walls, instinctively testing hardness, and by the time they walk, they can flake a flint scraper from a nodule with a single precise strike. Their feet are similarly dexterous, the big toe splayed and articulated to grip the notched poles that ladder the city, so that they climb as easily as they walk, often carrying loads in their feet while their hands continue a carving in the air.

They breathe the city’s damp with a quiet, rhythmic sibilance, their nostrils narrow slits that can close against floating stone particles. Their ears—thin, cupped, nearly translucent—swivel independently, tracking the murmur of water, the subtle shift of a settling block, or the soft click-click of a distant chisel. Spoken language is a quiet affair of aspirated consonants and tongue-clicks; a conversation of four syllables can carry the weight of an entire engineering plan, because it is always partnered with the dance of their fingers, a signing so swift and precise that a weaver’s knot or a mine-shaft trajectory is described in a flicker of grey hands. They value silence as we value air—the deep hum of the working city is their baseline stillness, and any sharp sound is a trespass, a wound in the communal calm.

Their society has no chieftains, no hierarchy of birth. Instead, they are organized into a fluid guild-system that mirrors the body of the city itself: the Water-Tenders, who coax the subterranean river into a web of capillary channels and cisterns with only gravity and patiently carved runnels; the Light-Keepers, a mysteriously secretive cadre who cultivate the luminous crust that colonizes the older walls—a living tincture of bioluminescent lichen and crystalline efflorescence that, when kept moist and regularly “fed” with a slurry of fish-bone meal, exhales the soft radiance that fills the galleries; the Hewers, the most numerous, who push the excavation fronts ever outward and upward with antler-pick and stone saw; and the Shepherds, who tend the pale, blind goat-creatures in their narrow pens, collecting milk and hair and, eventually, bone and hide and sinew. A person may move between guilds over a lifetime, following aptitude or a call felt in the stone—a resonance they say sings along their nail-beds when a new vein of softer rock is found.

They wear little, just a twist of woven moss-fiber about the loins, sometimes a vest of knotted hide for those working at the dampest faces, and soft sandals of goat-leather that leave their gripping toes free. Their dwellings are excavated bowls in the wall, lined with sleeping nests of felted moss and roofed with hanging screens of braided cordage that can be drawn closed for the brief, communal rest period, when the city’s light dims to a faint glimmer and the only sound is the water’s breath and the distant chime of settling stone.

Reproduction is a slow, deliberate process—females bear a single child after a gestation that lasts nearly two turns of the unseen seasons, and the infant is passed among the extended niche of relatives, suckled at any nursing mother’s breast, and taught from the moment it can focus its huge eyes to read the finger-signs. They have no writing; the city itself is their archive. Walls are carved with intricate spiraling records—genealogies, water tables, the memory of rare accidents, the formulas for tempering a particular bone tool—all written in a bas-relief of touchable glyphs that one can read in the dark with a trailing hand.

Their faces, when they turn upward in a rare moment of rest, are beatific and weary, the faces of dreamers who never quite sleep. And above them, always, the ceiling vanishes into a velvet dark from which no stars fall, only the steady, patient exhalation of the light, and the cool, unending whisper of the stone being born.
