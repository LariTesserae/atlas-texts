The people of this high, drowned place are called the Haeli, though they rarely name themselves aloud—sound is a currency spent sparingly, and names are known by the shape of a breath, a gesture in the dimness. They are human, or near enough, but they have been unmade and remade by the air they breathe. The atmosphere’s viscous weight has shaped them into beings of slow, deliberate motion, their bodies elongated and attenuated, as if drawn out like taffy in the persistent humidity. Their limbs are long, their torsos slightly compressed side-to-side, ribs flaring wide to accommodate lungs that must work against the dense, liquid air. Their skin is pale as the undersides of mushrooms, with a faint bluish undertone from the blood that runs cooler than a lowlander’s, and it is uncommonly smooth—hair is a liability in a world where every surface grows something, so they have little, just a fine, colorless down along the forearms and nape that traps a micro-layer of warmth.

Their faces are placid, with large, dark-adapted eyes that catch the bioluminescence with a tapetum lucidum, a glint of milk-blue when a fungal lantern passes close. The pupils are horizontal, almost rectangular, a heritage of some ancient adaptation to scanning the layered mists for the silhouettes of stone-backs or the flicker of moth-wings. Their nostrils are narrow, able to seal against the constant drizzle, and their mouths are small, as if words are a precious exhalation not to be wasted. Their feet are broad, toes long and prehensile, perfect for gripping the woven liana bridges and the slick stone ledges. Their hands are the same—slender, with an extra joint in the fingers, a subtle elongation that makes their grip secure on ropes and tools. They don’t walk so much as flow, each movement a continuous, considered unfurling, like a fern frond uncoiling. Quick motions are startling, almost taboo, a breach of the world’s contract.

The Haeli are born into the damp, and they die into it, and in between they live a life of patient craft and quiet accretion. Childhood is a long, slow thing; infants are carried against the chest in slings of woven moss-fiber, their first years spent constantly touching skin, learning the rhythms of breath and heartbeat in a world where sound is muffled. They learn language in whispers, in the placement of a hand on a throat to feel the vibration, in the subtle sign-talk of fingers against a palm when visibility fails. Their speech is a liquid thing, full of glottal stops and breathy vowels, words that condense in the air and fall soft as mist. There are few abstract nouns; the language is built on texture, on density, on the specific feel of the world. “Water” isn’t one word but twenty, distinguishing the weight of a droplet, the cling of a fog, the sheen of a trickle on stone. They do not ask “why” often, but rather “how” and “what does this become?”

Their culture is built around the act of weaving—not just fibers, but time, community, and the landscape itself. Each individual is a strand in a vast, continuous braid that stretches back to a time when the mists first rose and swallowed the peaks. Their history is not written but knotted into cords, a system of quipu-like records where the type of fiber, the style of knot, the pattern of twists encodes genealogies, events, and the slow migrations of the stone-backs that occasionally shift the city’s foundations. The record-keepers, the *Skein-Tenders*, are elders who have memorized the tactile language, their fingers dancing over the cords in a trance-like state, reading the past in the dark. There is no chief or ruler; decisions are made by consensus in slow, whispered councils that can last for days, the pace deliberate, each opinion woven into the next like strands in a basket. Violence is almost unheard of—not because of any moral purity, but because the atmosphere resists it. A swung fist feels like punching water; a shouted word is swallowed by the wet. Conflict dissipates, condenses, becomes something else: a grudge held as a small, cold stone in the gut, carried for years until it is worn smooth and eventually dropped into a vent and forgotten.

Their dwellings are not static. The city, known as *Pallad*, meaning “the catchment,” is a living machine, constantly adjusted. Houses are built onto the backs of the great stone-backs when possible, their slow, decade-long passage through the settlement tracked as a kind of clock, a calendar marked by the shifting of a doorway. The Haeli have a symbiotic relationship with the slow beasts: they clear parasites from the leathery folds of their necks, harvest the mineral-rich flakes from their shells to temper pottery, and in return, the stone-backs provide mobile foundations and, occasionally, a deep, rumbling vibration that the Haeli believe is a form of song, felt through the feet rather than heard. The creatures are never ridden—that would be an imposition of haste—but they are honored, and when one dies, its shell becomes a revered building, a community hall slowly hollowed and shaped over generations.

Food is a constant, gentle harvest. The Haeli are mycophagists and cultivators of the bioluminescent mycelia. They tend vast, hanging gardens of fungi, grown on slabs of pumice suspended from the bridges, where different humidity levels yield different crops: the soft, bread-like *loom-cap*; the sharp, crystalline *rime-fungus* that tastes of lightning and minerals; the nutty *stratospore* that must be cured in the heat of a vent. They also farm the slime molds, like the one that powers the pulley system, guiding them with pathways of nutrient paste to move treadles or mix compost. Protein comes from the water-snails, harvested by the handful, and from the occasional moth-like creature, whose larvae are rich in fat. There is a ritual of eating—a slow, meditative act, food held in the mouth until it dissolves, flavors unfolding in stages like the landscape itself. Feasts are rare but profound, involving a communal dish made from the fermented sap of a particular tree, a viscous, purple liquid that induces a state of dreamy synesthesia, where one can taste the humming of the cloud-ocean.

The deepest rhythm of their lives is the *Dhar*, the slow, sub-audible vibration that occasionally rises from the mists. The Haeli don’t call it a sound; they call it a “pressure-phrase,” and it is the closest thing they have to a religion. They believe the world is a kind of embryo, still forming, and the hum is its heartbeat or its shaping voice. Their mythology speaks of a time before the cloud-ocean, when the peaks were naked and the sky was a hard, empty blue, but that world was a mistake, a sketch abandoned. The world woke and began to breathe moisture, to swaddle itself in a womb of vapor, to remake the bones of the mountains. The Haeli see themselves as midwives to this process, their slow lives a form of listening. At certain times—when the mists part just enough to glimpse the jagged crowns below, or when a new vent opens with a wet sigh—the entire community will gather on the widest suspension bridges, linking hands, and stand utterly still for hours, breathing in unison, feeling for the Dhar through the soles of their feet. They emit no prayer, no chant; they simply attune, adding their own heartbeats to the slow pulse of the deep.

And yet, they are not without a sense of beauty. Their art is tactile and ephemeral: arrangements of water droplets on a flat stone, coaxed with a hair-fine brush into patterns that evaporate in moments; temporary sculptures carved from a block of cloud-frost that falls rarely from the upper canopy; songs that are more vibration than melody, hummed into the hollow trunks of trees to resonate a low note that lingers in the chest. Their most treasured objects are *rain-glass*—obsidian that has been polished and then etched by centuries of acid condensation into milky, swirling patterns, held to a fungal lantern to scatter blue light into something like a captured sky. These are passed down, not as heirlooms but as companions, each with a name that means something like “the way the light fell on the day my grandmother became water.”

The Haeli are not many; the settlement numbers perhaps a few thousand, scattered across the bridges and clusters of dwellings that etch the face of the submerged peaks. But they are connected by a vast, slow web of rope-bridges and vine-ladders that span the chasms between rock spires, some so long they sag into the mist and vanish, reappearing on the other side as a dark thread. Travelers move with padded feet and patient hearts, carrying messages knotted into cords, or a small colony of a new bioluminescent strain to trade. The Haeli know each other by the rhythm of their steps on a bridge, the particular way they breathe. Strangers are rare, but when one arrives—a person from a distant, sub-merged city, perhaps—they are greeted with a silent, communal gathering, a shared meal of loom-cap, and a slow, careful exchange of touch: hands on cheeks, on chests, feeling the shape of the other’s life. Then, the stranger is woven in, given a strand in the Skein, and the city breathes on.

And sometimes, in the quietest moments, when an elder sits alone on a swaying bridge, fingers reading a history-knot, the mist will part a hundred feet below, and they will see a new shape rising: a vent pushing forth a fresh tongue of stone, glistening and black, already fuzzed with spores. The elder will nod, tie a new knot, and whisper a single word into the damp air, a word that means “welcome, newborn bone.” The world is not done yet, and neither are they.
