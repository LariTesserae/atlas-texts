The crickets are the first to notice when the turbine’s pitch wavers. They don’t *know* it, not the way you’d know a machine is failing, but their chirping slows a fraction, a collective hesitation that ripples across the metal grating. Their song is tied to the thrum of the Parsons turbine—the steady 60-hertz pulse that shivers up through their feet—and any deviation is a kind of weather to them. Weather they’ve never had words for, only bodies.

A cricket in the dark lives by vibration. Her world is a map of trembling surfaces: the warm, low-frequency hum of the concrete island, the sharper shiver of copper pipes, the minute percussions of dripping water on tufa. She has no image of the cavern, no concept of “cathedral” or “steam,” but she navigates a terrain of frequencies the way you’d walk through rooms. Her antennae, three times her body length, brush the air and read it: the cool, still pockets above the grating, the saturated thermals nearer the pools. Taste-smell-sound are a single sense to her, a constant stream of data that paints the world in gradients of heat, moisture, and the chemical signatures of her kind.

They are each other’s landmarks. A cricket’s resting place is never chosen alone; it’s a node in a shifting network of bodies, spaced just so that a neighbor’s leg-flick can be felt through the substrate. When one moves, the information propagates outward like a stone dropped in a still pond—a wave of minute adjustments, of antenna-swivels and sidesteps, that eventually settles into a new equilibrium. The colony is never still, but its movement has the slow, tidal quality of something vast and diffuse. It’s a superorganism without a brain, only a million tiny decision-making units that each follow simple rules: stay near warmth, stay near food, stay near the thrum.

The food is the station itself. Not in the way you’d think—they don’t chew metal, don’t gnaw insulation. The crickets live on the biofilm that coats every warm, wet surface: a slick of bacteria and algae that thrives in the mineral-rich condensate. Their mandibles are gentle scrapers, leaving behind faint, polished trails on the pipes like the grazing marks of snails. They in turn are the geckos’ entire world.

The geckos have eyes. It’s a profound difference, almost unimaginable to the crickets. A gecko sees the dim yellow pools of light, the veils of steam, the silhouettes of catwalks against the gloom. She sees the crickets as a mobile carpet of pale, bite-sized shapes. But she hunts by stillness—a patience so absolute it borders on geological. A gecko on a pipe-joint will not move for six hours. She becomes part of the station’s architecture, her body temperature matched to the iron, her skin blending with the patina of rust and copper sulfate. Her heartbeat slows to a few lazy pulses per minute. Only her eyes move, tracking the nearest cricket’s wandering path, calculating a trajectory that factors in the slight gravitational pull of the warm air rising from the pools.

When she strikes, it’s a single fluid motion: a toe-pad release, a silent fall, a mouth open and shut in the space of a blink. The cricket’s world ends in a sudden compression of darkness, a shift in vibration that the colony around it registers and forgets within moments. The gecko lands softly on the grate, tongues her own eye clean, and climbs back to her joint to resume her vigil. She’ll eat twice more tonight. That’s plenty.

The moss is even slower. *Schistostega*—if that’s what it is—doesn’t hunt, doesn’t sing, doesn’t move. It waits. Its pale green stars are not photosynthetic in the way of sunlight plants; they’ve learned to live on the narrowest sliver of the spectrum, the blueish glow that leaks from the industrial bulbs and reflects off the milky water. Each cell is a tiny lens, funneling that scant light toward a chloroplast that would starve in any normal forest. The moss grows where the condensation is thickest and the light faintest, forming constellations on the underside of ledges, in the crack between a steam pipe and the cavern wall.

Its life cycle is a slow race against calcite. Every day, the steam deposits a microscopic film of minerals on its leaves. If the film gets too thick, the light can’t penetrate, and the moss suffocates under a coffin of its own making. So it grows upward, always upward, at a pace that’s visible only if you come back weeks later—a millimeter here, a new pale-green speck there, a tiny crust of deposited travertine left behind like a fossilized footprint. The fight is invisible but constant. The moss is losing, overall. But “overall” means centuries, and the moss has no sense of time, only the patient, relentless instruction of its cells: *reach for the blue*.

Twice a year, a different kind of life enters the station. The workers descend the spiral stair with their hammers and acid sprays, their carbide lamps swinging and cutting through the steam. They are alien to this place—noisy, upright, radiating a dry, cold purpose. The crickets feel them coming minutes before they arrive: a new vibration, a heavy, arrhythmic tread that sends the colony into a subtle reorganization, bodies shifting away from the stairwell in a slow-motion tide. The geckos melt into crevices. The moss, of course, does nothing.

The workers talk, their voices muffled by the thick air, but the words are irrelevant to the station’s permanent residents. The workers chip away stalactites, scrape mineral crust from the turbine housing, spray acid on the catwalk railings to eat through the calcite glaze. They curse the heat, the crickets that land in their hair, the geckos that skitter over their gloves. They never stay long. In a few hours they’re gone, back up the spiral into a world of sun and seasons, leaving behind the faint chemical tang of their presence and a few new blemishes on the rock. The station breathes out. The crickets reoccupy the cleared spaces. The geckos re-emerge. The moss resumes its infinitesimal climb.

And the turbine spins on, because that’s what it does. It has no mind, but it has a kind of life: a heartbeat of steam pressure and magnetic flux, a voice made of bronze bearings and spinning steel. It’s the oldest inhabitant, the one that all the others have grown around. Its rhythm is the station’s rhythm, the bass note beneath the crickets’ two-tone chirp, the hiss of steam, the *drip, pik, splash*. Together, they make a single, composite creature: a hot, dark, breathing thing, sealed in limestone, building its own bones layer by calcite layer while the small, pale lives of the cavern drift and multiply and devour each other softly in the gloom.

If you stand there long enough—long enough for the crickets to forget your feet aren’t part of the grating, long enough for the geckos to resume their liquid lunges, long enough for a new drip to shimmer at the tip of a pipe and fall—you might start to feel like one of them. Blind. Patient. Warmed through to the marrow. A small, temporary node in a slow, slow circuit that doesn’t know it’s alive.
