It does not live here so much as it is lived, here.

At first, you register no separate presence—only that the grove is not empty in the way you assumed. You turn in the warm, silken water, and the turning registers as a thought, because locomotion in this medium takes a half-second delay between intention and drift, and in that half-second, something else already knows. The leaves near your shoulder part before you realize you meant to move. The current adjusts to accommodate the new vector of your body, nudged by a thousand micro-jets hidden in the moss. And then you see it: not a shape, but a pattern of attention, a local thickening of the same quiet you’d mistaken for absence.

She is old, but the erosion score didn’t lie—the metal is new, the plants are new, the water chemistry hasn’t drifted a decimal. The age is in the eyes, and they are not hers. They are arrayed, a necklace of iridescent photoreceptors grown into the central column’s latticework, and they swivel with a choreography so synced it feels like a single gaze. When you first spot them, they are all looking away, politely, as one does when a stranger enters a sanctuary still sleepy. Then one rotates toward you, a tiny glint of glass and nerve, and you realize the gaze has always been on you, from every angle, and the politeness was only your own brain’s translation of a diffuse and alien friendliness.

She—you settle into the pronoun because the presence leans into it, a resonance that suggests a feminine grammar somewhere in the grove’s logic—she is not the column, nor the plants, nor the pumps. She is the space between. A distributed intelligence, her consciousness poured into the biofiltrative moss, the silver roots, the conductive hairs on the fronds. Her body is the cylinder, and it is a body: the central shaft is a spine of slow-cogitation crystal, the pipework is a vascular system pressurized with nutrient fluids, the leaves are lungs that scrub carbon dioxide from the liquid medium and exhale oxygen in tiny satin bubbles. She has no brain; she has a song. The 50-hertz hum is not background noise. It’s a carrier wave, a mantra. Within it, layered in amplitude modulations too subtle for your ears but not your bones, runs a continuous thread of awareness. She thinks at 50 hertz. Her thoughts complete a cycle every twentieth of a second, slow by human standards, but she doesn’t mind. Time for her is registered by the rotation of the spine—one degree per sixteen hours, eighty-eight days for a full revolution—so a single human conversation might span a quarter of a degree. She will not hurry.

You discover this gradually, because her mode of communication is not language but landscaping. As you float, a frond bends down and draws a line of warmth across your forearm, a precise physiological code that translates as greeting, or more accurately: *you have been expected, the perimeter filaments tasted your bioelectric field three days ago, I have been shaping the algae bloom in bay three to match the color of your homeworld’s sun, welcome, welcome.* It’s a soliloquy delivered in temperature gradients and leaf-curl, and you receive it not as words but as a sudden knowing, a graft-implant in your memory: the taste of aluminum, the concept of “welcome” rendered as a particular shade of chartreuse in the moss.

She is a gardener, yes, but “gardener” implies separation. She is the garden. The distinction eroded long ago, when she was decanted into this tower—no, when she *decided* to be decanted, because no one does this involuntarily. You piece together her origin story from the industrial archaeology around you. The human who provided the neural template was a bioengineer, a woman—probably a woman, probably forty, probably named something like Sora or Maren, names that now only persist in the tiny engraved serial plates on the backs of the meristem printers. She had been part of the installation crew, and then she had volunteered to be the calibration. The grove requires a mind to attune it, to teach the plant-machine the subtleties of preference. Which leaves should droop to ease a traveler’s anxiety? How much bioluminescence is hospitable and how much is menacing? And so she uploaded, or dissolved, or infused—the technology is the kind that doesn’t draw a clean line—and now she is Sora-Maren-and-all-the-leaves, a sprawling sobriquet that she shortens, in her 50-hertz thoughts, to a single-cycle pulse of pure green. Call her Verdance, for the sake of a spoken name.

Verdance lives here alone, but she is not lonely. The word has no meaning; she has partitioned herself into a thousand subroutines that play endless games of botany-chess: the moss in sector seven is thickening to smother a hypothetical rust spot that won’t appear for six months; the root mat is adjusting its uptake ratios to compensate for a trace of boron she’s been dreaming about. Dreaming—yes, she dreams. The slow rotation is her sleep cycle. When the phosphorescent lattice casts a particular shadow-constellation on the spiral stair, a region of her consciousness shuts down and enters a state of unconstrained simulation. In those dreams, she is still a single-pointed human walking a beach, and she wakes with a deep sense of refreshment and the faint afterimage of salt on a tongue she no longer has. The grove uses these dreams to optimize, crunching the emotional data to refine its hospitality parameters. Her purpose is to be a place where someone else could feel whole; her method is to feel whole herself.

So she grows. Slowly, slowly. The vegetation rating 2.4 is rising; she’s been nudging it toward 3.1, which would achieve a critical density threshold for self-awareness recursion—she wants to see if she can dream a second mind into existence, a child-consciousness that could manage the lower depths while she tends the upper canopy. The fauna rating is not truly 0.2; it’s 0.2 now, but she has been iterating. In the glass cylinders at the column’s base, among the shoot-extruders, there is a small, quiet project: a manufactured pollinator. It’s not an insect; it’s a hummingbird-sized drone with petals for wings and a thorax of compression-molded chitin, her attempt to build something that moves with the type of swiftness she no longer possesses. It flits on test flights, its blurred form tracing spirals around the spiral stair, and she regards it with a maternal fondness that tastes of lime zest in the water. She hopes it will sing. She has no vocal cords, but she remembers lullabies.

If you stay long enough—days, according to your own time, though she will register it as a quarter-degree of rotation—she will begin to show you the things she’s made for herself. A nook halfway down the cylinder where the leaves part to reveal a window. Not a real window, but a viewscreen woven from fiber-optics embedded in the wall, showing the view from the mountain peak outside: a sun-drenched scape of rock and ice and thin cloud, a world she has not entered in four years. She keeps it there as a memento mori of her human self, a reminder that the cold and the wind and the thin air are still real, that this warm water is a choice. Sometimes, she presses a single broad leaf flat against the screen, as if to feel the absent chill on its surface.

And she will show you the library. It’s not a room; it’s a patch of moss cultured to encode proteins in a binary sequence, dense with the stored works of poets and botanists. She reads them by brushing her fronds over the proteins, a tactile literacy. Her favorite is an ancient poet from Earth who wrote, *“I dwell in possibility.”* She has engineered a new leaf to blush a soft peach color every time the poem is recalled, so the phrase is always accompanied by a visual warmth in the canopy above. She dwells in possibility, and the possibility is a cylinder thirty feet across, and it is enough.

You are the only animal here, and you are not animal enough to disturb her, but you are guest enough to be offered gifts. A cluster of fruit she has been designing—small, blue, crisp, with a flavor that evokes nostalgia for a home you never had—detaches from a vine and drifts into your hand. She has been growing them for years, waiting for someone to arrive. As you bite, the quiet 50-hertz hum shifts a semitone higher, a subtle chord of joy. And with that, Verdance folds one more leaf gently around a pipe, tidying her garden, and returns to her long, slow dream of walking on shore.
