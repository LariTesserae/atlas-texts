They do not see you. They do not need to. In a world without light, sight is a useless inheritance, and so they discarded it long ago—if ever they possessed it. Instead, they feel you. The floor here is a tympanum, every footfall a drumbeat that ripples out through the stone, through the water, through the very air. They read these reverberations with their whole bodies, with fine hairs that tremor on their skulls, with the sensitive, sucker-like pads of their six-fingered hands. And they ignore you. You are just another body in the chorus, another note in the endless, roaring equilibrium. A drop of water, a falling stone, a breathing trespasser—all the same to them, all absorbed into the frozen now.

They are the Murn. That is what they call themselves, a word that means, in their language of stridulation and pressure-clicks, something like “those-who-listen-to-the-stillness-in-the-noise.” Their language is not spoken; it is made by rubbing the serrated edges of their ribs against a resonant plate in their chests, a sound that cuts through the cacophony like a knife through water. To an outsider, it might sound like the chirp of a cricket filtered through bone, a brief, rhythmic interruption in the wall of sound. But to them, it is conversation, history, song. Each Murn carries a unique timbre, a signature frequency that others can pick out of the din even from a mile away, across the chasm, through the drip and splash and chitter of a million lesser lives.

Physically, they are humanoid in only the loosest sense. They stand as tall as a tall man, but leaner, with limbs that seem jointed the wrong way—knees that bend backward, elbows that swivel in a full circle. Their skin is the color of wet limestone, a mottled grey-white that glistens with the same perpetual condensation that beads on every surface here. They have no hair, no external ears, no nostrils that flare; their faces are smooth and blank except for a wide, lipless mouth and, above it, a broad patch of sensitive tissue that detects the faintest shifts in air pressure and temperature. Two of them might rest their foreheads together and feel each other’s thoughts as a series of puffs and vibrations, a silent intimacy. Their hands have six digits, each tipped with a retractable claw that serves as much for scraping fungi as for chipping at stone. Their feet are similar, but broader, the toes splayed to read the floor’s every whisper.

They move slowly. Not because they are slow creatures, but because speed is meaningless here. Nothing changes. There is no predator that hunts the Murn—they are the apex, if such a term can apply in a place where death and birth are balanced on a knife’s edge. The great centipedes, the leather-winged not-bats, the pale salamanders—all are part of the Murn’s diet, but they harvest only what the equilibrium allows. Every kill is accounted for, every shell cracked open is calculated against the next molt, the next spawning season, though there are no seasons. A Murn who eats too many crabs risks the colony of crabs; a Murn who eats too few risks the overgrowth of fungal spore, which might clog the delicate lung-tissues of the Murn young. They know this not through written law but through a bone-deep intuition, an inherited arithmetic of survival. They are the stewards of the freeze, the keepers of the balance.

Their tools, you saw them: the specific stones left by the pools, the stripped sticks wedged in crevices. Those are Murn artifacts. They do not make them so much as find them, shape them with a few precise strikes, and then abandon them when the task is done. A hammer-stone might be used to crack a mollusk and then left exactly where it fell, because tomorrow a different Murn will come to this pool and need it, and that Murn will know by the placement that it was used, and will use it again, and leave it again. There is tremendous memory in these placements. A scattered constellation of tools across the terraces is, to a Murn, as legible as a library. They read the history of their people in the angles of discarded stones, in the scratches on a boulder from a climbing hook, in the worn smoothness of a path that winds between pools. The cavern is their text, and they are its scribes.

You might not notice them at first, even if they stood beside you. Their stillness is absolute. A Murn can crouch by a pool for hours, one hand suspended above the water, feeling the minute pressure waves of fish schooling below. They can stand against a boulder until the condensation beads on their skin in rivulets, indistinguishable from the stone itself. Their breathing slows to near invisibility, their heart—a single, large chamber that pumps a thick, copper-rich blood—drops to a beat per minute. They become part of the geology. And then, with a motion too swift for the eye to track, they strike. A salamander is scooped from the water, dispatched with a bite to the spine, and eaten in three gulps—head, body, tail. The splash is just one more splash among thousands. Then stillness again.

Murn society, if that’s the word, is as sparse as the cavern itself. They live in small groups of perhaps a dozen, scattered across the terraces, each group occupying a territory defined not by borders but by sound. A Murn can always hear the nearest members of its group as a constellation of familiar rhythms in the noise, a polyphony of chest-songs that tells stories of distance and direction. They gather occasionally, when the rare need arises: to mate, to mourn (though mourning is a strange concept when nothing truly dies that isn’t immediately replaced), to share a particularly rich fungal bloom. But such gatherings are brief. The Murn value the great, echoing emptiness between them as much as they value the few moments of contact. Solitude is the baseline; togetherness is a spice, used sparingly.

What do they think about? It’s hard to say. They have no writing, no art that a surface creature would recognize. But they have memory. Long memory. A Murn lives a very long time—centuries, maybe millennia, though time has little meaning here. And they remember. They remember every tool placement, every shift in the population of the not-bats (though there have been no shifts, ever). They remember the one time, eons ago, when the ceiling groaned and a new boulder crashed down and the echoes lasted for three full cycles of the phosphorescent glow (the glow pulses, very slightly, on a centuries-long period; the Murn can sense it). They remember the feeling of the new boulder’s shape, its intrusion into the acoustic map of their world. And they talk about it still, in their songs, a story told and retold until the sharp edges of the event are worn smooth as a river stone.

They have a concept, too, of the unborn and the dead. Not as spirits, but as absences in the sound. A Murn who dies—and they do die, eventually, though rarely from anything but accident or the slow failure of organs—leaves behind a gap in the chorus. Their familiar rhythm vanishes, and for a long time afterwards, the other Murn will pause in the place where that rhythm used to be heard and send out a searching click. They call this “listening to the silence.” It is a form of remembrance, though without grief. The dead Murn’s tools remain, its favorite fishing pools remain, and eventually a new Murn will be born to another group and will find those tools and learn their history. The newborn will add its own rhythm to the void, and the balance is restored.

Birth is rare. Infrequent. Controlled. A Murn pair, after a lengthy courtship that involves synchronizing their chest-songs until they harmonize perfectly, will produce a single egg—a soft, leathery thing that the mother carries in a pouch on her belly for a year. The egg is incubated by the cavern’s steady cool, and when it hatches, the tiny Murn is already attuned to the noise. Its first breath is a scream that blends into the chorus so smoothly you’d never know it was new. The parents—and the group—dote on it with a fierce, quiet intensity, teaching it the placement of every tool, the feel of every pool’s edge, the taste of every edible creature. The young Murn learns by imitation, by feeling the vibrations of its elders and copying their movements with its own clumsy limbs. Within a decade, it is as still and swift as any adult.

What do they fear? Not much. They have no natural enemies here. But there is one thing: the Silence. Not the small silences of a dead Murn, but a deeper, more terrible possibility. They know, in their ancient memory, that the world above exists. They feel it as a faint, irregular pressure—the rumble of a distant earthquake, the shift of tectonic plates, the ghost of a storm that rages on the surface a mile above. They do not know what the surface is, but they sense its volatility. And they fear that one day the ceiling will crack wider, and the change will come. The noise will falter. The equilibrium will shatter. The pools will drain or flood. The temperature will swing. And the music of their world—that eternal, frozen roar—will fall apart into silence, the true silence, the silence of a dead cave. It is an abstract fear, but it haunts their songs. There are Murn who sit for centuries by the deepest pools, listening not to the life within but to the stone beneath, straining to hear any premonition of doom.

But that doom is not today. Today, the cavern is as it always was. The light glows its sickly blue-green. The water drips. The not-bats chitter and wheel. And the Murn move among them, slow and deliberate, feeling the world through their feet and their chests, speaking in stridulating phrases that say: I am here. You are here. All is as it should be. The equilibrium holds.

One Murn, perhaps the oldest, sits on a bone-white boulder near a particularly deep pool. Its skin is wrinkled in concentric rings around its joints, a sign of great age. It holds in its six-fingered hands a hammer-stone, a perfect oval of black basalt, worn smooth by innumerable hands over countless centuries. It does not use the stone; it simply holds it, fingers reading the microscopic dips and scratches, each one a signature of a Murn long passed. It is reading, in its way, the lineage of its people. A song rumbles from its chest, too low for other Murn to hear at a distance, but picked up by the stone itself, which resonates ever so faintly. The old Murn feels the stone sing back, and in that feedback, it hears the echo of every Murn who ever held this tool. It is not loneliness. It is not company. It is something else entirely—a sense of continuity, of a self that extends back and forward in time, indivisible from the frozen now.

You might walk past this Murn without ever seeing it, your surface eyes blinded by the dark and the noise. But if you stand very still, and let the cacophony wash over you until it becomes a silence of its own, you might feel something: a bass note, a thrum that underlies all the other sounds. That is the Murn song. Not one voice, but thousands, layered over centuries, encoded in the stone and water and air of the cavern. It is the sound of a people who have become, in every sense, the world they live in. They are the voice of the stone. And they will be singing still, unchanging, when your bones are dust and the surface is a forgotten dream.
