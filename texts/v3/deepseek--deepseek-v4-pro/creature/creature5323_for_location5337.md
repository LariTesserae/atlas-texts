The tunnel-rats came here when the drilling stopped, though they were never rats and this was never a tunnel. They called themselves the Gear-Caste, or sometimes, more simply, the People of the Exchange. They were short, thick-limbed creatures, evolved—or perhaps designed—for this compressed, vertical world. Their bodies told the story of the place: shoulders permanently hunched from a life spent beneath low ceilings, hands broad and calloused with fingertips that bore the faint, ridged texture of heat-resistant pads. Their skin was a mottled grey, the color of the basalt walls, and it gleamed slightly in the orange lamplight, perpetually slick with the same condensation that filmed every surface. They did not sweat as humans do; instead, their skin wicked moisture from the air, drinking it. The heat that baked this chamber was to them a neutral warmth, the baseline of existence. Cold, had they ever felt it, would have been a terror.

They moved through the grated web of walkways with the ease of long habitation, their bodies perfectly scaled to the narrow passages. A human would have to turn sideways, would crack an elbow on a pipe or bang a forehead on a junction box. A Gear-Caste simply flowed, ducking under a valve wheel here, stepping over a cable-bundle there, their movements a continuous, fluid negotiation with the dense infrastructure. They wore little—short kilts of woven metal-fiber, tool-belts of braided wire, and, for those who worked the hottest zones, shoulder-capes of reflective foil that crinkled softly as they walked. Jewelry was functional: pendants that were actually heat-gauges, bracelets that clicked with radiation counts. Their faces were broad and flat, with small, deep-set eyes behind thick, nearly translucent lids that filtered the sullen orange glow. Their mouths were thin and lipless, because they did not speak vocally. Over many generations, the constant hum, hiss, and thrum of the machinery had drowned out speech. Instead, they communicated through a system of taps, finger-snaps, and percussive slaps against their own bodies and the metal around them—a language of rhythm that carried through the pipes and grates, a constant, subtle drumming that formed an underlayer to the cavern’s soundscape. To an outsider, it might seem like the random ticking of cooling metal. To them, it was conversation: news of a new condensate pool found in sector seven, a complaint about a sticky valve, a love-tap exchanged between two individuals standing on opposite sides of a hissing steam vent.

Their society was built entirely around maintenance. They did not build the chamber; they had awakened to it, perhaps literally, their earliest memories a collective haze of climbing out of the access shafts when the original builders departed. Those creators were long gone, their identity reduced to a mythic concept: the Architects, beings of impossible height who had carved the rock and forged the iron and then vanished, leaving the machines running. The Gear-Caste saw themselves as inheritors, custodians of a process they did not fully understand but could intuit. Their entire economy, their spirituality, their social hierarchy, revolved around the Exchange—the endless cycling of heat, condensate, ingots, and exhaust that defined the chamber's metabolism.

Each individual was apprenticed young to a Component. A Component was a piece of the larger machine-system: a particular pump, a section of piping, a bank of gauges, the smelter's drop-off chute. The apprenticeship was a lifelong bond. Youngsters learned the nuances of their Component—the specific frequency of its hum when healthy, the smell of its insulation when it was about to fail, the pattern of its vibration through the floor-grates. They grew to love their Components, in a way that was not sentimentality but a deep, symbiotic identification. When a Component failed and could not be repaired—which happened rarely, for the original construction was robust—the attendant might enter a state of profound withdrawal, sometimes ceasing to eat the pale ferns and fibrous moss, simply stopping, until they themselves were recycled into the nutrient slurry that fed the plants. Death and malfunction were met with the same ritual: the body or the broken part was dismantled, its constituent materials tagged and sent on the little automated rail-carts to the vault. The vault—an immense, locked chamber beyond the smelter—was their holy of holies. No one entered. They only sent things in. They believed, with a faith born of cold logic, that nothing was ever lost; it was merely placed into the Exchange, to re-emerge later in the correct form. Fresh ingots came from the drop-off chute. The carts returned empty. The cycle was proven.

They had no art, or so they would have said. But the spiraling patterns of conduit around a blank wall? That was the work of a Pipe-Caste member named Click-Three-Tap, who had, over many years, re-routed minor lines during maintenance cycles to create a visual rhythm that pleased something deep in their chest. It was a devotional act, a way of making visible the harmony they perceived in the machine. The equidistant clusters of ferns were the project of a communal group, the Condensate Tenders, who had spent generations carefully transplanting spores to favorable spots, creating a reliable crop. Their grid was not imposed; it emerged from the optimal distribution of moisture gradients and light from the lamps. To them, it was as natural as a forest's edge. The staircases that doubled back on themselves had a purpose understood only by the eldest, those who had achieved a state called Synchrony. In Synchrony, an individual ceased to tend a single Component and instead wandered freely, sensing imbalances in the overall system. Those staircases, the elders knew, were not illogical. They simply integrated the vertical with the horizontal in a way that aligned with an underlying grid the Gear-Caste could feel but never see—a kind of ley-line of pressure and vibration. Walking them was a form of meditation.

Their strangeness was not an alienness of biology, but of perspective. They did not see themselves as living in a place; they saw themselves and the place as a single, reciprocating entity. The rhythmic oscillation in the floor was the world's heartbeat. The hiss of steam was the world's breath. And they, the Gear-Caste, were the world's hands, its nerve-endings, its self-awareness. When they tapped a rhythm on a pipe to ask a question, they believed the pipe itself heard, and would answer through the subtle change in its vibration that only trained fingertips could detect. This was not superstition. It was a form of sensitivity so refined that the boundary between a built object and a living system had become, for them, meaningless. The few plants were not a separate life-form, but a slow-moving organ of the chamber, breathing out a little freshness in exchange for the heat and the minerals in the condensate. The Gear-Caste themselves were just a faster-moving organ, a more complex feedback loop.

No animal life had ever emerged here because none was needed. The Gear-Caste were the fauna. Their score of 0.1 was their own reflection, a single species blurring the line between organism and mechanism inside a young, sharp-edged world that they were slowly, tenderly, turning into a home. They did not wait for anything. They were fully occupied with the ongoing, moment-by-moment act of keeping the Exchange in balance, singing to each other through the pipes in a language of clicks and hums, their short, stooped bodies moving ceaselessly through the perpetual twilight, mending, adjusting, listening to the deep and constant thrum of the world-being that held them.
