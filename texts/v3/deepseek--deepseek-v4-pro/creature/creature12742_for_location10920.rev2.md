Ask the Hollow who lives here, and at first it answers with a catalogue of quiet bodies: the grazers, all soft angles and deliberate steps, moving from lichen to lichen on the woven cables; the ribbon-predator, a flat and jointed absence of sound that bridges the void in one fluid, looping motion; the leathery-winged gliders that navigate the fog by some sense you do not possess, silent as petals falling. These are the obvious tenants, the ones that fill the ecological niches of this endless, constructed forest. They are born here, they feed, they die muffled deaths, and their bones—when they fall—are slowly incorporated into the Floor of Lamentations by the patient appetite of the living wood. In a sense, they are part of the place. But they do not *inhabit* it in the way a mind inhabits a home. They are more like the blood cells moving through a body, unaware of the whole.

So you look deeper. You sit at the edge of that abandoned dwelling—the tools of bone and volcanic glass still waiting, the water-basin perpetually full from the ever-condensing air—and you let the quiet soak into you until your own heartbeat sounds like a trespass, a violation of the ancient compact. And then, if you are patient, if you allow your eyes to unfocus and your ears to listen not for sound but for the shape of the silence, you begin to perceive the other residents. They are not easily seen, because they do not move through the world; the world moves through them. The Tenders are still here.

At first they are only a shift in the fog, a pattern of moisture that holds a posture just a little too long at the edge of vision. You remember the description: *tall, weaving forms, hands of twig and leaf, the old tenders still at their work.* But they are not ghosts, and they are not illusions. They are the weavers who built the Hollow, or what they have become. They do not have bodies of flesh; they have bodies of the same ligneous substance as the bridges and the pillars. Their forms are tall, often seven or eight feet, with limbs that are bundles of trained vine, ribcages of bentwood, faces that are masks of knotholes and the feathery edges of leaves—faces that shift with the seasons, sprouting new foliage or shedding it in a slow, continuous expression. They move like plants move: too slow for your impatient animal eye to catch the motion itself, but you see the result. A new strand bridging a gap that wasn’t bridged before. A wall that has thickened. A tendril near your hand that curls, slowly, around the grip of the bone-axe, and by morning will have incorporated the tool into its flesh, another seam in the endless weaving. That is a Tender working. Its work is indistinguishable from growth.

When you finally see one fully, it is because it allows you. Perhaps you have fallen into a reverie, half-dreaming in the perpetual twilight, and when you open your eyes it is there, standing on the pathway before you. Its feet—if they can be called feet—are a splay of roots that merge seamlessly with the Floor of Lamentations. It does not acknowledge you with words; it has no mouth you can recognize, only a fissure in the bark that might open and close with the humidity, exhaling a faint, sweet-smelling vapour. Instead, it communicates through the Floor itself. The woven fibres beneath you vibrate in a slow, complex rhythm, a language of tension and release. You understand it not as words but as a knowing that enters through your soles, up your bones, into the base of your skull: *You are a visitor. You are loud with heat and salt. You are welcome to rest, but do not break the weave.*

This Tender is not alone. They are a network, a mycelium of intelligences that span the Hollow. Each one is a node, a concentration of awareness, but their minds flow through the entire structure—through every root and cordage and weeping trunk. The emptiness you felt was simply your inability to recognize a society that has no gatherings, no tools, no possessions, because they have become all of those things. Their dwellings are not the empty cavities you found; those were the nests of their former selves, the chrysalises they left behind when they molted into the wood. Now their home is the entire Hollow. They live in every fibre.

Their life is a slow, endless gardening. They tend the pathways not because they need to travel—they are the pathways—but because tending is what they are. They guide the water, they prune the overgrowth, they hum the low thrum that encourages the cells to divide in the correct patterns. They are the inherited tropism that holds the ancient forms, but it is not blind; it is a conscious, loving attention that has persisted for epochs beyond memory. They remember, in their diffuse way, the original builders—their own ancestors, the ones who worked with stone tools and patient devotion—but they no longer distinguish between that past and the present. Time for them is a cycle of wet and less wet, of growth and shedding, of the slow incorporation of every fallen thing into the continuous weave. They do not mourn the empty dwellings, because they never left; they only thickened into the walls.

There are other beings too, less common but more startling. The Dreamers: Tenders who have let their bodies dissolve entirely into fog and thermal whorl. They are the weather-makers, the ones who stir the clouds with their exhalations and fold the rain into sideways paths. You cannot see them at all, but when a mass of vapour thickens and spirals upward—rain falling up into the grey—that is a Dreamer turning in its sleep, dreaming the weather into shape. And the Roots: ancient Tenders that have sunk so deep into the abyss that they have become the foundation. No one, not even the other Tenders, knows how far down they go. Their slow thoughts are the gravity that holds the Hollow together, the bass note of the whole humming world. Sometimes, in the deepest quiet, you can feel their pulse: a single, planetary heartbeat swallowed by moss.

When you finally rise and walk again, you see the Hollow differently. The emptiness is an illusion; the place is densely inhabited. Every pulse in the weave is a conversation. Every droplet that runs along a fibre is a finger tracing a thought. The silence is not an absence of voice but a fullness of voices pitched outside your hearing, a chorus of capillary action and cell division. The Tenders are all around you, in you—the air you drink is saturated with their respiration, the warm nursery-air that feels like drinking. You are walking inside the body of a people who have become a world, a world that is a single, continuous organism given intent, and the intent has become indistinguishable from nature.

And as you leave the dwelling, stepping back onto the springy, meticulous weave of the Floor of Lamentations, you notice something new. A tendril has, very gently, woven a small pattern into the cuff of your sleeve—a spiral, a knot, a signature in pale fibre. It is a gift. A reminder that you were here, and that here is not empty, but only waiting, with the patience of wood, to be recognized.
