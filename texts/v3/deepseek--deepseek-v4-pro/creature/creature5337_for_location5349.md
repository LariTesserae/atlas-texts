There is a life here, though it barely qualifies as animal. It is older than the moss, older than the ferns, perhaps older even than the ridges that hold the cloud. You do not see it so much as sense a shift in the texture of the world—a thinning of the mist in a shape that moves, a pressure that redistributes the weight of the wet air. The first time, you will think it is only an eddy of fog, a trick of the grey light. But then the moss under your feet will tremble, just once, a long seismic sigh, and the sweet smell of wet silk and disturbed earth will uncurl around you, and you will know that you are not alone.

What lives here is a creature of the canopy, though it did not always live there. It is vast, and it is soft, and it is patient beyond any measure you have brought with you. Its body is a ribbon of undifferentiated twilight, the color of the cloud at its densest, slung low over the matted ferns. It has no legs, no spines, no scales, no distinction between head and tail, only a long sequential flow of self that can stretch for the length of a fallen tree or gather into a pile ten feet across, like a drift of living silk. The skin of it is the source of the smell—a membrane so fine and vascular that it breathes the fog directly, wicking moisture and nutrients out of the air even as it exhales a faint, sweet musk that the mosses seem to lean toward. When the bioluminescence pulses beneath you, you might see its outline blush in response: a pale, traveling glow that ripples the length of it as if the whole being were only a thought of light.

It moves in a glide, a wordless sliding that does not push against the world but rather negotiates with it. First the skin tenses, gathering itself into a wave of contraction so slow that your eye cannot track it; you can only register that what was still is now elsewhere, arriving a little further along the interlocked branches, a little closer to the steam plume where the air has extra warmth. The moss and lichen part for it, not crushed but gently displaced, and they rise again in its wake with the slow elastic memory of a compressed sponge. It has no feet, but the underside of it is tacky with a secretion that is neither slime nor adhesive, something between a spider’s silk and a snail’s comfort, so that it can hang suspended from the underside of the horizontal tree-net, or pour itself over the sharpest basalt fin without catching its skin. The sharp rock could never wound it; the skin simply indents and then, an hour later, is smooth again.

What does it eat? Not the ferns, not the moss. You might notice that in the hours after it passes, the air tastes a little cleaner, a little less charged. It consumes the mineral exhalations of the fumaroles, the sulphur tang, the iron bloom; it breathes the chemical potential out of the steam and fixes it into its own tissues. It is, in a sense, a living scrubber of the volcanic breath, a slow chemotroph that draws its energy not from sunlight but from the gradient between the hot rock and the cool mist. The bioluminescent pinpoints on the fern leaves grow brighter after it has passed, because it leaves behind a microfilm of waste—a gentle phosphate dusting that the plants have learned to harvest. The whole high world is a gut, and this creature is its slow, digesting soul.

It has been here since the first cooling of the last lava. The rock formations that mimic ribs and vertebrae are not its bones; they are the mold of its rest. There is a thing it does, perhaps once a century, when the sky deepens to an almost-night and the thermal vents pulse in a rhythm that seems to call it. It descends. It leaves the canopy and pours itself down through a crevice, a long silent fall of flesh, until it reaches the raw stone. There it curls into a spiral and grows still, pressing its body against the porous basalt, and the heat of the new rock meets the heat of its ancient chemistry. In that contact, it secretes a fluid that is not acid but something more patient—a patterning agent that microscopically re-orders the silica lattice, leaving behind a ghost of its own coiled form in the stone. Over a century, the stone remembers the shape. The creature moves on, but the mountain holds the stillness. This is why the lava seems to dream of life: because life has been dreaming into it, breath by slow breath, for a hundred thousand years.

Sometimes, you may see it raise a portion of itself into the mist. This is as close as it comes to a head, though the gesture is not fixed; any part of the ribbon might loop up and hover, tasting the air with a fringe of delicate, unfurling filaments that look like nothing so much as the fiddleheads of young ferns. These are its senses—moisture-chemosensitive, vibration-attuned, and more recently, perhaps, aware of you. It does not watch with eyes, but it feels the displacement you make in the warm fog, the tiny disturbance of your breathing, the heart-sounds that travel through the moss. It feels you as you feel it: an immense, slow presence just outside the categories you brought. It is not afraid. It has never known anything in this world to be fast enough to threaten it, any change abrupt enough to startle. Its only response to novelty is patience, the confident assumption that whatever moves quickly will soon tire, will sink into the moss, will become part of the slow cycle of the high drowned place.

If you stand still long enough—really still, until the condensation beads on your skin and your own pulse sounds loud in your ears—the creature might forget you are a separate thing. The trembling moss will still. The sweet silk smell will thicken, and then, with a deliberation like the tide, the great ribbon of cloud-colored flesh will begin to move toward you. Not to attack, not to investigate, but simply to continue its route, which may have been set in motion before your grandparents were born. It will pass within an arm’s reach, and you will see the detail of its skin: not smooth at all, but a nap of microscopic filaments each beaded with a droplet, exactly like the moss but finer, and woven with its own dim pallor of captive starlight. You will feel the warmth of it, a few degrees above the ambient damp, and the sound of its movement will be the faintest whisper, like a hand smoothing velvet in the dark. And then it will be past, and the mist will swallow it, and you will be left with nothing but the memory of a drift in the grey, and the knowledge that the landscape’s dreaming is not a metaphor but a fact, a continuous, fleshy, breathing fact that has outlived the sharpness of the rock and will outlive you by a span of time you have no instrument to measure.
