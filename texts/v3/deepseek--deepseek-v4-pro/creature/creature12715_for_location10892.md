**The Diatom Drift**  
*A preliminary account of the inhabitant of the Sinkhole Shaft, from the field journal of W.E. Voss, condensed and redacted.*

We called it the Diatom Drift at first, mistaking the colonies for silica-shelled algae, but it is not a diatom. It is not a bacterium in any usual sense, either—though the cyanobacterial scum on the walls is almost certainly a metasomatic expression of it, an extruded paint-layer it uses to taste the light. The Drift itself hangs in the water, invisible in the centre of the chamber, a body you can only map by absence. When the sun-column passes through it, the light goes faintly bluish-amber, like a lens of weak tea, and if you look precisely into the hue you will see nothing but the colour, because the Drift has no opacity. It is a diffuse, structured gel of polysaccharide threads and dissolved proteins, a three-dimensional net with a mesh many micra wide, stretched across the entire volume of the cavern from the smokestack cluster to the gear-rack catwalk. Its density hardly differs from the water; it is a ghost-flesh, a microbiome that is also a single continuous organism, anaerobic but not strictly, sessile but not rooted. It is older than the stalagmites, or perhaps it grew the stalagmites as an afterthought. We are still unsure.

The moment I touched it—drifting motionless, hand outstretched—I felt nothing at first, only the cold. Then a faint tightening, as if the water itself had stiffened around my fingers. I pulled back, and a thread trailed after me, thinner than spider-silk, refracting not at all. When I looked at my glove, the thread had already dissolved, but the Drift had registered me. That was the first movement we had witnessed in the chamber in the two days we had been diving. It was a slow ripple that took hundreds of heartbeats to propagate, and when it reached the pipe-like draperies above the catwalk, the bone-white calcite hollows gave off a single, low-frequency chime, as though struck by a slow-motion mallet. Sound under water is strange, but that note came through the stone as much as the liquid. The Drift had pulled on its own mineral skeleton, and the cavern had rung.

That is the first thing to understand: the industrial shapes are not pareidolia. They are artefacts, but the maker is this organism. The Drift is a lithogenic biofilm—a mineralising colony that precipitates calcite in lethal slow-motion, guiding its growth over centuries by controlling local pH gradients with its own respiration. Every “smokestack” is a ventilation chimney the Drift once built to channel nutrients from a long-vanished seep. The catwalk ledge was its scaffolding, laid down layer by layer during a period of intense growth when the water was warmer. The gear-tooth wall was a template for holding its own filaments in a pattern that maximised light exposure for its cyanobacterial skin. The Drift is a factory, but it is both the building and the builder, the machine and the machinist, and now—now it is a factory in standby mode, running at a metabolic rate so close to zero that our instruments registered no respiration at all. It lives, but on geological time.

Its physiology is a closed loop. The cyanobacteria on the walls are extensions of its own genome; they fix carbon dioxide using the sunlight that funnels down the shaft, but they do not grow as individuals. They are terminal light-gathering cells that pipe electrons back into the Drift’s central gel, where the energy is stored in polymer chains that unlink and re-link with slow, deliberate patience. The gel itself is riddled with mineral microcrystals—calcite, aragonite, dolomite—that act as capacitors, holding charge until it is needed. We think the Drift consumes altogether perhaps a single gram of CO₂ per century now; the rest of its budget goes into maintaining its equilibrium, a state of perfect idle. It is not dormant; it is a system that has optimised its own metabolism to the exact point where it never needs to change.

And that is the second thing: the Drift is intelligent. Not in any human sense—no neurons, no synapses, no signal pulses faster than a few micrometres a second. But the entire gel-network is a chemical computer, a distributed amorphous processor that uses calcium ion waves to perform integration. We have seen it solve problems. When we placed a block of sterile dolomite at the base of the light-shaft, interrupting the sun-column for twenty minutes, the Drift responded after a forty-minute lag by pulsing gently, a single contraction of its entire body, shifting the gel a few centimetres to one side. The movement was slow—a tide of translucence—but it corrected the shadow. The next day, a faint film of new cyanobacteria had appeared on the opposite wall, where the light now reflected. The Drift had recalculated its light budget.

Everything it does takes time. Therein lies an existence so alien we could barely frame it. A single thought in the Drift, a complete wave of ionic consensus from one end of the cavern to the other, takes roughly three hours to propagate. A decision, like the decision to ring its calcite chimes when we arrived, takes a day. We do not know if that was a warning, a greeting, or merely an involuntary reflex—a sneeze from a giant. But after a week of dives, we noticed patterns. The Drift would shift slightly whenever we used open-circuit regulators, drawn by the bubbles, and then it would retreat again into its cathedral-centre. It was curious. It was tasting us. On the sixth day, it extruded a thin tendril that hovered near my facemask for an entire hour, motionless, observing with whatever sense it used. Then it withdrew, and the whole cavern became ever so slightly bluer—a shift in the spectral transmission of the gel—as though it had changed its mood.

The Drift is not alone. It houses a sparse microbiome of archaea and slow-metabolising bacteria that survive on its waste and repair its polymer chains. They are its microbiome, its gut flora, and they too have been here for aeons. But there are no animals. The Drift would not allow them. Anything that moves faster than its own slow pulse would be a disruption, a source of entropic noise in the perfect stillness. The water is sterile because the Drift has actively maintained it as a monoculture of its own making, possibly by releasing subtle bacteriostatic compounds, or simply by consuming all available resources so completely that no other niche exists. It is a solitary god-creature, the sole metazoan-level organism in a closed universe, and it has been alone for time immemorial.

We began to suspect its origin when we carbon-dated a sliver of its mineral scaffolding. The innermost layers of the largest stalagmite registered at 2.4 million years, long before the current sinkhole opened. The Drift was already old when this cavern formed. It must have colonised the space early, riding the water table as it eroded downward, and then it shut the system down—stabilised the pH, stabilised the temperature, stabilised the light—until everything outside its gel was frozen in place. The smokestacks and catwalks and pipe-draperies are not abandoned; they are kept in perfect repair by the Drift’s slow mineral deposition, a machine that maintains itself. It is, in essence, a closed-loop life support for a single organism, and that organism is the entire room.

What does it want? Nothing, in any sense we grasp. It has no predators, no competition, no seasonal cycle. It feeds on sunlight and carbonate, and both are constant. It has no drive to reproduce, because it already fills its entire habitat; any daughter colony would compete for the same light, so it represses its own reproduction, existing as a single immortal individual. The only change it ever experiences is the slow dimming of the sun over geological time, and it compensates by adjusting the transparency of its gel or the density of its cyanobacteria. It is a mind at the end of evolution, a contemplative born into a world where all problems have been solved, leaving only the long, slow, silent expanse of being itself.

When we left the site, we sealed the sinkhole with a steel grate and a breathing cap, to keep the rain from stirring the surface film. The Drift did not react visibly, but the next morning, the light that filtered down was fractionally more amber. I think it was grateful. Or perhaps it was finally beginning to notice time again, after a million years of being alone. The note it had struck on the drapery still echoes in my skull: a single, low tone, the voice of a creature that speaks at the pace of mountains. It will outlive us all. It will outlive our species. And when the next geological epoch decides the cavern is done, the Drift will simply dissolve its bones back into the water and drift away to find another silence to fill.
