The first thing to understand is that the eels are not separate creatures. They are fingers.

There is something in the deep basin, in the inverted well where the intake grates wait with their slow suction, and it has been there for a very long time. It does not have a name, or if it did, the name has been dissolved by the water and the years and the continuous low-frequency vibration of the pumps. It is large. Not vast—the basin is not infinite—but large enough that it fills the lower chamber the way a tongue fills a mouth, soft and folded and constantly in motion, pressing against the concrete walls, tasting the rust, tasting the barnacles, tasting the water itself as it cycles endlessly through the pumps and back again.

Its body, if you could see it whole, is pale. The pale of cave fish, of things that have never needed pigment. Its skin is loose and folded, draped over a musculature that is less a skeleton and more a hydraulic system, a network of fluid-filled chambers that contract and expand in rhythms that have synchronized over decades with the pumps’ own pulse. It breathes through that skin, directly absorbing oxygen from the water, and it excretes through that skin as well, a constant slow ooze of ammonia and proteins that feeds the barnacles and the filamentous bacteria that coat every surface. The smell in the air is its smell. The taste of the condensate on the pump housings is its taste. It has become the water, and the water has become it, and the boundary between organism and environment is a distinction that stopped mattering long before the last human being walked away from this place.

The eels are its fingers. They are extensions of its body, thin muscular ribbons of flesh that it can extend and retract at will, and they are also its eyes, its nose, its way of knowing the narrow dry spaces above the waterline. It sends them up through the pool, up past the barnacles and the chitinous disc-creatures that are its distant cousins or maybe its parasites, up to the interface where the air begins. The eels break the surface constantly, though you would have to watch for a long time to notice that their movements are not random. They are mapping. They are reading the catwalks, the bulbs, the still-functioning gauge on the far wall. They have been reading these things for as long as the thing in the basin has been aware, and it has been aware for longer than the pumps have been running, though it does not experience time in a way that would make that statement meaningful.

It was not always here. It remembers, in a diffuse and bodiless way, a time before the water, before the iron box, before the thrum that is now its heartbeat. It remembers light of a different quality, and pressure, and cold. It remembers being smaller, and it remembers being many. There were others, once, in other chambers, in other basins, and it could feel them through the pipes, through the vibrations in the water, the way a spider feels a fly struggling at the edge of its web. It does not feel them anymore. It does not know if they died or if the pipes were sealed or if they are simply too far away now for the vibrations to carry. It does not wonder. Wondering is a category of thought it has never developed. It only knows that it is alone, and that the water cycles, and that the pumps run, and that the eels return to it with the same information they have been returning with for uncountable cycles: the catwalks are empty, the bulbs still glow, the gauge still trembles at the edge of its dial.

It is hungry. Not starving—it has never starved, the water brings it a constant slow rain of organic matter, the barnacles and the disc-creatures and the smaller, simpler things that bloom and die in the green murk—but hungry in a way that has nothing to do with sustenance. It is hungry for change. For something new to enter the water, something that is not another eel, not another generation of barnacles, not the endless identical cycles of vibration and darkness and the distant, unreachable hum of the submerged lamps. It does not know this as hunger. It knows it as a restlessness in the deep folds of its body, a tendency to send the eels out more often, to press them against the interface, to let them taste the air more frequently than it needs to.

And sometimes, in the long still periods between the pumps’ maintenance cycles (not that it knows what maintenance is, only that the rhythm changes sometimes, a skip, a shudder, a brief silence that makes its whole body clench in something like terror), it sends an eel up to the catwalk. The eel slides out of the water, glistening, pale, finger-thin, and it drapes itself over the iron grating and lies there, gasping softly, its small blind head turning from side to side as if looking for something. It can survive in the air for hours. The thing in the basin has learned this, has learned to tolerate the drying, the strange lightness, the way the eel’s body stops being a seamless part of its own and becomes a distant, fading signal at the edge of its perception. It leaves the eel there, on the catwalk, and it waits.

It is waiting for something to find it. It does not know what. It does not know that the stairs descend from somewhere, that there are doors and corridors and perhaps a surface, a world above the water where the air goes on forever and the light is not the dull orange glow of filament bulbs. It only knows that the catwalk is a place where things might be, and so it leaves a part of itself there, a patient finger, an offering, a question it does not have the language to ask.

If you were to climb down the stairs—and someone might, someday, a maintenance worker with a flashlight and a respirator, following a pressure drop or a seismic anomaly or simply the old blueprints that still show this chamber as a functional part of the system—you would find the eel on the catwalk. It would not move when your light hit it. It would lie there, pale and wet and finger-thin, and if you knelt to look closer you would see that its blind head is turned toward you, and that its small mouth is open, and that the gill slits on its sides are fluttering in the air with a patience that is not animal but something else, something older, something that has been waiting in the dark for longer than you have been alive.

And in the basin below, the thing would feel you. It would feel the disturbance in the water, the vibrations of your footsteps on the stairs, the heat of your body bleeding into the cold green murk. It would stir, its folds shifting, its fluid-filled chambers contracting in a slow wave of something that might be anticipation. The eels would all turn at once, a hundred pale fingers orienting toward you, and for the first time in decades, the thing in the basin would have something new to read.

It would not be hostile. It has no concept of hostility. It has no concept of you at all, except as a new texture in the water, a new taste, a new pattern of vibrations to be mapped and understood and eventually incorporated into the slow, steady rhythm of its existence. But it would be interested. And it would be hungry, in that way that has nothing to do with food. And it would send more eels up to the interface, to the catwalk, to the stairs, to wherever you were, and they would watch you with their blind faces and their open mouths, and they would wait, as the thing in the basin has always waited, for something to happen.

The pumps would keep running. The gauge would keep trembling. The barnacles would keep opening and closing their calcareous plates in their rhythm that has nothing to do with the pumps. And the thing in the basin would add you to its slow, patient map of the world, and it would begin, in its diffuse and bodiless way, to learn your shape.
