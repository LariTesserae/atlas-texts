They don’t call themselves anything. The place doesn’t have a name, or if it does, it’s a vibration felt in the bones, not a sound. But if you stood in that swallowed-silence street long enough, you’d begin to see them—not as figures emerging from the mist, but as parts of the mist that thickened, grew limbs, and stepped forward.

They are tall, if height means anything where the ground rearranges itself underfoot, and they move with a deliberation that makes the swaying fronds seem jittery. Their bodies are architecture in miniature: a lattice of something between chitin and carbon-fiber, dark and faintly iridescent, threaded with veins that pulse a slow amber light. That light is the only steady glow you’ll see on a dim evening, a signature of their metabolism that matches the low, pervasive hum of the city. They are not clothed, not exactly. Colonies of living moss and lichen—the same species that coats the gantries—grow directly from their exoskeletal plates in patterns that shift with their moods, or perhaps with the needs of a conversation you can’t hear. The moss is their coat, their camouflage, their memory; when one of them presses a palm to a wall, the moss there blooms in recognition, a temporary wallpaper of shared history.

Their heads are not heads as you know them. The “face” is a delicate basketwork of hollow struts, and nestled inside, where a face would be, hangs a cluster of bioluminescent fungi. The fungi are not passengers; they are organs. Each cap flushes with a different color to signal emotion or intent—pale blue for inquiry, deep gold for acknowledgment, a flicker of violet for pleasure. Because sound doesn’t travel here, they have let go of hearing and voice the way a snake lets go of legs. Instead, they converse through a language of light and subtle gesture, their hands—six-fingered, with pads that feel vibrations in the ever-damp air—doing much of the “speaking.”

You’d watch them at their tasks, which are not jobs so much as functions in a shared physiology. They are the city’s immune system, its gardeners, its sculptors of the emergent geology. One of them will stand motionless before a wall that is still shimmering with crystallization, trailing its mossy fingers down the surface, and where it touches, the wall firms into something like marble veined with mycelium. Another will kneel by the slow, thick canal, dipping a basket-weave cup to collect a cup of mineral scum, then pour it over the roots of a young plant—a plant that, in a day, will be a structural column, its bark already hardening into load-bearing steel. They are midwives to the constant making, and undertakers to the unmaking: when a district crumbles back to primordial slurry, they gather the slag in long, silent processions, carrying it in hammocks of their own moss to be re-extruded elsewhere.

Yet they are not drones. If you stayed long enough—if the place allowed you to stay—you’d see them pause at a ledge to watch the slow-winged not-birds, or touch the glowing fungi of another forehead in what you’d swear is tenderness. You’d see small ones, shorter and with moss that hasn’t yet formed full coats, learning to tend a pipe-fern or to feel the pressure-shift that means a new chamber is being born. They learn by becoming, not by being taught; their memories are stored in the very moss they wear, and when an elder presses close to a young one, both their mosses mingle, and the younger knows what it needs to know. They are a distributed, growing memory, a living log of every crystallization and collapse.

They have no art that you’d recognize, because for them, the whole city is a canvas they’re perpetually brushing into existence. A doorway is never just a doorway—it’s a verse in the long poem of a wall, opened and closed by a gesture that also tells a story. The “shops” you glimpsed are not commerce but conversation: one offers to another a carefully grown crystal that holds a certain vibration, and the other accepts it with a flash of gold, adding it to a lattice in their chest where it will hum a new memory into being. What you took for “coin” is a piece of themselves, exchanged like a word.

They are utterly silent, yes. But watch their light-language long enough, and the silence becomes a kind of music, a symphony of colored pulse and flicker that fills the mist like a visible echo. They are not lonely in the quiet; they are the quiet. And they are not the city’s masters—they are just another cell in its vast, dreaming body, the ones who walk, tend, and remember, while the factories below dream stone into being and the leaves breathe out the perpetual fog. If you left, you would carry the memory of their slow, deliberate grace, and for a long while, every mossy wall in your own world would seem to lack something vital.
