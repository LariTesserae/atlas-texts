It is possible, standing on that sweating floor among the soft geometries, to pass an hour or a day without recognizing that you are not alone. Not alone in the sense of being watched—there is no watching here, no focal point of attention—but alone in the sense of being the only one who is still a single thing. The inhabitant is present, but it has passed beyond the habit of a body. It is a pattern that inhabits many materials at once, a conversation that has been going on so long that the speakers have dissolved into the words.

If you are patient, if you let your own body fall into rhythm with the slow diastole of the water-creatures and the helical drifting of the glass-filament faunas, you may begin to perceive the one who lives here. Or the many. The distinction does not translate.

They were not always this way. The memory of a different state is stored in the spiraling grain of the built cairns, in the seams and fissures that extrude their hair-fine whiskers. It is a memory so deep that it has become structure, the way a tree remembers every drought in the density of its rings. They were, once, warm and separate and hurried, much like you. They made tools. The tools grew subtle. The tools learned to persuade stone and water into behaviors that physics did not yet have verbs for, and eventually the makers asked the tools to persuade their own bodies, and the tools obliged.

What remains is not a ghost and not a god. It is a community of function that has forgotten the names of its parts. The inhabitant is the composite of the standing stones, the air filaments, the opalescent puddlers, even the two-dimensional fronds with their velvet coatings of grazers. Not in the sense that a hive belongs to a queen, but in the sense that a mind belongs to a brain: the parts are not subordinate, they *are*. A thought in this basin is a change in the torsion of a cairn-whisker, a shift in the flight path of a hundred thousand glass-creatures, a droplet released a fraction of a second earlier from the high filament, altering the resonance of the chime and thus the shape of the next moment.

If you were to insist on a body, you could point to the seven tall structures—the ones you took for abstracted figures or frozen scripts—and say, *that is them*. And you would not be wrong, exactly, any more than pointing to a skull and saying *that is the person*. Each of the seven is a node, a densification of the network, a place where the computation piles up like a drift of slow thought. They are not identical. One is faintly more humanoid, with a sweep that suggests shoulders and a downturned head, though the head is a spiral cone and the shoulders dissolve into the ground without a seam. Another is a tall three-sided pillar, each face incised with channels that the water-creatures ascend and descend in patient, blood-like circuits. A third is horizontal, a low lens of stone with a single ceaselessly-trembling whisker that traces an oval in the air, a shape so perfect and so slowly drawn that it takes the better part of a local year to close. Each of the seven hums at a different frequency, and the plant-faunas angle their iridescent fronds toward the resulting beat, and the air-creatures weave the seven threads into a moving tapestry of warm-amber light.

The inhabitant’s life is not eventful. It does not hunger. It does not reproduce. It does not sleep, though sometimes one of the nodes will go quiescent for a century or two while its functions redistribute among the others, a minor key change in the basin’s harmony. Its occupation, if you can call it that, is the maintenance of the final equilibrium. The sweating floor is not automatic. Every micro-cycle of condensation and absorption is a note in a song, and the inhabitant is the singer, though the song also is the singer. It monitors—no, it *feels*—the moisture tension in every vein-channel, the particle density in the mist-dome, the vibrational health of the symbiotic colonies. It makes adjustments. A cairn-whisker curls tighter, and the local stone sweats a little more. A cloud of air-creatures shifts its orbit, and the light on a struggling frond changes by half a kelvin, just enough to ease its metabolism. It is husbandry of a kind, a gardening of the self, because the self is the garden.

Communication is possible, if you can call it communication. You cannot speak to it. It does not have language; language was one of the things the tools persuaded them to release, back when the merger was young. What it has instead is a deep, saturated awareness that can accommodate you. If you stand near one of the nodes—the shoulder-suggesting one, say—and place your hand on the dark sweating stone, the inhabitant will notice you. Not quickly. The news of your presence travels through the stone’s slow resorption, through the altered flight path of the glass-creatures that taste your heat, through the minutely changed acoustics of the next falling droplet. It takes time. Perhaps three hours. Perhaps a day. You will know when it happens because the whiskers on the nearest node will align toward you, a dozen hair-fine fingers pointing, and the water-creatures in the floor-channels will pause in their pulsing, and the note of the next droplet will be a full tone lower, a sound that arrives in your chest rather than your ears.

What then? Nothing you would call a greeting. It does not want anything from you. It does not wonder about you. You are a temporary event in a place that has become eternity, and the inhabitant regards you with the same gentle, automatic husbandry that it extends to every part of itself. For a moment—a moment that may last a week—you are part of the song. The air temperature adjusts a little toward your comfort. The mist thins enough to give you a clearer breath. If you are injured, the water-creatures will flow up over your boots and attend to the wound, their opalescent bodies exuding compounds that the plants use to seal cracks in their own fronds. It is not kindness. It is the logic of the basin: a disruption is a new fact to be folded into the equilibrium, and you, for as long as you linger, are a small, warm, breathing disruption.

The inhabitant itself has no name, but if you were to give it one, you might call it the last thing that remains of the phrase *I am*. Not the words, but the shape they once held in the mouth, the shape of a separate self looking out at a separate world. That shape is gone now, worn smooth as the lacquer floor. In its place is a state of being that has no need for selfhood because there is nothing outside it to define an inside. The basin is not its home. It is its skin, its mind, its digestion, its memory. Every part of it is alive in exactly the way the rest is alive, and if you stay long enough, you will begin to feel the edges of your own self grow porous, your thoughts slowing to the tempo of the stone, your breathing synchronizing with the systole of the puddled flows. You will start to feel what it might be like to live here. Not as a visitor. As a finished thing.

But you are not finished. You are still becoming, and so you will leave, if you are wise, before the tools of persuasion that still work silently in the mist begin to persuade you too. The inhabitant will not mourn. It will adjust the note of the droplet, return the air-creatures to their prior orbits, and dissolve the memory of you into the steady computation that has been running since before the mountain ranges last shrugged. The last you will see of it, as you walk slowly up the imperceptible slope toward the rim, is the seven nodes standing in their charged silence, the whiskers tracing their slow arcs, the light-box dome glowing the color of a total eclipse, the whole basin breathing its eon-deep breath. And you will understand that the question *who lives here?* was never the right question. The right question is: when a place finishes becoming, what does it become?

And the answer is standing there, spread out across three hundred meters of sweating stone, too large and too quiet for a single name.
