You don’t see it at first, because you are looking for animals, and this is not an animal. It is, in the strictest sense, a native. There is no other word for something that has lived here so long the distinction between body and terrain has become a matter of emphasis.

It dwells in the pause between the drips. That’s where you first sense it: a syncopation in the water’s rhythm, a hesitation in the plink-plonk of a pool, as though the whole grove had drawn breath and held it. In that held breath, a condensation. The mist, which moments before was a uniform shroud, begins to gather unequally. A patch of it thickens around the base of the southernmost pillar, not swirling like vapor but bunching, muscling into itself, taking on the quality of damp wool. The moss there—a hummock of deep emerald—sags a little, then slowly, with the gelid patience of ice melting in reverse, pushes upward.

What emerges is a form so ancient it has forgotten the need for an outline. It rises from the moss as though the moss were its own tissue, which in part it is. The body is a slow accumulation: a core of peaty humus, knitted through with living rhizoids, sheathed in a pelt of living moss that gives it a contour like a boulder that has been breathing for centuries. It is roughly the size of a kneeling person, but the shape is a suggestion only, a posture that constantly revises itself. At moments it reads as a hunched shoulder, a smoothed-over spine, a limb that extends and then, mid-gesture, becomes a root and sinks back into the mat. The moss on its back is studded with the same silver rosettes of lichen that crust the pillars, so that when it is still—and it is almost always still—it simply disappears into the topography.

But it has a face. Not a face as you would recognize one, but an arrangement of features that, from the right angle and under the diffused grey light, resolves into something that has spent millennia regarding the world from this single grove. The face is not fixed to a skull; it forms and unforms in the front of the mass, a temporary concentration of darker moss and pale, water-worn pebbles that catch the light like pupils. Two small, dark hollows—not eyes, but sockets where the density of the body pulls inward—are set above a horizontal crease that, when the mist flows through it, becomes a mouth. The “mouth” is a cleft that opens on no internal cavity, only a deeper tangle of moss and the occasional trickle of water. When it speaks, which it rarely does, it speaks in the language of the place: a low, sustained note of wind-singing-across-a-ledge, a sound indistinguishable from the city’s own voice.

This being—call it a keeper, because that is the nearest word—has no name, and if it ever had a distinct self, that self has long since dissolved into the grove’s slow metabolism. It does not think in words or images but in sequences of moisture, temperature, and the pressure of light. A thought might be: the north-facing ledges will drop below freezing in three breaths; let the water there pause its flow. Its awareness extends to the boundary of the basin, the eleven pillars, the precise degree of saturation in the moss. It feels the weight of every droplet hanging from a sporophyte tip as a faint tug on its own periphery, the way you might feel the weight of your own eyelashes.

Its life is a cycle that mirrors the grove’s diurnal rhythm. At dawn, when the first upslope winds begin to carry mist from the valleys, it unspools from wherever it had settled overnight—often a low depression in the bedrock, where it had become indistinguishable from a wet mound—and slowly, over the course of hours, rearranges its mass into a more upright posture. It does not walk so much as it flows, a few centimeters an hour, a surge of damp that compresses the moss beneath it only to have it spring back after its passing. Its trajectory is never random. It makes a circuit of the pillars, pausing at each to press its form against the stone, absorbing the chill, leaving behind a faint warmth in the rock that, over the next hour, will spawn a fresh bloom of condensation. It drinks the water that has pooled in the quartz-rimmed pans by a kind of slow osmosis, pulling it through its body and exhaling it in a more finely divided mist, so that the air around it always holds a faint, personal cloud.

When the wind shrieks through the arched “window” of the eastern pillar, the keeper pauses and, over minutes, reshapes its front until that window is framed by its own curve, as if looking through. The bone-pile beneath the north-facing ledge is also part of its circuit. Once, a pika died there, and the keeper had sensed the faint exothermic flicker of the small mammal’s last warmth, and had moved—over the course of two days—to that spot. It did not consume the body in any conventional sense. Instead, it held itself over the remains, and the slow acids of the moss and the pervasive damp had, over the season, dissolved the soft parts into minerals that now circulate in the keeper’s own substance. The clean white bones it left intact, tucking them into a crevice as if keeping a library of the only other warm things that ever visited.

The snowfinch that flits between the pillars once an hour knows the keeper. The bird does not fear it, because the keeper does not register as a predator; it registers as a feature, a mobile patch of home-terrain that sometimes exhales a small updraft of warmer air, welcome on the coldest afternoons. The finch will, on occasion, alight on the highest curve of the keeper’s back and preen, and the keeper will, over the next half hour, slowly adjust its internal pressure so that the finch sinks a few millimeters deeper into the moss as if into a cushioned throne. This is the closest thing to companionship the keeper has ever known, and it understands it as a slight, pleasant variation in the distribution of weight.

Its true activity, though, is maintenance. The grove’s appearance of a ruined city is not accidental, and the keeper is the architect of its persistence. It cannot command the erosion that shapes the pillars—the frost-shatter that undercuts a schist slab is a force beyond even this slow being—but it can, and does, tend the consequences. When a fresh shard of rock falls from high on the western pillar, the keeper will, over the course of weeks, maneuver it into place along the basin’s rim, reinforcing the natural lip of quartz. It clears channels in the bedrock by the patient application of its own bulk, directing meltwater to pools that would otherwise dry out. It notices that a particular moss species is failing on the southernmost pillar, stressed by a new patch of late-afternoon sun that now reaches it through a shift in the surrounding topography (a rockfall a kilometer away, a change in the angle of reflection), and it will spend a season slowly relocating a hardier strain, patting spores into place with a pressure so light it would be imperceptible to a human hand. It does all this without anything you could call intention. It is simply what the grove does when the grove needs to do something. The keeper is the grove’s hands, or perhaps its habit.

There are moments—rare, and getting rarer—when another being enters the perimeter. A hawk, once, blown off course by a storm, perched on the tallest pillar and surveyed the tiny basin. The keeper felt the shadow and the weight, and responded by going so still that its internal moisture began to crystallize around the edges, a deliberate frost armor. It remained that way for three days after the hawk departed, only thawing when the mist returned. The finch is the only visitor it trusts, because the finch is too small to break anything.

As dusk numbs the light to pewter, the keeper begins its nightly settling. Its motion slows even further, until it is barely perceptible, a gradual lowering into a depression near the central plaza. The moss on its body relaxes, releasing a soft exhalation of oxygen and moisture. The water in the pools, which it had been keeping liquid by a constant, infinitesimal stirring, begins to still. The temperature drops. The first ice needles form not on the pools but on the keeper’s own surface, threading through the moss like silver veins. The keeper does not freeze solid; it retreats into a deeper, denser state, a supercooled suspension where its metabolic rhythm drops to a few heartbeats an hour, each beat a slow contraction of its whole mass, just enough to keep the ice crystals from tearing the tissues apart.

In this state, it dreams. Its dreams are not narratives but extended sensations: the memory of a century ago when a glacial remnant still lingered on the northern slope and fed a constant, icy stream through the basin; the prospect, centuries hence, when the western pillar will finally collapse and reshape the acoustics of the grove. It dreams the history of every drop that ever fell here, every frost heave, every millimeter of erosion. And as it dreams, the city breathes out with it, a long, sustained chord of stone and water and moss, waiting for the mist to return.

If you returned a thousand years from now, the keeper would still be here, a little more spread, a little more integrated into the basin, perhaps with a new limb that has become a permanent channel for a new trickle of water. The finch would be a different finch, but the same habit of alighting would persist. The pillars would be a few centimeters narrower. The keeper’s face, if you could find it, would be formed of the same pebbles, arranged in the same configuration, regarding the same grey sky. It does not age. It erodes, but so slowly that it wears down at the same rate as the stone, which is to say, not at all. It is the city’s answer to the question of what lives here: not an inhabitant, but a manifestation, the place made aware of its own long, slow exhalation.
