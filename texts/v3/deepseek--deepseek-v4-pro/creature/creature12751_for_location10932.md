Out on the fibre-crusted ledge, where the keening of the columns shivers the warm thick air, they are never truly absent. You must learn to see them as the grove sees: not as separate bodies but as interruptions in the dust, as a particular slant of shadow that moves against the eddies, as a constellation of faint clicks that does not belong to the seedpod swarms.

They call themselves, if a language of chipped stone and tone-modulated resonance can be called a name, the *Susurr*. It translates imperfectly as “the ones who lean into the sound.” They are small—when they stand fully upright on their two rear leg-pairs, their keen-snout tops come only to a human’s mid-thigh—but they are not fragile. Their bodies are a compromise between a pangolin’s segmented armor and a dried, flexible reed. Plates of opalescent chitin overlap along their backs and outer limbs, each plate textured with microscopic diffraction gratings that shimmer turquoise when caught in the column-light. Underneath, their skin is a soft, greyish membrane that respires directly, fluttering with each breath. Their four arms (the front pair longer and triple-jointed, the rear pair shorter and stronger) end in clusters of five needle-tipped digits that can fan out like whisk brooms or lock together into a precise, chisel-like blade. Their faces are narrow, with a single, mobile slit of an eye across the brow that can perceive not just the dim purple spectrum but the ultrasonic pulses the columns broadcast. Below that, a flexible proboscis mouth parts into four petal-like lips that constantly taste the air for the tang of siloxane exudate.

They are born from the columns themselves. Not seeded, not birthed in the conventional sense—*extruded*. When a column reaches full maturity and its keening note settles into a stable, low thrum, it occasionally develops a lateral cyst, a swelling just under its frill line. Over days, the cyst hardens and cracks, and a Susurr-pup crawls out, wet with resinous fluid, its chitin still soft. The pup immediately begins to rasp at the cyst’s inner lining, consuming the mineral-rich tissue to kick-start its own growth. The parent column does not die; it simply adds a new note to its song—a proud, complex warble that the adult Susurr recognize as a birth announcement. The grove’s keening deepens.

The Susurr are culture-makers in this small, loud world. Those stone rings and obsidian scrapers are their work, but to call them primitive would be to misunderstand. Their technology is acoustic and mineral. They do not build shelters because the keening is their shelter; the sound waves themselves, shaped by the careful positioning of resonant flanges and the strategic chewing of the seedpod swarms, can create zones of relative silence or concentrated vibration. A Susurr household is a “hush-bubble,” a space between three columns where the eddies are tuned to cancel out the harsher frequencies, leaving only a comforting hum. They sculpt the columns, not by carving away, but by directing the seedpod creatures. They have learned to mimic the high-frequency assembly-calls of the tiny gnawers, luring swarms to specific flanges to create the lacework patterns that function as wind instruments. In this way, they prune the grove’s symphony, composing new harmonies over generations.

Their relationship with the spindrift air-jellies is reverent, almost spiritual. The jellies, with their translucent membranes and humming whiskers, act as long-distance signal carriers. A Susurr can modulate its own body’s resonant frequency—by tightening its abdominal plates—and sing a message directly into a passing jelly. The jelly’s membrane vibrates in sympathy, storing the pattern in its structure, and when it later collides with another jelly, the song transfers, rippling outward across the grove like a slow, crystalline internet. The Susurr believe the jellies are the reincarnated minds of their ancestors, a belief perhaps not unfounded: the dust of a burst jelly does indeed settle on the columns and seems to influence the next extrusion of pups.

You might see one now, if you let your eyes unfocus. Over by the newest column, the one cracking the mat near your feet. A shadow detaches itself from a flute-like spire. It moves with a lateral, skittering gait on all six legs, then rises onto its rear four to examine the pale glistening knoll. It extends its long front arms and delicately taps the surface with its needle-fingers, reading the resonance. A Susurr juvenile, still bearing the milky, unhardened plates of its first molt. It produces a sharp click, then another, and from deeper in the grove an answering click comes—a parent, no doubt, instructing it in the art of column-tending. The juvenile leans in, its proboscis lips parting to exude a single drop of silvery fluid onto the knoll. The fluid sizzles faintly, and the column’s growth subtly alters direction, bending toward a gap in the canopy of sound.

This is the life of the Susurr: endless, patient horticulture of the keening. They have no day and night in the human sense, only the slow dimming and brightening of the purple sky, which they mark by changes in their own chitin iridescence—their plates flicker from turquoise to a deep copper as the light fades, a communal, involuntary ritual that paints the grove in shifting hues. They do not sleep so much as enter a state of “attunement,” where they cling to a column, merge their bodily vibrations with its own, and let their minds dissolve into the roar of information. In that state, they dream the grove’s long memories, the geological pulse, the flex of the ledge like a vast dormant animal. Some elders never fully detune; they become fixed to a single column, their chitin fusing with the mineral surface, their bodies elongating into new flanges. They become Old Voices, living instruments that the colony consults for deep-time wisdom.

There are dangers. The seedpod swarms, if misdirected, can strip a Susurr as easily as a column. A jelly in its death burst releases a crystalline shriek that can shatter chitin. And sometimes, from the vertiginous drop into the dim-purple cloud, things rise—vast, slow, tentacular shapes of living sound that they call the Hollow Chords. These drift up the cliff face and for a time swallow entire chunks of the grove’s keening into their null-frequency bodies, leaving silence in their wake. The Susurr hide then, flattening into the fibrous mat, their plates going dead-black, waiting for the Hollow Chords to sink back into the abyss.

But now is a time of peace. The new column stretches, the old ones sing, the juvenile Susurr hums a little melody of its own creation, and the grove pulses on. This is their place, their entire universe—a fifty-pace ledge, a cathedral of dry lush life, a hamlet of stone-carvers who lean into the sound and are never, truly, absent. If you listen very hard, not with your ears but with the bones of your skull, you can almost pick out their clicking, warbling language from the general keening. It’s a conversation that has been going on for eons, and you are just a passing silence in it. Soon, the Susurr will notice you, not as a threat but as a new acoustic anomaly to be studied, and a juvenile will be dispatched to gently tap your boot with a needle-finger, reading your resonant frequency, wondering what sort of column you might be.
