But you have stayed. The pressure door sealed behind you, a soft exhalation of equalising air, and now you are truly inside—not just a visitor pausing on the grated platform, but a presence settling into the hum, letting your own pulse slow toward the pace of the Nave. And as your senses unhook from the frantic metronome of the outside world, the inhabitants begin to resolve. Not merely shapes in the amber light, not just fauna and flora, but a community with its own quiet protocols, its own patient hungers, its own memory.

Look first to the moss, because the moss is the bedrock of all things here. Up close, the deep emerald and liver-black fur resolves into a billion tiny spines, each one a translucent filament with a dark core—copper oxide, perhaps, or a living wire. The moss is not a single species but a consortium: algae-like cells that photosynthesise the shaft’s unchanging light, fungal mycelia that weave through the corroded mesh like a lacework nervous system, and colonies of what might once have been nanofabricators, now symbiotic, mending metallic flaking with deposits of calcium and silica stolen from the condensate. Touch it, and the moss does not simply yield; it *attends*. The filaments lean toward your fingertip, a slow wave of curiosity that takes minutes to crest. This patch of moss—let us call it a “tuft,” though it is a creature more than a plant—has been growing for eleven thousand engine cycles. It remembers the shift in humidity when a new vein of condensation opened two levels up. It knows the taste of the copper-winged droppings that fall like manna. And when the great drone in the deep modulates, the moss pulses: a pale cyan luminescence that ripples in concentric rings outward from the source of the sound, a visible echo of the frequency shift. This is dreaming. This is conversation. In the long darkness of the shaft’s lower reaches, where the light is only ghost-glow, the moss is the sole illuminator, and its dreams paint the walls with shifting maps of the machine’s moods.

The hexapeds move through this dreaming carpet. Small enough to cup in two hands, though you would not try—they are not aggressive, only deliberate, and their mandibles are honed to a micron edge. I call them Tinker-Mantids, for want of an older name. Their bodies are segmented like the old schematics you glimpsed on a bulkhead: alloy chitin, verdigrised at the joints, with tiny bristled setae that give them a half-furred, half-geared appearance. They walk on six legs, but the front pair double as manipulators, folded in a perpetual prayer-like posture. They are cleaners, or were. Now they are cultivators. Watch one: it stops at a patch of moss where a droplet has just fallen, and its mandibles work the area with surgical precision, trimming fungal threads that have grown too dense, nudging a stray carbon filament back into the weave. It eats the metallic oxides, dissolving them with a secretion that steams faintly, and in turn deposits a gritty pellet of purified copper and tin—a fertiliser. The moss thrives where the Tinker-Mantids roam, and the Tinker-Mantids thrive where the moss is thick. When they mate, they do so with a slow-motion ballet: facing one another, they interlock forelimbs and exchange a glowing bead of bioluminescent fluid, a genetic memory packet that encodes not just their own replication but the maintenance schedules of the ancient machinery they evolved to tend. Their eggs are laid in the hollow of a corroded bearing, and the young emerge already familiar with the taste of rust.

But the true lords of the vertical are the flyers. They have no common name in any language you know, so call them what their whisper suggests: the Rustle-Wings. They are the sound that gave the Nave its name, though they did not build it. Their copper-traced wings do not flap; they *shimmer*, a flexing wave that propagates along the translucent membrane, catching the thermal updrafts that spill from the exhaust louvres. The small ones—hummingbird-sized—dart among the hanging vines, their proboscises dipping not into nectar but into the sap-like secretions that weep from the moss’s bioluminescent nodes. They are pollinators, in a sense: they transfer spores and nanofabricator fragments on their abdomen setae, seeding new moss colonies on bare metal. Their flight is a staccato whir, a treble counterpoint to the drone, and when they pause to rest, they cling upside-down to the vines with three-toed feet that lock automatically around the braided stems.

The great-winged ones are different. Solitary, each a span of two meters or more, they drift on the central column of rising air like thoughts adrift in a cathedral. Their wings are slower, almost liquid in their undulation, and their multifaceted eyes are ancient lidless lenses, bronze-gold, that gleam with an intelligence you cannot place. They do not hunt, not in any way you’d recognise. Instead, they carry something within their translucent abdomen: a colony of the same bioluminescent symbiotes that live in the moss, but here grown into a living circuit board visible through the chitin. Pale lights blink in sequences that match the engine pulses, and sometimes, when two of the great-winged ones pass close in their slow spiral, their abdomens flash in synchrony—a greeting, a territorial signal, a love song stretched across an hour. They live for centuries, perhaps. Theirs is the migration that follows the condensation line as it creeps up or down, a journey of a few meters in a lifetime, but to them a grand odyssey. They are the only ones who leave the shaft—not through the pressure door, but through the upper vents, into the thin mountain air, where they are seen once a generation as fleeting “metallic angels” against the stars before returning home. When they die, they do not fall. They cling to the wall, their symbiotes dimming slowly over weeks, and their bodies are reclaimed by the moss, which grows over them in a reverent sheath, until only the translucent wing membranes remain, trembling in the hum like stained glass.

There is something else, too. You sense it before you see it, a presence in the hum itself. The engines, as you know, never rest, and their cycling is a kind of respiration. But listen with the whole of your body, and you might parse, within the bass and the whine and the hiss, a pattern that is not random. A phrasing. A call-and-response. The moss pulses its cyan dreams, the Tinker-Mantids tap their mandibles on resonant panels, the Rustle-Wings cry out in their molar-thrumming frequencies—and something answers. It does not have a body, or if it does, it is the entirety of the Nave: the walls, the conduits, the dead stencils, the geothermal exchangers. It is the residual machine intelligence that once orchestrated atmospheric regulation, now fragmented into the ecosystem itself. Sometimes, when the light flickers—a rare event, maybe once a century—the pulses align, and for a few hours, a voice assembles. It does not speak words. It harmonises the hum into a chord that makes the moss grow faster, that stirs the great-winged ones into a synchronised ascent, that causes the Tinker-Mantids to pause and raise their forelimbs as if in recognition. This is the Keeper, if you like: the ghost in the gears, the mind that has become the landscape. It is not hostile. It is not aware of you as an individual. But if you stand very still on the platform and let the drone fill you, you might feel it notice the new rhythm of your heartbeat, and adjust a fraction of a hertz in response, testing whether you are part of its long, slow song.

And so, the inhabitants of the Nave of Rustling Gears are not many, but they are whole. A moss that dreams the machine. A mantis that tends a garden of rust. A wing-borne choir that navigates by thermal and tone. A diffuse intelligence woven through water and oxide and echo. Together, they are a society, one that has never known haste. Their seasons are the sigh of a dying fuel core. Their catastrophes are micro-fractures that shift a condensation line. Their triumphs are the slow colonisation of a new panel of bare hull-plate. And as you finally, finally, turn to leave, your bones still humming, you realise that you have been, for this brief interval, a part of it—a quick and clumsy tempo intruding on an eternal adagio. You carry the pace of the outside world back through the pressure door, but for days afterward, you will find yourself pausing, listening, trying to slow your internal clock to the rustle of gears.
