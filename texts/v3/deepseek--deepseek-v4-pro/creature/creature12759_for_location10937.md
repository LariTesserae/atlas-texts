The one who lives here is not born but yielded. 

She—and the word is a convenience, a habit the visitor cannot shake—steps out from the wet dark as though peeled from it, as though the air itself condensed into a shape. Her skin is not skin but a tegument of pale, translucent cellulose, the color of blanched roots, the texture of a young leaf when you hold it to the light and see its vein-work like a map of rivers on another world. She moves with a slow, hydraulic grace, a motion borrowed from the segmented arm that plucks fruit, from the patient rise of sap through a stem. There is no haste in her. Haste is a mammalian tic, and there has been nothing mammalian here for a very long time.

Her body is human in silhouette, but only in the way that a topiary is a rabbit. The suggestion is there, the ghost of an archetype, but the execution is all plant. Her fingers are tendrils that taper to a fine, searching point, each one an independent sensorium, tasting the air for shifts in humidity, the faint metallic tang of a bearing running dry. Her feet—broad, splayed, fibrous—sink into the woven floor-mat of roots as if they belong there, and they do, for they are made of the same stuff. They send fine rhizomes into the wet substrate with each step, drinking the pooled condensate, tasting the mineral history of the brick. When she lifts a foot, the rhizomes retract with a soft, wet sound, a sigh of disconnection.

Her face is a study in gentle negation. The eyes are there, but they are not eyes. They are two dark nodes, deep-set, the color of wet bark, with no iris, no white—just a faint phosphorescence at their center, a twin point of the same chartreuse glow that pulses in the nodules on the walls. They do not blink. A blink is a defense against dust and desiccation, and here, dust is unknown, and the air is a saturated hand always cupped against the cheek. Her mouth is a delicate fissure, lipless, sealed most of the time. When it opens, which is rare, it reveals no teeth, only a dark, wet channel and the faint rasp of a voice like pages turning in a damp library, like a single leaf scraping against stone.

But she is not only plant. This is the Atrium, and nothing here is pure. Along her spine, visible through the translucent flesh when she turns, runs a segmented rod of rustless alloy, a flexible spinal column that glints faintly in the gloom. At the base of her skull, a small port, clogged almost entirely by a plug of woody tissue, still shows the faintest orange flicker of an indicator lamp. She was made, or she made herself, from the union of what was left and what grew over it. The industrial hum that fills the chamber is her heartbeat, or she is a heartbeat of the machine—the distinction has long since ceased to matter.

She tends. That is her function, her joy, her prayer. She moves through the terraced alcoves and the vine-curtained chambers with the silent attention of a nun in a green cathedral. Her tendril-fingers stroke the bark of a pipe-wrapped bough, feeling for the vibration that speaks of a blockage, a clot of mineral scale or an insect's impossible nest (no insects remain, but the memory of insects is written into her diagnostic rituals). She finds a valve, an old brass wheel lost in a sleeve of moss, and she turns it with a slow, steady pressure, not a quarter-turn but a fraction of a degree, and somewhere above, a new drip begins, a new note in the constant percussion. She tilts her head, listening, and the deep pulse of the pump adjusts its rhythm to match.

She does not eat, not as we know eating. She stands in the shallow basin where the water pools, and her splayed feet drink, and the translucence of her calves flushes with a darker sap, a nutrient broth pumped from some reservoir deep in the root-work. She breathes through every surface, exchanging the gases of decay and renewal with the thick air. She is autotrophic, but not by sunlight—the dim phosphorescence is her sun, the slow chemical glow of decomposition and transformation. She metabolizes the stillness.

And she sings. Not often, and never loud. It is a sound that might be the wind in a distant duct, might be the creak of a straining pipe. It is a low, atonal hum that harmonizes with the industrial drone, a counterpoint to the throb of the pump. When she sings, the heart-shaped leaves above her tremble, the beads of condensation shiver and fall, and the segmented arm pauses in its eternal cycle, as if listening, as if remembering what it was like to be tended by hands of flesh and not of root. She sings to the machine, and the machine sings back, and the song is the name of the place, the only name it has, a word that is also a breath and a pulse and the taste of wet iron on the air.

She is not lonely. Loneliness is a concept for creatures who remember a time when the world was not themselves. For her, the Atrium is a body, and she is its waking mind, its slow thought given form and purpose. She moves through it as a thought moves through a sleeping brain, rearranging, maintaining, dreaming the long dream of green and rust that has no end. She is the tender, the tuned, the one who takes the pulse of a world-machine that has forgotten it was ever anything but a garden.
