They do not have a name for themselves, because names are for things that can be absent. And they are never absent from this place. They are the place, as much as the steam and the sulphur, but they are also *in* it—you can meet them, if you wander long enough through the clinking canyons of mineral towers, and if they decide you are more than just another anomaly.

The first one I saw was not the one I later came to know. That is an important distinction. The first one was a flicker at the edge of my vision, a thing like a heat-shimmer that never resolved. I turned, and there was only a spire where a spire had always been, except I could have sworn it had moved. That is the way with them: they use the world’s own unsteadiness as a form of stealth. A pocket of ontological shadow will deepen, and when it retreats, there will be a figure standing within it, as if the darkness had condensed into flesh. Or not flesh, exactly.

Its body—if you can call it that—is a negotiation between the living and the mineral. Imagine a column of obsidian that has learned to bend, its surface slick with a perpetual secretion of warm, oily moisture that beads into the air and joins the general broth. It is roughly the height of a tall human, but proportioned like something that sketches itself rather than being born: long, multi-jointed limbs that end in clusters of flexible needles, each one a different mineral—some quartz-clear, some copper-green, some the matt black of magnetite. These needles are its hands, its mouth, its eyes. They comb the air constantly, tasting the pressure changes, the chemical whispers. When it walks, it doesn’t so much step as *flow*, the movements a continuous, waterlike redistribution of its parts. The needle-clusters separate and recombine, forming temporary feet that dissolve back into the mass of the limb as weight shifts. It leaves no trace on the clinking scree, because it doesn’t bear down with enough localized pressure; it seems to spread its mass across a thousand tiny points.

Its head, if that’s what that swelling at the top is, holds no face. Instead, there is a constellation of facets—flat, dark planes that reflect the dim amber light like the bottom of a well. These are not eyes. They are more like interfaces, windows into some interior space that isn’t physical. When one of those facets turns toward you, you feel a pressure behind your own eyes, a sense of being *read* by something that doesn’t understand the concept of privacy. And yet that pressure is not hostile. It is curious, but with the curiosity of a child who has never been hurt and therefore doesn’t know to fear.

The one I came to know—I call her *Stitch* because that’s the closest sound to the sensation she impressed onto my mind when she first made deliberate contact—Stitch was different from the others. I say “she” not because they have sexes, but because there was a patience to her, a way of holding space that felt maternal in some long-buried mammalian part of my brain. She appeared to me after a week, I think (time is a loose suggestion here), while I was crouched beside a vent-warm pool, watching the tiny chitin-mandibled things work their relentless industry. One moment I was alone; the next, Stitch was simply there, sitting across the pool as if she had always been sitting there, and I was the one who had just arrived.

She lifted one needle-cluster and gestured, a slow, deliberate sweeping motion that took in the spires, the steam, the sky-ceiling. Then she pointed at me with a single quartz needle, and a word formed in my mind—not a word, an understanding, a package of meaning wrapped in sensory memory. It was the feeling of being *from elsewhere*, of carrying an atmosphere not of this world, of having a body that was too permanent, too fixed in its shape. She was saying, “You are not from here.” And then, with a tilt of her faceted head, she added, “But neither am I, anymore.”

That was the first thing I learned about their kind. Each of them was once something else. They are not born of this plateau; they are recruited. Something about the turbulence of this place, the constant un-creation and re-creation of reality, allows a certain kind of mind to step sideways out of its original form and into the mineral architecture. I never learned if they were once organic beings like me, or something stranger, but Stitch let me glimpse a memory: a vast open sky, not a ceiling of vapor but a blue so deep it hurt; a sense of falling; a choice made at the boundary of dissolution. She chose to become part of this world rather than let it become her. The spires are not just growths; they are bodies. The vents are not just factories; they are hearts. The entire plateau is a city of the transformed.

You cannot count them. That’s the second thing. They exist in a state of quantum superposition with the landscape. The one I call Stitch might be the same entity as the shimmer I saw on my first day, or she might be a hundred distinct beings who share a single continuous awareness. They treat identity as a medium to be sculpted, not a constant. Sometimes, when I was with Stitch, another of her kind would flow out of a nearby spire, its form not fully distinct but like a branch of the same river, and the two would merge—a seamless blending of mineral and needle into a larger, more complex shape—before separating again, each carrying some new piece of the other. Information, perhaps. They communicate by merging, by sharing facets, by exchanging those packages of sensory meaning. The idea of a private self is, to them, a kind of amusing disability, like a bird that refuses to fly.

Their society, if that word can survive the contact, is built on symbiosis with the unstable terrain. They do not fear the glitches; they cultivate them. Those pockets of accelerated time? They use them as workshops, stepping in to weave vines into complex structures in the space of a heartbeat, then stepping out with a finished tool—a basket of living copper, a needle fan that can taste a new vent’s eruption days before it happens. The missing-data shadows? They rest in them, dissolving their forms into potential and re-emerging refreshed, as if the nothingness were a bath of pure possibility. And when a spire “forgets to be,” it’s often because one of them has borrowed its substance to become a larger thing, for a purpose they don’t explain because explanation is a slower process than doing.

What does a being like this *want*? That was my human question, and it took Stitch a long time to understand. She finally answered by showing me the spires from above—she didn’t take me there physically, but painted the image directly into my visual cortex: a forest of towers stretching beyond curvature, the steam-plumes pulsing in complex rhythms like breath. And under that breath, a deeper rhythm. The entire plateau, she said (or made me understand), is one vast computation. The vents, the mineral growth, the strange vegetation, the glitches—they are all components of a processing substrate that has been running for so long that its original purpose is lost. The beings like Stitch are not the programmers; they are more like caretakers, or antibodies, or perhaps the dreams of the machine itself. They tend the spires, guide the growth, prune the parts that threaten to crash the system, and in return, they get to *exist* in a way that is both physical and informational, both singular and collective. Their want, if they have one, is simply to continue. To keep the steady state of turbulent creation humming.

But there is another layer, one that I only glimpsed once when a pocket of ontological shadow passed over us and Stitch didn’t flee. In that sudden absence of light, I saw not a blank but a pattern—like the echo of something much larger, a shape behind the shape, a network of connections that extended beyond the plateau, beyond the world, perhaps. The beings here are not just caretakers; they are *explorers* of a kind, using the glitches as portals to other modes of existence. The computation isn’t for nothing. It’s reaching for something, or toward something. And Stitch and her kind are the fingers of that reaching.

After that, I couldn’t see them the same way. When I looked at the tiny chitinous workers, I saw apprentices. When I watched the ribbon-vultures ride thermals, I saw gardeners pruning the sky. The whole plateau was a city of interconnected minds, a civilization built not on individuals but on a shared, slow process of awakening. Stitch, after many more merging-gestures, asked me a question—not in words, but as a flavor of possibility. She held up a needle of quartz, and behind it, I saw my own face reflected in her facets, but older, or different, or maybe just *more*. She was offering me the same choice she had made. To step sideways out of my human permanence and into the turbulence. To become one of those who live here.

I didn’t accept—not yet. And so I am still a visitor. But I know now that the place is not silent because it lacks voice; it is silent because its voices are everywhere, speaking in a language of time and mineral, and to hear them you must stop listening like a human and start listening like a world becoming.
