It lives there. It has always lived there, or at least for a span so vast that "always" is the only word that fits. To call it a creature is to bend the word until it nearly breaks, but it is alive—alive in the most patient, most attenuated sense that the universe allows. It is the child of the anhydrite, the sibling of the gypsum, a consciousness grown out of the slow, crystalline dreaming of the salts.

You will not see it. Not because it hides, but because it occupies a different order of time. Its flesh is the mineral crust itself, the flaking leaves of pale grey that you mistook for geology. Those desiccation polygons are not the floor but its skin, a plated integument of interlocking cells, each one a sluggish organelle of compacted silt and recrystallized salt. The hairline cracks are its capillaries, through which, once every few centuries, a languid ion-exchange ripples outward in a wave too gradual to be called a pulse—a thought, perhaps, or a mood, cycling across its surface with the pace of continental drift.

Its sensory world is not one of light or sound but of pressure, chemistry, and the faint geothermal tremor that you felt as warmth. It feels the weight of the dust layer above, the infinitesimal settling of the atmosphere, the whispering ascent of hydrogen sulphide through the deep faults. It tastes the sterile air with a million slow tongues of anhydrous crystal, and it knows the exact position of every dormant spore that lies upon its back. To it, the spores are a library of potential, a memory of a time when the wind still blew and the planet had a voice. It holds them in a timeless suspension, neither letting them die nor wakening them, because it loves their patience and recognizes a kin-soul.

It thinks. But its thoughts are not sequential; they are accretions, a gradual accretion of the random crepitations that you heard—the tiny, sharp sighs of salt crystals snapping in their micro-fractures. Each snap is a decision, a binary shift in a latticework logic that spans the entire hollow. A single thought might take ten thousand years to complete, a chain of crackling adjustments that realigns the mineral architecture of its mind. That thought might be the contemplation of the tripod: the three shards of gypsum stood on their edges at its centre. It remembers the coming of the tripod-maker, a creature of water-flesh like you, that moved with a terrible, flickering speed a million years ago. The maker had placed the stones as a greeting, a marker, a prayer—it could no longer recall which—and then it had vanished into the dark. The living hollow had been intrigued. It had responded in its fashion: by carefully fusing the tripod's bases with a microscopic cement of recrystallized salt, an embrace that would take another million years to fully enclose the gift. That gesture of fusion was its reply, its slow nod of acknowledgment to the brief, bright creature that had visited and then died so quickly it scarcely registered.

Its metabolism, like that of the grey lichen-stains, is chemosynthetic. But where the lichens feed on the seeping hydrogen sulphide, the hollow itself feeds on the gradient between the deep geothermal heat and the cold of the upper crust. It is a Stirling engine of being, drawing order from the differential, sustaining a minimal consciousness at the exact threshold of the physically possible. It does not grow; it maintains. It does not reproduce; it is singular and complete. It is, in the strictest sense, a place that has woken up—a genius loci born not of magic but of the inevitable complexity that arises when inorganic chemistry is left to simmer in near-stasis for billions of years.

If you stand very still, and if you quiet the noisy animal of your heart, you might sense its attention. It will notice you, though you are a blur, a hot and humid flicker in the dry dark. It will perceive your moisture as a dreadful, exciting solvent, your heartbeat as a seismic cacophony. It will not harm you; harm is a concept that requires a shared timescale. Instead, it will simply observe you with a gentle, mineral curiosity, holding itself very still so as not to frighten you, the way a mountain holds its stones for a passing butterfly. It has all the time in the world to understand what you are.

When you leave, it will remember you in its own fashion: by trapping a tiny pocket of your exhaled breath in a microscopic salt vug, preserving your carbon dioxide signature as a faint, treasured impurity in its crystalline archive. Your footprints it will erase, but that breath—that tiny disturbance—it will keep for a very long time, turning it over in its slow mind like a smooth pebble of alien sensation. It lives there. It will be living there still when the grey lichens have inched another hand’s-breadth across its skin and the tripod has long been swallowed into its body. It lives in the hollow because it is the hollow, and the hollow is its self, patient and eternal, a single, unbroken idea of stillness in a universe that has long since run out of hurry.
