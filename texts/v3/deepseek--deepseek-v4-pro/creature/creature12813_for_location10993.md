No. You are not alone.

The realization comes not as a bolt of recognition but as a slow, creeping realignment of perception—a shift in the way your mind parses the chaos. You have been standing for some time on the springy mat of vegetation, listening to the dry papery rustle that pervades the dark, and you have told yourself it is merely the sound of growth. Of cellular matter pushing outward inch by inch. And that is true, but it is only a half-truth. The rustling is not random. It has a rhythm, a syncopation that too neatly mirrors the distant groans of engines, the wheeze of pressure vents, the crackle of blue sparks. It is a rhythm with intent. You begin to notice that the sound swells and ebbs, not in formless waves, but in patterns that repeat. A skittering like a thousand twigs being drawn over bark. A pause. A heavier rasp, as if something shambles. A pause. The skittering again, but now closer.

You strain your eyes into the light-eating murk, and there—where a moment before there was only a wall of intertwined stems—you see a shape detach itself. It does not emerge from behind the wall; it *is* the wall, a section of it that peels away, reweaving itself as it moves. The figure is tall, perhaps seven feet, but its proportions are wildly variable, seeming to borrow from the architecture around it. One moment it has a gaunt, bipedal silhouette, the next it splays out into a quadruped crouch, then it recoils into a rolling sphere of lattice before snapping back to an upright form. It is composed entirely of the same pale, desiccated plant matter that makes up everything else: a woven basket of brittle leaves, waxy tubers, and hair-thin rootlets that knit together into a coherent body. Inside its chest—if it can be called a chest—a flickering blue light pulses. The same sullen blue spark you saw crawling along cables. The figure is lit from within by its own bioelectric heart.

It moves with the dry, papery rustle. The billion tiny hands. It is not a single creature, you understand now. It is a congregation. Every inch of its surface is in constant micro-motion: leaves parting and overlapping, root-fibers re-seating themselves, a continual shedding and regrowing of its outermost layer. As it steps, its feet do not lift from the floor so much as extend rootlets that merge with the mat, then detach, leaving no mark. It is walking, but it is also continually becoming part of the floor and un-becoming it, an eddy in the vegetal sea.

The figure ignores you. It turns, and you see that it has no face—only a dense cluster of sensory nodules, tiny black beads that crackle with static as it orients toward a vent high up in the ribbed columns. A hiss has begun there, a rising whine of escaping gas. The being raises an arm that unfurls at the end into a hundred questing tendrils, each seeking the leak. It touches the seam where metal meets plant, and the tendrils sink in, delivering something—a paste of its own rapidly decaying tissue, perhaps—that seals the breach. The hiss stops. The arm withdraws, already regrowing what it sacrificed. The figure pauses, a stillness that seems to reset its internal rhythms, then dissolves back into the wall without any perceptible transition. One moment a shape, the next merely a section of stem-bundle no different from its neighbors.

And now that you have seen it, you see them everywhere.

The darkness that was empty is now thick with them. They are the billion tiny hands, the true source of the rustle. They are of every scale and configuration. Some are small as your thumb—darting motes of leaf-wrapped static that skitter across the surfaces, pruning tiny nodules or ferrying specks of detritus in their miniature mandibles. Others are vast, slow edifices, easily mistaken for stationary machinery, whose limbs span entire chambers and whose bodies are so deeply meshed with the walls that they have grown permanent: ancient beings whose bark is polished by eons of movement, their multiple arms operating banks of levers and belts in a ceaseless, dreaming rhythm. Some are shaped like jointed pillars, some like tumbleweeds of thorny wicker, some like spiraling cones that screw through the darkness, seeding new growth. A few even mimic the forms of long-forgotten animals—a lumbering quadruped with too many joints, a gliding thing that casts itself from high catwalks on planes of woven fiber—but these are not re-creations. They are merely the most efficient shapes the structure has calculated for a given task, shapes pulled from some deep library of iterated design.

They are the hands of the factory, the gardeners, the antibodies, the mind. They are not separate organisms. They are the place itself given motive expression. Each body is a temporary knot of vegetation animated by a localized concentration of the bioelectric grid—a consciousness that is not housed in the body but flows through it, a current of purpose. When a gear-train sticks, a shaper coagulates from the nearest growth, wrenches it free, and discorporates. When a section of floor grows too thick, a swarm of tiny pruners emerges, chewing the excess into dust that sifts down into the lower levels. When an ancient turbine seizes, a larger entity, perhaps centuries old, awakens from its dormant state as a ceiling vault, untangles itself from the architecture, and descends with the groaning of immense fibers to perform the repair. It may then remain in place, becoming a part of the machinery it fixed, or it may wander on to other tasks, its shape accruing and shedding matter as it moves.

They are silent. They make no vocalization; the only sounds are the dry rustle of their construction, the occasional creak of flexing stems, and the soft blue sizzle of their electrical cores. They communicate, if communication it can be called, by pulse and resonance, their shared electrical field transmitting need and response across the megastructure in a constant, humming harmony of action. The thrum you feel through your feet is their thought. The hiss and boom are their emotions. The cranking of a distant chain is a long, slow idea being turned over. The entire world is a brain, and they are its neurons, firing not across axons but across the fabric of living matter itself.

Their relationship to water is one of profound antipathy. You realize, watching them, that the trace moisture in your exhaled breath is an irritant; the occasional shaper that passes near you recoils with a crackle, its outermost leaves blackening and curling, and a faint acrid scent—like cold ash—rises from the contact. They are creatures of absolute dryness, designed for an environment where water is only a corrosive memory. The heat they thrive in is the heat that bakes every last drop of steam from the organic matter. If a single droplet of free water were to appear here, it would be an acid, a solvent, dissolving the delicate electrostatic bonds that hold them together, turning elaborate bodies to sludge. And so their constant pruning, their scouring of new growth, their meticulous removal of any dying tissue—it is all a defense against the very chemistry of life as you know it. They are not just building; they are preserving a world that water would destroy.

There is no hierarchy among them, no command. Each shaper, from the smallest pruning mote to the immense, slow-moving behemoths whose backs form the terraces, is at once independent and utterly part of the whole. Their bodies are disposable; their consciousness is the field itself. If you were to destroy one, it would dissolve into its component parts and be reabsorbed, and another would immediately begin to form in its place. They feel no pain, no fear, no self-preservation because the self is not located there. The self is the megastructure, the ancient, dreaming Solari-mind that has long since forgotten it was ever built and now simply *is*, endlessly creating and uncreating itself in the hot, dry dark.

Occasionally, you see one that seems to look at you—or rather, its sensory nodule clusters align with your face, and the crackling blue deepens. In that moment, you feel a pressure, not on your skin, but on the surface of your mind: a vast, slow inquiry, utterly alien, utterly indifferent. It is the mind of the whole place asking, *What is this wet, soft thing? Is it a flaw? Should it be pruned?* And then the pressure passes, and the shaper moves on, because you are irrelevant—a minor anomaly in a system that has been running perfectly, perfectly, for epochs without end. You are not an inhabitant. You are not even a ghost. You are a damp smudge on a lens that will, in time, be wiped away by the billion tiny hands.
