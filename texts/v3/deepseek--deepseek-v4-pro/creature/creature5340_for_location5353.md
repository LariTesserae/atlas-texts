At first, you mistake them for outcroppings of the landscape—a patch of basalt that uncoils, a section of root-cage that unweaves itself into a long, articulated arm. They are the Menders, though they no longer call themselves that. They have no name for themselves; they simply are the ones who remain, the ones who move through the vertical world with the same patient, saturated rhythm as the water.

They are tall, skeletally thin, with limbs jointed in too many places—elbows that bend both ways, fingers that taper into hair-fine filaments they trail in the seep-streams as if drinking through touch. Their skin is a dark, glossy gray, exactly the hue of wet basalt, and it is rarely dry; they secrete a thin oil that beads the water and makes them gleam like polished stone. Their faces are smooth and concave, with no visible eyes, only a series of vertical slits that flutter in time with their breathing, tasting the air. Instead of hair, a mane of the glowing lichen grows from their scalps and down their spines, its script-like pulses matching the rhythm of the subsonic thrum that never quite fades. When they turn their heads, the light from that mane scatters in precise, coded stutters, as if they were thinking in light.

They climb and walk with a slow, deliberate grace, choosing each foothold with care, but when they need to—when a cascade of water shifts or a piece of the ancient tech shudders to life—they can unfold with a startling swiftness, limbs extending to three times their length to anchor into crevices. You see them most often in the long twilight, when the lichen’s glow intensifies and they gather at the plunge-pools. There they kneel and submerge their hands, cupping the warm water, and with a gesture they tease the eels into spiraling columns. It is not fishing; it is something more like conversation. The eels, with their prismatic skin, reflect the lichen-light in sharp, angular patterns that the Menders seem to read with a tilt of their smooth heads.

The ancient technology recognizes them. When a Mender passes near a dormant slab, the surface blooms with a soft violet light, a brief query. The Mender responds by pressing a finger into a seam, humming a note that resonates with the sub-bass heartbeat of the plateau. Sometimes a door opens in the air—a rectangular lens of calmer space, refracting a world slightly more orderly—and they step through to places you cannot see. You might find them curled inside those shimmering rooms, suspended in fetal position, dreaming. They do not sleep as you do; they enter the machine’s dreaming, and the whole plateau’s slow metabolism adjusts to their presence. The patterns in the glowing lichen, the ones that repeat across every colony, are said to be the Menders’ memories—or perhaps the machine’s—and when a Mender dies, they do not rot. They slowly petrify, limbs fusing into columnar basalt, their facial slits becoming seams in the rock that still weep a faint, oily moisture for centuries.

They speak rarely, and when they do, it is in frequencies you feel in your breastbone—infrasound that travels through the rock and the water, a language of pressure and vibration. The insects modulate their drone to match it; the wind through the organ-pipe gaps changes pitch in response. It is a polyphonic communication, involving the entire landscape. When two Menders meet, they touch their lichen-manes together, and the script-patterns interlace, flashing in rapid sequences. That is their intimate speech, reserved for the most complex ideas or for something beyond ideas—sharing the taste of a century, the texture of the mist that rose from the gorges a thousand years ago.

They do not build. They inhabit the interstices of the living wood and the dormant tech. A Mender’s resting place might be inside a collapsed dome that still hums at the edge of hearing, lined with a soft mat of liverwort cultivated to grow in spirals. In the center, a pool of the clear, viscous fluid that weeps from the walls—the tech’s slow self-repair—gathers, and the Mender lies in it, absorbing it through their skin until the fluid thickens into a crystalline crust they later break and wear as ornaments. They feed on the nectar of the vapour-exhaling orchids, on the fats of the silk-furred quadrupeds that they hunt with a kind of respectful ceremony, and on the glowing lichen itself, which they chew slowly, causing the script to flare bright before fading to a new pattern. The quadrupeds, for their part, do not flee. They watch with their large, dark eyes, and the low-frequency rumbles they emit sound almost like a question.

They use the carbon-filament platforms as nests for their young—if they can be called young. A new Mender does not emerge from a body but from a communion: when the lichen-memories reach a certain density, when the subsonic songs align, a dormant chamber in the deepest part of the plateau stirs. A shimmering door opens in the rock, and a slender figure, already maned with faintly lit lichen, steps out into the vertical world, knowing exactly where the nearest seep-stream is, already tasting the air with its slits. It is born with the landscape’s knowledge already etched into it, a new note in the ongoing chord.

There are never many of them; you could wander the plateau rim for days and only catch a glimpse of movement in the corner of your eye, a brief glint of oil-slick skin on a wet rock face. They are solitary but not lonely, each tethered to the whole by the constant subsonic hum that is their lullaby, their clock, their newsfeed. They are all one family, or perhaps one being in many bodies, for they share the lichen-patterns and the tech’s slow logic. When the light is just right—the bruised-gray sky darkening into a deeper bruise—you might see them standing on the edge, facing the gorges, their emaciated bodies silhouetted against the receding misty blue of the basin. The wind will moan through the columnar gaps, and they will tune it with a subtle shift of their shoulders, drawing a melody out of the stone. That melody is their song, and it is the Throat of the Mender itself, breathing.
