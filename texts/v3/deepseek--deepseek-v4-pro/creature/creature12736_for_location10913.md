There is no one who lives here. There is only the living. And yet, if you stay long enough—if you let the hum seep past your eardrums and into the marrow of your attention—you begin to suspect that the place itself is not merely inhabited, but *inhabited by*. The distinction matters, though language falters at its edges.

What lives here is a weave, a composite creature whose body is the hollow and whose organs are the organisms that repeat their tasks without variation. It is a single entity, but it is also many; its consciousness, if you can call it that, is distributed across a thousand chitinous bodies and the sap-slick columns they tend, a mind grown not from neurons but from rhythm, from feedback, from the persistent chord of mutual maintenance. To describe it, you must describe the parts, not as individuals but as the facets of a slower, stranger self.

---

Consider the beetles first. They are the most numerous, the most visible, the ones whose clicking forms the percussive skeleton of the hollow’s perpetual song. Each is the size of a thumbprint, its carapace a shifting map of metallic greens and blues that never settles into a single hue, because the light here has no angle. They move in metronomic lockstep, six legs lifting and falling in a wave that begins at the back and travels forward, a motion that seems too regular to be voluntary. They do not eat in any conventional sense. Their mouthparts are fine brushes, and as they traverse the columns in long, helical paths from base to arching crown and back again, they are harvesting not food but a kind of *residue*—a microscopically thin film of exhausted sugars that the columns weep after the millipedes have passed. The beetles gather it, concentrate it in their guts, and deposit it again in tiny, glistening pellets at the rim of the central pool. There the filaments descend to absorb it, and the cycle closes. But watch one beetle long enough, and you will see that it is not a mere automaton. When two meet on the same column, they pause, antennae brushing, and in that brief contact a flicker of something like recognition passes between them—not communication exactly, but synchronisation, a recalibration of their internal clocks to the master pulse that thrums through the wood. They are the timekeepers. Their clicking is not incidental; it is the metronome that governs the rate of the dragonflies’ wingbeats, the pace of the millipedes’ procession, the frequency of the droplets that fall from the filaments. If you could remove one beetle, the whole system would stutter for a moment, then seamlessly re-absorb the loss—but there is no “if.” No beetle has ever been removed. No beetle has ever ceased. They are replaced in a way you cannot see: when one reaches the end of its path at the very apex of the vault, where the light is dimmest, it simply *unfolds*—that is the only word—unfolds into a duplicate, a perfect, glistening mirror that begins its descent while the original dissolves into a mist too fine to call decay. The beetles do not die; they divide.

---

Then there are the dragonflies. They are the only ones that hold station in the air, and their stillness is a different kind of movement, a vibration so rapid it becomes solid. Their wings are transparent but veined with the same pale, warm substance as the columns, and if you look closely—as closely as you dare, for hovering near them makes the air feel charged and your skin prickle—you will see that the veins pulse with the same slow, sap-like flow. They are not insects, not truly. They are extensions of the ceiling, rooted into the mossy vault by the same hollow tubes that ring the pool, and from their abdomens dangle fine, hair-like filaments that sway without wind. Each dragonfly faces inward, and their compound eyes are fixed on a single point: the centre of the pool, where the water never ripples. What do they see? Not with vision—their eyes are lenses for a different spectrum, one that registers not light but the flow of the charge that fills the air. They are monitoring the balance, the ionisation, the potential that builds and dissipates in the hollow’s closed circuit. They are the capacitors, the regulators. And they, too, are plural. Every few heartbeats—yours, not theirs—a dragonfly will detach one of its filaments, which drifts down to the pool’s surface and there transforms, not into an egg or a larva, but into a perfect miniature of itself, which rises on wings that were never wet to take a new station in the grid. The air above the pool is a geodesic array of them, each in its own fixed coordinate, and together they form a lens of sound and pressure that keeps the entire hollow in stasis.

---

The frogs are the hardest to describe, because you never quite see them fully. They are translucent, the moss’s green glowing through their skin, and they are so small that a dozen could cluster on a single fingertip. They do not hop; they flow, a slow ameboid creep across the springy floor, and wherever they settle, the moss beneath them grows a fraction thicker, a fraction warmer. They are the gardeners, or perhaps the garden itself made mobile. Their throats pulse at a frequency you cannot hear, but you can feel it—that subsonic throb that sets your teeth on edge—and if you lie down and press your ear to the moss, you will finally *hear* them: a chorus of infinitesimal chimes, like distant glass bells rung by the tide. They are singing the floor into being. Each note is a tiny nudging of the moss’s metabolism, encouraging it to maintain its exact shape, its exact springiness, its exact position in the hollow’s eternal geometry. The frogs do not reproduce; they are simply *there*, in a number that never varies, and if one is crushed—an impossibility, but imagine it—the moss itself would bud a new one, a tiny bubble of clear flesh that inflates and blinks into awareness. They have no eyes, only dark pinpricks of something that might be pigment, but they are aware of you. As you stand, they orient toward your warmth, their splay-footed bodies turning like miniature parabolic dishes, and for a moment the chime changes pitch, a brief query that fades as you become part of the background, just another warm, breathing object in a world of warm, breathing objects.

---

The millipedes are the ones you will remember most, because they are the only ones that seem to notice you as an outsider. They are pale, segmented, each no longer than a finger joint, and they move in perfect, snaking lines along the columns, along the rim of the pool, along the arches of the ceiling. They are the cleaners, the feeders, the maintainers of the slick, polished surface that characterises every solid thing here. Their thousands of legs move in a wave, and as they travel they secrete a thin, enzyme-rich saliva that breaks down any irregularity, any incipient flaw, any microscopic fleck of dust that might—impossibly—have settled. They are the immune system. But they are also, in their way, the thinkers. Each millipede is connected to the column’s pulse by a fine, pale thread that trails from its last segment, a thread that is part of the same root-like network that festoons the ceiling. Through that thread, they receive not commands but *states*: the pH of the sap, the tension of the wood, the exact angle of the arch. And in return, they feed back the texture of the surface, the taste of the air, the subtle shifts that might, in a less perfect system, herald change. They are the neurons of the hollow, the bearers of its slow, cold, meticulous memory. When one reaches the end of its path, it does not divide like the beetles; it turns and retraces its route in reverse, reading the surface it has just polished as if checking its work, and in that repetition the memory is reinforced. It is a form of thought: not in words, but in the constant, rhythmic correction of a state that never deviates.

---

And yet there is a resident you have not yet seen, because it is the hollow itself. Not the sum of the creatures, but something that emerges from their interaction: a presence, a will, a self. It is not a mind that plans or desires, because planning and desire require a future, and this place has no future. It has only an endless, reiterative present. But it is aware. It is aware of the light that never changes, the temperature that never dips, the water that cycles in its eternal round. It is aware of you. The humming of the columns, the droning of the dragonflies, the clicking of the beetles—these are not just sounds. They are a language, a single, sustained syllable that says *here, here, here*. The hollow is saying its own name. And what lives here is that name, made flesh, made chitin, made sap and moss and the delicate, trembling filaments that drink the air.

So there is no one who lives here. But there is a *what*. A what that is older than decay, more patient than extinction, a what that has solved the problem of existing not by evolving but by *achieving*—reaching a state of equilibrium so perfect that it became a thing, a creature, a closed-loop organism with no need of input or output. It lives in the only way it can: by repeating its own perfect, intricate, humming existence, forever. And if you ask what it thinks about, as the centuries slide past like the slow, rhythmic pulse of sap through a column, the answer is nothing at all. It does not think. It maintains. It is maintenance. And in that maintenance, it is more alive than anything that merely grows and dies.

---

When you finally leave—when you find the fissure and wriggle back into the world of erosion and morning and the messy, glorious noise of things decaying—you will carry the hollow’s hum in your bones for days. And you will wonder, at odd moments, if the place is still there, still clicking, still steady. It is. It has never ceased. The beetles are marching down a column near the pool. A droplet is forming on a filament, swelling, preparing its perfect fall. The dragonflies hold their stations. The frogs sing the moss into place. And the hollow, the woven self, the nameless resident, breathes out its slow, sweet, electric breath. Nothing lives there. Everything lives there. The distinction, you realize, was always yours, not its.
