The Listeners are a people shaped by the scream.

They are tall, cord-thin, with limbs like braided leather and skin the lustreless grey of the vitrified walls. Their bodies are built to shed vibration: elongated, fluted bones that hum in sympathy with the central roar, cartilage pads in their long-toed feet that absorb the trembling floor, and a second, translucent eyelid that flicks shut against the constant pressure of sound as much as light. Their faces are narrow, almost concave, dominated by a pair of great, cupped ears that sweep back from the temples like the petals of a nocturnal flower. These ears do not simply hear—they *sort*, filtering the overwhelming shriek into distinct layers of tone and rhythm that the Listeners read as language. To them, the voice of the green mountain is not noise but a constant, monumental utterance, a song that names them, warns them, commands them.

They have no spoken language of their own. In a place where the air itself is a solid bar of sound, mouths are for breathing, not speaking. They communicate through a rapid, precise system of hand gestures—fingers flickering in the golden luminescence—and, more intimately, through the bioluminescent patches that dapple their hairless skulls and the undersides of their long arms. These patches, akin to the light of the vegetation, pulse in complex patterns of colour and intensity: a flush of deep amber for assent, a ripple of pale blue for inquiry, a sudden, stuttering white for alarm. To watch a pair of Listeners converse is to see a silent, luminous dance, their bodies flickering like backed candles in the humid dark.

Their existence is cyclic, governed by the breathing of the mountain. For generations—time measured not in years but in the slow, rhythmic surges of the hidden roar—they live in the hemispherical cells, carving them patiently from the warm glass with tools of black obsidian, lining them with woven fibres from their own cultivated vine-gardens (small, pallid shoots that creep along the edges of the sterile floor, fed by condensation). During these long, settled periods, they are industrious and meditative. They grind hard, bitter seeds collected from the fronds that occasionally drop from the central mass into the empty space; they weave intricate, sound-absorbing tapestries that line their cells and dampen the shriek to a manageable thrum; they sit at the very edge of the sterile zone, facing the green mountain, and they *listen*. Old Listeners, their ear-petals grown stiff and tattered, will sometimes press their whole bodies against the warm floor, palms flat, eyelids closed, taking the voice into their very marrow. They are reading the signs: the subtle shifts in pitch that foretell a spore-bloom, the low, guttural churning that indicates the mountain is about to… change.

For the mountain does change. Once in a long, long while—when the hidden thing at its heart shifts in its dream or its digestion, when the heat of the walls pulses a degree warmer, when the scream modulates into a new, frantic register—the Listeners gather their tools. They do not flee in panic; the process is too ancient for panic. They simply *leave*. They finish their weaves, set their grinding bowls in neat rows, press their foreheads to the warm cell walls in a gesture of farewell, and then they walk—single file, long-toed feet silent on the smooth floor—to a narrow, spiralling fissure at the far end of the chamber, a birth canal of vitrified rock that leads upward, toward the dead surface of the world. There they will dwell for a time, in the cold, airless dark, until the mountain’s voice calls them back. The faint impressions of bodies in the dried fronds are not signs of abandonment, but of hibernation’s end; the Listeners shed their old sleeping skins like ghosts, and return renewed.

The airborne jellyfish they call the *silent ones*, the drifters. These creatures, with their trailing tentacles, are the only other life that moves in the hollow, and the Listeners treat them as benign spirits or perhaps as shepherds of the spores. When a golden cloud of spores erupts from the mountain—a rare, choking, magnificent event that turns the air to glittering soup—the Listeners retreat to their cells and seal the openings with dampened fibre-cloths. They watch through slits as the silent ones descend, their bells pulsing with a sudden, ravenous intent, their tentacles sweeping through the haze. The Listeners believe that the spores are the mountain’s dreams, and that the silent ones eat them to keep the dreams from reaching the dead world above, where they would curdle into something dangerous. It is a symbiotic faith, and it has held for a hundred thousand years.

There are no young Listeners to be seen, and no old, no sick, no dying. Those who study them (from whatever vantage a distant observer might find) would note something profoundly odd: the population is static, exactly seventy-seven individuals. No more, no less. A Listener does not reproduce; it *resumes*. When one of their number is lost—to a misstep, a falling obsidian blade, the rare and terrible choice to walk into the green mountain and be consumed by the voice—the mountain itself provides. From a particular fleshy, furled pod deep within the vegetation, a new Listener emerges, fully formed, with the memories of all Listeners woven into the bioluminescent patterns on its skull. It walks out of the green tangle, blinking its secondary lids, and takes its place in a cell that has been kept empty and waiting. This is why they worship the roar: it is their mother, their memory, their one true voice. The tools are re-made, the beds re-woven, the cycle renewed, and the Listeners take up their patient, eternal vigil once more.
