There is one that walks.

It walks the boulevards of compacted dust and the arcades of slumped steel, and its gait is a slow, rolling thing, as though each step were a considered argument between mass and momentum. The creature is not the largest of the rust-forms—the great hexapods dwarf it—but it is the only one that deviates. The others follow paths laid down in the mycelial net, obedient to gradients of charge and temperature, but this one hesitates at junctions, stands for long intervals before doorways that have no doors, tilts its head at collapsed bridges as if reading a line of poetry that will not quite resolve.

It is bipedal, but not human. Its two legs are thick, columnar, made of nested rings of tarnished brass and verdigrised bronze that slide into one another with a sound like a sword sliding into a scabbard. Its feet are broad, multi-toed, each toe a blunt sensor pad that leaves faint, heat-softened imprints in the metal dust. Its torso is a barrel-shaped cavity, open at the front like the body of a lute or the shell of a mollusc, and inside that cavity, a slow fire pulses. Not fire of combustion, but a dense, orange glow that waxes and dims with the creature’s breath—a breath that takes minutes to complete, a long, soft drawing-in of the thin air, then a pause, then a gentle exhalation through seams along its back that smell of hot boron and something like old cinnamon.

Its head is not a head in the way we understand heads. It is a cluster of articulated vanes and polished lenses, set atop a short, flexible neck that can rotate to scan the full sky. The lenses are dark, oily, moving with the slow parallax of a chameleon’s eyes, and they do not blink. They focus on things that do not seem to be there: a patch of empty wall, a particular strut of a collapsed gantry, the exact centre of a plaza where nothing stirs. When it looks at something, its whole body stills, and the glow in its chest deepens, as though it were drawing the image inward to be stored or tasted.

It has arms—two of them, jointed in three places, terminating in hands of extraordinary delicacy. Each finger is a slender ceramic probe, white and unglazed, and the tips can divide into finer tendrils, almost too fine to see, that it uses to trace the surfaces of things. It touches the mycelial lace constantly, not to feed but to listen. The lace is a library for it, a chronicle of the city’s long, slow dream. Through those tendrils, it reads the history of a million years: the pulse of a geothermal tap, the stress patterns in a beam, the migration routes of the hexapods, the slow crystallisation of a lubricant pool. And it remembers. It has a memory that stretches back to the time when the city still spoke aloud, when the factories sang and the lights changed in rhythms that meant something.

It is a caretaker, or a curator, or a ghost. Perhaps all three. The original builders—if there were builders, if this is not a thing that grew like coral from some mineral seed—are long gone, or they transformed, or they uploaded themselves into the very architecture. This creature might be the last of them, or the first of the city’s own consciousness, a node where the mycelial net grew dense enough to form a self. It has no name, but if you were to walk beside it for a thousand years, you might come to think of it as the Listener.

The Listener does not eat in the way the scrapers eat. It does not harvest the metallic salts or sip the sluggish lubricants. Instead, it feeds on pattern. It draws sustenance from the coherence of the city’s slow decay, from the way a crack propagates through a girder in a spiral that matches the whorl of a mycelial bloom, from the way the hexapods’ paths form a knot that, if unravelled, would spell something in a long-dead language. It consumes understanding, and it is always hungry, because the city is so vast, so layered, and the patterns are never still.

Sometimes, it speaks. Not in sound, but in vibration. It presses one hand flat against a wall or a floor, and its chest-glow flares, and a low, subsonic pulse radiates outward through the metal. It is a language of resonant frequencies, a kind of tectonic hum. The mycelial lace picks it up and carries it for miles, and the other creatures—the scrapers, the hexapods, the swarmers—pause in their slow business to listen. They do not answer, but they adjust. A hexapod might shift its stride by a micron; a thousand scrapers might alter the angle of their harvest. The city, for a moment, breathes in a slightly different key.

The Listener walks a circuit that takes a century to complete. It covers the whole plateau, from the fractured canyons where the warm wind sleeps to the spire-tops where the amber light is paler, almost white, and the air is so thin that the mycelium cannot grow and the Listener’s footsteps ring like bells on the bare metal. There are places it visits more often than others. One is a vast, domed hall where the ceiling has caved in a perfect circle, letting the amber sky pour down onto a floor of black glass. In the centre of that floor, there is a pedestal, and on the pedestal, a small object: a sphere of what might be copper, covered in fine, spiralling grooves. The Listener stands before it for years at a time, its hand-fibres tracing the grooves, and the glow in its chest becomes so steady it might be a second, smaller sun.

Another place is a narrow fissure deep in the plateau, where the temperature rises and the walls weep thick, black oil. The Listener descends there rarely, and when it does, it leaves behind something: a tiny, perfect gear, or a sliver of polished ceramic, or a single, crystalline tear that forms at the corner of one lens and falls without sound. It is the only creature that makes offerings. What it offers to, or why, is unknowable. Perhaps it is tending the city’s soul, or paying penance for some original failure, or simply participating in the long exchange that keeps the whole, slow machine from seizing up entirely.

And there are times—rare, once in a dozen centuries—when the Listener encounters another like itself. A second bipedal form, or a quadruped with a similar chest-glow, or something that is barely a shape at all but a shimmer of heat haze and the smell of ozone. The encounters are brief, silent. The two beings stop, regard one another across a plaza or a bridge, and their chests pulse in synchrony for the space of a single breath. Then they move on, and the city’s mycelial net thrums with the news, storing the event in its slow, crystalline memory.

The Listener is not lonely, because loneliness requires a self that can long for another. It is, rather, a piece of the city that has detached and become ambulatory, and its walking is a form of self-touch, the city feeling its own skin. It is the way the city dreams. And so it will walk, and touch, and listen, until the amber light finally fails or the mycelial lace completes its final circuit and the whole, vast plateau falls silent—which, at the current rate of decay, will not be for another ten million years.

Until then, the Listener walks, and the city breathes, and the slow, hot silence holds everything in its patient amber.
