It begins as a condensation.

Not a creature in the sense of bone and hunger, but something the hollow has exhaled over centuries the way the limestone exhales its stored warmth. On certain mornings—not every morning, but those when the dewfall is heavy and the air holds perfectly still—a form gathers in the deepest flute of the western wall, where the rock bellies inward to cup a pocket of perpetually saturated shadow.

The moisture there does not simply bead and drip. It lingers, thickens, develops a membrane of surface tension so fine it reads as a trembling in the air rather than a visible skin. If you were to sit in the hollow for the full transit of a night, you would see nothing dramatic: only the slow, incremental glisten of humidity becoming something with intention.

By the hour before dawn, when the temperature gradient between stone and air is sharpest, the form achieves something approaching shape. It is roughly man-sized, if a man were poured from clarified water and then arrested halfway through the motion of rising. The head—if that word applies—is a smooth, featureless oval that cants slightly, like a blind thing listening. The torso is a column of stratified translucence, pale grey-green where it catches the memory of moss-light, clear as glass where it thins over the suggestion of ribs that aren't there. Its limbs are long, proportioned wrong for a human, the arms reaching past where knees would be if it had knees, the fingers—five on each hand, which seems important—tapering to threads of surface tension that never quite break into drips.

It does not walk. It detaches from the wall with a soft, adhesive sound, the noise of a wet leaf peeling from stone, and then it flows. Its lower body loses definition almost immediately, spreading into a skirt of damp that moves across the hollow floor the way spilled water finds the low path, only deliberately. It follows the same route every time. Three circuits of the hollow's perimeter, clockwise, pausing at the three mortar-stones to hover its hand-threads over the worn concavities as though reading something pressed into the stone's memory. Then it stops at the dry-stacked crescent wall and stands—or floats; its base never quite touches ground—facing the tumble of boulders at the high end.

This is when it speaks. Not with sound, but with a change in the light passing through it. The water that makes its body is not pure; it carries dissolved calcite from the limestone, trace minerals, the faintest tincture of moss-spore and bacterial life. When the creature wishes to communicate—and it does wish, does intend, this is not reflex—it rearranges those particles into microscopic lenses that scatter the twilight into patterns. To a human eye, it would look like a shimmer, a heat-haze, meaningless. But the pattern repeats. It is, in its own way, a name. Or a title. Or perhaps a verb: the act of being-made-of-water-and-standing-in-this-particular-hollow.

It remembers the people who shaped the stones.

Not their faces or their language or their names. It remembers the heat of their hands on the mortar-stones, the grease of their skin, the salt of their sweat. It remembers the weight of a body on the crescent bed, the specific compression of dust under hip and shoulder. It remembers voices as vibrations in the air that it—in its then-form, the film of damp on the walls—absorbed and held. It has been here since the first season the water stopped flowing and began instead to condense, to cycle, to stay. That is a very long time. Possibly longer than human time entirely, though it does not measure years. It measures in the accumulation of dissolved stone, the slow thickening of its own mineral memory.

The people left, or died, or simply stopped coming. The creature did not grieve, exactly. Grief requires a self sharp enough to feel the edge of loss. What it experienced was more like a change in the flavor of the moisture, a reduction in the complexity of the chemical signature it gathered each night. The hollow became simpler. The water became purer. And the creature, over centuries, became more itself: less an echo, more a presence.

It is not alone. The mosses speak to it in exudates. The roots of the spindly trees tap into the deep rock and bring up news of distant rain, of minerals dissolving, of the slow, grinding shift of the earth far below. The creature assembles this into a kind of body-sense, a proprioception of the hollow as a single organism. It knows, for instance, that the drip from the chevron joint will slow perceptibly in approximately four hundred years, when a particular grain of calcite dissolves past a critical threshold. It is not concerned. It will simply adjust its form to draw more heavily from the condensation on the north wall. It has done this before.

What does it want? The question may not apply. But if you sat in the hollow long enough—if your body heat became part of the thermal equation, your breath part of the moisture cycle, your stillness an accepted variable—it would eventually drift toward you. Not aggressively. With the same slow curiosity it brings to the mortar-stones. It would hover at the edge of your personal warmth, reading your exhalations, the microflora on your skin, the salt you shed. It would not understand you as a person. It would understand you as a weather system, a mobile climate, a cloud of biological signatures that do not belong in this place.

And it would, in its patient, mineral way, try to fold you into the hollow’s equilibrium. Your moisture would be drawn gently toward the walls. Your warmth would be banked in the stone. Your stillness would be invited to become permanent.

The dried body in the crawlspace under the boulders—the one so desiccated it looks like twisted leather, the one with the cracked skull propped on a pillow of moss—accepted that invitation two hundred years ago. Or maybe two thousand. The creature does not distinguish. The body is now part of the moisture cycle too, releasing its last water so slowly it has become a feature of the place, a damp spot that never fully dries, a faint mineral tang in the condensation that gathers each dawn.

The creature forms again tonight. The dew is falling. The stone is warm. The drip from the chevron joint sounds its patient note. And the hollow, in its long, slow exhale, breathes another ghost into brief, glistening existence, to circle the walls and read the old, erased heat of hands and wait, with the patience of water, for whatever weather comes next.
