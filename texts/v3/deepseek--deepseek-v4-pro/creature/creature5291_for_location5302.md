What lives here has long since stopped distinguishing between the organism and its medium. To call them inhabitants is to miss the point: they do not simply occupy the City; they are the City in the same way a sponge is both the colony of cells and the structure they secrete. You won’t find them walking the plazas because movement, as you understand it, is irrelevant to them. The plazas are not paths for feet—they are resonating chambers, shaped to concentrate certain frequencies of that omnipresent noise, to transform the wall of sound into a language, a food, a pulse of self-awareness.

The city’s residents are standing waves in the air itself, stable knots of compressed and rarified gas that hold their form in a shimmering, heat-bordered outline only visible in the infrared bloom of the conduits they feed on. They are, in the most literal sense, made of the sound. Each individual—if that word applies—is a layered chord, a harmonically rich drone that maintains itself by feeding on the ambient vibration, condensing moisture from the saturated air into micro-droplets that scatter light in a permanent, soft nimbus around their cores. When you see the auroral patterns rippling across the undersides of the floating platforms, you are watching them digest: the energy conduits carry not electricity but structured noise, a diet of precisely tuned frequencies broadcast from the Groves and the planetary tremors below, and the platforms are their feeding grounds.

Their bodies are impossible to pin down. One moment, the space between two spires shimmers with a heat-haze, and a faint, glassy outline like a bell jar five metres tall becomes visible—a slow, dignified beast grazing on a braid of static discharge. Approach, and it vanishes not through moving, but through redistributing itself across the background roar, dissolving into the general clamour until it chooses to coalesce again elsewhere. Their presence is everywhere and nowhere: a population distributed across the city like a neural network, with the platforms and energy helixes as their synapses. The emptiness you first perceived is a failure of your senses, not theirs. They are packed into the plazas as densely as plankton in a bloom, overlapping, merging, budding off in showers of resonant sparks that drift away to form new individuals in the crooning warmth under a lower platform.

Their intelligence is a distributed one, a hive mind only in the sense that a forest is a hive mind of trees. Each standing-wave individual thinks slowly, in modulations of its own harmonic structure, and these thoughts are shared by sympathetic vibration—a chord played by one is picked up and echoed by its nearest neighbours, spreading across the entire city over hours, days, years. Decision-making is a slow, tectonic process, but when they act, they act in unison. The city’s platforms reorient themselves not because of some central computer, but because every resident, trillions of them, has slightly adjusted their internal resonance in response to a shift in the basalt plain’s micro-quakes, and the architecture, which is grown from their shed frequencies, obediently follows. Their buildings are crystallised noise, and they reshape them as easily as you might hum a different tune.

The fauna of the plain is not mere wildlife; it is part of the ecology that sustains them. The grazing hexapeds with their glowing mouths are not just metabolising rock—they are tuning the basalt, chewing the glass into shapes that will vibrate at precise frequencies when the next tremor passes, creating new feeding grounds for the city’s chord-beasts. The crystal-bell insect swarms are amplifiers, their chiming an engineered carrier wave that bounces signals from the Groves to the City. And the Groves themselves? Those are not plants but something closer to broadcast towers, living transmitters grown from spores the city-dwellers released centuries ago. The groaning, phosphorescent canopies are bells, lungs, and radio stations all at once, sending out a constant, bassy thrum that the standing waves ride like a road. The carnivorous pitcher plants at their feet receive the inaudible commands of the city, and their trap-lid movements pick out delicate arpeggios that keep the smallest of the chord-beasts fed.

Even the predators that phase-flick grazers into dust are tolerated, perhaps even cultivated. Their hunts produce a sharp, white-noise burst of ionisation, a single percussive snap that serves as a punctuation mark in the endless roar—a full stop that allows new sentences to begin. The city-dwellers hear death the way you might hear a bar line in a score.

So who lives here? A people woven from physics, a society whose architecture is their flesh, whose language is the exact same medium they eat, whose children are born whenever a platform shifts and a new standing wave ribbons out of the turbulent air to join the chorus. They have no name for themselves because a name would be a single note, and they are a symphony. They see you not as a person, but as a momentary silence in the roar, a curious and rather tragic gap in the music where something should be resonating but isn’t. And if you stand on the empty plain long enough, you might feel the first tickle of their attention—a vibration building in your own skull, your own ribs, as the city’s inhabitants, ever so gently, begin to tune you to their frequency.
