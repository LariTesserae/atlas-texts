The first sign of them is never visual. It’s a change in the rhythm of the place—a syncopation in the factory’s heartbeat, as if the great pump-roots paused to listen to themselves. Then you notice the mist gathering oddly, not the formless drift of the plateau’s perpetual seep, but a slow spiraling, a hesitation. It collects around the base of a tower-factory, curling upward along the fluted cambium, and as it reaches a certain height it thickens, refusing to disperse. Something is pulling the moisture together, not consuming it exactly, but borrowing its materiality, drawing shape from the air.

They emerge like this: not born, not built, but precipitated. Condensation figures. For a long while, you might mistake one for a trick of the light, a shimmer in the corner of your eye. But then the shimmer holds, and the form resolves into a column of damp translucence, man-high but not man-shaped. The proportions are all off—limbs too long, torso too narrow, the head a tapering ovoid like a teardrop of fog. They stand on the root-mat as if floating, their contact a mere dampening of the surface, no weight. They have no skin, no membrane you could call a boundary. Their substance is a temporary truce between the water in the air and the pollen-dust of the city’s exhaust: a living colloid, a walking cloud. The plant-factories ignore them entirely, or perhaps they regard them as one might ignore one’s own breath.

But they are not passive. They move with purpose, these mist-formed beings, drifting along the broad avenues between towers with a gait that is less walking and more flowing, their outlines trailing faint eddies. The industrial noise changes when they’re near—the hisses modulate, the thumping steadies. They are attending to something.

Watch one long enough, and you’ll see it approach a valve-lipped aperture in the base of a tower-factory. A slender limb extends—it doesn’t exactly touch, but imposes itself into the aperture’s space, and the steam that vents there suddenly shimmers with color, the same silent flash of abnormal spectrum the plateau is prone to. The being’s form drinks the flash, and for an instant it becomes solid, its surface a glossy, opalescent membrane stretched over something that is not entirely body, but a complex internal scaffolding of fibers and tubes, like a sketch of a circulatory system drawn in sap. Then the color fades, and it is mist again, moving on.

They are the operators, or perhaps the operation itself made visible. The plateau’s glut of interconnected cycles—weather, biology, machine—produces a kind of surplus, an informational residue. Over geological spans, that residue gained a kind of coherence. Not intelligence in the sense of thought, but something more like a distributed reflex, a maintenance routine that learned to externalize. The mist-people are the city’s proprioception, its sense of its own body. They don’t have names, though if you stood close enough to one and listened past the creaks and groans, you might catch a sound like a single syllable drawn out into a whole breath: *szhhuuuur*, the sound of sap under pressure, the foundational note of their existence.

They are not solitary. In the spaces between towers, you might see several at once, a handful of them converging where a frond-belt has frayed, or where a resin-cake has hardened too early. They don’t work on the machines in any mechanical way—they can’t grasp or push. Instead, they lean in, merging their diffuse forms with the plant-material, and reality softens. The frayed belt re-weaves itself from loose fibers that drift upward as if gravity had forgotten them. The resin cake un-hardens just enough to be channeled properly. The effect is temporary; the material slips back to its natural state afterward, but the moment of correction is all that’s needed. They are the city’s immune system, you might say, except the city isn’t under threat—it’s just sustaining its ancient, impossible equilibrium against the wear of eternity.

Perhaps once they were clearer, more distinct. There are places near the center, where the heartbeat is loudest and the ground feels most like a membrane over a void, where the mist-people congregate in greater numbers, and there you can almost see the shape they might have held long ago: a more humanoid silhouette, definite hands, a suggestion of features. But time has worn them down as it has everything else, smoothing their edges into these elegant, anonymous wisps. They don’t remember being anything else. Their existence is the performance of a function, and the function is the only self they have.

They are eerily beautiful, especially when the light brightens to its pale silver maximum. Then their bodies become faintly prismatic, scattering rainbows onto the damp root-avenues. They leave trails of moisture that glisten like snail tracks, and the air around them smells, for just a moment, of something that isn’t water or sap or industry—a clean, raw scent, like ozone after a lightning strike, the smell of the interface where biology bleeds into physics and back again. It’s the scent of the reality breakdown itself, and they carry it with them like a forgotten memory.

You might wonder if they are aware of an observer. The answer is: probably not. They are not designed to notice. Their attention is entirely inward, focused on the vast internal hum of the city. But if you stood in their path, they would not walk through you. They’d eddy around, like a stream around a stone, and for a fleeting second you’d feel a sensation—not cold, not wet, but a profound hollowness, as if your own interior spaces were briefly echoing with that single syllable, *szhhuuuur*. It’s not a communication; it’s just what happens when a living body intersects with a body that is only process. And then they’d be gone, following the next pressure-drop, the next creak calling from a tower half a mile away, leaving you alone in the gentle, industrial, eternal hush of the Needleleaf Plateau.
