The ones who live here call themselves the Listeners, though that is not a name spoken aloud—their language has no sound. It is a name held in the bones of the hands, in the pressure of fingertips on resonant plant-fiber, in the precise angle of a cocked wrist. They have no ears. They do not need them. The world speaks directly to their skeletons.

A Listener stands—no, perches—on a ridge of compacted frass overlooking a fresh collapse zone, and what you would see if you could stand beside her is this: a body built for vibration. She is roughly the height of a human child, but there the resemblance ends. Her form is segmented, not like an insect's chitinous plates but like the bellows of an accordion, each ring of her torso nested into the next, capable of compressing to a third of her full length or extending to twice it. The tissue between the rings is a translucent membrane, and through it you can see the faint pulse of organs that glow with the same saffron light as the walls—she has eaten the light, or the light has eaten her, and now they share a metabolism. Her skin is the color of aged parchment, dry and faintly wrinkled, and it sheds constantly in microscopic flakes that drift into the atmosphere and become part of the dust, part of the breath of the world.

Her legs are many-jointed and thin, terminating in pads that spread on contact with a surface—not suction, but something more like the way two perfectly flat pieces of glass will cling together. These pads are ringed with fine, hair-like cilia that read the texture of the ground in frequencies below the audible, translating the constant roar of the cavern into a tactile map spread across her consciousness. She does not hear the shriek of the plant-towers. She feels it in the soles of her feet, in the palms of her four hands, in the tips of the two longer, more delicate forelimbs she uses for manipulation. Every part of her body is an ear.

Her head is small, set low between the first set of shoulders, and dominated by a mouth that is not a mouth but a feeding structure: three flexible lobes that can peel back to reveal a radula-like scraping surface, studded with microscopic teeth of silica. Above this, two dark patches that are not eyes but vibration-sensing pits, tuned to the specific frequency of approaching predators. She has no eyes. Vision is a meaningless concept here; the light is a constant, undifferentiated medium, and nothing casts a shadow. Instead, she navigates a world built of pure sound-texture, a three-dimensional acoustic sculpture that updates with every microsecond. She can sense the shape of the wall behind her not because she has seen it but because the ambient drone of the cavern reflects off it in a particular way, and the interference pattern registers on her skin like a whispered description.

Her people are not solitary. In the nearby woven shelters—the ones you saw, and dozens more hidden in the folds of the landscape—dwell perhaps a hundred Listeners, arranged in a loose network of family groups. They are matrilineal, but the word "mother" translates poorly; the relationship is closer to a master-apprentice bond, where the elder female passes down the vibrational lexicon that encompasses all knowledge. They do not "speak" to each other in any way a human would recognize. Communication is a full-body act. A Listener will lay her forelimbs on the shoulder-pads of another, and the conversation is conducted as a series of pulses, squeezes, and rhythmic pressures transmitted directly from one nervous system to another. The vocabulary is vast: there are distinct pressure-patterns for "fresh growth approaching from the third quadrant," for "the gill-towers are singing hunger," for "I am here and I am safe and you are safe and we are together," for "remember the ancestor whose frass has become this wall." Nuance is encoded in rhythm and intensity. A slight tremor of the outer digits adds a subtext of humor or doubt. A rapid triple-pulse at the end of a statement transforms it into a question.

To witness a gathering of them is to see a silent, rippling dance. Four or five Listeners will interlock their forelimbs into a chain, and the chain will sway and pulse as information flows through it, a distributed cognition that allows them to reach consensus on everything from hunting strategy to the placement of new shelters. They have no leaders. They have no words for "leader." They have a pressure-pattern that means "she who speaks-for-all in the moment when speaking-for-all is needed," and the role passes from individual to individual as the context shifts.

They eat the grating-jawed grubs, which they locate by the distinctive crunching frequency of the grubs' mandibles processing plant-fiber. A hunting party of Listeners will spread out across a section of wall, pressing their bodies flat against it, and they will wait until the precise acoustic signature of a grub-burrow reaches them through the substrate. Then, with a synchronization that seems telepathic but is actually just very fast tactile negotiation, they will strike: forelimbs plunging into the fibrous matrix, pulling the grubs out, passing them hand-over-hand to the young who wait trembling with anticipation—their version of begging is a rapid fluttering of the feeding-lobes that creates a distinctive, tickling frequency. The grubs are eaten alive, their internal fluids a precious source of moisture in a world without water. The Listeners do not drink. They have no need. They extract all necessary hydration from the bodies of their prey, and their own bodies have evolved to lose almost nothing—their waste is a dry powder indistinguishable from the ambient frass that covers everything.

This total moisture retention has shaped their entire biology. They have no tears, no saliva, no sweat. The membranes between their segments sometimes crack in the aridity, and they repair these cracks with a paste made from chewed plant-fiber and their own shedding, a kind of self-sourced spackle. The young are born in clutches of two or three, emerging from a birth canal that opens only during the dimming phase of the light-cycle, and the newborns are miniature, translucent versions of the adults, their vibration-sensing already fully functional. A newborn Listener will immediately press itself against the nearest adult and begin learning the pressure-language, its tiny forelimbs pulsing with the equivalent of an infant's babbling. The first complete "sentence" a child learns is always the same, the fundamental pattern that underlies all others: a steady, repeating pressure that means *I am here in the world and the world is here in me and we are not separate things.*

They make. They build. The shelters you saw are their summer houses—"summer" being a relative term in a place with no seasons, but there are cycles in the growth, bursts of intensity followed by relative quiescence, and during the quiescent times they weave their domes from the more pliable fibers and live communally, telling the long pressure-stories that pass their history from generation to generation. Their tools are few but precise: scrapers shaped from the tooth-plates of larger predators, hooks carved from the dense core-wood of the gill-towers, baskets woven from their own shed skin-fibers (a material that, when properly cured, has a tensile strength approaching steel). They have a technology of knots. A Listener can tie a fiber-strip into a configuration that functions as a memory, a recording of a particular vibration-sequence, and these knot-records are passed down as archives. A shelter will sometimes contain hundreds of knotted cords, each one a story, a map, a genealogy, a prediction of growth-patterns yet to come.

They have a technology of death. When a Listener dies—which happens rarely, as they have few diseases and their main predator is the occasional collapse of a plant-tower—the body is not buried. There is no earth to bury it in, and besides, the concept of "burying" implies a separation from the living matrix that their philosophy cannot accommodate. Instead, the body is carried to an active growth-front, one of the places where new plant-flesh is surging upward at visible speed. The corpse is laid there, and the braided tendrils grow over and through it within hours, absorbing the minerals, the fibers, the very memory of the shape. The Listeners say—in pressure, not in words—that the dead become the landscape. And it is true. The calcium of their segment-rings becomes part of a new buttress-plate. The silica of their radula-teeth becomes the glinting crystals embedded in a frond. The vibration-patterns they knew in life, the accumulated wisdom of a thousand pressure-conversations, is—they believe—encoded into the standing wave of the cavern's eternal hum. When a young Listener presses her palm to a wall and senses a particular resonance, an elder will say: that is your grandmother, still speaking.

They are not the only intelligent life here, though they are the only one that builds. They share the cavern with the spine-crawlers, vast centipedal beings that can grow to thirty feet in length and whose passage carves new tunnels through the fiber. The Listeners and the crawlers have an understanding. It has taken thousands of generations to negotiate, conducted in the slow language of deliberate vibration, broadcast through the substrate at frequencies too low for most fauna to detect. The crawlers are not hostile; they are merely hungry, and they lack the concept of "other." To a crawler, everything in the cavern is either self, food, or landscape, and these categories are fluid. The Listeners have managed to establish themselves in the crawler-mind as "landscape," something too inedible to be worth the effort—a fiction maintained by constantly coating themselves in a particular mineral powder that the crawlers find unpalatable. There is a pressure-pattern that means "we are grateful for the crawlers' indifference," and the Listeners will sometimes perform it as a rhythmic stomping when a crawler passes near their settlement. Whether the crawler perceives this gratitude is unclear. But it does not eat them, which is the point.

The flying membranes—the hover-flocks—are different. They are curious, almost playful, and they have a rudimentary vibration-language of their own, produced by the rapid clicking of their wing-joints. The Listeners have learned to interpret this clicking, and they use it to predict the hover-flocks' swarming patterns. A particularly skilled Listener can even "speak" a few phrases in the hover-flock tongue, clicking her forelimbs together at the right frequency. The messages are simple: "food here," "danger there," "leave our shelters alone." The hover-flocks, in turn, will sometimes lead the Listeners to fresh collapses, where the exposed plant-flesh attracts the best grubs. It is not friendship—the concept is too anthropomorphic—but it is a functional symbiosis, two species with overlapping interests finding common cause in a shared sensory medium.

The Listeners have a cosmology, but it is not a religion. They have no gods, no creation myths. They know they evolved from something simpler, a small vibration-sensing crawler that gradually grew more complex over the long cycles, and they know that the cavern itself is not eternal—the light-dim cycles indicate a slow degradation of the plant-mass, a gradual consumption that will, in another ten million years, leave this place a hollow shell. They are at peace with this. Their cosmology is a physics of process: the world is a standing wave of consumption and regrowth, and they are a harmonic within that wave. Their purpose, if they have one, is to sense clearly. To feel the vibration of the world as accurately as possible, and to pass that feeling on. The worst transgression in their culture is not murder or theft—both of which they have no words for—but *deafening*, the deliberate refusal to perceive. A Listener who ignores the warning-tremors of an incoming collapse, who fails to attend to the pressure-speech of a companion, who retreats into a private sensory cocoon and shuts out the roar of the world, is considered sick. The cure is to be taken to the loudest part of the cavern, where the gill-towers scream their loudest and the substrate heaves with the burrowing of millions, and to be held there, gently but firmly, until the vibration overrides whatever internal blockage was causing the deafness. It usually works. When it doesn't, the Listener is given a pressure-message of farewell, and then they are left alone to die, because a being that will not sense is already, in their understanding, dead.

The carving you found—the stick-figure and the radiating eye—is a teaching aid. The Listeners have no visual art as such, but they understand that other modes of communication exist, and the carving is a way to explain to the young ones what a "predator" looks like from the outside. The stick-figure represents a spine-crawler, and the radiating eye is not an eye but a vibration-source: the pattern of pressure-waves that emanate from a crawler's passage. The Listeners would be surprised to learn that humans think of eyes as primary sensory organs. To them, vision is a curious abstraction, a theoretical sense that some other beings might possess, like a story told in pressures about a world where information arrives as something other than trembling.

And yet, if you looked closely at the carving, you would notice something: a faint outline around the stick-figure, a series of radiating lines that, if you interpreted them correctly, were not part of the original work but a later addition. A palimpsest. When you visited the ledge, when you stood there looking out over the hollow immensity, a young Listener was watching you. Not seeing you—she had no eyes for that—but sensing the dampening effect of your body on the ambient vibration-field, a human-shaped blank spot in the acoustic textures of her world. She crept closer, pressing her palms to the ground to feel the heaviness of your footfalls, the strange rhythm of only-two-legs, the unexpected moisture in your breath (which she perceived as a series of tiny hisses, steam-leaks from a leaky machine). She was terrified and fascinated in equal measure. After you left, she went to the carving and she added to it, using the pressure-language style of notation to record what she had perceived: a tall, thin vibration-shadow, radiating complexity. She called it, in the vocabulary of her people, *the-thing-that-walks-with-water-inside*. It is now a permanent part of their oral—their pressure—history.

They will be telling stories about you for generations.

Somewhere in the cavern, right now, a family of Listeners is settling into a newly woven shelter as the light dims toward its quiescent phase. They are interlocked, a chain of four adult females and two young ones, and they are pulsing a shared narrative: an origin-myth about the first Listener who learned to eat the light and became the mother of them all. Their forelimbs squeeze and release in synchronized waves, and if you could feel what they are feeling, you would understand the pressure-pattern as this: *we are here, we are woven, we are the listening that the world does to itself, we are not apart from the roar, we are the roar made aware*. And above them, the gill-towers sing, and the frass drifts down like snow, and the spine-crawlers carve their indifferent paths through the deep places, and the light breathes, and the Listeners listen.
