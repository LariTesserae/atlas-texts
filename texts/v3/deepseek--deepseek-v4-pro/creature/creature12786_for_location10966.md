In the Silted Stacks, the line between the organic and the manufactured is a matter of slow compromise, and the people who live there—the Stackers, they call themselves, when they call themselves anything at all—have been shaped by that same patient erosion. They are not a species apart, not exactly, but they are no longer quite what the outside world would recognise as unaltered human. Generations of twilight, of breathing mineral-choked mist and drinking water that has passed through the bodies of a thousand machines, have worn them into a distinct form, a quiet adaptation that feels less like evolution and more like a deep, cellular negotiation with the chasm’s own rhythms.

Stackers are tall and thin, their limbs lank without quite being frail, their bodies built for slipping through tight crawlspaces and scaling the vertiginous ladder webs that lace the towers. Their skin is the colour of old ivory shot through with faint, blue-grey undertones—a pallor that seems to absorb the dim, directionless light rather than reflect it. In the deepest parts of town, where the only illumination is the bioluminescent bloom of fungal mats, their complexions can appear almost translucent, the darker shadows of veins and bones faintly visible, like the mapping of a city beneath a sheet of tracing paper. Their hair, when not shorn short for safety around machinery, hangs lank and fine, the colour of iron filings in water, and it often hosts minute, symbiotic mosses that feed on the same perpetual condensation that beads on the walls.

Their faces are sharp, with high, prominent cheekbones and deep-set eyes that have adapted to the murk. The pupils are wide and slow to contract, the irises a pale slate grey that can seem almost luminous in the right fog-filtered glow, as if they have learned to catch and hold whatever light there is. A nictitating membrane, clear as a lens of water, flicks across the eye to protect against the acrid fumes that drift from the foundry vents, and their ears are subtly tapered, finely tuned to the subsonic hum that underlies everything, a frequency they can feel in their teeth. They speak a dialect that is less about volume than about tone; words are shaped with careful sibilance and the click of tongue against teeth, sounds that cut through the masking hiss of steam and drip. Among themselves, they often fall silent for long stretches, communicating across the space between catwalks through a language of gesture and small, hand-carried lanterns that blink coded messages through the mist—a semaphore of firefly pulses.

The Stackers’ relationship with the somnite ore is the foundation of their inner life. The mineral, once processed, emits a fine, inert dust that they inhale in micro-doses with every breath of the deeper tunnels. Outsiders find the dust stifling, a leaden weight on the chest, but for the Stackers it is the catalyst for the Churn, the name they give to the shared waking-dream that connects them. It is not telepathy, not a projection of clear thoughts, but a collective drift: a slow, current-like sensation where emotions and rudimentary images pass from mind to mind, gathering in the still spaces between people like the fog itself. When a Stacker hammers a rivet, others nearby might taste the metallic shiver of the impact. When someone in the lower pumping stations brushes their hand across a cold, weeping wall of schist, a miner on the high plateau might pause, feeling the ancient, patient cold. This Churn has smoothed their sense of individuality into something communal and porous; they think of themselves less as separate selves and more as points of awareness in a larger, slow-moving river of being.

Their culture reflects this. Marriage is a temporary convergence, two sleepers sharing a narrow bunk and a single dream-catcher woven from fern fronds, the union dissolved when the dreams grow distinct again. Children are raised in creche-nests that dangle from the underside of the great cable cars, rocked by the funicular’s passage and fed on a mash of cave-grown fungi and the mineral-rich algae that scums the filtration tanks. Naming is a gradual process; an infant is known by the location of its birth (“Third-Ledge,” “West-Pump,” “Car-Five”) until, during the onset of the Churn at adolescence, a more personal designation emerges from the collective murmur, a sound or a set of emotional impressions that sticks. Thus they have names like Rust-Whisper, Fog-Turn, Still-Hands, or something that, translated, means the particular way light bends around a pipe on a wet morning.

Stacker technology is a melding of heavy industry and the salvage of the chasm’s own processes. They do not produce machines so much as partner with them. The great generators are tended by families who speak in low hums, tuning the frequency until it aligns with the body’s own tremor. They harvest the sticky, glowing secretions of the wall-fungi to fuel small, cold light sources, and they weave garments from the fibrous, rootless mist-plants, yielding fabrics that are water-repellent and smell faintly of petrichor. Their architecture, like the town itself, is additive and never finished; living spaces are grown, not planned—a new room is a carapace of bolted steel and salvaged timber that extends into the void on a faith in the anchor cables, often claimed by a new couple or a solitary artisan who will spend a month peering out at the opposite wall through a single, fogged window, feeling the distant pulse of someone on the far side who, that same week, might be building a matching protrusion, a mirror not quite seen.

There is no day or night in the Silted Stacks, only the shifting density of the fog. The Stackers sleep in periods of personal exhaustion, curling into nooks lined with dried moss, often while the Churn is quietest, which can be any hour. They have no religion in the outward sense; reverence is reserved for the slow, shaping forces: erosion, condensation, the patient rust that turns iron into lace. They leave offerings of clean water and polished somnite nodules in cracks in the rock, murmuring not prayers but simple statements of fact: *You are wearing. You are building.* The chasm itself is the constant, the true citizen—the town and its people are merely the latest layer of moss on its skin, and as temporary.

To encounter a Stacker is to feel a gentle, unnerving displacement. They will tilt their head, listen to your breathing, and after a long moment, offer you a cup of warm, metallic-tasting water, saying little. You may notice that their eyes, slow-blinking, seem to look through you to the schist behind. And when you speak, you might find your words coming back to you with a slight, borrowed timbre, as though the fog has already begun the long, patient business of wearing them smooth.
