The first thing you notice, when you finally notice it, is that the shrimp are not swimming at random. You had accepted the dense, churning clouds as a kind of living weather—a blizzard of pale bodies and pinprick bioluminescence that swirled wherever the currents willed. But you have been standing here long enough now for your own shock to settle, for the mineral steam to bead on your eyelashes and the roar of a thousand tiny cascades to press into your skull like a steady weight, and in that stillness you begin to see the architecture beneath the chaos. The shrimp are avoiding something. Not fleeing, not schooling—simply parting, the way water parts around a stone, leaving a shape in negative.

It is a shape you cannot quite hold in the mind. Every time you think you have traced its edge through the absence of blue flashes, the shrimp surge back, or the water shifts, or one of the naked bulbs overhead swings on its cable and throws the shadows into lurch. But the impression holds: a corridor, a breathing room, a blankness that moves through the crowded pools with a slowness that is not quite geological but is certainly older than any animal you have ever known.

The second thing you notice is the sound. Not the water—you have learned to live inside that noise now, to let it become a kind of silence—but a new frequency, a crawl of vibration that comes up through the soles of your boots and makes the bones of your ankles hum. It is rhythmic, a pulse that does not align with the arthritic clank of the pumps. Those still cycle on their timer: hiss, rise, pause, clank, fall, hiss. This new throb is slower, a deep subsonic oar-stroke that seems to travel not through the air but through the stone itself. You feel it in your molars long before you hear it as sound. When you open your mouth to breathe, your jaw aches with it.

The bats know. Overhead, the ribbon of their exodus still pours out through the ragged oculus, but its edge has begun to fray. Individuals break from the column and wheel back into the cavern, their chittering rising to a frantic screech of echolocation that you feel as a needle-shower on your skin. They are mapping something, you realize. Something that has shifted position, something large enough to return a shape in the cross-talk of their clicks. You watch the bats spiral and dive, and in the sodium-vapor stain that passes for light, you see them avoid the same empty architecture that the shrimp do. For an instant, a shape is carved out of the mist by sound and absence together: a long, low mass in the deepest of the stepped basins, a form that does not belong to the elegant geometry of the rimstone dams.

Then the third thing happens, and it is light.

Not the sulfurous orange of the dying bulbs, not the blue-gray spectral hint of the daylight shaft, and not the brief constellations the shrimp throw off with their tails. This light is own. It begins as a smudge of faint green on the terrace edge just below you, a color you recognize from the bacterial mats—that corpse-glow that thrives on the mineral broth. But the glow is moving. It spreads in slow, deliberate veining, tracing outlines that are not random. You are looking at something like a pattern, something like a map, and it is spreading across the surface of the water in filaments that branch and reunite, a luminous dendritic map. The filaments are alive. They are a mycelial net of some kind, a biofilm of connected light-pulses that ride the surface tension, and they are crawling toward the shape in the deep pool with the purpose of a returning limb.

The shape receives them. It does not move; it merely ceases to be an absence and becomes a presence, the way a rock emerging from a lowering tide becomes first a shadow, then a solid form. The shrimp withdraw further. The blue lights of their tails dim as though hushed. And the thing in the basin—it is a basin now, you see, a single vast pool that holds the whole hollow’s overflow, a final sump before the water drains into the fissures that birthed it—the thing in the basin begins to glow in answer.

It is not one thing. Or rather, it is one thing that has become many times itself. The body—if body is the word—is a composite of overlapping plates and whorls, a living travertine reef that has grown not by accretion but by intention. The mineral has been laid down along organic templates: chitin, collagen, cartilage, something more ancient than any of those. The creature is huge, stretching perhaps thirty feet along the long axis of the basin, and its shape is nothing the surface world has a name for. Imagine a caddisfly larva built at the scale of a whale, its case assembled not of pebbles but of its own excreted calcite, sculpted over centuries into a labyrinth of chambers and spires. Imagine a giant tube worm that has erupted from its shell and spread across the floor, its soft, segmented body bearing rows of bioluminescent gills that shift from corpse-green to a deep, tender blue. Imagine a cephalopod that has forgotten the ocean and learned the patience of stone, its arms not tentacles but slow, hydraulic fans of tissue that finger out through the shallower pools, their undersides fringed with the same bacterial fur that paints the terraces.

All of these are true. None of them is sufficient. The being you see is a palimpsest of forms, a creature so deeply co-written by its environment that it has become taxonomically illegible. It breathes. That’s the pulse you feel in the stone: a massive, internal gill-bellows sucking the mineral-laden water through a series of sieving mouths and expelling it, cleansed of calcium, through vents along its ridge. Each cycle takes nearly a minute, and with each exhalation, a fine cloud of unused calcite drifts up to settle on the nearest surfaces—on the rusted catwalks, on the frayed cables, on your own unmoving boots.

It does not have eyes, not in any sense you could recognize. But it sees you. The mycelial net on the surface is its sensory organ, a living retina of glowing green that spreads across every pool in the cavern, reading the vibrations of footfalls, the taste of unfamiliar metals in the water, the heat-ghost of your body prickling the steam. The net has already encircled your position; it has been doing so for the last ten minutes, you understand now, creeping up the rimstone dams and along the catwalk supports, and what you took for a slowly brightening patch of bacteria on the railing beside your hand is actually a deliberate, local concentration of attention.

Its intelligence reaches you not as a voice but as a rearrangement of the noise. The hiss and splash and gurgle of the terraces, the chitter of the bats, the clank of the pumps, the thrum of its own breathing—all these, which have filled the chamber as a steady-state roar, begin to phase. They cancel and reinforce, setting up a standing wave of sound that forms a pattern in your skull. It is a pattern that repeats, and in the repetition you begin to recognize a structure, something almost like language. It is not words. It is older than words. It is the sound of a place being born, and it is asking you a question.

The question, as far as you can translate it into thought, is this: Are you here to be consumed, or to be kept?
