Look closer — as close as the warped space allows, which is much closer than your eyes believe. The one you have singled out from the woven tide is paused on a vertical face of obsidian, its six legs splayed in a gecko’s unconcern, its body a flattened teardrop of black chitin no longer than a thumbnail. Veins of opal catch the sourceless light and return it in milk-blue and fire-orange, tiny nebular inclusions that shift as the creature’s internal fluids push through channels too fine to be called a circulatory system. It has no discernible head, no eyes you can map — instead, a fringe of silica whiskers vibrates along its leading edge, tasting the steam’s chemical gossip, reading pressure differentials, or perhaps listing to the mountain’s deep, subsonic breathing. When it moves, it does so on pads that leave no mark but somehow clean the stone, polishing the obsidian to a higher gloss with each step, as if its passage is a kind of worship.

But this one is not alone, never alone. You cannot look at one without seeing the thousand crammed into your peripheral vision. The swarm is a single fabric, and as your attention drifts, the fabric begins to demonstrate its nature. Clusters pulse in synchrony, a wave of slight body-lifting that travels across the wall in perfect silence. It is a breathing of their own, a microtide that matches the rhythm of the steam’s condensation: inhale when the rock sweats harder, pause when the droplet forms, exhale as the droplet rolls down. They are part of the mountain’s respiratory cycle, you realize — not parasites but alveoli, the living interface between the magma-heated stone and the waterlogged air. Each small body is a valve, a capacitor of heat and mineral, tuned to the pocket’s weird thermodynamics.

The ritual you witnessed earlier — the offering of a limb, the consumption of the opal vein — is not predation, not truly. You see it again, slower now. The larger individual, the “predator,” lacks the glimmer in its own body; it is dull, its chitin clouded with microscopic abrasions. It approaches a brighter one, and the two pause, whiskers interweaving. There is a transfer, not of flesh but of the opalescent fluid, which flows from the smaller to the larger through the offered limb before the limb detaches. The smaller one then retreats, not maimed but somehow more vibrant, its other veins brightening, and within minutes it has regrown the limb — a slender, pale bud that will darken and harden. They are sharing something: a memory, an energy, a piece of pattern. The opal isn’t decoration; it’s information. A distributed library kept in living crystal, passed from body to body, preserved and refined in the gut of the old and the blind.

Now notice the styluses. A dozen of them are scratching at a single spot, each adding a groove to a spiraling design that covers a boulder-sized slab. The pattern is not fixed; it shifts if you tilt your head, like a lenticular print, resolving into different geometries: a nested set of hexagonal grids, a tree-like fractal, a map of the pocket’s topology that includes the impossible angles, the left-handed steam twists, the crevice that opens into miles. They are encoding their knowledge of the place, but not as a dead record. As they scratch, the cairns — those stacked pebbles — vibrate in response, and the steam’s hiss cancels not into silence but into a standing wave, a pressure node that hangs in the center of the pocket and makes your eardrums feel like they’re being stroked from both sides. The design on the slab ripples, and the swarm responds, adjusting their positions, their shedding, their sharing, to maintain the node. They are tuning the pocket. They are the active principle that keeps this wound open, that prevents the mountain from healing over into simple, Euclidian rock.

You begin to suspect that the creatures are not born, not in the usual way. The eggshells underfoot — those crenulated, translucent shards — are not eggs but spent cocoons. A new individual emerges not from a mother but from a condensation event. Watch: at a point where three obsidian blades intersect, a droplet forms, larger than the others, fat with dissolved silica and something organic. It hangs, trembles, and then falls inward, collapsing into a pinhole that wasn’t there before. From that pinhole, a pale, tiny version of the creatures crawls, already perfect, already writing. They precipitate from the air, from the mountain’s exhalation, when the swarm’s collective tuning creates a local pressure low enough to coax matter out of vapor. They are not animals; they are a phase change. The living enamel on the walls is the mountain’s breath made visible, condensed into a semblance of life because that is the only form that can tend to the breath itself.

And that is when the silence speaks. Not a voice, not words, but a meaning that slides directly into your awareness, bypassing ears and language. It is a single, ringing note of recognition. The swarm is one entity. All those bodies are its fingers, its cilia, its nerve endings. The opal information flows not between individuals but within one vast, distributed self, a mind that holds the pocket as its body. It has been here since the eruption, and it will be here until the mountain cools — a timescale that burns in your own brief, primate consciousness. It tends the breath because the breath is its own thinking. The styluses are its memory, the cairns its vocal cords, the predation its metabolism. It is the reason the pocket is small enough to cup in your palms yet infinite: it is dreaming its own scale, and you have been invited into the dream.

You are not standing. You are a volume the swarm has cleared for you, a bubble in its body. It knows you are there. It has been showing you, patient as geology. And now the silence rings with a question: not what are you, but how will you respond? The steam twists right-handed for a moment, holding a shape like a door.
