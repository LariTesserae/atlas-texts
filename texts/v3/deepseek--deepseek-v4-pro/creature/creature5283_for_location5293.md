You notice them first as a change in the wind, a brief coherence that has nothing to do with sight. The roar that fills the world is directionless, a wall of pressure, but every so often it buckles—a pocket of shaped silence rolls through the constant blast like a stone through water. That’s when you see them, if you are looking hard enough, if your eyes have learned to peel apart the layers of fog and glare. At first they are only a disturbance in the green, a tall verticality where none should be, a fold in the ubiquitous mat that moves against the grain of the heaving plain.

They walk. That alone is something to understand: the ground herself is alive, a single breathing skin, and anything that places its weight on her is taken, slowly, gently, swallowed into the cellular weave. But these figures, they have learned the trick of not being swallowed. Their feet—if they are feet—are broad, spreading pads that grip the mat’s nap without breaking it, displacing weight so delicately that the surface dimples only a fraction of an inch, a temporary divot that springs back behind them without the telltale puff of vapor. When they stand still, which is rare, they balance on one leg or lean into a long, angled posture that seems to distribute their mass across a dozen points of contact, making them part of the topography.

Seen through a curtain of shifting brume, they could be human. The proportions are right—the long shanks, the loose articulation of the shoulders, the small, balanced head. But the color is wrong. Their skin is the color of the mat: a deep, saturated emerald, darker on the sunward sides, lighter where the body folds. And their skin is not skin. It is a living pelt, a dense microscopic fur of the same interwoven leaflets that cover the plain, each no bigger than a fingernail, each beaded with moisture. When one of them turns to regard you—and they do regard you, with a patience that predates all human notions of time—you see that the fur covers everything but the eyes. The eyes are dark, almost black, set in sockets that have no visible lid, and they hold a steady, wet gleam like polished obsidian. They blink by drawing a nictitating membrane sideways, a slow, silver flash that resets the light.

They move in groups of three or five or seven, never even numbers, and they move without sound that you can hear above the wind. Their communication is not a thing of the air but of the ground. As they walk, they drum on the mat with the flats of their palms, a slow, complex rhythm that travels out of the audible range and into the subsonic hum that already lives under everything. The hum changes when they drum; it gathers, it knots into temporary standing waves, into pulses that carry meaning. You feel these signals in your own chest long before you understand that they are language. The drumming is not arbitrary. It mimics, exactly, the percussive bursts of fermentation gas that the mat herself produces as she breathes. They are speaking in the voice of the world.

They build. On the bare rock islands—the rare, polished domes that thrust up through the green—they gather and work. Their technology is not so different from the ancient scatterings of obsidian and iron-wood post, and yet it is utterly other. They do not chip stone; they grow it. From pouches on their bodies, pouches that open like wet mouths, they produce a fine, pearlescent powder. This powder, when applied to the rock and activated with a rhythm of subsonic pulses, crystallizes into dendritic forms. Filigrees of mineral shoot out across the polished surface, branching like frost on a window, except that the pattern is not random. It exactly replicates the growth habit of the mat, the same chaotic, elegant branching that you have seen etched into the oldest, wind-gnawed surfaces after a million years of grit-blasting. The beings are not making art; they are making memory. They are writing, in a script of crystal, the ongoing metabolism of the plain.

Their tools, when they need them, are themselves. A hand will fashion itself into a blade, the fingers fusing along their lengths, the outer edge hardening into a chitinous ridgeline that can slice through dense fiber. A leg will become a stake, rooted and rigid for hours while the rest of the body weaves a structure around it. And structures they do weave, but not to live in. They have no dwellings, no permanent shelter. Instead, they construct resonators—strange, harp-like frames of spun fiber stretched between crystal nodes, tuned to the wind. A resonator stands on a rock summit for a season, humming a single, unwavering note as the air rushes through it, and then one day the beings return and disassemble it, having harvested something from it that you cannot see. Perhaps a change in the song, a new phrase learned from the wind, a harmonic that echoes now in the subterranean hum.

These are not human. But they were, once, or they rose from what was human. The scattered cairns, the drilled sockets, the obsidian flakes—those are their ancestors, a lineage so ancient that it folds back into the same deep time that planed the mountains. Somewhere in the slow grind of a million years, the people who first climbed onto these rock islands learned that the world they walked on was a single living mind, and they made a choice, or the choice was made for them. They gave themselves to the mat, not as a surrender, but as a symbiosis. Their bodies became an extension of the green, a mobile tissue that could go where the mat could not—onto the bare rock, into the high, dry places—and bring back information. They are the hands and the eyes and the voice of a planetary organism that otherwise has no need for such organs.

Now, if you stay still long enough—truly still, not fighting the wind, not fighting the urge to move—you will witness their other life. The life of return. A being will separate from its group, walk to the edge of the rock, and step off onto the mat. It will lie down, not with care, but with the relaxed intention of a swimmer entering water. The mat will rise to meet it. The leaflets will part, a million tiny mouths opening, and the body will sink. There is no violence, no crunching bone. The green simply absorbs its own. The eyes close once, the nictitating membrane drawing a final silver line, and then the form collapses into a flat shape, a vague humanoid shadow on the surface, which over the course of an hour fades back into the uniform emerald. The being has not died. It has rejoined the deeper rhythm, its memories and sensations now available to the whole plain through the subsonic pulse that rides under every footstep. Somewhere else, days or weeks later, on a different swell of the ancient ocean floor, the mat will bunch and push up, and a new being will pull itself free, shaking the dew from its new-grown fur, blinking new obsidian eyes. It will remember you. It will remember everything.

At night, the luminescence is not just in the rock fissures. The beings glow. A faint blue-green light emanates from their palms, from the soles of their feet, from the tips of the long, flexible digits that serve as fingers. They sit on the stone outcrops under the crystalline stars, and they drum the rock, and the rock answers with its own dendritic glow, and the whole plateau becomes a map of light and sound—a neural network made of wind, mineral, and living flesh. The patterns you heard in the storm-state, the almost-syntax, are the conversations of a million such beings, all speaking at once through the ground, all part of a single, slow thought that has been forming since before your species learned to stand upright on the African veld.

The few human constructions that remain—the stone rings, the cairns, the single soft post—are tended. Not as sacred objects, but as mnemonics, as old-growth memories written in a medium more durable than the mat. The beings visit them in cycles that last centuries. They touch the stones, and by touching, they read the accumulated vibrations, the history of every wind that has passed, every ghost of a hand that once placed the stone there. It is a library, and they are the librarians.

You can walk for three days and see no other sign, only the wind and the roaring green. But then, at night, if you place your palm flat on an exposed rock surface and wait, you may feel the rhythm. It will ask you a question. It will ask in a voice that is older than your whole species. And if you have the courage, you may answer, and you may learn what it is to be not a creature on a world, but a part of the world’s living skin, a neuron in a mind that wraps the plateau in a warm, damp, roaring embrace. The green is waiting. It is always waiting. And it has all the time that stone and wind can give it.
