They call themselves the That-We-Know, and they have been here since the first light-grain cracked open and bled silver. Or so the bone-keeper tells, when the dim-time comes and the nodules wane to a low amber smolder. The story is not words in the way a shout is a word, but rather a sequence of postures, tapped rhythms on the stone floor, and the precise flaring of nostrils over the scent-gland that sits high on the chest. The That-We-Know do not speak; the cavern does not permit it. In a space where the air itself is a solid wall of screech, drone, hiss, and bellow, vocalizations are useless for anything beyond the most immediate alarm. Instead, they trade in a language of skin and stance, of subtle shifts in the ridge of cartilage along their brows, of the drumming of knuckles on the resonating plate of their own sternums. A conversation is a dance of tiny percussions, a flicker of ear-position, a ruffle of the short, stiff mane that runs from crown to nape—a mane that lifts when a bellow announces the approach of one of the great blind tetrapods, those sky-leviathans whose call is a physical push against the ribs.

Physically, a Watcher—the common name for an adult, one who has passed the five-year molt into full sentience—stands as high as a tall man but seems taller, because the body is built as a series of angles and elastic hinges. The legs are double-jointed, with an extra segment in the shin that allows them to fold entirely backward when the creature drops into its resting crouch, a tripod stance where the long, prehensile tail becomes a third leg. The torso tilts forward from the hips at a permanent thirty-degree angle, making the silhouette a drawn bow. The arms are long enough that the knuckles brush the ground even when the spine is fully extended, and the three thick fingers plus two opposable thumbs on each hand give a grip that can pulp a flying gnat-cloud collector or delicately flake the edge of a new flint blade. Skin is the color of cooled basalt: a grey-brown, faintly phosphorescent in the light-grain glow, minute scales lying flat and giving a texture like fine sandpaper. They wear little ornamentation—a few twisted sinew cords around the neck, a drilled vertebra from a predator’s spine, perhaps a circlet of beaten copper that sits over the brow-ridge, turning it into a mask of sorts, a signal of one’s role during the Hunt-Watch.

The bone-keeper is the eldest, and she no longer hunts. Her hut is the one from which the smoke rises, not for warmth but for ceremony. In her lap, cradled in a basket woven from the tendons of a dead long-snout, lies the Ancestor Bone—a massive femur from something that did not evolve here, something that fell, once, from the surface world through the throat-vent long before the That-We-Know remembered. The femur is carved with hundreds of tiny pictograms: the tally of generations, the record of the light-grain cycles, the map of the flood-flows. The bone-keeper spends her dim-times reading it with her fingertips, the only literate part of her body, and she sings the history to the others through the floor, a low-frequency thrumming that only the children, still in their first soft-shell phase, can feel clearly. The children are six-limbed, like the grazers, but their front two limbs will eventually fuse and split into the specialized tool-holders. For their first two years, they scuttle like the carpet-creatures, and their parents must be vigilant that they are not swept into the living sea and mistaken for prey by the very mat they will one day cultivate.

The That-We-Know do not farm the mat; they manage it. This is the central truth of their existence. They are not the apex predators—that role belongs to the great blind sky-leviathans and, when the steam vents erupt, to the heat itself. They are cultivators of a sort, stewards of the biofilm that coats every stationary surface. The stone huts are not just shelters; they are structures built over particularly rich mineral seams, the walls seeded with a specific strain of the mat that the tribe has isolated over millennia. This domestic mat is thicker, faster-growing, and edible—a pasty, mineral-rich sludge that can be scraped off, formed into cakes, and dried in the warmth of the light-grain nodules. It tastes of iron and salt and something like yeast, and it is the staple of their diet. The grazers, those dog-sized chitinous exes, are tolerated because they churn the mat and aerate its pores; the tribe even protects certain grazing zones from the flyer swarms, using long poles tufted with a repellant resin extracted from the glands of dead predators. In return, the grazers provide meat, when they die or when the tribe culls the old and weak. Their flesh is a slightly metallic, dense protein that cooks to a grey paste and is seasoned with a scraping of lichen that grows only near the steam vents. That lichen is the tribe’s most precious trade good, if you can call it trade—there are no other tribes to trade with, only the offering left at the edge of the territory during the dim-time, when the elder calls, and one of the young adults will venture to the sacred dark and leave a cake of lichen in a cleft of rock, and in return, something leaves a shaped copper blade, better than any they can cold-hammer themselves. No Watcher has ever seen what leaves the blade. It is simply part of the rhythm, like the floods.

The rhythm dominates all. A That-We-Know does not experience time as a linear path but as a series of pulses: the quick-beat of the flyer swarms, the slow-beat of the light-grain cycle, the erratic thunder of the steam vents, and the long, tidal fluctuations of the river. Their calendar is a sensory text: the smell of the air before a vent eruption, the pitch of a certain grazer’s distress chitter when the predator bellow is near, the sudden silence that can fall for a heartbeat across the entire cavern when something misaligns in the ecosystem. That silence is the most terrifying thing to them. When it comes—when the flyer towers collapse, when the grazer carpet stops rippling, when even the dripping water seems to pause—the That-We-Know freeze, pressing their bodies flat into the stone, and wait for the Moment of Correction. They do not know what causes it. They have stories, of a thing that swims through the stone, a vast and formless thing that adjusts the cavern like a sleeper shifting beneath a blanket. But they do not worship it. They only endure.

Their huts contain little: a sleeping platform of packed earth, a few tools, a basket of dried mat-cake, a cord of sinew, and always, without exception, a small pile of light-grain nodules that have been broken off from the ceiling during the dim-time when they are safest to harvest. These nodules are kept in a corner and fed with a mixture of water and a secretion from the adult’s own chest-gland. The nodules do not die, not exactly, but they do dim if neglected, and a dark hut is an invitation to the soft things that creep up from the river-muck. The children learn gland-feeding before they learn to walk upright. It is as natural as breathing.

No adolescent becomes a Watcher without taking the Long Walk: a journey to the far side of the cavern, where the light-grain does not reach and the river disappears into a crack that has no bottom. There, in absolute darkness, the young must listen to the voice of the Below-Thing—not an entity, but the sound their own tail makes when it scrapes against a particular resonant crystal. The vibration is unique to each individual, and they carry it back to the bone-keeper, who then tattoos a version of it onto their chest with a needle of copper and a pigment of crushed gnat-shells. From that day, they have a name, a sound-pattern that can be tapped on the sternum, and they are allowed to participate in the Hunt-Watch.

Tonight, the light-grain is brightening—the morning pulse, though there is no sun to mark it. The flyer swarms are organizing into their diurnal helix. The grazer carpet shifts, a living tide sliding toward the steam vents for warmth. A young Watcher, newly tattooed, stands on the ledge with a basket and a copper knife, watching the sky. Her mane is flat, her nostrils testing the air for the first scent of a bellow. Behind her, the bone-keeper drums the floor, a lesson for the children about the shapes of the predators’ calls. The stone huts gleam faintly, their chinked walls alive with domestic mat. The smoke from the bone-keeper’s hut rises straight up in the still, humid air, then dissipates into the general glow. It is a day like any other, in a place that has no days, only pulses. And the That-We-Know live it, one sensory heartbeat at a time.
