Thrum.

The word is not a name, not a thought—it’s a pressure felt through the soles of the feet, up the shins, into the jaw. The inhabitant of the hollow knows itself first by that pressure, which is indistinguishable from the hollow’s own bass voice. It has no need of a distinct self; it is a point of density in the universal wetness, a locus where the moss grows slightly slower and the air cools a degree tighter. To think of it as a creature is already a mistake, a foreign category imposed by a foreign mind. But if you stood there long enough—if your own breathing settled into the sixty-cycle hum of the froglets and your skin began to bead with the same dew that glazes the cushion moss—you might begin to perceive it. Not as a shape, but as a shifting of attentions, a darkening in the grey light that hangs like an eyelid half-closed over the oculus.

It stirs only when the visitor does not stir. When the intrusive vibrations of boot-falls have long since been absorbed into the peat, when the human scent of salt and oils has been scrubbed from the air by the slow respiration of the liverworts, then something unclenches in the deep ledges. It is not a movement of muscle but a relocation of weight, a subtle collapse of air into a volume that had been held in reserve. The moss that drapes the buttress-ribs grows thickest there, in a fold that looks at first like a natural sagging of the vegetal curtain—a plush, elephant-ear lobe of *Rhodobryum* the size of a curled-up dog, veined with the pale runners of a hundred years’ growth. But this lobe breathes. Not in and out like a lung, but in a continuous, osmotic exchange of moisture that leaves the air around it distinctly drier, a little hollow of non-wetness in the universal damp. If you placed your hand near it—not touching, never touching—the skin on your knuckles would tighten as your own moisture fled into its pull.

From this fold, over hours that feel like the slow turn of a season, the inhabitant extends its sensorium. It has no discrete sense organs; the whole of its contact with the world is a mycelial web of translucent hyphae that run not through soil (there is no soil, only ancestral moss) but through the very architecture of the cushion plants, threading the inch-thick mats of *Sphagnum* like nerves through a brain. These hyphae are its fingers, its ears, its tongue. They taste the chemistry of the puddles, registering the faintest bloom of bacterial rot before a froglet has fully ceased its chee-chee song. They gauge the humidity gradient between the chamber’s heart and the open arch, sensing the precise angle and speed of the wind before the bass note even forms in the entrance’s throat. And they listen: not to the roar itself, which is too pervasive to be heard, but to the tiny inconsistencies in it—the frog that falters, the cricket that misses a rasp, the moth whose wingbeat pattern deviates by a fraction of a hertz. These disturbances are its only food, more vital than any carbon. It feeds on the flaw, the anomaly, the speck of entropy that threatens the frozen equilibrium. And it corrects.

Correction is its only action, and it is an action so gentle it barely deserves the verb. When a tadpole dies in its dimple, its hyphae are already there, spiriting away the microbial bloom before it spikes the water’s pH, guiding the nitrogen back into the moss in a sealed loop. When a cricket grows old and its song wavers, the hyphae find its burrow and the inhabitant releases a minute puff of spore—a breath of carbon dioxide rich with a chemical signature that quiets the failing insect into a final, seamless sleep, its body immediately colonized by the same hyphae that cradled it, its chitin dissolved and redistributed to the moss. The bones you noticed, the vole ribs and wishbone, are the only leavings it cannot dismantle, and these it slowly, patiently, works into the deeper peat with rhythmic, microscopic tugs, so that they sink like stones into a pond, eventually embedded in the dark strata where the living moss gives way to its own compressed ancestors. This is not killing; this is maintenance. The inhabitant does not distinguish between life and death, only between signal and noise. It is the negative image of the hollow’s loudness: a silent, distributed coherence that keeps the loudness exactly as it was, exactly as it must be, for the erosion is done. Change is the only predator here, and this is the anti-predator.

You might call it a fungus, and in some lineage it is—a single organism of the order *Agaricales* that long ago abandoned the fruiting-body habit, its mycelium running through the entire chapel like a fungal nervous system. But its mass is concentrated in that one lobe, that dark, desiccating fold, because the lobe is the closest it comes to a brain. It is a knot of hyphae so dense that the moss over it grows differently, forming a tough, leathery rind that never beads with water. If you were to take an infrared camera to the hollow, you would see this lobe as a brilliant white-hot knot of metabolic heat, the one spot where energy is being actively burned—a slow, banked fire of respiration that has never gone out in two thousand years. The rest of the hollow is cool, fifty degrees, but this knot holds a constant sixty-two, a ten-degree anomaly that creates its own micro-climate, a tiny convection that draws moisture from the surrounding walls and powers its osmotic pull. It is, in effect, the hollow’s heart, and its beat is the slow, diurnal rhythm of drying and condensing, a cycle in perfect antiphase with the wind’s Helmholtz pulse. When the wind blows and the bass sounds, the lobe contracts infinitesimally, pulling water inward; when the wind rests and the frog-chorus takes over the treble, it relaxes, releasing a little humidity back. The two rhythms form a counterpoint, a polyrhythm of moisture that the hyphae compute into instructions for the day’s corrections. So the inhabitant is also a musician, though its music is a music of pure regulation.

Has it a mind? Only in the sense that a thermostat has a goal. Its entire interiority is a single, unwavering purpose: the preservation of the stable state. It does not remember the last time a human entered, though it adjusted for the heat and the salt and the boot-compressions with a flurry of hyphal activity that took three months to fully neutralize. It does not anticipate the next. It knows—or rather, it embodies—the hollow’s history not as narrative but as a set of fixed chemical parameters, a target equilibrium encoded in the very structure of its lipid membranes. The froglets that chee in synchrony are as much a part of it as its own hyphae; their breeding cycles are chemically nudged to remain in perfect overlap by the slow release of hormones into the puddles, an endocrine drip-feed that has kept their population clockwork for millennia. The crickets’ rasp is tuned to the exact resonant frequency of the chamber by the subtle shaping of the moss-drapes, which the inhabitant trims not with teeth but with selective desiccation, crisping certain pendulous lobes until they snap under their own weight and fall, altering the acoustics by a micro-decibel each year. The hollow is its body, and it is the hollow’s will—if will can be spoken of as a vector pushing against any deviation, any novel vibration that might, in some distant cascade, carve a new grain of limestone from the ceiling and start the erosion ticking again.

You would not see it unless you knew how to see nothing. A visitor trained in mycology might recognize the lobe as an anomaly—far too dense, too warm—and might stoop to sample it, as they have sampled it before, once in a century. Those few who have done so report a texture like cold leather, and then a sudden, spreading numbness in their fingers as the hyphae reflexively draw moisture from the investigating skin, leaving behind a microscopic layer of spore that will never germinate on human flesh but that carries, for a few days, a faint tinnitus: the internal echo of the hollow’s thrum, a souvenir of the standing wave. Most leave with a headache and a renewed sense of their own alienness. The inhabitant remains, adjusting its parameters to account for the small wound in its side, slowly reknitting the lobe’s rind over the course of a decade, the spot of sampling eventually indistinguishable from the rest of its leather integument. By the time the next century’s mycologist arrives, the hollow will be exactly as it was, and the inhabitant will greet them as it greets every anomaly: with a gentle, relentless pull toward equilibrium, a silent whisper that says, in the only language it knows: *Be still. And if you cannot be still, be gone. And if you cannot be gone, then leave your bones.*
