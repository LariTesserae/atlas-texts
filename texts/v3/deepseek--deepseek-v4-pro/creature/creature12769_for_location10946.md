They are called the Erubim, or sometimes simply the Gardener-kin, and they are as endemic to this place as the moss and the steam-birds. No one among them can say when their ancestors first came to the Stack—whether they were the original builders who never left, or refugees from some surface calamity, or something else entirely. They have been here so long that the iron door is a legend, a myth of a “Outside” that is spoken of in children’s chants but never quite believed. The Stack-Garden is the world entire, and the Erubim are its voice and its hands.

Physically, they have been honed by verticality and damp heat. They stand tall and lean, with long arms and longer, dexterous toes that can grip a pipe or rung as well as any hand. Their skin carries a faint greenish patina, a sheen of symbiotic algae that lives in the outer layers of their epidermis and helps them wick nourishment from the perpetual humidity—a quiet adaptation across millennia. Their eyes are large, the irises a pale yellow or grey, with a mirrored tapetum behind them that makes the eyeblink shine like a cat’s in the dimmer galleries. Their ears are cupped and slightly mobile, tuned to filter the ever-present engine thrum from the subtler sounds of dripping water, shifting metal, or the approach of a co-worker. Hair is sparse; those who have it wear it in long, oiled braids woven with copper wire and ceramic beads that clink softly as they move.

They clothe themselves in what the Garden provides: tunics of woven fibre peeled from the broad-leafed vines, leggings of mammal pelts tanned in condensed tannin from the fruit trees, boots soled with the supple inner bark of a certain fern that clings to the drainage lips. Every garment is a library of patches and re-stitchings, because nothing is discarded in a closed system. They adorn themselves with found treasures—a belt of interlinked gear-teeth, a necklace of vacuum tubes whose filaments still glow a faint orange when warmed, rings of polished copper that is never allowed to go true green but is kept at a rubbed, ruddy sheen. The elders of each gallery tier file their incisors to points, a tradition whose origin is lost but which gives their smiles a faintly feral cast.

The Erubim do not merely inhabit the Stack; they tend it, and are tended by it in return. Their society is organised around the slow cycles: the dimming of lamps that simulates a long, lingering twilight, the deep throb of the turbines that waxes and wanes over the course of three hours. They sleep in shifts, some always awake to listen to the structure’s breathing. Settlement clusters hang at various levels, woven into the lattice like nests: living quarters are fashioned from the hollowed-out casings of ancient heat exchangers, broad flat platforms of grating covered with layers of moss and hide, ropes of braided vine serving as ladders and bridges. Each family group stewards a particular “reach”—a vertical slice of the Stack that might include a condensation terrace, a bank of distillation columns, and the orchard troughs on tier twelve. Their knowledge is deep and slow, passed down through chant and hand-sign demonstration rather than written word, for paper rots in the humidity and the old terminal screens are long dead.

Their days are a rhythm of cultivation and listening. They prune the vines with curved knives ground from sheet steel, guiding new growth along trellises of old piping. They collect fallen fruit, share it with the small mammals and the birds, and reserve the seeds for planting. They coax the insects to new tasks by smearing fermented pulp on certain cable trays, orchestrating the endless decomposition that keeps the place from choking on its own detritus. And always, always they listen to the machines. The engines are not inanimate to the Erubim; they are the deep heart-voices, the Grandparents. The Keepers on the lower levels spend their lives in the hot, dry halo around the turbine bank, reading the tone of each casing with a stethoscope made of a copper tube and a membrane of stretched gut. When a bearing wears, they replace it not with a new one—there are no new ones—but with a bearing they have carved from the heartwood of a seventy-year-old vine that grows in a particular damp shaft. The technology is industrial and old, and the Erubim approach it with a reverence that borders on the liturgical. They anoint pistons with rendered mammal fat, adjust belt tensions by the feel of the vibration in their own finger-bones, and when a machine dies—truly, finally stops—they paint its casing with ochre from the rust and hold a wake that involves a deep, droning hum that matches the missing frequency.

This humming is one of many sounds they make that are not quite language. The Erubim speak to one another in a flowing, pattering tongue full of clicks, pops, and whistles, many of them pitched precisely to carry over the ambient noise or to mimic the calls of the birds and mammals. A conversation between two gardeners working on different levels can be a duet of whistles that bounce off the copper walls, punctuated by the flash of a mirrored palm. They can address the animals as well—not with true speech, but with a system of signals and offered rewards that has, over generations, become a kind of inter-species pidgin. The birds with the elongated middle claws, called “pipe-grips,” are deployed as messengers, carrying small bundles of fibre or a single berry from one tier to another. The prehensile-tailed mammals, the tiskers, are trained to sniff out blockages in narrow conduits and signal with a scolding chitter when they find one. It is a symbiotic web, and the Erubim sit at its centre not as masters but as the nerve cluster that interprets and responds.

The weirdness that an outsider might note—the 0.8 of strangeness—is to the Erubim simply the fabric of existence. The chamber where a cluster of machines dances its slow, purposeless ballet for no reason that can be remembered is known as the Hall of the Old Uncles. The Erubim go there to sit and watch the arms move, to let their breathing synchronise with the rhythm, and they believe—with a certainty that goes deeper than faith—that if the dance were ever to stop, the entire Stack would groan and collapse. They leave offerings at the feet of those machines: a perfect blossom, a smooth pebble of polished ceramic, a twist of copper wire shaped into a symbol that looks like a spiral. The vines have woven around the dance in what could be an embrace, and the Erubim see that as a blessing.

The triangular marks etched into walls and catwalks—relics of some ancient hazard code—are taken as boundaries of a different order. Every Erubim child is taught from the time they can grip a rung that you do not cross a triangle unless you carry an offering of oil and speak the names of all your ancestors. The small mammals naturally avoid them; the Erubim see this not as an animal instinct but as a sign that the triangles are places where the world’s skin is thin, where the spirits of the Builders can reach through. They call these spots "wound-wards," and they maintain them carefully, repainting the triangles with a mixture of rust and grease so they never fade. Sometimes, in the steam that gathers under a vent, a shape will form that looks like a face, and an Erubim will take that as a message from the Builders—not a clear one, but a mood, a nudge. They read rust impressions on fallen metal plates like a form of augury, tracing the patterns with a fingertip and humming a response.

Their societal structure is loose but resilient. They have no king, no chief, only “Voice-Catchers”—individuals who have spent so many years listening to the structure that they can interpret the deep groans and tell when a tier is settling too fast or when a water course has shifted. Voice-Catchers are chosen by consensus when they demonstrate a knack, and they wander the Stack freely, singing low, resonant notes that they feel in their chests and using the echo to read the integrity of walls. Disputes are rare; space is plentiful in the vertical, and the Gardens provide enough. When two gardeners disagree over the placement of a vine or the timing of a misting cycle, they resolve it with a ritual of shared breath—standing close, breathing each other's exhaled air in the warm, incubating steam, until the rhythm of their lungs synchronises and the anger dissipates.

They have no written history, but they have a vast oral archive. The Great Recitation is an ongoing, communal event that can last for weeks, passed from voice to voice around the dimming cycles. It tells of the First Builders, who raised the walls and laid the pipes, and of the Great Silence when the engines stopped for a generation and the Gardens almost died, and of the Bargain where the animals agreed to help in exchange for a share of the harvest. The Recitation is not linear; it loops and branches, and the Erubim believe that by speaking it they are actively maintaining the Stack’s memory, ensuring that the structure never forgets how it was made.

Life stages are marked by climbing. An infant is strapped to its mother’s back until it can grip a vine rope; a child’s coming-of-age involves a solo journey from the lowest inhabited tier to the highest, a pilgrimage through the orchards, past the Hall of the Old Uncles, through the bright gallery where the windows look out onto another interior vastness (the Erubim call that adjacent space the Ever-Grey, and they treat it as the boundary of the world), and finally to the mist-veiled summit where the steam gathers thickest. There they sit for a full dimming cycle, listening, and when they descend they are given their first copper bracelet. Old age is not a weakness but a gradual ascent into ancestorhood; the very old become stationary, choosing to sit in a particular niche and be fed by the community until they pass, at which point they are laid in the moss to return to the Garden’s cycle. Their bones, cleaned by insects, are sometimes crafted into tools or flutes.

And through all of this, the Stack-Garden breathes around them, holding them in its warm, wet embrace. The Erubim do not feel trapped; they feel held. The constant throb of the engines is a heartbeat, and the dripping water is a lullaby. They dream intricate dreams of verticality—flying dreams, climbing dreams, dreams of copper turning green and blooming into flowers. They do not look at the iron door with longing; they rarely remember it is there. When, once in a great while, a Voice-Catcher’s echo reveals a faint, cool draft from that direction, they seal the leak with moss and grease and sing a calming phrase, putting the Outside back out of mind. The 0.8 weirdness is their normal, and they are, in every sense, alive in a way that is indivisible from the place that shaped them. They are the Garden’s thinking layer, its conscious self, and they have grown as naturally from the steam and the steel as the ferns grow from the drains.
