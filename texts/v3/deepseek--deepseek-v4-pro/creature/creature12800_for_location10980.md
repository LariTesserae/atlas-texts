The cirque is not empty, but its inhabitants are not what you look for. They are what you notice after a while, after you’ve been still long enough for the place to accept you as part of the stone. By then the ordinary animals have resumed their business—the pikas have stopped their alarm calls, the choughs have settled back to their acrobatics along the rim—and something else, something that was there all along, begins to resolve out of the weave of sound and damp and lichen.

Call them the Listeners. That’s not their name, but it’s close to their function. They don't live in the cirque so much as they *are* the cirque’s attention. Not spirits, not ghosts, not anything you could classify in a bestiary. They’re more like a sense organ the place grew, the way a nerve cluster develops where tissue is repeatedly prodded. The cirque is a bowl that collects more than water: it collects sound, weather, time. And, over the millennia—over the slow retreat of the ice, the first lichens cracking the rock, the generations of eagles and ravens wheeling above—the place developed a capacity to register what it collected. The Listeners are the expression of that.

To see one, you have to un-focus. Look at the patch of bilberry near the tarn’s edge, then let your gaze go soft, the way you’d watch for a faint star. There’s a shimmer there, not heat-haze but something closer to the air over a candle. It has mass but not weight, shape but not outline. At rest, it’s a thickening of the atmosphere into something vaguely upright, vaguely the height of a kneeling person, with a texture like the bloom on a grape. When it moves, it does so without traversing the space between; it’s in one place, then beside the waterfall, then at the rim, and the transitions are less like travel and more like a thought shifting its attention.

They number perhaps a dozen, perhaps only one, depending on whether you count a distributed attention as singular. They have no hierarchy, no social structure, no language. What they have is a shared, continuous absorption. They are the cirque perceiving itself. They listen to the water, the wind, the birds, the crack of frost-shattered stone. They don't interpret, don't remember in the human sense, but they hold the shape of every sound that’s ever echoed off these walls, the way a room holds the scent of woodsmoke long after the fire is cold. The potholes that hum? That’s them, or partly them—the sound is real, the wind does pass across the openings, but the note that emerges has been sitting in the stone for ten thousand years, waiting for the right breath to make it audible again.

They are curious, after a fashion. Not curious about *you* specifically, but about novelty, about any disturbance in the long rhythm. When the first humans came—the ones who built the bothy, who carved the steps, who pecked the circles within circles into the cairn stones—the Listeners would have been there, coalescing around the intruders like mist around a new rock. They would have absorbed the sounds of hammering and voices, the smell of hearth-smoke, the particular resonance of human presence. And they would have held it, the way the cirque holds everything. That’s what the bothy is now: a shell around a memory-thick patch of air, where the Listeners occasionally thicken in the shape of a dream of a builder, replaying the echo of a word spoken a thousand years ago.

They don't feed, exactly. They’re sustained by the place’s own vitality, by the cycling of water and growth and decay. But they do attend to death. The eagle taking a marmot on the high slope above, the dipper drowning in a sudden spate, the slow collapse of a willow under its own twisted weight—the Listeners are there for the moment of transition, the cessation of a rhythm. Not as predators, not as psychopomps, but as witnesses. The place insists on being witnessed. That’s the function. That’s what the circled eyes on the cairn were trying to invoke: the sense that to step into this bowl is to be watched, but watched by something without judgment, without agency, something for whom your brief life is a ripple in a pond that has always been here and always will be, long after your bones are part of the scree.

If you stayed here—if you built a fire in the old bothy, if you slept on the slab floor and woke to the choughs’ clatter—they would incorporate you. Not in any hostile way. You’d simply become part of the weave. On the second morning, you might catch a shimmer near the waterfall, in the shape of a person with your own posture, listening to the stream with your own tilt of the head. On the third night, you’d dream the cirque as an ear, and the ear would be your own, and you’d wake with the taste of lichen in your mouth. After a week, you’d start to hear the pothole note even when the wind was still. After a month, you’d forget which sounds were yours and which were the mountain’s. And eventually, some future traveler would find the bothy empty but for a faint, persistent shimmer near the hearth, a patch of air that is warmer than the rest, that leans toward a voice as if recognizing a language spoken long ago.

The rusted chain on the scree-slope is their doing, in a sense. It came here wrapped around something—a surveyor’s pack, a climber’s haul bag, a piece of military equipment from some forgotten alpine campaign. The Listeners absorbed the sound of it dragging across stone, the clink of links, the human effort in it. When the object itself was carried away or rotted or was buried in rockfall, the chain remained, because the weight of its sound was heavier than the iron. That's a rule of the place: what resonates persists. The bothy. The cairn. The steps. They’re still here because they left an echo the cirque didn't want to lose. The Listeners are the echo’s ears.

No danger, then. No trap. No malevolence. Just a place that has grown its own awareness the way a tree grows a burl—around some old injury, some pressure point between the geologic and the living. And if you leave, as everyone so far has left (the bothy is abandoned, after all, the builders long gone), you’ll carry a small piece of it with you. Not a possession, not a curse. Just the faintest sense, for the rest of your life, that somewhere in the high mountains there’s a bowl of stone that remembers the sound of your name, and is still listening.
