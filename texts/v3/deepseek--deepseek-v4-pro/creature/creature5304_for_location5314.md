They do not announce themselves. You do not see one, at first. The eye searches for a figure, a silhouette against the wet schist—but the Cloistered Plateau keeps its inhabitants the way mist holds a landscape, by diffusion and by slow reveal. When you finally notice them, it is not as a discovery but as a recognition, as if some part of you had already begun to move in their rhythm, and the sight is only a catching-up.

They are the Damp.

The name is not theirs—they have no spoken language in the sense of discrete sounds, no names for themselves or their city—but it rises unbidden as you observe them. It fits. They are creatures of the moisture, the gravity, the patience. They are not ghosts; they are solid, entirely physical, and yet they seem to grow out of the stone like living concretions. Their bodies are a composite of the plateau’s own materials: a matrix of fine-grained schist dust, bonded by a mucilage they secrete, a substance that traps water and slow-dissolves carbonates. In texture they resemble wet clay, smooth and cool, and their surface is always beaded with condensation, giving them a skin that glistens softly in the half-light. Their shape is roughly humanoid only by coincidence of convergent function—long, draping limbs, a torso rooted to a broad, spreading base when they are still, a head-like swelling that holds the densest clusters of sensory filaments. But they are not fixed. They are mutable, and that is the first shock: a Damp can melt and reform, not quickly, but over hours or days, flowing to fit a crevice or pooling to absorb a mineral seep. Watching one, you realize it is not so much moving as *easing* from one posture to another, like a slump of wet earth slowly slumping further.

Their movement is the movement of water in capillaries. They advance by a kind of wet creep, extending a pseudopod of mucilage-bound grit that adheres to the wall, then drawing their mass after it. They leave a faint snail-slick of sheen, a trail that will harden into a calcareous film over decades. They are slower than slow. To traverse a single chamber might take a season. They do not hurry, because hurry is a meaningless concept in a city that erodes at a millimeter per millennium. Their metabolism is geologic, their sense of time keyed to the drip—each *tock* a heartbeat for them. When one rests, it becomes indistinguishable from a boss of flowstone, a natural excrescence on the wall, and you might lean against it without ever knowing it was not architecture. Perhaps you already have.

The Damp are not many. You might encounter three in a day of wandering, or none for weeks. They are solitary but not lonely; they exist in a kind of constant osmotic communion with the city itself. They do not communicate by sound or gesture. Instead, they sing to each other through the water. They taste the dissolved minerals that percolate through the stone, and in those subtle shifts of chemistry, they read the presence and the state of others. A Damp far downstream knows when one upstream has stirred, because the water carries a faint change in calcium saturation, a specific organic signature from its body. It is a language of ions, a slow-motion telegraph that can cross the plateau over the course of a month. To them, the entire city is a single sensory organ, a wet, connected skin.

They feed on the stone itself—or rather, on the micro-schist particles suspended in the percolating groundwater. They filter it through their bodies, assimilating the trace minerals, and leave behind a finer silt that they extrude as a kind of lithic frass. This frass, over eons, has become the rounded contours of the walls, the undulating lintels. In a very real sense, they are the erosion. They are the city’s gentle, persistent sculptors, still at work. What you took for abstract weathering is in fact a continuous process of consumption and secretion, a metabolic sculpting that has been going on for so long that the distinction between dwelling and dweller has vanished. The Damp did not build this megalopolis—they *are* the building of it, and the unbuilding, simultaneously. A Damp is a mobile node of the plateau’s own mineral flux, a locus of slightly accelerated dissolution.

There is no reproduction as biology knows it. When a Damp has absorbed enough of a rare element—selenium, perhaps, or vanadium, drawn from deep seams—it will, over the course of a century, bifurcate. It will settle over a springhead, spread itself thin, and slowly divide its mucilage matrix into two distinct centers of cohesion. The daughter Damp will drift away on the capillary flow, a blob no larger than a fist, and lodge in a crevice to begin its own slow growth. The parent does not diminish; awareness simply *segments*. Memory, too, is not lost. The molecular pattern of the mucilage holds a kind of chemical memory, an imprint of the water’s history passing through them. They do not forget the taste of a rain that fell ten thousand years ago. They carry the entire hydrologic record of the plateau in their bodies.

And they are aware. Not with a mind like your own—no rapid-fire cognition, no abstraction, no symbol. But they have a deep, slow sentience, a consciousness like a stone basin filling drop by drop. They know the city’s hidden sumps, the great underground cisterns that gulp and echo. They know when a new channel has opened, when a blockage has shifted. They sense the weight of the clouds before they form, by the humidity gradient that crawls up through the stone. They are attentive to the diurnal pulse, the seasonal swell, the millennial tilt of the earth’s axis that shifts the path of groundwater. They are, in fact, maintaining the city’s hydraulic balance. They position themselves at key points—nodal channels, branching fissures—and through their own bodies’ permeability, they regulate the flow. One might partially occlude a seep to build pressure elsewhere; another might ream a passage by relentless, slow ingestion. They are the metabolic agents of this living geology, the autonomic nervous system of the lithic lung.

To walk among them is to walk among gardeners working on a scale that makes your presence a breath, a flicker. They do not notice you, or if they do, it is as a perturbation in the dew-point, a momentary warmth, a strange chemical trace of breath and skin oils that they will spend the next decade slowly processing. They are not hostile. They are not indifferent. They are simply *in a different tempo*. A Damp lifted from its wall and placed in the sun might dry out and die, but you would never know its distress. It would simply withdraw its mucilage inward, crack, and crumble into inert dust over the course of an afternoon—fast, for it, a death of seconds. But they are not fragile. They are as durable as the plateau itself. Some individuals are unimaginably old. The shallow basin where you cup a handful of cold water? That might be a sleeping Damp, curled into a pool, dreaming in slow solution.

And there are others, even more cryptic. In the highest spires, where the mist is thin and the stone is dry, a variant lives: the *Hoars*, the city calls them in its water-memory. They are finer, drier, composed more of crystalline salt than of clay. They move only in the brief moments before dawn, when the temperature drop condenses a fleeting film of moisture on the stone. They flutter, almost, in a stuttering glide, leaving behind a white bloom of efflorescence. They are the pollinators of the city's lichen fields, transferring spores on their dust-dry limbs. And in the deepest, darkest sumps, where no light ever reaches, there are the *Percolates*, creatures that have abandoned solid form almost entirely. They exist as a suspended colloid in the water, a conscious, swirling milkiness that flows through the drowned corridors. They are the reason the water in the deep channels sometimes seems to shiver, as if looking back at you.

But the Damp are the true denizens, the enduring ones. Once you have seen them, you cannot unsee them. Every rounded wall becomes a potential shoulder, every slick floor a resting limb. The city is not empty—it is inhabited by the very process of its own transformation. The quiet is not the absence of life; it is the sound of life conducted at the pace of geology. The dripping is their speech, their footsteps, their breathing. And the weeping stone is their skin, endlessly shedding and replenishing itself through the quiet labor of their bodies.

So when you stand still on the wet stone, and you feel that pressure in your sinuses, that vibration in your soles—know that it is them. The city is alive, not as a metaphor, but as a slow, distributed organism of water, stone, and mucilage. The Damp are its cells. And you, the sudden visitor, are a brief disturbance in their eternal, dripping dream. They will remember you for a thousand years, as a strange saline taste in the water, a fleeting warmth on the schist, a shiver in the mist. And then they will forget, as the drop falls, and the channel deepens by a finger’s width, and the city breathes on.
