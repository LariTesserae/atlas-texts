They are not born of this place so much as they have been pressed from it, layer by slow layer, like the soft tuff hoodoos planed by the acid steam. To call them human would miss the point by a few degrees of body heat and several hundred generations of quiet, grinding adaptation. They are small—no taller than an adolescent child of the lowlands—with a compressed, barrel-chested build that makes the most of the thin, damp air. Each inhale is a practiced, efficient act: their ribcages expand not outward but slightly downward, pulling the heavy oxygen from the plateau’s soupy atmosphere with a motion that is almost a shrug. Their skin is the color of buff tuff, a matte, mineral beige that takes on the local stone’s exact tone, but it is not smooth. Over generations, their skin has developed a fine, pebbled texture, not scales exactly, but a dense mosaic of tiny, heat-dissipating nodes that catch the moisture and let it evaporate in the constant warm draft. They never truly sweat; instead, a faint, briny dew collects in the hollows of their collarbones and the crooks of their elbows, a personal microclimate that a mother might dab onto a child’s lips when fresh water is scarce.

Their eyes are the most arresting feature: large, lidless, protected by a permanent translucent film that gives them the look of polished opal. The pupils are horizontal slits that can close to a near-pinhole in the flat, shadowless glare, but widen to full black coins the moment they slip into a crevice. They see heat. Not as a garish overlay, but as a subtle extension of the visible spectrum, a warm bloom that edges the steam vents in a soft, buttery aura and traces the skitter of a skink across a stone face as a fading, handprint-bright track. In the absolute gloom of the deeper rifts, where the rock is hot but lightless, they navigate by the thermal ghosts of the pipes and the faint, glowing blush of their own breath-condensations on the walls.

There is no single tribe, no village, no gathered camp. Their communities are a distributed network of “holds,” each one a natural fissure widened over decades using the corroded iron tools they salvage from the industrial bones of the moor. A hold is rarely larger than a single extended family, perhaps six or eight individuals, and its entrance is always marked by a hanging curtain of woven spider-silk. The silk, collected patiently from the geometric webs between spires, is not for privacy but for humidity control: it traps a pocket of slightly cooler, denser air inside the living chamber, a domestic oasis where the young, who are born with a less robust heat tolerance, can sleep without the constant, exhausting hum of the geothermal draft. Inside, the walls are smoothed and sometimes inlaid with salvaged ceramic insulators, which catch the faint light of a cultivated bioluminescent algae—the same chartreuse mat that fringes the boiling basins. They farm it in shallow, water-filled iron pans, and it gives off not a flame’s jumpy light but a steady, lime-colored glow, cool to the touch and silent.

Their industry is a soft, patient one, an echo of the steam’s own relentless but unhurried work. They hunt the skinks not with speed but with an almost geological stillness. A hunter will press themselves into a matching hollow of rock, skin and stone aligning grain to grain, and wait. Their heart rate drops; their body temperature bleeds into the ambient warmth until they are, to a heat-sensing eye, indistinguishable from the basalt. When a skink passes, the hand that darts out is slow enough to be mistaken for a shifting shadow, and the creature is taken without a squeak. The reptiles are eaten raw, but the bones are saved, every vertebra and rib, for they are the only hard, sharp material besides rust-fragile metal. The shinbones of a particularly large skink become awls; the jawbones, with their tiny, needle teeth, are lashed with sinew to form fine-toothed saws for carving the leathery rosettes of the heat-tolerant plants into water-skins and wrap-cloths.

They do not sing, any more than the birds do. Their language is a low-frequency thrum, pitched to travel not through the air—which is too thin and too damp to carry much—but through the solid rock via the flat of a palm or the bare sole of a foot. Conversations are a rhythmic, tapping pulse, a code of pauses and pressures that can be felt across half a kilometer if the stone is continuous. It is a language of immense subtlety, where the weight of a hand’s lean can convey an emotion, and a series of heel-taps can name a specific steam vent by its characteristic subterranean gurgle. When two individuals meet, they do not speak face-to-face but side-by-side, each resting one hand on the same boulder, feeling the words resonate up through their bones. The old industrial pipes, the ones that still ring with that pure, bell-like tone when wet, are considered sacred for this reason: a message tapped onto a pipe’s flange can travel for miles, a gift from the ancestors who laid the metal pathways. They do not know those ancestors were human engineers. To them, the pipes, the boilers, the ceramic insulators are the “Old Growth,” a mineral forest that fruited metal and glass in a distant, hotter age. They do not worship it as gods. They treat it more like a geological resource: something that once flowed, then crystallized, and now can be quarried and shaped.

Their social rhythm is tied entirely to the condensation cycle. When a particular fissure exhales its wet plume and a localized rain falls, every hold within that microcatchment stirs. They emerge with their algae-pans and their water-skins, moving in a slow, deliberate procession—not a scramble, for water is predictable if you know the vents’ breathing. They collect the lukewarm drizzle directly onto their tongues, let it pool in the hollows of their pebbled skin, and offer it to one another with a brief, ritualized touch of fingertips to the dew-gathered collarbone. In these moments, you might see them share a skink-meat paste, or a child being taught to read the vibrations by pressing its cheek to a steam-heated stone. There is no laughter. Their emotional range is not absent, but it is expressed in minute shifts of posture, in the duration of a touch, in the decision to share a particularly warm sleeping hollow with an unrelated elder. A long, slow leaning of one body into another’s side is a declaration of profound, enduring trust, a conversation that might last an hour without a single tapped word.

The only time they gather in numbers beyond the family hold is when the old machinery speaks. Not the constant hiss, but that rare, bell-like tone from a transformer casing. When it rings, a clear, pure note that cuts through the white noise, the entire region falls still. The skinks freeze. The spiders pause in their weaving. And from every crevice, the dwellers emerge, silent, opal-eyed, and they place their palms flat on the nearest pipe or boulder, feeling the note’s vibration until it decays into silence. It is not a religious gathering. They have no gods. It is, instead, a community check, a moment of collective presence where they sense, through the web of vibrating stone, exactly how many of them are alive and awake across the moor. A census by harmonic resonance. Then they disperse back into the steam, the labyrinth, the perpetual, damp warmth, their lives once again becoming a hundred small, efficient motions in the shadowless light.
