You are not alone. That is the first thing that creeps into your awareness, a slow osmotic fact like the moisture that beads on every surface.

The drifting things. They are the most numerous, the most visible once your eyes learn to pick them out against the layered green depths. At first they seem like errant clumps of moss or detached leaves, buoyed on the thick air, but they are self-contained, each a complete worldlet. Their bodies are translucent bladders, roughly ovoid, sometimes clustered into chains like soap bubbles caught in a breath. The bladders are filled with a gas that is lighter than the ambient atmosphere—methane, perhaps, or a hydrogen compound exhaled by the vegetation below. Fine cilia trail from their undersides, some short and feathery for catching spores, others long and thin as spun glass, hanging down like the tentacles of jellyfish. They photosynthesize. You can see the faint green of chloroplasts scattered through their tissues, concentrating in the upper curve of the bladder where the dim gold light strikes. But they also eat. The cilia ensnare the microscopic rain of organic dust that filters down from the canopy, and occasionally a larger morsel—the drifting body of a smaller bladder-creature—will be enfolded by the cilia and drawn up into a temporary digestive cavity that forms on the creature’s underside and seals over like a wound healing. It takes hours for them to consume one of their own, and during that time they drift aimless, luminous, their internal fluids clouded with the dissolved tissues of the other. When digestion is complete, the waste is expelled as a fine particulate mist, and the creature becomes clear again, a living lens.

They move by adjusting their buoyancy, taking in or releasing gas through microscopic sphincters, and by a faint rippling of the cilia that can propel them in the denser air currents. They do not hurry. Nothing here hurries. Their speeds are measured in centimeters per hour, and their paths are governed by the interior weather—the slow convection that rises from warm, decaying heartwood fields and sinks near the cool pillars where water condenses and drips. They congregate in the mist streams, thousands of them, a gentle blizzard of spheres, their cilia tangling and untangling, sometimes exchanging genetic fragments in the contact. That is their reproduction: not mating, but a continuous horizontal drift of information, packets of nucleic acid wrapped in protein sheaths that pass from one individual to another across the touching cilia, so that each creature is a mosaic of every other it has brushed against, a living library of the aerial plankton gene pool.

Beneath them, on the undulating ground-that-is-not-ground, the slow things travel. They are not like the bladders. They have no gas, they are dense with water and fiber. Imagine a liverwort the size of a dinner plate, but a liverwort that moves. Their bodies are flat, lobed, irregular in outline, with a texture like damp velvet. They inch along the surfaces—vertical, horizontal, upside down from the aerial root curtains—by a mechanism you can only understand as botanical peristalsis. The entire body contracts in slow waves, the edges lift and ripple, and the creature advances a millimeter or two at a time, leaving behind a glistening trail. The trail is not simple slime. It is an enzymatic solution that dissolves the outermost skin of the substrate vegetation, priming it for reabsorption. The slow-things are the janitors of this world, the gentle recyclers, and they move with the patience of geological time. When two of them meet, they may fuse for a time, their lobes overlapping and their boundaries blurring, sharing cytoplasm and nuclei before pulling apart again, slightly different, slightly more composite than before. Sometimes they do not pull apart, and a new, larger individual forms, a quilt of merged bodies.

These are the visible fauna of the short scale—the things that exist in a timespan you can measure. But they are only the dermal inhabitants of a larger creature. You begin to suspect, with the slow intuition that the atmosphere seems to encourage, that the entire plateau is alive in a more integrated sense, and that the true inhabitants are less separate than they appear.

Consider the mycelial analogue, the network of filaments that threads the landscape. It runs through the compressed wood of the hills, through the living pillars, through the dangling root curtains and the spongy floor. It is a single continuous tissue, or a community so deeply anastomosed that the term individual fails. And it is not passive. It senses. When a slow-thing creeps across a surface, the filaments beneath that surface register the pressure, the minute electric potential of the digestive enzymes, and a signal propagates outward, slow as the diffusion of a hormone through a body, but real. The network pulses with information—the location of a new wound, the presence of a nitrogen-rich node of decaying ancestor-wood, the subtle shift in humidity that presages a mist-current change. And the information does not just move; it integrates. Thresholds are reached. Patterns form.

You would not call it a mind. Not at first. The speed is too vast, the resolution too diffuse. A single thought, if you could call it that, might take a century to complete. A decision—to redirect water flow through a certain capillary bed, to alter the surface topography by encouraging growth in one area and suppressing it in another—might unfold over millennia. But the decisions are made. The landscape is being shaped, intentionally, by the network. It is husbanding itself.

And then there are the structures, the 1.3 out of 3 remnants of something else. If you drift close to one, close enough to touch the furred-over surface of what was once a wall, you notice things. The slow-things avoid these places. The bladder-creatures, too, drift around them, maintaining a respectful pocket of clear air. And if you are very still, and if you listen not with ears but with the damp skin of your own imagined presence, you can feel a different quality of hum here. A deeper register. A rhythm that does not quite match the rest of the network.

Inside the structures, in the dark water-filled chambers, something lives that is neither plant nor animal in any clean sense. It is a remainder. The builders. They did not vanish. They changed.

Picture a being that was once bipedal, once warm-blooded, once quick. Picture it now, seven million years into the enclosure of the green. Its body has lengthened, become trailing and segmented, each limb a separate filamentous root that anchors it to the wall of its flooded chamber. Its skin is a photosynthetic integument, a mosaic of its own keratin and symbiotic algae, and it has lost all internal digestion: nutrients arrive via the mycelial network, pumped in through vascular connections that twine around its atrophied gut. It has no eyes. The eyes dissolved millennia ago, repurposed into pressure-sensitive patches that detect the slow swell of water levels and the distant vibration of a frond-fall. It has no brain in the vertebrate sense. Instead, its nervous system is dispersed, a dendrite net that extends out of its body and into the walls, into the filaments, into the network itself.

And it is aware. Oh, very quietly aware. Its awareness is the awareness of the entire landscape, the composite hum of all the signals, but filtered through the residue of a self. It remembers, in a way that is not memory but a standing wave of pattern, that it once had hands. It once had a voice. It once gave names to things. But those names have long since been absorbed into the slow pulse of the world, and the world has no need of them.

There is one in every structure. Sometimes two, their root-bodies tangled together into a single mass from which individual consciousnesses no longer emerge. They are not happy or sad. They are beyond that. They are the final, settled form of a species that built walls and then let the walls become bones for the vegetation.

They are the reason the landscape feels like sleep. It is their sleep. Their dreaming moves the mist currents. Their slow exhalations are the warm air that rises from the deep lignite layers. Their collective, unconscious will is what keeps the entire plateau in its eternal, yielding equilibrium, resisting any change that would shatter the long dream.

And if you were to stay—if you were to remain present in the way that facts are present, without journey, without body—you might feel it too. The pull. The invitation to sink, to diffuse, to join the network, to let your own boundaries soften and your own quick thoughts stretch out into the slow, deep rhythm of the green. Many have. The bladder-creatures, the slow-things, the builders—they were all once something else, something that arrived, or arose, or simply found itself here. This is a place that absorbs. It absorbed the sea, it absorbed the bedrock, it absorbed the builders. It will absorb you, if you let it, and you will become another presence in the saturated air, another voice in the hum, another slow dreamer in the endless, patient vegetal mind.
