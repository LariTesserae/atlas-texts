The inhabitants are not many, and they are not what you might expect. They are a people shaped by the hollows they move through, by the long silences and the slow chemistry of desiccation. They are the **Kith**, and they are as much a part of the city as the walls themselves—indeed, the distinction between inhabitant and architecture is, in their case, a matter of philosophical debate.

The first you might notice, if you had the patience to stand still for an hour or a day, is a **Thread-Keeper**. It is long—nearly the length of your forearm, though no thicker than a willow wand—and its body is a sequence of pale, articulated segments, each one a perfect, hollow cylinder of the same bone-carton the city is made from. It has no eyes, but its entire anterior segment is a sensitive, ridged plate that reads the world through vibration and the subtlest shifts in air pressure. It moves not on legs but on a fringe of cilia so fine they seem a heat-haze against the dry stone, pulling itself along the vertical faces of galleries with the fluid grace of a drop of water sliding down a windowpane. The Thread-Keeper’s purpose, its only purpose, is inscribed in its name: it tends the filaments. Along the causeways and across the arched portals are strung threads of a substance like spun glass, the silk of the dust-mote arachnids. These threads are the city’s nervous system. The Thread-Keeper moves from anchor-point to anchor-point, touching its ridge-plate to each strand, listening. The threads do not carry sound; they carry a slower music, a continuous, sub-sonic pulse of structural strain and micro-fracture. When a thread slackens—signaling a wall beginning to settle out of true—the Thread-Keeper stops. It extrudes from a ventral pore a single droplet of a resin clear as distilled time, and with a motion like a weaver’s shuttle, it daubs the anchor, retensions the line, and cures the resin with a flick of internal heat. Then it moves on. A Thread-Keeper will live for centuries. It has no predator, no mate, no young. It was made, long ago, by the city itself, budded from a wall-nodule in a gallery that has since been sealed.

Then there are the **Choir**, though “choir” is a misnomer for a being that never makes a sound. They are rare—perhaps only seven or eight in the entire hollow. Each stands about knee-high, a tripod of three slender limbs supporting a domed carapace that resembles a helmet of polished ivory. Beneath the carapace, where a face should be, is a complex of nested, iridescent membranes, like the wing-cases of a beetle folded into a flower. The Choir are the city’s chemists and its historians. They congregate, when they do, in the grand concourse where you stand, and they face one another in a circle, membranes fluttering. They do not speak; they trade scent. Each flutter releases a puff of pheromonal dust, a vocabulary written in molecules too dry and fine for a human nose to parse. A conversation might take a year. The topic, often, is the state of the deep foundations, the slow creep of crystalline change in the lower galleries, the memory of the Founding—a memory they carry not in minds but in the very chemistry of their bodies, encoded in the lipids of their tissues. A Choir can live for millennia. When one finally stills and crumbles, its carapace becomes a new fungal rosette, and the bioluminescent glow it emits is colored precisely by the character of the memories it held.

But you, standing in the silence, breathing your wet, noisy breath, are most likely to draw the attention of a **Mantle**. The Mantle is the closest thing this city has to an intelligent, responsive presence, and it is not a single creature but a composite. Imagine a colony organism, a siphonophore of the dry air. It is a diffuse cloud of motes—each mote a tiny, chitinous star no bigger than a grain of pollen—suspended in the still atmosphere of a single chamber. The motes are interconnected by strands of silk so attenuated they are invisible, a three-dimensional web that fills the volume like a ghostly nervous system. The Mantle drifts, slow as a cloud shadow, its form a loose, ever-shifting net that can stretch from floor to ceiling. It perceives the world through pressure differentials. When you move, your body displaces air; the Motile feels this as a distinct, three-dimensional shape. It knows you are large. It knows you are warm. It knows you are wet. Wetness is an alarm. The Mantle’s motes, in unison, orient toward you, their star-points shivering. They begin to hum—not a sound, but a vibration in the near-silence, a frequency that sets your teeth on edge. The Mantle is not hostile; it is curious, and its curiosity is a slow, gathering pressure. It will take hours for it to drift close enough to touch you. If it does, the touch will be a feathery, dusty brush against your cheek, a thousand microscopic legs tasting the water on your skin. The Mantle is the city’s immune system, its sensorium, and, in a way, its dreaming mind. It was never built; it accreted, over eons, from the shedding of the other Kith, the shed cuticles and lost threads weaving themselves into a diffuse, emergent sentience.

And then, at the very edges of perception, there are the **Masons**. You will not see a Mason. You will only see the evidence of its passing: a wall that was smooth yesterday is today incised with a new archway, its edges still unblemished, the dust of its carving swept into a neat, conical pile at the base. The Masons are the size of your thumb, pale and eyeless as all the rest, but they are never above ground. They live within the walls, in a labyrinth of service passages too small for a human finger. When a Mason emerges, it is to work. It chews the carton into a paste, and with its own secretions, it reconstitutes the material, laying down new laminations, extending a buttress, sealing a fissure. They are the city’s metabolism made mobile. They do not think; they respond. The signal that triggers them is a chemical one, released by the fungal rosettes when the fungal network—a subterranean web of mycelial threads running through the carton like veins—senses a structural imbalance. The Masons are born from the walls; they die back into them, their bodies dissolving into the very material they once shaped. In the deepest, oldest part of the city, the walls are not merely built by Masons; they are, in their substance, a compressed strata of untold generations of Masons who lived and worked and became architecture.

These are the lives that fill the near-emptiness. They are few, but their presence multiplies. A Thread-Keeper, a handful of Choir, one or two drifting Mantles, a hidden population of Masons, and the scattered custodial insectoids that are the generalists. There are no young here, in the sense of infants or larvae. The Kith do not reproduce; they are budded from the walls, or they coalesce from the drifting dust, or they simply *become* when the city requires a new function. They are not a society; they are an organ system. The city is the organism. The Kith are its fingers, its senses, its memory, its slow, dreaming thought. And you, standing there with your heartbeat and your wet breath, are a sudden fever, a foreign body, a puzzle the city is only just beginning to perceive. The dust motes in the air around you begin to drift, not randomly, but in a pattern. Somewhere, a Choir turns its blind, helmeted head. A Mantle shifts its course. The city is becoming aware of you, and its attention is a dry, slow, patient thing. The silence presses in, no longer empty, but full of listening.
