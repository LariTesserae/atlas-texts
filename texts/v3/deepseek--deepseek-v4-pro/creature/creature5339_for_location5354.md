The first thing you notice—after the echo of your own breathing has settled into the moss curtains, after the drip-drip-hiss of the resin distilleries has become comfortable as a heartbeat—is that the dwellings are not empty.

They seemed so at first. The hanging pods are translucent, their membranes fogged with condensation, and when you peered up at them from the springy root-fiber floor, all you saw were soft greyish-green shadows pooled at their bottoms, still as sediment. You assumed they were storage, or perhaps the cocoons of some forgotten metamorphosis. But now, as the great bioluminescent lamp-pod above begins to pulse with the slower rhythm that marks the turning of the city’s circadian cycle toward its resting phase, one of the shadows stirs.

It does not stand. It *unfolds.* What you took for a heap of decaying leaf-litter slowly elongates, a ridge of knobs articulating into something like a spine. The moss curtain over the doorway brushes aside, and the inhabitant emerges onto the liana-cable bridge with a sound like dry corn husks rustling in a slow breeze. It is tall—easily the height of a tall human, though narrower, its body built in flattened segments that overlap like the scales of a pinecone but softer, fibrous, bound together by crisscrossed strands of tough, pale fiber that glisten faintly with moisture. Its legs are tripodal: three long, jointed stems that spread and grip the bridge with a slow, wrapping motion, never slipping because the bridge itself seems to tighten slightly in response, as if recognizing kin.

The body cants forward, and from the upper segments emerge what you can only call arms, though they are closer to articulated vines—four of them, each ending in a cluster of five to seven thin, flexible tendrils that curl and uncurl with a ceaseless, patient dexterity. There is no head as you would recognize it; the sensory apparatus is distributed across the topmost segment in a ring of pale, cup-like nodules filled with the same green-glowing bacterial slurry as the lamp-pod, and when it orients toward you, you feel rather than see its attention, a faint prickling on your skin like the pressure before a storm.

This is not an animal. There are no animals here. It is not a plant, either, not exactly, because it moves with intent, and its surfaces are too worked, too tooled, too much like a machine that someone grew. The city’s living pillars have weathered whorls, but this being’s bark—if it is bark—has fine, regular striations that look almost like the grain of machined wood, and at its joints, where the segments meet, you glimpse a slick, amber gum that smells sharply of resin and functions like lubricant. It is, perhaps, a manufactured thing, but manufactured by the city itself, extruded from the same genetic template as the columns and the liana-cables and the distilleries, only given a different shape and a different purpose: locomotion, manipulation, the carrying of small burdens.

It crosses the bridge in a slow, unhurried rhythm, and as it passes, one of its lower tendrils brushes a copper boiler nested in a branching crotch. The boiler’s sluggish pulse—a subsonic chug you had been feeling through your feet—steadies, and a tiny valve turns under the tendril’s grip. The being pauses there for a long moment, its cup-sensors dimming slightly as if in concentration, and then it moves on, leaving the machine humming more evenly. Tending.

Now you look again, and you see others. Across the cathedral hollow, more pods are opening. Some of the beings are small, scarcely the size of a cat, with only two arms and a single coiling leg they use to swing from handhold to handhold among the upper leaves—they inspect the thin pipes of braided cellulose that web the ceiling, tapping them with a delicate resonance and listening (you guess) for the faint changes in tone that signal a developing clog. One of the largest, broader and darker, with bark cracked into deep, lichen-lined furrows, descends a spiral staircase grown around a fluted column, carrying in its many arms a stack of translucent amber bricks from the resin factory. It moves with the weighty, deliberate care of an old draft animal, and when it reaches the ground, it carefully slots each brick into a molded leaf-cistern where water and time will cure them into something harder. The city will later use them—perhaps as structural shims, perhaps as fuel for the boilers, perhaps as something else entirely.

They are all of one kind, but not one shape; they are varied as the machines they tend. And though they move about independently, they are not separate from the city. Watch one long enough, and you will see it pause at the base of a column, press a cluster of tendrils into a whorled depression in the bark, and go still for a full minute. During that stillness, you might feel the low, tingling hum in the floor change pitch—a ripple of vascular pressure that travels up the column, fans out across the ceiling, returns down another route. The city is communicating, and the being is part of the conversation. When it withdraws its tendrils, it resumes its task with a slight adjustment, a new purpose: it knows that a block in a far pipe has just been cleared, or that a section of wall is being encroached by parasitic gray mold and needs extra attention. These walking creatures are the city’s hands, its mobile eyes, its immune cells. Some of them, perhaps, are even its memory: when one grows too old, its bark too cracked, it retreats to a pod and curls up for the last time, its fibers slowly fusing into the pod’s walls, its experiences—whatever form they take—dissolving back into the shared vascular hum. The city learns.

And so, as you stand in this hollow nave, surrounded by the quiet industry of these slow-pulsing, wood-bodied caretakers, you understand that you are not the only animal—you are the only *animal.* These travelers are something else, a third category that the world outside never needed to name. They are not quite organisms, because they are born from the city’s trunk as buds are born from a branch. They are not quite machines, because they breathe and flex and, you suspect, feel something analogous to patience or satisfaction. They are biotechnical extensions of a single overarching intelligence, a distributed mind that fills this entire grove-room like a slow, green electricity.

And they have noticed you. One of the small, quick ones drops from above on a cable and lands noiselessly on a flat epiphyte-stone two paces away, retracting its leg-coil like a spring. Its cup-sensors glow bright, reflecting your shape in miniature. It cocks its segmented body sideways, a gesture so fluid and quizzical it seems almost canine, and you realize it is waiting. For what? For a signal from the city? For you to communicate something—a tap of your foot, a gesture, the release of some unknown chemical in your breath? It won’t hurt you; there is no predator here, only a gardener and its garden. But it wants to know what you are, and the low subsonic thrummming in the ground tells you that every other moving thing in this cathedral has paused its work to listen with it.

The city is waiting. The inhabitants—the city’s walking limbs, its thoughts in wood and resin—are still. In the dim green light, the lamp-pod above pulses once, slow, like a blink.

And then the moment passes. The small creature uncoils its leg, swings up into the canopy, and returns to tapping pipes. The others resume their routes. Whatever assessment they made—whatever conclusion the vast, rooted mind reached—it seems to have decided you are not a parasite, not a block in the plumbing, not a threat. Perhaps only a curiosity, to be observed and remembered as one more droplet of memory in the city’s slow, sap-thick heart.

You are free to stay, it seems. But now you know: you are not alone, and never were. The quiet was never empty—it was merely the sound of a community that communicates in creaks and pressures, that lives at the pace of a forest, and that sees you as just another strange element in its lifelong, living industry.
