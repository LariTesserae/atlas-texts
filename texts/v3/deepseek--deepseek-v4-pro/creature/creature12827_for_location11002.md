Among the people of the basin, there is a woman called Kith. Like all her kin, she was born into noise and wet, into the ceaseless murmur of steam vents and the peeping of frogs, and she has never known a single moment of true quiet. Silence, to her, is not a sensation but an abstraction—a rumor passed down from the old stories, a condition that might exist somewhere else, in some other world where the light shifts and the ground changes. She does not miss it, because a person cannot miss a thing they have never imagined clearly. Instead, she inhabits the sound as a fish inhabits water, her whole body tuned to its frequencies, her voice pitched to cut through the roar like a hot copper chisel through travertine.

Kith is twenty summers old, though “summer” is a poor word for it: there are no seasons, and the passage of time is marked only by the slow growth of a child’s bones and the wear on a sinew rope. She counts her age by the number of new chambers her family has cut into the rock since her birth. She was born in the fifth chamber below the eastern outflow, a room smoothed by generations of bare feet and walled with hides scraped so thin they glow a translucent amber in the constant twilight. Her birth was attended by the usual chorus—her mother’s shouts blending with the frogs, the midwives chanting rhythmically to keep time with the contractions—and the first thing Kith’s ears registered, before her eyes ever focused, was the sound of her grandmother tapping a bone needle against a copper pot. That tapping became her name-song, the private rhythm her family uses to call her across the terraces. Every person in the basin has such a song, a unique percussive signature beaten out on stone or metal or stretched hide, because in a world of overlapping roars, a shouted name will not carry ten paces.

Kith is small and wiry, with the pale grey-tinged skin common to all her people—a color that blends with the steam and the stone, so that when she stands still she seems to dissolve into the landscape. Her hair is the white of the bacterial mats in the hottest pools, and she keeps it cropped short to prevent it from catching on the low doorways and dripping edges of her home. Her eyes are large and very dark, adapted to the dimness, with an extra fold of lid that closes sideways to filter the ever-present moisture. She moves through the basin with a liquid economy, her bare toes gripping the slick stone as instinctively as a frog’s pads, her body always leaning slightly forward into the mist as if she is perpetually on the verge of greeting someone.

Her days are built of small, necessary tasks, repeated with the precision of ritual. She is a tender of the larval pools, the shallow basins warmest to the touch where the flies lay their eggs in rafts that float like grey pollen on the mineral skin. It is her job—inherited from her mother, who got it from hers—to scrape the bacterial mats that feed the larvae, to thin the swarming populations when they grow too dense, and to harvest the mature pupae before they hatch into the clouds of adults. This last task is the most delicate: if she takes too many, the fly clouds will thin and the birds will starve. If she takes too few, the adults will overwhelm the air with their bodies, clogging the vents and smothering the frogs. The balance is exact, and Kith knows it not through calculation but through a felt sense, a kind of bodily attention that she has cultivated since she first learned to scoop. She can read the heat of a pool with her palm, the density of larvae with a glance at the water’s surface, and she adjusts her scraping with the unthinking grace of a bird adjusting its wings to a current.

The larvae she collects are pressed into cakes and dried on racks of rib-bone that jut from the walls of her family’s chamber. They form the staple food of the basin, a bland protein-rich paste that can be eaten cold or dissolved into the hot water from the vents to make a thin, nourishing broth. Kith eats three times a day, squatting on her haunches at the edge of the pool she tends, her bowl cupped in one hand while the other remains free to flick away any curious shrimp. Meals are not social occasions; the noise discourages extended conversation, and the ever-present humidity makes lingering over food unappealing. She chews quickly, swallows, and is back to work.

Her clothing is minimal: a loin wrap of tanned frogs-kin, soft and porous, and a short cape of woven sinew that sheds water without holding heat. She wears no ornaments except for a single copper ring through the cartilage of her right ear, a gift from her mother on the day she first bled. The ring is not decorative so much as functional; it is a tool, a tiny curved blade embedded in the metal that she uses to trim overgrown bacterial mats in the narrowest fissures where her fingers cannot reach. Every item she carries serves at least two purposes. The bone fish-hook tucked into her waistband is also a needle for repairing hides. The clay pot she uses for collecting pupae doubles as a drum when struck with a knuckle, producing a deep, resonant tone that can be heard three terraces away. The people of the basin do not separate beauty from utility; the two concepts have merged over countless generations into a seamless whole, so that a well-made scraping tool is admired for its elegance as much as its sharpness, and a pair of sandals woven from shrimp husks will be woven in patterns that delight the eye even as they grip the stone.

Kith has a lover—or rather, she has a tie that approximates what an outsider might call a lover, though the people of the basin have no word for romantic attachment as distinct from practical partnership. His name-song is a rapid double-click followed by a low thump, a sound made by striking a knuckle against a hollow bone tube and then tapping the tube on stone. She calls him Clicks-end, and she has known him since they were both children chasing each other through the mist, their feet slapping on wet rock. He is a hunter of the larger frogs, the ones whose legs provide the festival meat eaten during the rare birthing ceremonies. He is taller than Kith, with the calloused fingertips of one who grips slippery skins, and his body is striped with the faint scars of copper knives slipped while gutting. They share a sleeping platform in her family’s chamber, a frame of antler and hide suspended from the ceiling by braided sinew, where they curl together like two frogs in a crevice, their breathing synchronizing without thought.

Their bond is not exclusive—the basin people do not pair-bond in that way, for the population must remain perfectly stable and too much attachment can lead to too many births. Children are planned, each one conceived only after a council of elders has determined that a death is imminent or has just occurred, and only by the pair selected through a complex system of lineage-rotation that Kith does not fully understand and does not need to. She trusts the elders, the old women and men who sit in the deepest chambers where the noise is slightly muffled and the steam is thick enough to soothe aching joints. They are the ones who count the people, who listen to the birth-cries and the death-rattles with the same attentive ear, and who maintain the Great Ledger—not a book, for there is no paper, but a wall of knotted cords, each knot a life, each color a lineage, each length a generation. Kith has seen the wall only once, when she accompanied her grandmother to an elder meeting. It covers an entire chamber wall, floor to ceiling, thousands upon thousands of cords swaying slightly in the updraft from a vent. To her, it was a kind of music made visible, a composition in fiber that hummed with the same steady order as the basin’s roar.

She is not a thinker in the way an outsider might recognize. She does not question the eternal twilight or the absence of seasons, because she has no framework for questioning them. She knows, in a bone-deep way, that the world is exactly as it must be, and that her role is to maintain her small part of it. When she scrapes a pool, she is not performing a chore; she is participating in the continuous creation of the basin, the endless arrival of rock and water and life that is, itself, the only reality she will ever know. Her sense of self is not bounded by her own skin; it extends out into the bacterial mats she tends, the frogs that call from the rims, the steam that beads on her shoulders. She is a node in a network so dense and so stable that it is impossible to say where she ends and the world begins.

Occasionally—rarely, perhaps once in a generation—something will change. A new vent will open with a violent hiss, rearranging the temperature gradients of a whole terrace, and the people will have to shift their pools and their sleeping chambers and their pathways. Kith has heard of such events from the elders, but she has never experienced one. She imagines it would feel like a wrong note in the eternal chorus, a tear in the fabric of the daily roar. The thought disturbs her, and she pushes it away, refocusing on the present, on the steady rhythm of her scraping tool against the travertine, the answered calls of the frogs, the warmth of the water around her ankles. This is her world, complete and entire, and she is its faithful inhabitant, forever arriving with it, forever still.
