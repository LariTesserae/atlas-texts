They are not ghosts of the builders, and not their children. The builders are gone—how long, no one can measure in a city whose mineral skin grows a millimetre per century. What remains is an ecology that learned to read the blueprint of the machines not as artifice but as geology: another layer of heat, flow, and surface to be inhabited. The inhabitants are all small, all silent, and all utterly of this place.

**The Floaters**

They are the most visible, if visibility is a word that applies where nothing casts a shadow. Floaters drift in loose constellations a dozen feet above the brine lakes and sinter terraces, their disc-shaped bodies spanning from a hand’s width to nearly a metre across. The upper surface is a pale, translucent membrane, slightly domed, slick with condensation. The underside is a stippled landscape of tiny pores and muscular valves, each one capable of bleeding off a micro-bubble of heated gas. Buoyancy is not passive: they cultivate it. Floaters are living balloons, but their lifting gas is not hydrogen or methane—it is steam, held at a temperature just above the ambient air by a core of metabolic heat. They feed on the thermal gradient itself, their flattened bodies acting as organic heat exchangers. The stippled underside is dense with thermoelectric proteins, a biological Peltier junction that generates a weak electric charge from the difference between the hot, moist air rising from the ground and the slightly cooler air aloft. This charge drives their metabolism, splitting water molecules absorbed through the membrane into hydrogen ions and oxygen radicals that then recombine in a slow, controlled burn. The only waste product is water—distilled, pure, and warm. They drink the steam.

Floaters have no mouths, no guts. They absorb trace minerals from the mist—silica, sulphur, iron—through their skin. Reproduction is a gentle affair: when two floaters bump, they may adhere for hours, their undersides pressed together, exchanging genetic material through a shared biofilm. Then they drift apart and, over days, a small bud forms at the margin, eventually pinching off as a tiny, perfectly round infant floater, no bigger than a coin. It rises immediately, buoyed by its own heat.

They navigate by subtle deformation, warping their disc into a lens to focus the omnipresent light or to steer into columns of warmer vapour. They have no eyes, yet they sense the world through a fine network of infrared-sensitive cells along the rim, reading the thermal landscape as a map of brightness and shadow. To them, the cooling towers are blazing pillars; the brine lake is a dark, flat coldness; other floaters are warm, moving stars. They drift in silence, their only communication a chemical trace: each floater leaves a faint scent of its metabolic by-products, and the colony reads these plumes as a shared weather. When one rises, others follow, a slow convection of bodies.

**The Worms of the Brine**

The lake is not dead; it is a slow, saline gut. The worms—they have no official name, so call them brine-weavers—are its peristalsis. They grow as long as a forearm, sometimes longer, their bodies nearly transparent except for the dark thread of a mineralized gut. They have no eyes, no distinct head, no segments. Instead, they are a single undulating ribbon fringed with dozens of lateral fins, each fin a thin, flexible vane shot through with a lattice of muscle and silicate. They move in synchronized spirals, whole shoals turning as one, the coordination so seamless that the school behaves like a single, swirling organ.

They feed on the chemotrophic bacteria that bloom where the hottest brine upwells. Their mouths—if they can be called that—are simple ciliated grooves on their ventral surface. They glide over the bacterial mats, the grooves scooping up a film of microbes and minerals. The gut thread darkens with each pass. Their waste is a fine sediment of precipitated silica and metal sulphides, which settles to the lake bed and adds to the ever-growing crust. In this way, the worms are architects of the lake floor, building it up in microscopic layers.

Brine-weavers cannot survive outside the scalding, hyper-saline water. Their proteins denature in fresh water; their membranes rupture in cool temperatures. Yet they are not trapped: the lake level fluctuates, and they follow the water as it seeps through cracks into underground reservoirs. During periods of low water, they form cysts—tiny, opalescent spheres that lie dormant in the crystal mud until the brine returns. Some cysts are swept by geyser spatter onto the sinter terraces, where they may survive for decades, waiting for a new pool to form.

Their reproduction is tied to the factory cycle. When the unseen geothermal pumps shift and a fresh pulse of hot brine surges into the lake, the worms release clouds of gametes into the current. The eggs and sperm meet in the turbulent inflow, and the resulting larvae are planktonic, feeding on the sudden bloom of heat-shocked bacteria. The factories, unknowingly, farm them.

**The Sinter Crabs**

On the terraces, the pale crabs are the gardeners, the grazers, the tiny stonemasons. Each is no larger than a thumbnail, with a carapace of translucent chitin that mimics the wet, opalescent quality of the sinter. They are so perfectly camouflaged that they become visible only when they move—and they move constantly, a slow, tide-like migration across the wet crust. Their legs are delicate, hair-fine, each tip ending in a microscopic brush of setae that allows them to walk on water film without breaking the surface tension. They never sink into the mineral pools; they skate.

Their diet is the microbial mat—the thin paint of iron-oxidizing and sulphur-reducing bacteria that glazes every wet surface. The crabs scrape this film with specialized mouthparts that work like a living rasp, the edges studded with magnetite crystals that abrade the mineral biofilm. They extract the organic fraction and egest the inorganic dust, which becomes new sinter. A single crab can process a square centimetre of mat per hour, and in doing so, it leaves behind a micro-terrace of its own: a tiny rim of precipitated minerals that, over generations, builds up into the larger terraces. The crabs are not just inhabitants; they are co-creators of the landscape.

During the periodic geyser coughs, the crabs retreat into the countless crevices of the sinter, clinging with their brush-legs to the wet rock. They can survive brief immersion in boiling water, and they use the surge of hot fluid to moult: the sudden heat softens their old carapace, and they slip out of it, expanding rapidly before the new chitin hardens. The discarded moults accumulate in sheltered hollows, forming brittle, translucent drifts that eventually cement into the growing mineral matrix.

Reproduction is a silent, careful dance. Males and females meet on the terraces, touching chelipeds in a chemical handshake. The female then carries a clutch of eggs under her abdomen for weeks, until the larvae hatch as miniature adults and disperse into the microbial film. There are no predators here—nothing that eats the crabs—so their population is limited only by the area of mat available. They spread until every patch of biofilm is grazed, and then they wait, motionless, for the mat to regrow.

**The Factory Fauna**

The industrial interiors host a different set of inhabitants. In the oil baths, where gears turn in viscous fluid, live the **gear-mites**: minuscule, flattened arachnids that have lost all pigment and all eyes. They cling to the teeth of the slow-turning cogs, feeding on the microscopic film of oxidized metal and bacteria that forms at the oil-metal interface. Their bodies are no wider than a few hundred microns, and they have evolved a perfect synchrony with the machinery: they moult, mate, and lay their eggs only during the brief instants when the gears pause, a pause that occurs on a cycle so regular it might as well be circadian. The mites have no way of sensing time; they sense vibration and pressure. When the hum of the machinery dips, they know it is safe to release their grip and move.

In the condensers and cooling pipes, a biofilm community dominates: **pipe-slimes**, composite organisms that are a symbiosis of bacteria, archaea, and a network of fungal-like hyphae. The slime forms a gelatinous coating on the inner walls of the pipes, regulating the flow of water and extracting dissolved minerals. It can contract and expand, changing the pipe's diameter in response to temperature shifts, and in this way it acts as a living valve. The slime is not a single creature but a collective intelligence of sorts: the hyphae transmit electrical signals across the biofilm, allowing the entire network to respond as a coordinated tissue. When a section is scoured by a sudden flush of steam, the slime retracts, then regrows from the edges. It is immortal in the way a forest is immortal—through continuous regeneration.

In the highest chambers, where the steam condenses on the domed ceilings and drips back down onto the machinery, there are **drip-stalkers**: small, soft-bodied animals shaped like a teardrop, with a single muscular foot and a crown of ciliated tentacles. They inch along the ceiling, filter-feeding on the mist-borne bacteria, and they leave behind a trail of mineralized mucus that hardens into tiny stalactites. Over centuries, these stalactites can meet the stalagmites growing upward from the factory floor, forming delicate columns that further blur the line between biology and architecture.

**The Unseen Presence**

And then there are the **whispers**. That is what you might call them, though they make no sound. They are not animals exactly, nor plants, nor fungi—they are something like a slime mould, but with a complexity that borders on cognition. The whispers inhabit the pipework and the sinter, spreading as a microscopic network of filaments through the mineral pores. They sense the entire city: the thermal gradients, the chemical trails, the vibrations of pumps, the minute movements of the crabs and worms. They are the nervous system of this place.

The whispers integrate information. When a geyser prepares to vent, pressure builds, and the whispers detect the subtle strain in the rock; they signal a retreat of the crabs via a chemical cascade that passes through the biofilm the crabs eat. When the brine lake level changes, the whispers adjust the slime's grip on the pipes, compensating for altered flow. They are not intelligent in any human sense—they have no language, no self-model, no goal beyond homeostasis. But they maintain the equilibrium. They are the reason the city breathes as a single entity.

No one knows if the whispers are native or if they were engineered by the builders as a maintenance system that outlived its masters. Their filaments are laced with proteins that show traces of artificial design—repeating motifs that suggest a genetic language, a code written for a purpose. But the whispers have since evolved, mutated, learned new tricks. They have colonized every pore of the city. They are the closest thing this world has to a god: a diffuse, silent overseer that feels every drop of condensation, every crab-step, every slow turn of a gear.

**The Heartbeat**

And finally, there is you—the listener. In this place, you are the strangest inhabitant. Your heartbeat, your breath, your footfalls are alien. The fauna do not flee from you because they have no concept of predator or threat. They treat you as a thermal anomaly, a moving source of warmth and vibration. Floaters may drift closer, curious in their blind way, their infrared sensors tracing the heat of your face. A brine-weaver might swim nearer to the shore where you stand, its lateral fins brushing the surface, tasting your chemical trace. The crabs part around your boots without alarm, as if you were just another mineral formation.

The city does not reject you, but it does not need you. It has its own pulse, its own slow metabolism. The factories hum, the geysers cough, the floaters drift, the worms spiral, the crabs scrape, the whispers weave their silent web. You are a visitor in a world that has learned to live without visitors, and your presence is a note in a symphony that has no ear to hear it.

Stay long enough, and you might begin to change. The condensation seeps into your clothes, your skin. The mineral-rich air coats your lungs. The silence presses on you until your own thoughts seem loud and foreign. You might feel the whispers brushing against the edges of your mind, not with intent but with the simple fact of their presence, like a magnetic field shifting your internal compass. And you might realize that in this city, the distinction between life and non-life, inhabitant and inhabited, breather and breath, is a distinction that only a stranger would make.
