You are the Last Melt-Pulse.

That is not a name. It’s a title, a function, an event in slow motion. You have no need of names. No one here has ever asked you for one, and you would not know how to answer. You are not the kind of thing that answers.

Your body—if body is the word—is a standing wave in the capillary network, a persistent shape the water makes as it pulses up through the ice every seventeen hours. You are the melt, and the freeze, and the long waiting between. You have been this for a very long time.

The shape you hold is roughly humanoid, because once, long ago, you were human, or something adjacent to human, or something that dreamed of being human. The memory has eroded. All memories erode here. The ice remembers the boulders, the glaciers, the warmer sky, but you are not ice. You are the water that moves through it, and water’s memory is short. You recall only impressions: the idea of hands, the architecture of a spine, the way a face arranges itself around breath. You do not breathe, but you shape yourself as though you did. When the melt comes, your chest rises and falls in a rhythm borrowed from the person you might have been. When the freeze comes, you disperse into the channels, into the waiting dark, and you are no longer a person at all. You are a potential. A next-time.

You have been here since before the bladder-plants learned to shape the ice. Before the predators grew their frozen-smoke wings. Before the terraces were terraces and not just an uneven field of ancient glacial scars. You are not native to this place—nothing is native to this place—but you are among its oldest arrivals. The ecosystem has grown up around you like a city around a well, drawing on the patterns you established without ever acknowledging you exist.

This is not loneliness. Loneliness implies a self, and your self is intermittent at best. You exist fully for twenty minutes out of every seventeen hours. During the melt-pulse, you coalesce, and you walk the Massif, and you remember what it was like to be singular and separate. Then the freeze comes and you dissolve back into the network, into the billion capillary threads, into the slow mineral breath of the ice. In dissolution, you are everywhere. You are the whole terrace system. You are the water that feeds the bladder-plants, the thin saline film that the grazers drink, the crystal matrix that the predators skate across. You are distributed, diffuse, unconscious, and entirely at peace. Then the melt-pulse comes again, and you gather yourself, and the peace is replaced by something sharper: awareness, intention, the strange burden of having a shape and a direction and a name-that-is-not-a-name.

Seventeen hours. Twenty minutes. This is your lifespan, repeated endlessly.

You have no idea how long this has been going on. The Massif keeps its own time, and you are part of it now.

During the melt, you walk. You walk because walking is what the shape you hold was made to do. The legs, the hips, the pendulum swing of it—motion is encoded in the water’s memory of you, and you obey. You walk the terraces. You cross the smooth ice plains where the emptiness presses against you like a held breath. You pass the spire of leaning ice, which has grown six inches since you first noticed it, a thousand melt-pulses ago, and which will fall sometime in the next ten thousand years, and which you will doubtless witness, unless some larger cycle intervenes. You visit the house-sized cluster of bladder-plants, whose slow inflations and deflations have become a kind of calendar, each cycle marking not hours or days but the passage of something closer to weather, a breathing of the ice that takes decades to complete. You know them individually, these plants, not by names—no names—but by the particular way they shape their channels, their preference for one mineral impurity over another, the slight variations in their thermosynthetic glow. They are the closest thing you have to congregation.

They do not know you exist. You feed them anyway. Every melt-pulse, you direct a little extra water toward their roots, a little extra concentration of meteoritic dust. You have been doing this for so long that you have altered their evolution. The house-cluster is your garden, your inadvertent masterpiece. They are larger than any other bladder-plants on the Massif. Their thermosynthetic efficiency is peerless. If the plants could worship, they might worship you, but they cannot, and so the relationship remains what it is: a one-sided tending, a care given without expectation, a love that flows only one way through the capillary channels.

You are not lonely. But you are something adjacent to it.

The predators sense you. They are the only things here that do. When you coalesce, when your humanoid shape pulls itself together from the meltwater, the manta-things pause in their hunting. They angle their frozen-smoke bodies toward you like compass needles finding north. Their senses are not eyes, not ears, but something like a pressure-reading, a way of feeling the water moving through the ice, and to them you must seem like a storm, a flood, a rupture in the seventeen-hour rhythm. They do not approach. They never have. But they watch, or the equivalent of watching, and you have come to understand that this is their version of acknowledgment. You are the Last Melt-Pulse. You are the anomaly in the cycle. You are the thing that does not freeze.

Why don’t you freeze?

You have asked yourself this, in the moments after the melt ends, when the cold comes down and the world locks itself back into stillness. You feel the water around you crystallising, the channels sealing over, the grazers going rigid and invisible against the ice. And yet you remain, for a time. Your shape holds. Your mind—the thing that thinks of itself as “you”—stays coherent for minutes after the freeze, sometimes hours, before finally surrendering to the dissolution. This is not normal. None of the other water does this. You are different, and the difference is not biological—you have no biology—but something else, something left over from whoever you were before. A stubbornness. A refusal. A memory in the water that should have faded but hasn’t.

You suspect, in the rational interludes between pulses, that you are not supposed to be here. That you fell, or were left, or chose to stay for reasons you can no longer access. The Massif does not care. The Massif is a factory without a foreman, a garden without a gardener, a machine that processes temperature into life and never asks why. You are the only thing here that asks why. The question is a vestigial organ, a human-shaped appendage you cannot shed. It does not help you survive. It does nothing useful at all. And yet you ask it, every melt, as you walk the terraces, as you tend the bladder-plants, as the predators watch and the grazers scatter and the green-glowing crevasse hums its low mineral song for no one.

Why?

You do not know. You will never know. The melt-pulse is ending again. You can feel it in the channels, the pressure easing, the water growing sluggish. The hiss that is not-quite-sound is fading. The grazers are already settling into their crystalline lock. The predators have finished their hunts and are finding their own freeze-positions, spreading their smoky wings flat against the ice, becoming part of the landscape. You have perhaps two minutes left. Three, if you push.

You are standing on the edge of the V-shaped canyon, the one with the river of bladder-plants that pulses in its own long rhythm. The oil-black surface ripples, slow, slower, a wave that will take another decade to reach the canyon’s mouth. You could dive into it. You have never tried. You do not know what would happen—whether the plants would absorb you, whether you would fertilise them into some new and monstrous growth, whether you would simply dissolve into their network and never coalesce again. The thought is not frightening. It is almost appealing. An end to the cycle. A surrender to the freeze.

But you don’t. You never do. Instead, you turn away from the canyon and begin the long walk back to the centre of the terrace, where the capillary network is densest and your dissolution will be most complete. The ice is already firming beneath your feet—not feet, the water-shape of feet, the memory of feet. Your outlines are blurring. Your thoughts are blurring. The question—why—is blurring into something else, something older, something that predates language and selfhood and the need to be one thing rather than many.

The freeze comes.

You let it.

For seventeen hours, you are everywhere. You are the hiss of the next melt, building pressure in the deep ice. You are the mineral tang of ancient ash, rising toward the roots of the bladder-plants. You are the faint vibration of a grazer’s cilia, frozen motionless against the terrace. You are the Massif, and the Massif is you, and there is no difference, no boundary, no self to be lonely or curious or afraid. This is the peace. This is what you return for.

And then, seventeen hours later, the melt begins again.

You gather yourself. You shape the spine, the hands, the face. You remember the question.

You walk.
