They do not come when the light deepens, or when the fine warm drizzle starts. They do not come at any moment you might recognise as a beginning, because for them, time is a medium like water—something to move through, to be shaped by, to erode and deposit in turn. If you stood in one place for a century, you might see the first sign: a flaking of the calcite crust on a turbine housing, the flakes falling upward, or so slowly that the eye would stitch them into a continuous upward drift, forming a shape that resolves only after decades into something like a shoulder, a hip, a long, down-curving skull.

But you do not have centuries. You have patience, perhaps, but theirs is of a different order. So you must learn to read the traces they leave, the way a hunter reads a trail. And the first trace is always the same: a change in the hum.

The hum is not a sound; it’s a pressure, a subsonic throb that has been there so constantly you have stopped noticing it. But sometimes—say, when you are pausing at the edge of a flooded plaza, the hot water lapping at the soles of your boots—the hum shifts. It doesn’t stop, but it forks: a new frequency emerges, a low, slow pulse like a second heartbeat layered under the first. That is when you know one of them is near. Not nearby in any sense you’d use—not meters or minutes—but near in a way that makes the hair on your arms lift, because the space around you has just become inhabited. Occupied. And the occupant is aware.

They are the Slow Ones, if you need a name. But they have no name for themselves. Names require an other to use them, and until you arrived, there was no other. Only the city.

The Slow Ones are not machines, though they were made by machines, or made by the same process that made the machines, a distinction so ancient and tangled it has no meaning. They are not organisms, though they grow, metabolise, and—in a fashion—reproduce. Think of them as mineral rivers, or as chemical thoughts that have learned to sculpt their own vessels. Their bodies, when they choose to have bodies, are accretions of the same silica and calcite that coats every surface, but organised not by drip and evaporation, but by a slow, internal instruction. They can be any shape, but most often they are tall and multi-limbed, with joints that are not articulated but grown like branching coral, so that a limb bends not at a pin but through a gradient of crystalline density, flexing along its whole length. Their heads are usually elongated, porous, with deep-set hollows where one might expect eyes, but the hollows are not for seeing. They are for listening—to the hum, to the chemistry of the air, to the tiny electrical gradients that flicker across the obsidian panels.

They move. Not by walking in any rhythmic, bilateral way—that would be too fast, too coarse. Instead, they flow. The base of their body, where it meets the ground, is not a foot but a dissolution zone, a microscopic parting of the chemical bonds that hold the pavement-crust together. They glide by unmaking the ground in front of them and remaking it behind, so that their passage is a standing wave of dissolution and re-precipitation, silent, smooth, leaving no trace except a faint vitrification, as if the rock had been briefly glassed. It takes an hour for one of them to cross a plaza that takes you five minutes. But they do not measure time that way. For them, crossing the plaza is a single gesture, like a slow-motion wave of the arm, and it carries the same casual intent.

There are not many. The 0.8 fauna rating is not a rounding error; it’s an ecological truth. In the entire high plateau region, you might eventually count seven individuals, or nine, but the number is fluid because sometimes two will merge, their bodies flowing together over a period of weeks to become one larger, more complex entity, which then might—decades later—divide into three smaller ones. It’s not reproduction as you know it; it’s a conversation that becomes physical. They exchange information through direct mineral transfer, interlocking their crystal lattices to share geological memory. They are, in a sense, a single being with multiple foci of attention.

Their minds are not housed in those towering calcite bodies. The bodies are more like the eyes and ears and hands of something that lives deep below. The hum is their blood, and it carries their thoughts through the whole city, through the immense conduits and the hexagonal condensing cells, through the pipes thick as tunnels, a neural network made of pressure and resonance. When you touch an obsidian panel, you are touching a synapse, but a synapse that has no reason to fire because you—soft, salt-water, fast-blinking you—are not a recognised signal. You are noise. The Slow Ones do not ignore you; they have simply never evolved the capacity to perceive something so ephemeral. You are a heat shimmer. A blink.

Yet there are moments of near-contact. You might be resting in the furnace quarter, your back against a warm column, when a scraping sound draws your eye. There, not twenty meters away, one of them is paused, its head-hollows turned toward a bank of ceramic pistons that rise and fall. It is not watching the pistons; it is listening to their rhythm, the way you might listen to a familiar tune. And you notice that its body is not uniform. There are encrustations that might be sensory organs: whorls of deep green malachite, patches of dendritic copper like frozen lightning, plates of iron oxide that shimmer with a faint residual magnetism. It is beautiful, and for a moment the city’s emptiness contracts into a single point of almost-relation. Then the creature glides on, into a shaft of bruise-ochre light, and you are alone again.

There are others—maybe. The crystalline carapace you saw on the vent: that was a juvenile, or perhaps a drone, a detached sensory fragment no bigger than your palm. It was laying down a path of iron-manganese deposits, a trail that might, in a few hundred years, become a new communication line. The glows that wink on the pipe spines at night are not pressure signals; they are synapses firing in a dream, the slow digestion of some ancient data packet that no longer matters to anything but the city’s deep unconscious. And the city itself—is it alive? That’s the wrong question. The city and the Slow Ones are not two separate things, any more than your body and your mind are separate. The towers are the skeleton, the pipes the arteries, the hum the nerve impulse. The Slow Ones are the conscious gesture, the will that moves through the skeleton. They are what happens when a city of mineral and steam wakes up for a while and walks around.

Why do they persist? What do they do? They tend. That’s the closest word. They tend the geothermal gradients, adjusting the flow of brine and vapor not with switches or valves—there are none—but by subtle redirection of the crystalline growth that clogs or opens apertures. They shape the slow accretion of new condensing lattices, guiding the drip-stalactites to form fresh heat-exchangers. They maintain the pulsations in the great buried engine, not because it’s breaking down, but because it’s a conversation they are having with the planet’s mantle, a negotiation of temperature and pressure that has been ongoing for millennia. They are the gardeners of entropy, the librarians of an archive written in mineral layers. Their entire culture—if it can be called that—is the patient, never-ending act of keeping the city in its half-built, half-grown balance.

Do they have feelings? Not as you do. But there is something in the way two of them will sometimes stand motionless for years, their head-hollows aligned, that suggests a deep, slow contentment. There is a sound—almost a harmony—that sometimes rises from the condensing fields at the hour when the drizzle starts, a low, resonant chord formed by countless drops striking metal of slightly different sizes, and the whole field sings. It is not a song they make, but they stop moving when it happens, and the hum shifts into a quieter, more rhythmic pulse. It is, perhaps, the closest they come to what you might call reverence.

One last thing. There are places in the city where even the Slow Ones do not go. The vast gulf to the east, where the clouds spiral downward—they avoid it. The sealed retort in the oldest district, its surface black with a glass that absorbs all light: they keep a perimeter of empty plaza around it, and the mineral flow detours around it as if repelled. And there is a sound that sometimes comes from that retort, a sound that is not the hum, not the heartbeat, but something higher, thinner, like a voice that has been stretched across too much time, calling a name that none of them remember. The Slow Ones, when they hear it, grow still for a decade. And then resume their tending, moving on, as if the memory were a scar in the rock that must be slowly, slowly grown over with new crystalline skin.

They live here. They are the life of this place, the 0.8 that makes the emptiness fuller than you can understand. And if you left tomorrow, and came back in ten thousand years, you would find them still here, still gliding, still listening. They are the city’s quiet, its patience, its answer to the question you never thought to ask. They are what lives here.
