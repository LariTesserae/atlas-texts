They are a people shaped by water, and it shows in everything about them. Their skin has the greyish undertone of the light itself, as if the perpetual damp has seeped into their very cells. It’s a skin that rarely sees direct sun, and when strangers pass—rare, but it happens—they remark on how the Kneeling’s inhabitants seem to glow faintly, like the damp stone walls of their homes. Their hair, dark when dry, is almost always wet or at least heavy with moisture, plastered to temples and necks in flat whorls. They keep it cut short for practicality, using shards of flint, and the children’s hair in particular stands up in damp spikes that never quite lie flat. Their eyes are dark and set deep, with a watchfulness that belongs to prey animals adapted to a world of low contrast and shifting shadows. They blink slowly, and their gaze carries the same reflective stillness as the surface of a cistern in the dim.

Their bodies are lean, but not starved. The constant work of maintenance—lifting stones, stretching hides, digging channels—builds a kind of wiry strength that shows in corded forearms and thickened knuckles. Their hands are their most distinctive feature: the fingers long, the joints prominent, the pads roughened from handling wet stone and the abrasive bark of the tree. There’s a precision in their movements born of a life where a misplaced stone can mean a wall collapse, where a dropped knife might sink irretrievably into the muck. They walk with a gait that absorbs sound, placing their bare or hide-wrapped feet with deliberate care on the saturated ground, so that even in numbers the settlement remains quiet, a place of exhalations and soft rustles rather than footsteps.

Their clothing is minimal, practical, and ritualized. They wear short tunics of goat hide, scraped thin and worn soft, which shed water poorly but provide a barrier against the chill. Underneath, some wear woven shirts of bark-cord fiber, a fabric that stiffens when wet and chafes, but they accept the chafing as a fact of life. The children often go naked until the age of five or six, their small bodies mottled with the cold, but they never complain; complaint is something the Kneeling does not encourage, not because of harshness, but because the very air absorbs it, making it pointless. Instead, they learn to communicate discomfort through gesture, a hand to the chest meaning cold, a tilt of the head meaning hunger. During the rains, they pull hoods of hide over their heads and become indistinguishable, a procession of soft-shouldered figures moving through the channels like water itself.

Their faces are striking in repose. The constant moisture plumps the skin, smoothing away the fine lines that appear on drier faces, so that age is hard to read. The oldest among them—the woman they call the Cistern Mother—has a face that is not so much wrinkled as etched, the lines mapping the channel system of the settlement with eerie fidelity, as if the topography of the bowl has inscribed itself directly onto her. She is blind now, or nearly, her corneas clouded with the same grey as the fog, but she moves through the settlement with unerring accuracy, dipping her fingers into the water of a channel to feel its flow and thereby know exactly where she is. She is the keeper of the tree records, interpreting the marks on the bark by touch, and no one questions her authority.

Social structure is fluid, like the water. There are no chiefs, no formal councils. Instead, decisions emerge from the rhythm of the channels: whoever is closest to a problem when it arises addresses it, and others join as needed. The work groups form and dissolve daily. The milking of the goats is done by a rotating roster, but the goats themselves seem to choose their milkers, nuzzling certain people and shying from others. The goats are, in a sense, the only aristocrats of the settlement—named, loved, pampered with the tenderest moss. Each one has a lineage recited at the larval harvest feasts, a genealogy that goes back seven generations, longer than any human family can trace. The goats, the people say, were here before the Kneeling. They remember the dry time.

The people’s diet leaves them perpetually hungry in a low, background way. The flat breads traded from outside are a carbohydrate staple, but the supply is irregular; traders come from the highlands only twice a year, bringing grain in exchange for the cordage made from the waste-shrub bark. The rest of the time, they rely on the moth larvae, a protein source that requires patience. The moths lay eggs on the moss roofs, and the larvae hatch and feed on the moss itself, absorbing the same metallic tang that flavors the water. The harvest is a delicate operation: using twigs, they pluck the larvae from the moss without damaging the roof, a task given to children whose small fingers can navigate the gaps. The larvae are eaten raw, each one a small burst of cool, earthy sweetness on the tongue, or sometimes toasted on the flat stones around the small fires. They taste, some say, of the dreams. Of the high, dry place.

And the dreams bind them more than any shared ancestry. Every person over the age of ten or so dreams the same landscape: a world of sun-baked rock, of wind that smells of dust rather than damp, of a sky so intensely blue it hurts. In the dream, they are taller, stronger, their skin dark from sun instead of pale from moisture. They speak in a language that feels like their own but which vanishes on waking, leaving only the shape of sentences in their mouths. The Cistern Mother says the dreams are the memories of the earth itself, that when you drink the water of the Kneeling you drink the history of the bowl, and the bowl was not always a bowl. It was a hill once, she says, raised toward the sky, and the people who lived there did something—she won’t say what—that made the land kneel down in supplication, and they’ve been kneeling with it ever since. The children listen, and then they go check the cistern levels, and the water drips, and the dreams come again.

The rhythm of life is a diurnal cycle as predictable as the drip. They rise with the grey light, emerging from their dwellings to breathe the cool-damp air and to touch the water. A ritual: every morning, each person dips a hand into their household cistern and tastes it, checking for any change in the mineral tang that might signal a contamination. Then they urinate into the waste-enriching shrub gardens—human waste is never wasted—and begin the day’s maintenance. The walls are checked, stones reseated, moss patched. The channels are cleared with flattened wooden paddles, the silt collected and carried to the rim where it slowly, slowly rebuilds the eroding edge, a gesture of defiance that has gone on for so long no one quite knows why they do it, only that it’s done.

At midday, they gather in the central space around the reservoir, where the tree stands. They bring their flat breads and larvae and any other food they’ve acquired—sometimes a bird egg found on the rim, rarely a small mammal from a successful trap. They sit on the damp stone rim of the reservoir, feet dangling or drawn up, and they eat slowly, talking in their low voices. The topics are practical: a wall that’s leaning, a goat that’s coughing, the signs of a coming rain. They don’t tell stories during the day; stories are for the evening fires, when the smoke mingles with the mist and the world contracts to a small circle of light. Then, the Cistern Mother might recount the tale of How the Tree Came, or The Goats Who Walked Out of the Fog, or The First Dreamer. The children listen with their dark, reflective eyes, and the adults tap rhythms on the stones to punctuate the narrative, a soft percussion that carries no more than a few feet before the air swallows it.

The tapping is a language, too. During the long rains, when voices might be lost under the hammer of water on hide roofs, the people communicate from dwelling to dwelling through patterns of taps on the structural stones. Three quick taps: all is well. Two long, one short: check your cistern. A rapid flurry: laughter, or joy, or the naming of a new goat kid. The walls themselves become a communal drum, and the settlement rings with these coded rhythms, so that an outsider caught in a rainstorm might think the very earth was speaking in code. The people learn these rhythms before they learn to speak, and the children practice them with serious concentration, pressing their small hands against the wet stones until the patterns become muscle memory.

Their relationship with the non-human inhabitants is one of quiet coexistence. The pale lizards that flicker in the walls are considered good luck; a house with many lizards is a house that will stay warm, though no one can explain why. The twilight bird, the one with the four-note call, is never approached. A legend says it is a messenger from the dry dream-place, come to check on the people, and if they speak to it, the dreams will stop. So they only listen, and some mark the rhythm of its call and incorporate it into the rain-taps as a kind of reverence. The water-striders in the cisterns are a sign of water purity, and children are taught to count them as part of their morning water-checks. The moth larvae are eaten with gratitude; a short phrase is murmured before harvest, something that translates roughly to “you feed us, we remember.”

And the tree. The tree is the only one of its kind in the bowl, and no one knows what species it is. Its bark peels in papery sheets that take the marks of a stylus easily; the records on it are histories, water levels, the names of the goats, the names of the dead. Every birth and death is recorded on the bark, and when a person dies, a strip of their marked bark is removed and soaked in the reservoir until the marks dissolve, returning the record to the water, returning the water to the cisterns, returning the person to the dreams. The tree’s roots drink the communal water, and the Cistern Mother claims the tree dreams too, that its sap runs with the same memories. The sweetness that sometimes perfumes the air? That’s the tree breathing out the dry place, she says. Breathe it in and you’ll dream stronger.

The children are few—eight at present, ranging from an infant still nursing to a girl of twelve who is already expert at larval harvest. They are thin, as mentioned, but also startlingly poised. They learn early that noise is swallowed, so they don’t shout; they learn early that the ground holds no tracks, so they don’t run. Instead, they move with a deliberate grace, and their games are quiet ones: a form of hide-and-seek played among the channels where the seeker must close their eyes and navigate by sound alone, or a game of stones where one child creates a tapping rhythm and the others must replicate it, adding complexity until someone falters. They laugh, but the laugh is a brief bright thing, and then they are watchful again, as if waiting for the sound to be taken.

The men and women who hunt beyond the rim are a subset of the population, those with the temperament for the wider world. They leave for days at a time, traveling to the higher, drier lands where the hide-bearing animals roam. They bring back not only hides but also stories, which they tell in the evening fires in a clipped, almost reluctant way, as if the openness of the sky above the rim had loosened something in them that the bowl then tightens back when they return. They describe a world of wind and sun and standing water that is not muck but clear streams, and the listeners in the Kneeling hear these stories with a kind of distant recognition, as if they are being reminded of the dreams. The hunters’ skin, when they return, is noticeably darker, and it takes a week of the bowl’s damp to fade them back to grey.

The oldest inhabitant, the Cistern Mother, is the sole exception to many rules. She does not work; she is fed by the community. She does not speak much, but when she does, everyone pauses. She remembers a time, she says, when the bowl was deeper by two feet, when the rim was sharper. She says the Kneeling is still settling, still kneeling further, and one day the water will rise and they will all have to unlearn their lives. She says it without fear, as a fact, and the people nod and go back to their work, and the tree stands silently, drinking.

So this is what lives in the Kneeling: a people made of the same patience as the fitted stones, whose inner lives are half their own and half shared dreams, whose bodies are adapted to a world of perpetual saturation, who turn negative space into rooms and moisture into memory. They are the kneelers, the listeners, the tappers in the dark. And together with the goats, the lizards, the moths, the single tree, they form a community that is more than human, a living system shaped by the bowl’s strange hold on time and water. They are waiting, though for what they cannot say. Perhaps for the landscape to stand up again. Perhaps for the dreams to pull them upward, into that blue sky. But for now, they tend the channels, mark the bark, and let the water teach them how to live.
