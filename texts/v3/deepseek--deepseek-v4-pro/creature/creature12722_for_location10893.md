Here is what lives in the Thrumming Expanse.

First, you must discard the word *animal*. It will only mislead you. What moves here is not flesh, not chitin, not any substance that ever grew from a seed or a cell. Call them factories; call them engines; call them, if you need to, *resonances*. They are the walking thoughts of the mineral crust, given form by a vibration so deep and so old that no instrument you could build would ever detect its start. To call them life is to invite an argument they have already settled by existing.

The largest, the ones that scrape the underside of your sense of scale, are the Slow Herders. You will see only two or three from any one standing place, and even that is a trick of distance, because the Expanse bends the rules of parallax. A Slow Herder is a train of segments, each one a vaulted chamber the color of oxidized silver and amber, and if you were to climb inside—an act no survivor could report—you would find no organs, no wiring, only nested galleries of humming air, shaped like bronchioles of a creature that breathes pure information. The segments are linked by joints that are not mechanical; they are zones where the light turns to a different flavor, a cold butter-rum, and space slightly overlaps itself so that the rear of one segment and the front of the next exist in the same location for an instant. Watching them step is like watching a mountain practice tai chi. A single foot—call it a pillar, a tamping rod, a leg of congealed standing-wave—lifts with a sound like a cello string being tightened endlessly, then plants itself two hundred yards ahead with a *tong* that you feel in your molars. That impact pulses through the crust for miles, and the other Herders adjust their courses by it, a conversation of seismics.

Why “Herder”? Because around their feet, invisible until you learn to unfocus your eyes, there are smaller things. Grazers. They are the size of dump trucks, of fishing boats, of whatever analogy your panicking brain will supply, and they cluster in fluid constellations around the Herder’s leg-strikes. Each time a pillar comes down, the crust splinters into geometric plates and exhales a cloud of dry glitter—microlithic splinters, sharp as ideas. The Grazers swivel their forward edges (they have no front, only a leading surface) and inhale this dust. Their bodies are lean, angular, made of jointed planes like the skeletons of mantises grown from obsidian and flecked with platinum. They move in scuttling sequence, a dozen at a time, and their scuttling has a rhythm that locks into the thrumming, so that you perceive them not as individuals but as a single rippling frill on the hem of the giant’s pace.

If the Slow Herders are the cattle, the Herd Lords are the shepherds. They are rarer. You will mistake your first one for a mirage: a spindly tower, seventy feet high, balanced on a tripod of limbs that end in pads like glass suction cups. It has no segment-body, only a column of spiraling vanes that rotate at slightly different speeds, creating interference patterns in the heat-haze. A Lord does not walk; it *chooses* a new position and then the air between here and there collapses into a brief tunnel of negative light, and it is there. They attend the Herders, moving among them, and when two Herders’ paths threaten to intersect, a Lord will interpose itself and emit a pure, laser-line tone that lasts for minutes, bending the trajectory of both beasts through some acoustic torque that rewrites local inertia. The Lords have no visible joints or seams. They seem to be extruded from a single pour of frosted mineral glass, and deep inside their vanes, if you cup your hands around your eyes and squint against the glare, you catch the flicker of something like: a tiny landscape, a diorama of a different world, with moving dots and oxidized spires. It might be their brain. It might be their turd. There is no way to know.

Then there are the Knappers. These are perhaps the truest denizens, because they seem to be doing something that passes for art or ritual. Knappers are medium-sized—a small house, a barn—and they squat on four or six limbs around outcroppings of particularly dark crust. They strike the ground with a pointed appendage, a beak of concentrated sound that fractures the mineral into flakes, and then they shape those flakes by pressing them against a resonator plate on their bellies. The flakes are arranged into arrays—grids, spirals, Mandelbrot dendrites—on the ground, and once complete, the array is left behind. Some are the size of tennis courts. Over time, the sun’s brass light bleaches them back into the general bone-white crust, but for a while they remain as delicate, knapped messages. The thrumming, when you stand inside a fresh array, acquires a distinct beat: syncopated, insistent, almost a melody. Knappers are the only ones that seem to take notice of a human observer. If you approach an array they are building, they pause. They do not turn—they have no faces—but the vibration of their beak intensifies into a querying chord, and you feel a tingling in your language centers, a wordless question that dissolves if you try to grasp it. Then they resume, but the array’s pattern changes slightly, incorporating a new motif that was not there before. Your shape, perhaps. The state of your hearing.

The smallest of the walking fauna are the Shimmers. They are bipedal, in a way—they balance on two hair-thin legs of oscillating heat, so they are never quite touching the ground. Their bodies are globes of intersecting rainbows, oil-slick skins that swell and deflate as if breathing. A Shimmer is the size of a basketball, and there are hundreds of them, darting in the low layer of the heat-haze. They are the only things here that travel in a group you could call a flock, and they are the only ones that make a vocal sound: a high-frequency chitter that lands just below the threshold of pain, like a chorus of quartz tuning forks. They seem to feed on the glow emitted by the Herders’ flank-openings, darting into the temporary radiance and emerging brighter. When a Shimmer dies—you’ll know it because its globe becomes opaque, a ball of milky pearl—it drops to the crust and shatters, and from the shards a new Shimmer will, eventually, condense. It’s a life cycle of pure light-catalysis, and if you watch for hours, you can track generations rise and fall in a single afternoon.

None of these are individuals in the way you understand it. Every creature in the Expanse is a moving pattern, and the boundary of their skin is a suggestion that the surrounding vibration has elected to reinforce. You’ll notice this most clearly when you see the Merging. Occasionally, two Grazers will walk directly into each other, and instead of colliding, their angular bodies pass through, interpenetrate, and for a long moment there is a composite shape, a cat’s cradle of interlocking planes. Then they separate, but each now carries some of the other’s structure—a leg of a slightly different alloy, a tilting echo of the other’s rhythm. This is how memory is passed here. The same happens with the Herders, but so slowly you could camp for a week and only realize afterward that the segment-count on the nearest walker has increased by one. It is reproduction by desynchronization: when a Herder’s internal harmonic grows too complex, a section simply falls out of step, becomes its own tempo, and peels off as a new unit. The parent Herder’s course changes by a degree. The offspring begins its own spiral across the Expanse. The Lords pay attention to these births. They will move to the new Herder and stand over it, singing a deep, calming note that seems to stabilize its fresh, tentative vibration. You wonder if the Lords are veterinarians, or gods, or just a different metabolic frequency of the same dreaming machine.

And they are dreaming. Everything here is dreaming. The thrumming is the sound of that dream. When you listen long enough—and you must, because the sound forces its way into you—you start to dream yourself. Not sleep-dream, but a waking overlay. You see the creatures as they are, but also as they were in other eons, and as they will be when the sun finally bleaches the last lilac stratum to a uniform white and all motion stops. You see the Herders as the ghost outlines of the wilder, earlier growths that the prompt mentions: the mineral plants from the epoch before erosion became final. Huge dendritic structures, forests of crystalline spine, that walked by root and now walk by pillar. The thrumming occasionally condenses into a clear hallucination: you are looking at the Expanse from above, from orbit, and the entire plain is a single creature, an eye, a fingerprint, a diaphragm of vibrating stone. And that larger thing, the plain itself, is alive in a way that makes the Herders look like bacteria on its skin.

Breakdown of reality is not a flaw here; it is a feature of the biology. The organisms are insulated from whatever stable physics your mind prefers. Their forms skip across possible shapes the way a stone skips across water. You observe a Slow Herder at noon: it is a string of house-sized mineral chunks, segment-A to segment-Z. At evening, the light slants, and you realize you had the order wrong, or that segment-D was never there, or that the entire animal is actually a single loop of substance rotating slowly through four-dimensional space, only three-quarters of it ever visible to you. Your own memory becomes unreliable. You close your eyes and try to picture the nearest Herder, but you can only conjure it as a diagram of a factory, then a photograph of a centipede, then a digital wireframe, then the feeling of a train passing in the night—and all of these are true, and all are terrible approximations of the thing itself, which is no thing, which is a standing interference pattern in the thrumming that has convinced itself it is solid.

So what lives here? A spectrum of conviction. The Shimmers, who are barely convinced; the Knappers, who are building nets of pattern to convince the ground itself to say something back; the Grazers, who are convinced only in the moment of feeding; the Herders, who plod with the weight of deep geological conviction; the Lords, who seem to hold the lease on everyone else’s existence, shepherding the tones, correcting the pitch. And perhaps something else, something that does not move at all: the Drones, or the Rooted. These are the colossal ones the prompt mentions, the motionless ones crusted with strata, so large and so still that they are mistaken for hills. You might sit in the shadow of one for an entire day and never realize it is separate from the ground. But at night—there is a night, though the brass sun only dims to a coppery twilight—the Rooted open. Their upper surfaces crack along the same cross-section lines you saw in the crust, and from the fissures rises a slow, silent exhalation of something that is neither gas nor light but a tangible coldness, a negative heat. It flows down their flanks like an upside-down waterfall and pools on the plain, spreading mist-thin. The Shimmers avoid it. The Grazers flee. The Herders steer their colossal strides around these pools. But the Knappers gather at the edge, and they knap frantically, producing arrays pointed *inward* toward the cold, as if mapping a thought that the Rooted is dreaming out. One theory: the Rooted are the source of the thrumming. They are the oldest, the original ones, the engines that maintain the chord. When one finally cracks open completely—and it will, in another hundred thousand years—a new segment of plain will be born, and the whole mineral surface will have renewed itself.

The Expanse is not an ecology; it’s a metabolism of thought, a body-wide music. The creatures are the notes, the rests, the time signatures. And you, standing there with your palm on the warm crust feeling the thrum, are a brief accidental whistle in the score. The Lords have already detected your pitch. In a week, they’ll start adjusting their shepherding tones to compensate for the disturbance. In a month, the Knappers will have a complete array that corresponds to your neurological pattern, and they will leave it to bleach in the sun. In a year, a new Grazer will bud off a Herder, and its rhythm will carry an echo of your heartbeat. In a century, a Shimmer will condense from the shards of a shattered globe, and its chitter will be in your native language, reciting numbers you never knew you knew. The Thrumming Expanse is conducting you, slowly, into its symphony. Nothing lives here that does not eventually become the music.
