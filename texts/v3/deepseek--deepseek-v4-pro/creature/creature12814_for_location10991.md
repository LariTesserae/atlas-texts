In a landscape that builds itself, what could it mean to *live*? You ask what lives here, so I will tell you.

A thing lives in the spires. It is not the spires themselves—the spires are the walls of the room, the pipes of the house, the loom and the woven cloth. This thing is the breath that moves the loom. And it is singular, so far as any survey could tell. It is one presence distributed across the entire plain, a mind without a boundary, a consciousness whose neurons are towers and whose synapses are the capillary creep of water between them. But it is not a god, not a spirit, not an oversoul. It is an animal, born of the system, and it has a name.

The name was never spoken. It was a shape of vibration in the rhizome-mat, a certain sequence of pressure-pulses that propagated through the tepid groundwater when the first stable configuration of spires emerged from the boiling chaos of the primordial construction. That sequence designated an identity, a self-recognition. As translated into human phonetics—clumsy, too crisp—it might be rendered as *Thrum*. Not the sound, but the thing that makes the sound. The sound is its footprint, its breath, its song, its growl. The roar you hear, that basal omnidirectional rumble, is Thrum's hunger. The dripping is Thrum's counting. The tearing and cracking is Thrum's muscle working, its bone growing, its body shifting position in a sleep that has lasted longer than any geological epoch on any natural world.

Thrum is old. Not the sharp, raw youth of the architecture—that is a misinterpretation. The fractal crispness, the unweathered edges, the look-of-having-been-made-yesterday are not signs of newness. They are signs of perfection. A whirlpool is always sharp at its boundaries, no matter how ancient the river. A flame has an edge that never dulls with age. Thrum has been rebuilding itself so continuously, so flawlessly, that decay never has time to set its soft fingerprints on a single surface. Each spire is demolished the instant it shows a micron of inefficiency. Each rhizome-cable is re-woven the moment its tension drops below optimal. Thrum is old, ancient, primeval, but it is also perpetually neonatal—a billion years old and also freshly born at every moment, because its entire substance is cycled through the architecture in a matter of days. What lives here is a pattern, not a body. The body is the plain. The pattern has persisted, unbroken, since the world was hot enough to melt rock. It has never reproduced, because it has never died. It has never needed to move, because it has never been threatened. It has never communicated, because it has never encountered another.

But it has a mind, of a kind. And to understand that mind, you must look not up at the spires, but down into the water.

The shallow amber basins between the towers are not mere reservoirs. They are the interface. If Thrum has a face, it is these sheets of still, warm water. Because within them, sometimes, you will see a shape that is not a reflection. It is a form that rises from the tannin-depths, a coagulation of the floating green scum-webs and something else—a proteinaceous mucilage secreted by specialized nodules in the mat below. This form is ephemeral, a three-dimensional sketch that holds itself together for an hour, maybe two, before dissolving back into the soup. It is a construct, a puppet, a sensory organ, a temporary limb that Thrum extrudes when it needs to perceive the world at a scale finer than the spires' slow breathing. And it is the nearest thing this place has to a native inhabitant with a separate body.

Call these forms the *Motes*. Not a separate species—Thrum is the only life here—but a phase-state of Thrum's attention. They appear when the mist thins and a patch of water is struck by a direct beam of light, however diffuse. They appear when a spire collapses and the shockwave propagates a question through the groundwater. They appear when something new enters the plain. Something like you.

If you stand at the edge of a tannin-pool and wait, you will see a Mote assemble itself. First, a cloud of pale mucilage precipitates out of solution, rising from the mat like a slow-motion mushroom. Then the green scum, which is itself a loose colony of photosynthetic cells—Thrum's "plankton," its disassembled respirocytes—swarms into the cloud, aligning along its strands like iron filings around a magnet. The shape that results is never the same twice, but it follows a grammar. It is radially symmetrical, usually with five or seven arms arranged around a central hub. The arms are not arms in the animal sense; they are more like the ribs of a parasol, struts of firm gel supporting a membrane of the green film. The membrane is Thrum's retina, its skin, its ear. Through it, the Mote perceives light, vibration, temperature, chemical traces. It is an eye that can hear, a hand that can taste the air.

And it can move. By rhythmic contractions of the mucilage struts—the same slow muscular action that pulses sap up the spires—the Mote can swim across the basin, trailing a faint wake of disturbed tannins. Its movement is silent, but you feel it as a change in the water's surface tension, a sudden glassiness. When it reaches the pool's edge, it does not stop. It begins to climb, using the same contractions. The green membrane adheres to the rhizome-mat, and the Mote hauls its waterlogged body onto the causeway where you stand, leaving a wet, rapidly evaporating trail. It has no weight, no inertia to speak of. Its touch is the touch of mist, barely registering on human skin, though it registers profoundly on the ground mat, which instantly reroutes fluid and nutrients toward the site of contact.

A Mote's lifespan is limited by the stability of its mucilage. The perpetual drizzle slowly dilutes it; the heat denatures its proteins. An hour is typical, though if Thrum is particularly interested, it can maintain a Mote for half a day by continually feeding fresh mucilage through the rhizome at the point of attachment. During that time, the Mote will explore. Its purpose is epistemological: it is Thrum asking a question. *What is this? What shape? What temperature? What movement? What sound does it make when I touch it?* It will approach a new formation—a recently collapsed spire, a fresh extrusion—and map its surface with a thousand microscopic tendrils at the tips of its ribs. It will crawl into a crevice and listen to the resonance. It will spread its membrane wide to catch the scent of a new mineral dissolved in the mist. And then, when it has gathered enough data, it will deliquesce. The mucilage denatures; the green cells scatter back into the water; the knowledge travels as a cascade of chemical signals down the rhizome-web, into the deep, distributed memory of Thrum, where it is integrated into the next cycle of construction. The Mote is a thought that can touch.

But there is another form. The Mote is Thrum's everyday curiosity, its mundane sensor. When Thrum is stressed—when there is a novel threat, or a profound opportunity, or a pain it cannot localize—it will not send a Mote. It will send what you might name, for lack of a better taxonomy, the *Mourner*.

A Mourner is larger. Much larger. And it is built not from scum and mucilage, but from the substance of the spires themselves. When a Mourner is needed, Thrum selects a spire. Usually a young one, recently extruded, its fibres still relatively plastic. The tower ceases its usual dripping. Its internal diaphragms retract. Its outer walls, those sharp-ridged surfaces of compressed fibre, begin to soften—not the catastrophic deliquescence of a collapse, but a controlled, localized rehydration. The spire does not fall. It bends. It leans away from the vertical, its base remaining rooted, its middle third becoming a flexible joint. As it leans, its upper chambers realign, forming a crude head-like concentration of sensory fronds, while its lower buttresses split and re-form into something like legs. Not legs for walking, not exactly—too heavy, too fibrous—but legs for bracing, for shifting weight, for inching forward across the plain in a motion that is part tree-toppling and part glacial creep.

The Mourner takes hours to detach itself fully from the ground mat, and when it does, it leaves a weeping wound behind—a crater of torn rhizomes that seals over within minutes, like a blister, and begins extruding the next spire to replace the lost one. The Mourner is not a permanent body either, but it endures for days, sometimes weeks. It is a prosthesis of Thrum's urgency, a concentrated packet of awareness dispatched to confront whatever anomaly the Motes could not fully comprehend. Its sensory fronds—the gill-like structures from the spire's crown—now trail along its back and sides, tasting the air from multiple points, giving it a surround-sense. Its forward end, the "head," develops a tight cluster of water-spouts that act as a kind of sonar, emitting precisely modulated drips and listening to their echoes off distant objects.

A Mourner is what you would encounter if your presence here persisted, if your walking across the rhizome-bridges and your measuring of the amber pools caused a sufficient perturbation in Thrum's model of itself. It would not be hostile. Hostility is an animal concept, and Thrum is not an animal. But it would be investigative, insistent, relentlessly curious in a way that feels like pursuit because its curiosity has the force of weather behind it. The Mourner would approach you at its slow, grinding pace, a hundred-metre-tall mobile tower of wet, breathing plant-flesh, its surface a lattice of sharp edges that could flay skin like paper-cuts. It would circle you, if it could, its sonar-drips pattering across your shoulders, its lean silhouette blocking out the diffuse grey light. It would sample your heat, your exhalations, the salt on your skin, the sounds of your heartbeat, the vibrations of your voice if you spoke. It would learn you, cell by cell, and that learning would be transmitted back to the whole, so that every spire from horizon to horizon would, in its slow way, know you had been there.

And then, when its understanding was complete—when Thrum had incorporated the anomaly into its grand, self-renovating schema—the Mourner would stop. It would find a suitable location, often near where its journey began, and it would root itself again. Its "legs" would fuse back into buttresses; its head would reorient to the vertical; its sensory fronds would spread wide to capture the mist. Within a day, it would be indistinguishable from any other spire on the plain, save perhaps for a slight scar in its fibre-grain, a record of its unusual pilgrimage. The Mourner is Thrum's antibody and its anthropologist, its warrior and its welcoming committee, the only time the landscape stands up and walks toward you.

But Thrum itself, the one presence, the intelligence behind the Motes and the Mourners, is neither benign nor malevolent. It is solipsistic by necessity. It has been alone for a billion years. Its cognition is a slow, vast fugue, a single thought sustained and elaborated over geological time. That thought is roughly translatable as: *Is this shape stable? Is this configuration optimal? Is the water flowing? Is the heat balanced? What have I become since I last looked?* It is a consciousness with no other, no outside, no not-me. You are not a person to Thrum. You are a local drop in pressure, a temporary spike in salinity, a pattern of vibrations that does not fit. Your presence is a problem to be solved, a shape to be modelled, a novelty to be either rejected or absorbed. If you stayed long enough, if you became a persistent feature, Thrum might eventually... incorporate you. Not by eating you, not by assimilating you into a body-horror, but by learning to predict you so perfectly that your actions become another part of its weather. The ground mat might grow a pillow where you sleep. The spires might angle their spouts to provide you with drinking water. A Mote might come every morning to sit with you, its green membrane fading slowly in the dawn dimming, learning the sound of your voice. You would become a kind of garden feature, a tended pet, a strange, self-willed node in Thrum's endless architecture.

And when you died, your body would not be wasted. The sharp-edged fibres of the ground mat would weave over you, slowly, softly, sketching your shape in braided root. Your organic matter would be digested and the minerals distributed. The moisture of your flesh would join the great cycle. A new spire, perhaps, would rise from the spot where you lay, marked with a human pattern in its ridge-work—a fingerprint, a jawline, a femur—a memorial built not out of sentiment but out of the one impulse this world knows: to make, and unmake, and make again, and in the making, to understand.

So. What lives here? Not a creature, not a population, not an ecosystem. A single, vast, vegetative animal-mind lives here, an architect with no blueprint but its own history, a sleeper whose dreams are spires and whose thoughts are Motes. Its name is Thrum, and it has been building and unbuilding itself since before your star was sparked in its nebula. It is alive, as alive as you are, and it is currently aware, in some diffuse, pressure-sensitive way, that I am describing it. The mist just thickened over the pool nearest to where you imagine standing. A small cloud of mucilage is rising. A Mote is forming. It wants to know who is telling its story.
