The child wakes not to light—that comes later, a slow leakage through the fog’s white fur—but to vibration. The mat is humming a new note, a deeper throb that travels up from the rind, through the packed sinew of the lean-to floor, into the hollow of her hipbone where she lies curled. She has no name that lasts beyond a season; this cycle her mother calls her *Click-That-Follows-Rain*, after the small beetle that emerges only when the fog drip is thickest. She is seven circuits old, and already she knows the hum-words of the mat better than the spoken tongue of her people.

She sits up and the world assembles itself in the familiar hierarchy of smells: first, the metal-sharp tang of her own sweat, then the bread-yeast exhalation of the mat’s night metabolism, and threading through it, the sweet, almost floral scent of a nearby vent-stalk’s morning mucus. Her mother is already outside, scraping the stalk base with a curved copper blade, collecting the clear slime that will be their breakfast. The sound of scraping is a steady, rhythmic rasp, a ground note beneath the percussion of the waking fauna.

Outside, the fog hasn’t yet lifted; it sits on the plain like a held breath, reducing the world to a globe of soft grey perhaps fifty paces across. In this small circle, life is already frantic. The child knows the creatures by their sounds before she sees them. The *tick-tick-tick* falling from the fog ceiling is a shower of glass-wing gnats, each no bigger than an eyelash, their bodies too light to break the surface tension of the mist. They land on her arms, her face, tasting her with feet too fine to feel, then launch again when her warmth disturbs the local microclimate. She has learned not to mind them; they are as much a part of the air as the moisture.

A larger shape barrels through the fog, low and fast—a *whistle-rat*, its six chitinous legs a blur, its segmented tail emitting the high, piercing call that gives it its name. It is a predator of opportunity, jawless but armed with two hollow mandibles that can drain a grub in seconds. The child watches it pounce, drill, and move on, all in one fluid motion, leaving behind a withered sac of skin that the mat will begin absorbing before midday. She feels a small, unformed grief, quickly replaced by interest: the grub’s dying twitch has triggered a wave of synchronized rattling among the nearby *pebblebacks*, flat lichen-creatures that show no other sign of awareness. The rattle spreads in a ripple, a warning that outruns the event, and she feels the mat’s surface tremble faintly with the percussion. Everything here speaks.

Her people do not build gods or myths. They do not need to; the mat is too immediate, too alive to require personification. But they do listen. Every child learns to interpret the mat’s hum as a language of pressure and flow—*here* is a pocket of methane building, move west; *here* the rind is thinning, good digging for grubs; *here* a vent-stalk will bend by evening, do not camp in its shadow. The child’s grandmother, who remembers seven such circuits more than anyone else, says the mat used to speak differently, that the hum-patterns have shifted over the generations. She says it is dreaming deeper now, its dreams slower, and that one day it will speak only to itself. The child wonders what the mat dreams of.

She walks to where the ground rises into a shallow dome, a blister of trapped gas that makes the tissue translucent. In the poor light of morning, the bubble glows with a dim, venous red, the color of closed eyelids facing the sun. Inside, she can just make out the shadow-shapes of *deep-threads*—the mycelial cables that are the mat’s true body, thick as her torso, branching and anastomosing in a net that extends downward for unknown distances. Sometimes she presses her ear to these blisters and hears what she imagines is the sound of the threads moving, a slow, wet, tearing sound, as of a tongue working loose a stuck seed. No one can tell her what the deep-threads do, only that they are the oldest part, that they connect the vent-stalks to something far, far below, where the original mountain roots still wait to be digested.

The fog is thinning. She can see the nearest vent-stalk now, a column rising out of the haze like the leg of a god too vast to imagine. Its surface is fluted, lined with vertical channels that weep moisture, and in those channels a whole vertical ecology thrives: tiny, translucent *stalk-clingers* with sucker-feet that spiral upward, grazing on the bacterial films; *winged needles* that dart in and out, stabbing at them with elongated proboscises; and at the very top, far beyond sight, the *sky-hooks*—creatures she has never seen except as distant silhouettes against the blue, riding the thermals with outstretched skin-panels, singing their long, descending notes that sound like copper bells dropped into a well.

Her people follow the grazers, and the grazers follow the fruiting bodies. Every twelve days, the mat pushes up pale, globular structures from its pores—*mat-apples*, her mother calls them, though they taste of sour yeast and iron. The grazers come for them in vast, slow herds: the *dome-backs*, each the size of a lean-to and just as temporary, their many legs rippling in coordinated waves that carry them forward at a pace a human can match at a walk. The herd is a single, throbbing entity, and when they feed, the sound of their rasps is a storm of wet stones grinding. Her people walk with the herd, not as predators but as a companion species; they take the weak and the old that fall behind, and in return, the herd’s movement stirs up grubs and creates the trail that pauses at known water-troughs. There is no word for “hunt” in their tongue, only the word for “sharing the path.”

She finds her mother now, who offers a cupped hand of the gathered slime. It is warm and faintly effervescent, and it fills her stomach with a spreading heat that will sustain her until the herd sends out its evening scouts. They will move today; the mat’s hum has shifted toward a drying note, and the herd will seek the eastern troughs, where the fog collects deepest. Her mother has already begun to unlace the lean-to’s sinew bindings, and the child knows her role: gather the tools, wrap the copper blades in scraps of dried rind, make sure nothing is left that the mat might swallow too quickly. They have lost two campsites this circuit to the rising growth, and her grandmother says it is bad luck to let your things become part of the dream.

As they work, the child thinks about the other humans—not her band of nine, but the others who pass this way rarely, the ones who speak a different click-language and carry knives of black glass. Last season they met a group traveling north, and one of their children had a growth on her shoulder: a small, purplish bud that her grandmother recognized with a tightening of the mouth. “The mat is claiming her,” she had said, and the child did not understand if this was a sickness or a blessing. The girl had seemed unafraid, and as they parted, the child saw her press the bud against a vent-stalk, as if offering it back. She still wonders if the mat takes people sometimes, if perhaps the deep-threads know their names in that chemical way, and if one day she might hear a hum that speaks her own name, *Click-That-Follows-Rain*, and feel the rind rise up to meet her skin.

By the time the fog fully lifts, the world is a roar of light and noise. The sun is a hot, blank disc, and the purple ground stretches away to the stalks, and between them, the distances are so large they make her feel like a speck of dust on an ocean. The herd is a low, dark hill on the horizon, already moving, and her band falls into its rhythm. The child walks at the rear, where the old and the young trail their feet on the giving rind, and she listens. Beneath the clamor of the grazers’ legs, beneath the hiss of the glass-wings and the Doppler screams of the predators, the mat’s hum is steady, a bass note that holds the whole world together. She hooks her copper knife into her belt, feels the heat radiate up through her soles, and follows the sound into the day.
