They are not born so much as separated—each from the rock, or so they say, though no one living remembers a time when they were not. They emerge from the wet dark of the lower chambers, the ones carved deepest into the basalt where the thermal vents breathe and the walls run with a thick, mineral sweat. An elder finds them: a small, curled form nestled in a cradle of travertine, skin slick and grey as the stone itself, eyes already open, already calm. There is no cry, no violence of arrival. The water carries them into the world as it carries everything else, slowly, without breaking.

In form they echo the basalt’s own geometry. Their bodies are long and slender, but dense—compact with a weight that suggests the stone they were drawn from. Their skin is a uniform grey, the color of wet rock in the perpetual overcast, with a faint iridescence when the light catches it, like the sheen of a still pool. It is hairless, smooth, and always damp; the mist condenses on them as it does on every surface, beading along their forearms, their shins, the ridge of their brow. Their faces are quiet escarpments: high cheekbones, a flat bridge of the nose, a mouth that rests in a line as straight as a cleavage plane. Their eyes are large and set deep, the irises a pale mineral green—the exact color of the moss that grows in the seam-cracks—and they reflect the diffuse light with a slow, liquid patience. They have no external ears, only a slight depression at the temples, and yet they hear everything: the layered song of drips, the distant shift of a settling slab, the faint hiss of a far-off vent. Their hearing is a kind of whole-body resonance; the stone speaks to them through their bones.

They move as the water moves, without urgency. Even a step is a considered act: the bare foot, long-toed and gripping, tests the slick basalt, finds the microtextures, settles its weight with a kind of geological deliberation. They do not slip, they never hurry. When one of them walks, they seem to flow across the stone, a grey ghost merging with the wet black, leaving no trace except the brief interruption of a dripping rivulet. Their hands are their most intricate feature: six-fingered, each digit tipped not with a nail but with a small, hard pad of what looks like polished obsidian—a living glass that can tease apart the finest mineral wool or grip a sheer wall without effort. It was with these hands they shaped the city, prying the basalt loose along its natural planes, fitting each block so tightly that no mortar was ever needed.

They wear no clothing; there is no need in the unchanging warmth, and the water is a second skin. But they adorn themselves. Thin cords of braided travertine—harvested from the rims of the cisterns over generations—circle their necks, wrists, and ankles, strung with pendants of polished basalt, chips of vivid green moss suspended in clear resin, or tiny, perfect spheres of condensed water encased in a hollow crystal. These are not ornaments of vanity but of identity: each pendant records an event, a memory, a turn of thought. An outsider might think them silent and undifferentiated, but to one another, they are as varied as the vegetation islands, each with their own internal climate.

Their days—if days can be said to exist in a place without time—are a rhythm of maintenance and attention. They tend the channels, not with tools but with their fingers, tracing the carved stone to feel for any incipient clog, any grain of mineral detritus. They never clear; they simply redirect, coaxing the flow. They visit the thermal baths, sinking into water that wells up from the earth’s hot memory, and there they stay motionless for long stretches, their bodies becoming part of the liquid, the mist rising around them. They cultivate the moss gardens, guiding the spongy green growth into patterns that only they can read—not for food, exactly, but for some sustenance. Do they eat? Perhaps they absorb. In the deep chambers, they press their bodies against the warm, sweating walls and enter a state of stillness that blurs the line between flesh and stone. The water carries dissolved minerals through their skin; the moss breathes oxygen into the air they draw in with their whole surface. They are not separate from the terrarium.

Communication among them is a subtle thing. They can speak, their voices a low murmur that blends with the water’s susurrus, but words are spare, each one chosen like a stone for a wall. More often, they converse through touch: a hand laid along a forearm where the droplets trace patterns that speak of pressure changes, of slight shifts in the heat rising from below, of the distant pulse of a new seam opening. And they listen, endlessly, to the water. The drips are a language; the trickles carry news from the far edges of the plateau. One of them—the Listeners—sits for long spans in a particular alcove where a single stalactite feeds a basin, and by the pitch and interval of the drops, she can tell you the precise saturation of the mist five ridges away, the birth of a new moss cushion in a cleft, or the slow, fractional tilt of a leaning slab.

They have no narrative that moves forward. They do not speak of ancestors or future generations, for they are both. When an elder grows too dense, too still, until they no longer rise from the bath, the others carry them—with immense care, over many increments of water-time—to one of the deep fissures where the basalt still glints with its original fire. They place the body into a narrow vertical shaft, and over a time that has no measure, the stone absorbs it. The skin greys to match the wall, the limbs become indistinguishable from the surrounding ridges, and a new vein of mineral runs through the rock, a faint twist of pale green like a frozen memory. And somewhere else, in a chamber below, a new shape curls in the warm dark, eyes open, already listening. They say the water transfers the pattern, that the rock remembers. They are not individuals so much as a single consciousness distributed through time and stone, a slow wave passing through the basalt.

What do they think, in the long stillness? They think of water. Not as an element, but as a state. They perceive the world as degrees of wetness, gradients of condensation, the way a particular slab holds a teardrop longer than another. Their science is a taxonomy of drip sounds: the sharp plink of a fall onto a hard surface, the soft thud into moss, the musical cascade of a multi-tiered channel. They have names for the different flows—the Weeping Wall, the Whispering Sheet, the Counting Pause—and they can read the health of the plateau by the balance of these voices. They think of shape, too: the endless fascination of how basalt fractures, the hexagonal pillars that emerge when the rock cools, the way the city mimics the natural prisms. Their art is to fit a stone so truly that the seam becomes a line of water, a thread of light, a signature you must feel rather than see.

Their emotional range is a calm spectrum, but not without depth. There is a quiet joy in finding a new configuration of moss, a subtle satisfaction when a channel’s flow is tuned to a particular note. There is a profound, resonant sorrow too—a collective low-pressure system—when a section of the city finally succumbs not to erosion but to the slow drawing back of the rock, a basalt fatigue that breaks a wall after eons of holding. Then they gather, not to rebuild, but to listen to the new drip pattern the fallen stones have made, and to accept it as a new limb of the eternal shape. They grieve, but their grief is the mist thickening for a time, an extra beading on their cheeks that is not condensation.

No strangers come here. They have no concept of war, or trade, or language beyond their own. They know, in a theoretical way, that the plateau ends, that below the cloud deck there is a world of thin air and harsh sun, but it is a myth, a dark tale told in the rare moments of communal storytelling. Their stories are not of events but of stones: the biography of a single slab, from its birth in the earth’s hot belly to its journey upward, its encounter with the sky, its slow marriage to water. The children—the young ones, fresh from the deep—learn these stone-tales by placing their palms on the walls and feeling the vibrations, each one a distinct timbre, a history encoded in the mineral lattice.

At the edge of the settlement, where the view opens across the misted valley of prisms, they sometimes stand and face outward, not to see but to feel. They extend their arms, fingers spread, and let the distant trickles of water-sound wash over them, each droplet from a far-off pool transmitting a tiny percussion through the stone beneath their feet. In this posture, they might hold for an entire cycle of the constant light, becoming a slender grey prominence themselves, another element of the landscape, indistinguishable from the basalt spires except for the faint glimmer of their mineral pendants. And when they lower their arms and turn back to the channel-work, it is with the same deliberate, patient motion, as if they are simply water returning to its bed, finding a new path among the stones they have always known.

They are the human shape of a world without time, the living flesh of the plateau's long exhale. They are the listeners, the stone-tenders, the water-readers. They do not fight the stillness; they have become it. And if you were to stand among them, your own breath loud and hurried, you might feel, for just a moment, the immense, quiet presence of a life that has learned to live at the speed of condensation, and you might understand that here, to live is simply to continue—a slow, wet pulse in the heart of the unmoving cloud.
