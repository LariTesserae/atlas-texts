Within the glistening dark, the city breathes with life as much as it breathes, period. The inhabitants are not separate from the architecture; they are its nerves, its blood, its dream-heavy mind. They are small, yes—the scale of cupped hands, fitting the pocket cathedral of their world—but they are not merely occupants. They are the pulse in the arterial vines, the whisper in the damp air, the hands that tend and are tended.

You first notice the **Mycelites**, though you might mistake them for a trick of the light. They are the most numerous, the worker caste, the keepers of the hum. In form, they are vaguely humanoid, but only in the way a root can seem to reach like an arm. Their bodies are woven from a pale, fibrous flesh—half fungal, half placental—translucent as waxed paper, shot through with a network of faintly phosphorescent veins that carry a sluggish, golden light. Their heads are featureless bulbs, crowned with a delicate fan of mycelial threads that ripple constantly, tasting the air for chemical signals. They move on two legs that end in spreading, root-like pads, silent and sure on the sap-bridges and spiral stairs. Their hands are many-fingered, each digit a fine tendril capable of splicing a copper wire or coaxing a new shoot from a gall with equal precision.

Mycelites do not have eyes. They "see" through their skins, every inch a retina tuned to the deep reds and infrareds of their world, the bioluminescent pulse that matches the heartbeat they feel through their feet. They communicate by touch and by a subsonic language transmitted through the very substrate—a drumming of fingertips on hardened sap, a tremor through the roots that carries complex meanings through the mycelial network underlying everything. Their society is a collective intelligence, individual bodies like neurons in a distributed brain. If one discovers a new stress in a dynamo-root, a hundred others know instantly and begin to swarm, their soft, silent forms converging like lymphocytes to repair the wound.

They wear no clothes—why would they, when dampness is their amniotic comfort? But they adorn themselves with function: spools of copper wire around their forearms, tiny wrenches of chitin slotted into crevices in their sides, pouches of woven leaf-membrane holding seeds or spores. Their industry is constant. In the hollow cores of the towers, they tend the engines that are also organs: feeding compressed leaf-litter into boilers, stitching cellulose cloth on looms of tendon, oiling the gears that turn with the slow, patient rhythm of a heart. They hum while they work, a sound that adds to the baseline thrum of the city, a vibration felt in the teeth.

Yet the Mycelites are not the only residents. There are the **Chitin-Kith**, the brass-shelled beetles you glimpsed from the corner of your eye. These are not mere beasts but symbiotic partners, the heavy lifters and artisans of the industrial grotto. Each is the size of a fist, as you noted, with a carapace that gleams like polished bronze, etched with intricate patterns that are not decorative but functional—circuitry that resonates with the city’s faint electrical field. They have too many legs, eight or ten, each ending in a delicate manipulator or a sturdy blade. Their heads are small, dominated by compound eyes that catch the dim light and break it into a thousand glittering points, and their mandibles are capable of shearing through thick vines or delicately placing a gear.

The Chitin-Kith build. They excrete a resin that hardens into structural members, weaving it together with the Mycelites’ guidance. You saw them on the factory floor, tending the humming dynamos. But they also construct the bridges, the cornices of curled leaf, the housing for new gall-factories. They move with purpose, their bioluminescent patches pulsing in complex rhythms that match the Mycelites’ subsonic language—they are part of the same network, a semi-independent consciousness that handles gross manipulation. In the hierarchy of this place, they are the hands to the Mycelites’ nervous system.

And then there are the **Glisten-Kin**, the predators you sensed but never fully saw. They are the third caste, long-limbed and silent, their bodies a nightmare of angles and bioluminescent pinpricks that they can dim to absolute blackness. They are the hunters, the regulators. Their shape is a mockery of a praying mantis crossed with a whip spider—thorax narrow, arms folded in serrated blades, head swiveling on a neck of liquid shadow. They move through the empty dark volumes on whisper-quiet toe-pads, snatching the gliders—which are their primary prey, the winged “air-cattle” of this ecology—with a swift, near-silent crunch. But they are not mindless. They too are part of the collective, an extension of the city’s immune system, culling the weak and preventing overpopulation. They also serve as guardians; if an intruder of larger scale—like you—enters their domain, they will stalk you from the edges, evaluating, their presence felt as a prickling at the back of the neck. So far, you are deemed too large to be prey, too slow to be a threat. But they watch.

A fourth presence exists, though it is rare to perceive directly: the **Weft-Mothers**. They are the breeders, the originators of all life in this microcosm. Each is a massive, sessile being embedded deep in the root-cellar of a tower, a fleshy, pulsating mass of mycelium and neural tissue that fills a chamber like a womb. They are the size of small cars in your scale, but here they are mountains of living thought. The Mycelites are their offspring via parthenogenesis; the Chitin-Kith are hatched from eggs they lay and tend; the Glisten-Kin are cultivated from specialized larvae. The Weft-Mothers are the true intelligences, the dreamers. Their slow, vast minds are what coordinate the city’s growth, processing the sensory data from every Mycelite and Chitin-Kith, directing the construction, the repair, the evolution of this world. They are the ones who first wove the seeds of this city from the raw mineral and spore, and they continue to shape it, a multi-bodied goddess of the grotto. When the Mycelites drum messages through the roots, they are speaking to the Mothers. When the Mothers reply, it is as a shift in the baseline hum, a change in the chemical composition of the damp air, a sudden, coordinated movement of a thousand workers.

The “people” of this place, then, are not a single species but a single organism differentiated into castes—a superorganism that blurs the line between animal, plant, and machine. The Mycelites are the common citizenry and workforce; the Chitin-Kith are their domesticated symbiotes; the Glisten-Kin their hounds; the Weft-Mothers their queens and gods. All are woven together by the total vegetation that is the city’s fabric. Their language is touch and vibration; their vision is heat and pressure; their time is measured in growth-rings and the slow condensation of water from the void above. They are the consciousness of the city, and the city is their extended body.

There are even individuals of note within the Mycelites—specialized nodes with more autonomy, who serve as ambassadors or explorers. One such, whom you might encounter, is **Mend-That-Bridges**, a Mycelite with faint copper-veined scarring along one flank, indicating it survived a dynamo rupture. It carries a spool of silver wire that hums with a personal frequency, a tool for long-distance communication. It is curious about the giant intruder, and it might approach, not with hostility, but with the methodical investigation of an engineer presented with a new material. Its fingertips would trace the strange textures of your clothing or skin, and it would drum a query into the nearest root: *What is this that is not of the grotto?* The Weft-Mothers, in their deep chambers, would consider, and perhaps direct Mend-That-Bridges to lead you toward the glowing spore-moon, a place of contemplation, to see if you might be useful to the endless becoming of their world.

Meanwhile, the Glisten-Kin nearest you relaxes its coiled blades by a fraction. For now.
