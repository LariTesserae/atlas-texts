They come from the old cities, the ones that sank into final sunset or locked themselves behind sealed domes. They come because the pay is stable, and stability is the only currency that matters here. The workforce of the plateau is a mosaic of pale, heavy-lidded faces, their skin tinged faintly blue around the lips from the constant chill and the trace argon in the air. They wear their hair cropped short or bound tight against the damp, because loose strands attract the fog’s condensation and then freeze into tiny icicles that tick against the cheek. Their clothes are not uniforms but a gradual convergence: long coats of felted wool, patched with leather at the elbows, their surfaces worked with wax until they repel water like the hide of a beast. Underneath, multiple layers of linen and a curious, locally made fabric woven from the fungal mycelium—soft as kid leather, faintly phosphorescent after long wear, so that in the darkest corridors workers glow at the seams like deep-sea fish.

They move with a deliberate economy. No one rushes on the plateau; haste is a dangerous misalignment with the world’s tempo. A worker walking from the dormitory to the stamping mill takes the same route every shift, but not out of habit—out of calibration. The path is a boardwalk of grated metal suspended above the moss, and each plank has a subtle give. Over months, a person learns the exact sequence of dips, the places where the fog has rusted a bolt just enough to make a particular creak, and this auditory map becomes a part of their body. If a step sounds wrong, they stop. They listen. They turn back. There is a department—the Office of Terrestrial Consistency—whose entire staff of sixty-three spends its days walking assigned circuits, listening with stethoscopes pressed to the rock, tapping with rubber mallets, and marking inconsistencies with chalk that dissolves in the next mist. Their faces are the most worried, their coats the most patched, and they are revered in the way people revere those who negotiate daily with the unformed.

Children are rare but not absent. They are small, quiet creatures who learn to read before they learn to speak loudly. Their first toys are miniature reality anchors—smooth steel spikes with rounded tips, painted in bright enamels—which they drive into the nursery moss with tiny mallets, mimicking their parents’ rituals. They play games of “freeze and flicker,” where one child suddenly goes still and the others must guess whether she is about to phase into a different shape. The schoolhouse is a geodesic dome lined with copper mesh, and the lessons are half engineering, half natural history, and half a kind of metaphysics that hasn’t been named elsewhere. A child of ten can recite the Three Rules of Persistence: *If it shimmers, wait. If it sinks, leave. If it sings, do not answer.* They know the life cycle of the whistle-rat, the smell of a probability flare (ozone and burnt sugar), and the correct way to address a foreman who has just witnessed his lunch pail dissolve into a puddle of light.

The foremen and forewomen themselves are a distinct breed. They are older, their faces engraved by decades of watching for minute deviations. They carry long-handled “consistency hammers,” which are less tools than instruments of inquiry: a gentle tap on a machine housing produces a tone that tells them the phase-state of the alloy inside. Their authority is absolute but soft-spoken. A foreman’s command never rises above a conversational murmur, because the engines converse in bass frequencies and a sudden shout might create a harmonic that sets the gelidite to slurry. They live in quarters attached to their mills, sleeping in bunks lined with heat-reflective blankets, waking every few hours to perform the “rounds”—a walk through the machinery that is as much a meditation as an inspection. In the oldest foremen, a change occurs: their irises slowly develop a metallic ring, copper or silver, and their skin becomes cool and dry to the touch, as if they are gradually embedding into the industrial system they tend. The workers say they are “going native” to the machines, and it is considered a mark of distinction. When a foreman retires, they do not leave the plateau; they simply walk into the deepest engine house one day, and the door seals behind them, and their name is engraved on a plate outside. Everyone understands that they have become part of the slow piston’s cycle, their heartbeat now one of the rhythms that keep the world solid.

Beyond the human hierarchy, the fauna is a teeming, shifting population that the city has learned to treat as fellow-laborers. The six-legged elk, called “phase-falls” in the local pidgin, are herded not for meat but for their antlers, which are harvested when they reach a state of maximal fractal branching. The antlers are made of a material that is half bone, half crystallized time; they ring with a pure tone when struck and are installed in the great engines as vibration dampers, smoothing the harsh frequencies that might accidentally unmake a wall. The herds are tended by a guild of herders who live out on the moss plains in small, dome-shaped huts that crawl on hydraulic legs, repositioning themselves whenever the ground shifts. These herders communicate with the phase-falls using a system of low whistles and rhythmic foot-stamping, a language that the animals understand as clearly as any human speech. A herder can walk into a herd of sixty beasts, each broad enough to crush her, and move among them with the casual confidence of a librarian among shelves. The animals in turn will gather around a herder who is injured, their breath warm and smelling of ozone, their bodies forming a protective circle while their antlers hum a soothing resonance that actually staunches bleeding.

The whistle-rats are the city’s shadow citizens. They are pests only to the uninitiated; to everyone else, they are a fact of life, as inevitable as the fog. They nest in the warm guts of the stamping mills, their bodies phasing through iron casings to sleep curled around steam pipes. Their droppings are a problem—they are slightly caustic and can eat through insulation—but the rats themselves provide a service. They are exquisitely sensitive to reality fluctuations, far more than any human instrument. A whistle-rat will flee a section of pipework an hour before a probability flare, emitting its namesake call—a shrill, modulating whistle that sends workers calmly exiting the area. The city has learned to map these calls. In every factory, a logbook is kept by the “rat warden,” who notes each whistle’s time, location, and pitch. Over decades, these logs have become the most accurate predictive model of the plateau’s metaphysical weather. The rat wardens are a ragged, solitary lot, their coats lined with tiny bells that chime when a rat phases through them, their pockets always full of dried moss-bread for bribing their informants.

The smog-bats hang in the thermal plumes rising from the smelters, their dark wings making them look like fragments of the permanent cloud cover stripped away and animated. They are filter-feeders, but what they filter is not just particulate matter—it is the fine dust of potentiality that coats everything here. Their droppings, the “bat-clinker,” accumulate in great piles that steam gently in the cold. These piles are the gardens of the heat-loving mycelium, a fungus that grows in thick, rubbery sheets, its color a deep orange like a smoldering ember. The mycelium is the city’s principal crop, providing insulation, a meat-like food when pressed and fermented, and a leather substitute. The workers who cultivate it are called “clinkerers,” and they live in a perpetual summer of their own making, their bodies sheened with sweat, their lungs adapted to the hot, spore-thick air. They speak a rapid, clipped dialect, a stark contrast to the measured cadence of the rest of the city, and their skin is coated in a fine, downy orange that they are never quite sure is their own hair or the fungus becoming symbiotic. They build their scaffolds over the clinker mounds, coaxing the mycelium to grow into specific shapes—tubes, panels, even entire simple buildings that will later be cured and installed elsewhere.

Then there are the things that live in the deep faults, the fissures that open unpredictably when the plateau stretches in its sleep. These are not fauna in any strict sense; they are entities that the bio-taxonomy committee has categorized under “Inconclusa,” a catch-all for lifeforms that exist only part-time. The most commonly sighted are the “glimmer-worms,” which appear as ribbons of bluish light undulating through the cracks, their bodies existing as pure electromagnetic pattern unless the fog dampens the charge, whereupon they collapse briefly into thin, gelidite shells that can be collected before they fade. They are harvested by a specialized team called the “glint-divers,” who rappel into the faults wearing rubber suits that ground them against the worm’s charge. Their work is lucrative and fatal in equal measure; a glimmer-worm’s touch causes neural overload, a burst of pure sensation that leaves the victim convulsing in something between ecstasy and seizure, their brain temporarily overwritten with alien thoughts. Surviving glint-divers come back changed, their speech peppered with words in a language no one speaks but everyone seems to comprehend on a gut level. They tend to marry each other, to form closed communities, and their children are sometimes born with faintly luminous fingertips.

In the deep, dim hours, when the fog is thick and the reality anchors hum at their loudest, a different kind of inhabitant emerges. The workers call them the “Persistence Men,” though they are not men. They appear as silhouettes of greater solidity than their surroundings, as if they are the only real objects in a world of suggestion. They walk the fog fields in straight lines, their shapes those of generic workers, but their proportions are subtly wrong—arms a little too long, heads a fraction small, as if rendered from a description rather than observed. They do not interact. If approached, they fade. If followed, they lead you in circles that eventually return you to where you started, but an hour later than you expected. The city’s policy is to ignore them. The Office of Terrestrial Consistency has a standing order: do not note them in logs, do not alter your route for them. They are, according to the most accepted theory, the world’s memory of the people who might have been, the inhabitants the plateau formed for itself before the real ones arrived. They are the original draft, and they persist in the margins, waiting for a final revision that never comes.

And somewhere at the center of it all, in the cathedral-sized geode that serves as the council hub, sits the oldest living resident of the plateau. Her name is not spoken; she is simply “the Listener.” She is ancient, her body having long since become inseparable from the geode itself—her limbs fused with the crystal formations, her hair a cascade of the same mineral growth, her eyes two calm, deep pools that reflect the faint light of the bio-luminescent fungi cultivated on the walls. She does not sleep, or if she does, it is on a cycle so slow that no one has witnessed it. Her role is to listen, literally, to the sound of the plateau. The geode’s walls are drilled with thousands of small boreholes, each connected by copper pipes to different parts of the city, the factories, the faults. These pipes bring her the sounds: the rumble of engines, the whistle of rats, the soft crackle of lichen growing, the distant, impossible noise of reality peeling back at the edges. She sits in the center of this auditory web and translates it into advice. Once a day, the council members enter and stand before her, and she whispers a single sentence, sometimes a warning, sometimes a cryptic encouragement, sometimes a piece of bewildering information (“The south-east stamping mill’s third flywheel will develop a harmonic on the next cycle; tune it to C-sharp minor”). Her words are carved into the official record and are always, eventually, proven correct. No one knows how she knows, or what she was before she became this. The children are told she is the voice of the plateau itself, given flesh so that humans can hear. The adults know it’s more complicated, but they let the story stand. She is the living proof that to live here is to become part of the place’s slow, uncertain becoming, to learn the rhythm of a world still learning its own.
