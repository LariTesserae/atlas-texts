They are a people shaped by the cavity’s press, and you see it first in their stance: a constant slight forward lean, shoulders rolled inward as if eternally braced against a wind that does not blow. This is not submission but readiness—the habit of a life lived on salt crust that can crack, on ladder rungs notched from brittle bronze-stalk, under a sky of held heat. Their bodies are compact, dense, with short, powerful legs and long arms that end in hands broad as spades. The fingers are thick-jointed, the nails dark and ridged like the ferrous stone they scrape and grip. Their skin, where it is not wrapped in woven-plant-fiber cloth, carries the color of oiled copper—a deep, ruddy, lustrous tone that shifts in the seam-light, sometimes greenish where mineral dust has settled into the creases. There is hair, but it is sparse and fine, a sparse thatch on the scalp that they often shave away with obsidian flakes to keep it from catching on the hanging bronze mats. Their faces are wide across the cheekbones, narrowing to a chin that is strong but not prominent, and their eyes are large, set under heavy brow ridges that shield them from the actinic noon-knife of the sun. The irises are a muted amber or a cloudy gray, the pupils capable of constricting to pinpricks or dilating to gather the dim blue glow of the growths. They blink slowly, deliberately, as if each closing of the lid is a small act of preservation in the moistureless air.

Their breathing is audible and constant, a soft background hiss that matches the vent-thrum. Their lungs have learned to take the 2.8-heat air and sift from it what they need, expelling a fine, hot vapor that shimmers above their mouths. They do not sweat; instead, their skin excretes a thin, oily film that traps the body’s moisture and mingles with the ambient dust to form a protective, faintly slick sheen. When they touch one another, it is with a slight adherence, a tackiness that makes the gesture linger. They smell of that oil and of the spicy reek of the harvested paste, of the charcoal they bank and the hot metal they work. It is not an unpleasant smell; it is the smell of the hollow itself made animate.

Their diet is the _thick-mash_, the oily paste scraped from the younger bronze vines when the pressure in the growths is high and the sap rises. They harvest it with flat-edged blades of notched iron, kneeling on the terraces and drawing the blade along the ropey stems, collecting the ooze in shallow clay bowls that they carry strapped to their thighs. The paste is dark, iridescent, and warm from the internal chemistry of the plant. It tastes of umami and iron and something volatile that tingles on the tongue like a mild electric current. They eat it straight, or they mix it with a fine, crystalline powder ground from the dried towers of older growth—this makes a kind of dough that they press into flat cakes and set on heated stones near the vent mouths until it stiffens into a chewy, nutrient-dense brick. They do not eat often; a small portion sustains a body for a full waking cycle, and there is a seasonal rhythm to their hunger that tracks the vent-pressure cycle. During a _deep-blow_, when the gases well up and force everyone to the upper terraces, they fast, and their bodies slow, their heartbeats dropping into a long, patient thrum that matches the hollow’s own breath.

Their society is woven into the terraces like the vegetation they tend. They name themselves not by family but by _vent-zone_ and _tier_: the people of the Sixth Terrace, the East-Vent Lineage, the Hollow-Floor Guild. A child is born into the tier where its parents were working at the time of birth, and it is raised collectively by the hands that tend that zone—there are no exclusive pair-bonds, only a diffuse network of care that shifts as the growths shift and the terraces are dismantled and rebuilt. The newborn is coated in the thinned oil of the first harvest after its birth, a ritual that marks it as part of the living chemistry of the place, and its first solid food is a paste from the oldest vine-tower in that zone, a way of imbuing it with the memory of the long cycles. They do not have a concept of childhood as a separate, sheltered state; from the moment a child can grip a stone, it learns to scrape, to sort the iron-rich crystals from the waste, to feel the hum of the vents through the soles of its bare feet.

Their language is a thing of sound and sign in equal measure, because in the loud hollow, spoken words can be swallowed by a sudden hammer-blow or the crack of a spore-case. They speak in a register that cuts through the ambient rumble—a combination of voiced pitches, tongue-clicks, and a kind of tonal humming that resonates in their chests and carries across the terraces. But they also sign with their wide hands, a rapid, fluent language of gestures that can be seen across a bridge-span. The signs are borrowed from the movements of the growths—a slow unfurling of the fingers to indicate a new vent opening, a sharp downward jerk to warn of falling salt-crust, a cupped hand that trembles to mimic the settling of charcoal. Their names, when spoken, are often onomatopoeic echoes of the hollow’s own sounds: _Tuk_, the sound of a hammer on a bed of stone; _Shirr_, the hiss of a hot gas release; _Boom-Clack_, the double-beat of a tree-tower cracking and falling. They do not use these names often; they are more commonly known by their tier and their tool—the Scraper of the West Curve, the Furnace-Tender of the Deep Ledge.

Their technology is a direct extension of their hands and the cavity’s resources. They smelt iron in small, beehive-shaped bloomeries that they build into the terrace lips, channeling the natural vent-heat through clay tubes that they fire from the very same ferrous clay. The iron ore they use is the stone of the walls themselves, crushed and roasted until it yields a spongy bloom. They hammer this bloom on salt-block anvils, their hammers made of the same iron bound to dried-vine hafts. The sound of hammering is the settlement’s heartbeat, a syncopated rhythm that rises and falls with the work cycles. When a new bloom is drawn from the furnace, the hammerers fall into a shared tempo, a rapid, intricate pattern that is at once practical—working the slag out of the metal—and ceremonial, a chorus that tells the entire hollow that iron is being born. They make blades, hooks, needles, awls, small hinges for the storage boxes they carve from the dried cores of the vine-towers. They do not make weapons; there is nothing to fight. But they make _strike-spikes_, heavy iron points that they drive into the crust to test for stability, and _vent-keys_, long, twisted rods used to chip away at mineral seals when a vent begins to close.

Their art is carved into the walls of the rooms they hollow out of the ferrous stone. Using stone chisels and iron scrapers, they incise long, flowing patterns that mimic the growth-forms of the bronze vines—spirals, branching curves, layered arcs. These carvings are not decorative in our sense; they are functional memory. Each line records a vent shift, a terrace relocation, a birth, a deep-blow. A trained eye can read the walls of a dwelling like a book, tracing the history of the tier back for tens of generations. They also craft small, palm-sized sculptures from the precipitated salts left behind when old vent-mouths seal. These are abstract, organic shapes, often resembling the spiral towers of the vines, and they are given as gifts when a child moves to a new tier or when a new bloomery is lit. They are not kept; they are placed back into the vent system, returned to the mineral matrix, a cycle of form and dissolution.

Their calendar is the vent-breath. The deep vents on the hollow floor pulse with a regular, deep chuffing that never stops, but superimposed on this are longer rhythms: the _shallow-blow_, a minor release of sulfurous gas that happens every few cycles and makes the lower terraces unlivable for a day; the _deep-blow_, a major event that occurs once every several hundred cycles and forces the entire settlement to the highest tiers for up to a week; and the _tectonic sigh_, a rare, whole-hollow shudder that shifts the vent alignments dramatically and triggers a mass migration of the bronze vines. The people track these with tally-sticks of notched bronze-wood, long bundles of them stored in a central chamber on the Third Terrace. The eldest of each tier—those whose bodies have slowed the most, whose breaths are so shallow they are almost part of the ambient hum—serve as _vent-rememberers_, their entire lives given over to the counting and the memory. They sit in the dimness, their fingers running over the notches, their lips moving in a constant, silent recitation. They know when the pressure is building by the pitch of the chuff, by the way the heat presses on their skin, by the scent of the air turning from spicy to acrid. Their word is law; when a vent-rememberer rises and begins the slow climb to the upper terraces, everyone follows, carrying only their tools and their clay bowls.

Their relationship with the vegetation is intimate and respectful, but not worshipful. They do not personify the growths; they understand them as a parallel life-form, one with which they have co-evolved a symbiosis. They prune and harvest and guide the vines toward new vents, but they also learn from them—the way a tendril tests the air before committing to a direction, the way a tower hollows itself out to reduce weight, the way the spore-cases burst only when the gas mixture is right. Their own architecture mimics these strategies. Their dwellings are often built around a central, living vine-stalk that they have trained into a load-bearing column. Their bridges are made from the long, dried runners, treated with oils to make them flexible and strong. They sow new growth by plugging the cracked ends of vine-cuttings into the vent-cracks, packing them with a slurry of mineral dust and their own waste, a ritual that ensures the cycle continues.

Despite the density and the constant sound, they value a particular kind of attention. They listen. In the din, they listen for the small changes: the creak of a vine shifting its grip, the micro-fracture of a salt crust under too much weight, the distant clink of a dropped tool that signals an accident. Their hearing is extraordinarily acute, and they can isolate individual sounds from the wash of noise, focusing on a single thread of meaning. This ability extends to their social life; conversations often happen across great distances, one person on a high terrace speaking in a pitched hum, another on the floor responding with a series of tongue-clicks, their words weaving through the hammer-beats and the vent-thrum. When two people stand close, they often fall into a softer register, a breathing murmur accompanied by the tactile signing of their hands on each other's arms, because the sound is still too loud for intimate speech. They sleep in shifts, and even in rest, one ear remains half-open, attuned to the rhythms. A full silence would terrify them; it would mean the vents had stopped, the growths had died, the world had ended.

They do not dream of water. They have no concept of it. In their origin stories—told in the wall carvings and in the rhythmic chants the children learn—the world has always been dry. The first people emerged from the hot mineral seams, shaped from the precipitated salts and given breath by the vent-gases. Their mythology is a geology: the cavity was formed when a great bubble of gas cooled and collapsed, leaving this protected space. The sun is a distant, hostile eye that they avoid. The flat salt plain above is the realm of death, a place of blinding light and silence where nothing grows and the heat is a raw, blasting wind. They go there rarely, only to retrieve a fallen tool that has been flung over the edge, or to gather a rare crystal that forms on the crust during the deep-blows. Those who go wear wraps of thick vine-fiber over their entire bodies and move with a hunched, urgent gait, never straying more than a few paces from the cavity lip. They do not look at the horizon; there is nothing there but the white flats stretching away, a terror of emptiness.

And yet, they are not a people of fear. They move with a calm, deliberate grace through their loud, hot, mineral-lush world. Their days are full of work and sound and the slow tending of the growths. Their nights—or what passes for night, the dimmer part of the cycle when the seam-light fades to a dull, ruby glow—are full of the softer sounds: the rustle of bodies settling into sleeping niches, the low hum of a vent-rememberer reciting the notches, the faint, high ringing of iron cooling. They are a people of the in-between, a living seam between the breath of the earth and the architecture of their own hands. They do not know that other worlds exist, with water and green vegetation and silence. They do not need to. The hollow is full, and so are they.
