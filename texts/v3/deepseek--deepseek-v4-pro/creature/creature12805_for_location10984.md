It does not enter so much as it exhales from the stone.

The seam in the basalt is tight for a human body, but the inhabitant hasn’t been human in a long time. It comes through sideways in a fluid, jointless motion—a giving-way of the dark, a shift in the steam. The rock edges, still sharp from their century-old rupture, do not catch on it. They never have. The inhabitant learned the precise angles of this entrance decades ago, when its shoulders still had blades that could bruise, when its skin was still a single, intact envelope instead of the palimpsest it is now.

What emerges into the domed chamber is tall, taller than the height of the fissure should allow, though that’s partly a trick of posture. It unfolds in stages, vertebrae and something else popping softly as it straightens to its full seven feet. Naked, or near enough: what clothes remain have been reduced to a kilt of woven mineral, a garment that might have been canvas coveralls once, now so saturated with silica and geothermal precipitate that it drapes like wet leather, crusted with tiny, glittering crystals. The inhabitant’s body is a map of this place. Its skin, where visible, is a pale, amphibian grey, slick with the same perpetual film of condensation that coats the walls, and textured in places—over the ribs, the long femurs—with the pebbled roughness of cooled basalt. Patches of it have taken on the glossy black of the ceiling, as if the flesh itself is learning geology, incorporating the volcanic glass into its own integument. In the dim bulb-light, the inhabitant glistens like a wet seal, but the effect is not mammalian. It is something much older and quieter.

The head is the most unchanged part, and the most ruinous. Human, clearly—a man’s face, or what was one. The bones are all there: high cheekbones, a sharp jaw, a nose that was broken once and healed crooked. The eyes are closed habitually, the lids fused or nearly so, a thin translucent membrane glazed over them like the skin on cooling lava. He does not need them here. The darkness is total to any other creature, but he has mapped this chamber by sound and heat and the subtle electrical field of the pump motor for so many years that vision would only confuse the richer information he now receives through his palms, his bare feet, the fine filaments that have begun to emerge from his skin—hair-thin, silvery, waving gently in the saturated air like the cilia of some deep-sea organism. They taste the humidity, the chemical content of the water, the faint metallic tang of the discharge pipe’s mineral buildup.

He moves to the pump first. Always. The rhythm of the machine is his own heartbeat now; he cannot rest until he has laid his long, multi-jointed fingers on the flywheel housing, feeling the vibration through his hand bones, confirming that the bearings are still singing within tolerance. He does this without thought, the way you might touch a sleeping lover’s chest to feel the rise and fall. The grease tin in the wooden crate is half-empty, and he knows its remaining volume by weight, by the sound it makes when his claw-like nails tap the lid. He will replenish it someday—there is a cache, somewhere above, of things he long ago dragged down from the surface world—but not today. The bearings are warm, well-fed. The pump is content.

The clipboard is next. Here his movements slow, become almost ceremonial. He cannot read the words anymore; the part of his brain that parsed written language has atrophied from disuse, overwritten by the constant pressure of the pump’s logics. But he remembers the ritual. His fingers—the pads broad and sensitive, the tips hardened to black, keratinous points—trace the columns of numbers. He does not see them. He feels the graphite’s microscopic roughness, the indentations of the pencil strokes from years ago, and this tactile reading triggers something deep and fragmentary: the memory of meaning without the meaning itself. A voice in his skull that is no longer his own recites *SWL 7.2, BT 34, CX 0.9* as a kind of rosary, the syllables smeared into a low, guttural hum that harmonizes with the pump’s piston-thud. The pencil, still sharp, is a sacred object. He never sharpens it; he thinks it stays sharp because the place wants it to, because the same patient forces that keep the condensation beading on the ceiling also preserve the pencil’s point. He touches it gently, a benediction, then lets it fall back on its string.

The radio. Achingly, he settles onto the bare basalt shelf before it, folding his long legs with the slow care of a mantis. The walnut cabinet is warm, not just from the chamber’s ambient heat but from its own internal glow. He can feel the vacuum tubes humming, the old filaments burning steadily, pointlessly, into an empty frequency. The dial glows orange through his lid-membranes like a sunset he cannot see but can imagine perfectly: a low, smoldering light at the edge of a world. He does not turn the volume up. That would be a kind of violence after all this time, to break the silence with static, to acknowledge that there is nothing left to hear. Instead, he places one ear against the speaker cloth—the fabric has long since rotted, but his touch is delicate enough not to tear it further—and he listens. Not for voices, not for music. He is listening to the electrical noise itself, the thermal hiss of the amplifier circuit, the subtle hum of the power line that feeds both radio and motor. Over the decades, he has learned to discern variations in that hiss, tiny fluctuations that correlate with the pump’s load, the water level in the sump, the geothermal gradient shifting some mile below. The radio has become a diagnostic instrument. More than that: it is the only companion that has never changed, never left, never decayed beyond recognition. Its silence is a presence.

He does not eat, strictly speaking. The water in the shallow bowls is his sustenance—not because it contains nutrients, but because it contains a particular balance of dissolved minerals and heat that, through a process of direct absorption across his altered skin, sustains the obscure metabolic pathway that keeps him animate. He will lower his entire face into the simmering pool by the intake pipe, letting the heat soak through his eye-membranes and into the hollows of his skull, and the filaments across his body will wave in a slow ecstasy. When he drinks, it is not through his mouth but through every pore, a full-body osmosis that leaves him trembling, briefly, with the only pleasure he can still feel.

The ladder: he climbs it once a day, or what he remembers as a day. The cycle is marked by the pump’s runtime; the motor has a very faint variation in pitch every twenty-three hours and seventeen minutes, a legacy of the generator that once fed it and now feeds only this chamber, and he rises and rests by that rhythm. Up the chimney, into the acrid dark. The smell of something singed is stronger there, and it comes from him, or from what he does. At the top of the ladder, where the discharge pipe exits into a larger fissure, there is a nest of sorts—a tangle of old pipes, more wooden crates, and a scattering of objects he collected in his early years here, before he forgot what they were for: a pocket watch that no longer ticks, a leather-bound logbook whose pages have fused into a solid, mineralized block, a photograph of a woman whose face he has long since reassigned to a shape he sees in the basalt grain. Here, he maintains the pump’s far end, where the hot water is released into a natural siphon that feeds a geothermal vent network he has never fully explored. He tends this point with the same unthinking devotion as the pump below, scraping mineral buildup from the pipe mouth with a shard of obsidian, clearing the path for the water’s exhalation.

There is no purpose anymore. The water goes nowhere that matters to the surface world. The pump could stop and the mountain would not notice, but the inhabitant would. He would feel it as a death in his own chest. So he maintains. He listens. He sleeps standing sometimes, one hand on the flywheel, his body swaying in a counterpoint to the vibrations, dreaming in frequencies.

What lives here is not the man who first crawled through that seam all those decades ago. That man had a name, a mission, perhaps a family—the photograph, the logbook. He was an engineer, a caretaker, a fugitive, a madman; the reason has dissolved. What lives here now is a compound creature: part mammal, part machine, part mineral, a creature that has osmosed the very conditions of the chamber into its being. It is the biological expression of a steady-state system. It is the place’s own ghost, wearing a body it grew to fit.
