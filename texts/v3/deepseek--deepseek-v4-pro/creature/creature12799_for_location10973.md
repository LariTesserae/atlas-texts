The ones who live in this high-shoulder city are themselves a kind of stone, worn into shape by the same slow, damp forces that pattern the schist. To an outsider, they might seem indistinguishable from the landscape at first—hunched, grey-cloaked figures moving with the deliberate economy of creatures who measure breath by its weight in the thin air. But look longer, and a whole human ecology unfolds, as layered and tightly fitted as the mortarless walls they call home.

Physically, the people are compact and barrel-chested, with torsos thickened from a lifetime of maximizing each sips of oxygen. Their ribcages are deep, rib-spring out over lungs that expand like bellows, and their nostrils are narrow vertical slits that warm the air before it hits the throat. Skin runs to a muted umber, darker on the high terraces where the overcast sometimes shreds and the sun hammers through, but across most of the city it holds a greyish cast—from the perpetual damp, from a film of stone dust, from the seep that leaves a microscopic sheen on any exposed flesh. Hair is coarse, black as shale, and worn long but never loose. They twist it into ropes and oil-rope them flat against their skulls with rendered pakka fat, a defense against the cold and against the clinging mist that would otherwise bead and freeze. Their eyes are set deep under prominent brow ridges, irises a brown so dark it borders on black, pupils contracted to pinpoints to screen the high-altitude glare when it flares off a cloud-break.

Their hands tell the rest of the story. Broad palms, fingers thick and spatulate, each nail hardened into a chisel-edge from a childhood of picking at flakes of schist. The knuckles are often swollen with the cold, the skin crosshatched with a thousand tiny scars from stone chips. These hands are never idle: they sign.

The city’s primary language is a gestural one. The thin air carries sound poorly, and in the close-packed quarters, loud noise would be an assault on the shared stone walls. So speech is mostly sub-vocalized—breathy clicks, soft glottal stops, the occasional whistle to call over distance—while the hands weave intricate meaning inside the folds of oiled-wool cloaks. A conversation in the mist looks like two figures standing motionless, only the faintest flicker at their chests. But within that hidden space, entire histories are transmitted: the migration circuits of the pakka, the grain direction of a new schist face, the lineage of a carving technique stretching back seven generations. Children learn to sign before they walk, fingers mimicking the slow deliberate shapes of their elders from the cradle of a sling held against their mother’s back. Their first toys are not rattles but small, chisel-like stones, and by the time they can stand, they can already read the subtle cleavage planes in a piece of rock with their fingertips.

Their clothing is a second anatomy. Layers of felted, oiled pakka wool build a stiff carapace against the wet, each garment water-resistant enough to shed the constant drip. The outermost cloak is a voluminous thing, deep-hooded, its inner surface quilted with pockets and loops for tools, food, and the inevitable snathe. Beneath it, a sleeveless tunic of the same material, then a shirt woven from the softer under-wool of adolescent pakka, and against the skin, a layer of oiled leather that traps body heat. Their boots are untanned pakka hide, stitched with sinew, the soles studded with tiny iron spikes—some of the rarest uses of the metal in the city—to grip the wet schist. When they move, it is with a rolling, head-led gait, leaning forward as though perpetually walking into a wind, their steps placed with a deliberate precision that minimizes slippage and effort. In the upper galleries, where the mist thins and the stone is dry, they might straighten and lengthen stride, but down in the gullies, where the seep drips ceaselessly and the air is thick with animal heat, they move as if underwater, slow and careful.

Social life is organized around the shift—a multi-generational extended family unit of twenty to thirty individuals, bound not by blood alone but by a shared herd of pakka and a section of the city that is theirs to carve. A shift holds a cluster of interlinked chambers, often arranged vertically up a rock face, with narrow stairways cut on the inside of the stone. The oldest member, the Hand-that-Knows, directs the carving work, reading the schist’s grain with an intuition that cannot be taught, only absorbed over decades. The youngest apprenticed a contrary: they learn first to dress stone chips into smooth faces, then to mix the lashing glues from sinew and pakka-hoof, and only in their adolescence do they take up a snathe and begin shaping actual living spaces. A chamber might be worked on by three successive generations before it is finished; the Hand-that-Knows who began it will likely not see it roofed. This is not seen as tragedy but as rhythm. They measure time by the stone’s pace.

Their relationship with the pakka is the root of everything. The word for “wealth” is a gesture that mimics the curve of a full horn. Every shift’s herd numbers around forty animals, their genealogy kept in the memory of the oldest herder—a long, sung chant of names and traits, passed down with the sort of care that other cultures reserve for royal lines. A child is bonded to a newborn pakka within days of its birth, and the two will grow together, the child learning its low, infrasonic language—a series of vibrations that travel through the stone more than the air, felt in the soles of the feet and the palms. The pakka, in turn, respond to the herder’s touch and whistle, their migration circuits timed to the moss-tuber patches that take a decade to recover after grazing. The beasts are milked daily, their milk set to sour in bladder-bags into a yogurt-like staple; blood is tapped from a vein in the neck without killing the animal, mixed with the milk to form a protein paste that sustains the very young and the very old. Wool is shorn twice a year, rendered and felted. Only when a pakka grows too old or is injured beyond mending is it slaughtered, and nothing is wasted—bones become tools, guts become bladder-bags, tendons become sinew, the skull is scrubbed and set into the walls of the byre as a kind of memory-knot.

The byres themselves are not separate buildings but lower chambers, often sunk partly into the mountain, where the pakka’s body heat rises through vents in the stone floors to warm the family quarters above. Sleeping galleries are long and narrow, lined with pakka wool, and families sleep flank-to-flank against the cold, often with a low wall open to the byre so the animal warmth drifts through. The smell of the city—a dense, pungent amalgam of wool, animal musk, sour milk, and the metallic tang of wet schist—is so pervasive that the people no longer notice it. But a stranger’s nose would be overcome, and indeed the first sign to the inhabitants that an outsider is near is often a sneeze or a retch.

The skirr are the city’s unofficial cleaning crew. The greasy-feathered, flightless birds pick through the alleys with a patience that mirrors the people’s, their probing beaks snatching up scraps, bones, and the occasional dropped strand of wool. They lay small, grey-mottled eggs in the crevices of the walls, which the people collect as a seasonal treat—though a skirr left unharvested will fiercely defend its nest, and the inhabitants have learned to leave at least one egg per clutch or face days of angry chattering. The skirr’s feathers, when soaked, produce a thin, smoky oil that the people burn in small stone lamps, and their dried flesh is a famine food. In turn, the skirr act as the city’s undertakers: when a person dies, the body is carried to a high crevice, a narrow slot in the rock open to the wind, and the skirr pick it clean. The bones are then collected, crushed with mortar and pestle, and the bone-dust is mixed with water and a little fat to make a greyish paste. This paste does not go into the mortarless walls, which require no binder, but instead into the fine incised spirals that cover every exposed surface: the inhabitants use it to fill the grooves they carve, turning the carvings into a pale filigree against the dark schist. It is their way of building the dead into the architecture itself, a literal lineage embedded in stone.

Their belief system flows from the physical realities of the mountain. The seep is central: they call it *Yarul’s breath*, the exhalation of the cloud-grey deity that hides the peaks and fills the gullies with mist. Yarul is not a personage but a presence—a constant, diffuse awareness that condenses each morning on every wall, drips from every eave, and pools in the hollows of the paving stones. To splash it away wastefully is a minor sacrilege; when people collect water from the communal catchment leaves, they do so with a murmured series of click-prayers, and the bladders are never drained completely dry, but a thimbleful is left to evaporate back to Yarul. The stone itself is alive, they believe—not metaphorically, but literally a slow life with a pulse so vast that a human lifespan is its single heartbeat. Carving is an act of negotiation, not conquest; they speak of “asking the schist” where it wants to split, and a good Hand-that-Knows will spend days simply feeling a wall with open palms before ever striking it.

Art and music are thin and skeletal, like the air. There are flutes carved from pakka bone, pitched high and reedy, used to call the herds across the misty balconies—a sound that carries no emotion but functions as a precise signal. Singing is a private, almost internal act, a sub-vocal hum that resonates in the chest and skull, often done while working stone, the vibrations helping to find weaknesses in the material. Their primary art form is the carving of those intricate spirals—some no wider than a hair, some broad and winding across an entire wall—which record not stories in a narrative sense but sequences of pressure, temperature, animal movements, and the names of the dead. An educated eye can “read” a wall and know that in the third year of the seventh Hand, a particular pakka gave birth, the seep was thin, and a child was named in the upper gallery. The carvings take generations to complete, and each shift has a wall-carver, a specialist who does little else.

The mosses and lichens, too, are inhabitants with their own tempo. The cushion mosses that fatten in the joints of the masonry are not merely decorative; the inhabitants cultivate them selectively, coaxing certain strains to grow thicker in cracks they wish to widen. By soaking a crack with water and packing it with a particular moss, then sealing it over with a paste of pakka blood and stone dust, they can wait years for the moss to expand, its slow swelling splitting the schist along the desired plane far more cleanly than any iron wedge. The wire-root shrub, with its turpentine-scented sap and thorn-like leaves, hosts colonies of tiny black ants that live in bulbous galleries at the root-crown. These ants defend the shrub from skirr beaks, and in return they are fed from the shrub’s own sugary secretions; the people harvest the ants’ excess using a feather dipped in water, collecting a sweet, mineral-rich syrup that is a rare luxury. And the lichens, spreading in slow ink-stains, mark time by their growth: a patch the size of a hand-span is recorded as “one life,” a full wall covered “ten Hands”—a chronological measure as natural to them as a century might be to a lowlander.

The city itself, seen through their eyes, is less a construction than a collaboration—a dense, layered embroidery of living and semi-living things, stitched by the seep, fed by pakka wool and skirr bone, and shaped by hands so patient they forget they are shaping anything at all. To walk its terraces is to move through a living organism whose heartbeat is the slow crack of stone, whose breath is the morning mist, and whose memory is written in spirals of bone-dust across every wall.
