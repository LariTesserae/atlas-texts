There is something that lives here. Not an animal—the distinction matters. It is a creature that the noise did not drive out, because the noise is its breath, its pulse. It has no ears, not in any sense you’d recognise. Its body is a flattened, many-segmented thing, pale as a cave fish but ridged with calloused pads that grip wet basalt like iron filings to a magnet. It moves precisely, one limb at a time, never dislodging a loose flake. The whole of its perception is tactile vibration: the engine’s rhythmic thud, the high-frequency hiss of condensing water, the sundering groan of a cooling slab—all of this is its world of sound, felt through subdermal nodes arrayed along its ventral surface. It does not hear; it *resonates*.

It is old. How old is hard to say. The engine has been running for decades, perhaps centuries, and this being has been here for all of that time and longer, born from the same industrial intent that placed the machine. It was left as a custodian, or it evolved from something left as a custodian—a maintenance bioform, a living gasket between the machine and the creeping green. Its body bears traces of its origin: a thorax of fused polymers, a spine of flexible ceramic, and one limb that ends not in a pad but in a many-jawed manipulator able to interface with the cooling fins, which it does, regularly, tightening things that have worked loose, wiping away mineral buildup with a secretion of weak acid. It is symbiotic with the engine, feeding on the thermal gradient between the exhaust port and the damp air, drawing its energy from the very thing that would madden anything else.

Its feeding is a slow, sessile act. It anchors itself high on the wall, directly above the exhaust vent, spreading its body into a concave dish that catches the intermittent orange glow. The heat differential drives a biochemical cycle within its tissues, breaking down the ambient moisture into hydrogen and oxygen, rebuilding carbon scraped from the rock into usable sugars. It does not eat the plants; instead, it prunes them. The manipulator-limb trims the vines that would otherwise envelop the engine’s intake, nips ferns that encroach on the boreholes, and carefully detaches mosses from critical surfaces, laying the cut vegetation in neat compost piles in the deepest crevices. The plants feed the engine indirectly—their decay humidifies the air, their roots stabilise the loose stone—and the engine feeds it. A closed loop.

It has senses beyond vibration. Along its dorsal ridge, a row of photosensitive spots detect the flicker of the exhaust vent, reading the pulse-rate of the misfire like a clock. That misfire is its timekeeper, its circadian rhythm. When the knock grows pronounced, it knows it is time to dismount, crawl down, and adjust the timing gears with that same acid secretion, eating away microns of metal to rebalance the cycle. It performs this with devotional patience, because its own life depends on the rhythm remaining slightly off: a perfectly smooth engine would produce a constant light, no flicker, and its photoreceptors would grow blind and starve. It maintains the flaw as a farmer maintains a furrow.

It reproduces? Not in the way of mammals. One day, every few thousand cycles of the misfire, the creature’s body grows a swelling near its tail segment. The swelling hardens into a capsule of the same matte black material as the engine casing. When the time is right—triggered by some internal tally of accumulated heat—the capsule detaches and falls into the undergrowth, where it begins to pulse with its own dim light, a perfect miniature of the parent’s rhythm. Inside, a new custodian gestates, feeding on the compost of pruned vegetation, until it is large enough to climb. Only one ever reaches maturity; the others provide raw material for the moss. The parent ignores them entirely. They are just another part of the garden.

It has no name. It has no need for language. Its mind—if you could call it that—is a pattern of maintenance tasks, thermal expectations, and the deep satisfaction of a system in homeostasis. It perceives the falling stone as a gardener perceives rain: a thing that might damage the delicate plants, but ultimately brings fresh mineral substrate. It perceives the vines as both resource and potential threat, a living thing to be guided, not fought. It is, in every meaningful sense, the mind of the crevasse, the organising principle that turns chaos into a functioning whole. The engine is its heart, the vegetation its lungs, the water its blood, and the rock its skeleton. And it lives there, as certainly as anything could.
