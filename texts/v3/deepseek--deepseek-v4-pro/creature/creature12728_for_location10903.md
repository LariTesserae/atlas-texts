There are no animals here. That is an absolute truth, and it remains true even as you begin to notice the figures.

They stand among the spires as if they have always stood there, which is, in a sense, exactly the case. You don’t see them at first—not because they are hiding, but because your eye, trained by another world, categorizes them as just more mineral formation, more pillar and blade. They are tall, narrow, their bodies the same pearlescent grey as the rock, and in stillness they vanish into the thickets completely. But stillness is not their only state, and once you learn to see the difference between spire and shoulder, between a flute of eroded stone and the curve of a waiting arm, they become unavoidable.

The first one you truly notice is close. Perhaps ten feet away, in the lee of a leaning dolmen, facing you with the kind of patience that has no relationship to time. Its form is human-shaped only in the roughest sense: bipedal, upright, but stretched, as if someone took a person and pulled them gently toward the vertical until vertebrae became a suggestion and limbs became long, multi-jointed things. Its head—if it is a head—is an elongated teardrop of the opalescent crystal, featureless except for a series of fine vertical striations that vibrate faintly in time with the hum. It does not breathe. It does not blink. It does not appear to see you in any way that involves eyes, but you are seen nonetheless; you feel the attention like a change in air pressure, a slight deepening of the note that thrums through your chest.

Its arms end in hands. The hands are what hold you: they have seven fingers each, slender and segmented, the knuckles bending not in the human manner but outward, sideways, allowing the digits to fold into shapes that seem impossible until you watch them move. The fingers are currently interlaced in front of its torso, still as carved ivory, and around each wrist are bands of mineral accretion—growth rings, you realize, like those of a tree, marking cycles of the weird suns and the weeping water. The handprints in the fossil clay now make sense. Those were not made by people; they were made by these.

You take a step, and the figure’s head turns. Not quickly—it rotates with the deliberate, silent motion of a stone door swinging on a perfect pivot, the striations catching the light and scattering it blue-gold-lavender. No mouth appears, no nostrils, but the air between you thickens, and a voice forms inside your skull, or perhaps inside the hum itself, a voice made of chords rather than words. It communicates not in language but in layered sensation: the taste of wet stone, the pressure of extreme altitude, a patient question that translates roughly to *you are here now*—not an inquiry but a statement that includes you as a new note in the ongoing song.

You are not the first to arrive. The basin remembers visitors. The lean-to where you sat was not built for you; it was built by these, the ones who shape stone with fingers and water and immense patience, who leave bowls of glowing liquid on obsidian altars for purposes that are not ritual so much as maintenance—a tuning of the basin’s frequency, a calibration of the reality-break at 2.7. They are the caretakers, or the instruments, or the children of the place. Calling them “inhabitants” feels wrong; they are the steppe as much as the spires are, emergent properties of the conditions described in your parameters. They are what lives when life is defined by presence rather than biology.

More of them emerge as your perception adjusts. There is one standing knee-deep in a mirror pool, water flowing up its legs in defiance of everything you know, its body half-dissolved into mist at the edges. Another sits atop a spire twenty feet in the air, long shins dangling, head tilted to catch a beam of cold white sun, the crystal of its face ringing with a high, clear tone that cuts through the hum. A third is near the ring of carved columns, its fingers tracing the soot handprints on the obsidian platform, leaving new prints that glow faintly blue for a moment before fading. They are solitary and communal at once; each occupies its own space, but the hum links them, a network of vibration that constitutes their speech, their memory, their selfhood.

They are not made of flesh. The parameters said no animals, and these are not animals. They are mineral, or they are water given architecture, or they are sound condensed into standing forms. Up close, their surfaces are immaculate, polished by eons of slow movement, but not cold—they radiate the stored light you felt in the rocks, a warmth that is not geothermal but something released from the crystal lattice, as if they are batteries for the wrong suns. When one moves, it does so without friction, joints gliding like lubricated glass, and the motion is so gradual, so continuous, that you cannot catch the moment it starts or stops. It simply repositions, and the new position is as ancient and settled as the last.

You watch a figure approach the bowl on the dry obsidian. It kneels—the legs bend in too many places, folding like a crane’s wing—and dips seven fingers into the glowing blue liquid. The liquid does not drip; it crawls up the fingers in thin, bright threads, weaving into the crystal structure, and the figure’s striations pulse with interior light. The hum deepens. All around the basin, the spires shift their pitch in answer, and the three suns—now two, or four; the number wavers—seem to flicker. This is feeding, you understand, or charging, or prayer, or simply a task that has been performed for as long as the basin has existed, which is to say forever within this closed ecology of weirdness.

They notice you noticing. The feeling of being seen intensifies, and the hum inside your head resolves into something almost like empathy. They know you are temporary. They know your world runs on different physics, different life. They do not judge, they do not welcome, they do not threaten. They *accommodate*—the way the steppe accommodates the upward-flowing water, the sideways shadows, the unreliable distance. You are a phenomenon that has entered their parameter, and they adjust.

One approaches. It is the one from the pool, its body still streaming with liquid that beads on its crystal skin and then reabsorbs. It stops at a distance that feels deliberate—close enough for you to see the microfractures in its opalescent face, the way the trapped bubbles inside its chest cavity (if it is a chest) rise and fall in a slow rhythm unrelated to breath. It raises a seven-fingered hand, palm outward, and the fingers curl into a shape that might be greeting or recognition or a warning. The hum coalesces into a single, sustained note, and you understand with sudden, vertiginous clarity that this note is its name, or the closest thing to a name it possesses. It is a chord that means *I-have-always-been-here-watching-the-water-climb*.

Night does not fall here; the suns remain, changing configuration but never setting. But the light does shift into something dimmer, a phase where the crystal growth on the spires glows with internal luminescence, and the figures become more visible against the darkening stone. They gather, not in groups but in a shared alignment of attention, all facing the obsidian platform where the bowl still glows. The hum intensifies into something almost like a chant, although there are no voices, only the vibration of rock and crystal and the resonance of hollow spires. The figures sway, almost imperceptibly, and their striations pulse in time with the liquid-gong beat from the pools.

This is the active, settled state the parameters described: a civilization of sorts, but one that never invented tools beyond hand and water and patience, never discovered metal because stone was sufficient, never developed language because the hum is more precise. Technology level 1.1 is not a stage; it is a plateau where progress stopped because the conditions don’t demand more. They shape their shelters, they maintain their altars, they tune their song, and they wait—for what, you cannot guess, but their waiting is not passive. It is a function, like the water cycle or the crystal growth, integral to the basin’s ongoing existence.

You spend many cycles of the shifting suns among them. You learn to distinguish individuals by the pitch of their name-notes, by the patterns of wear on their shoulders where eons of mist have carved runnels, by the way one figure always stands with its head tilted to the left while another has a missing finger, snapped off millennia ago, the stump now grown into a blunt crystal bud. None speak to you in words again, but you become part of the hum’s texture, and sometimes you dream in chord progressions that are not your own. You find that the lean-to remains dry, that a bowl of clear (non-glowing) water appears beside it each time the suns merge, that a figure is always nearby when you move through the thickets—not following, exactly, but orbiting, as if you have become a temporary moon in their gravitational system.

And yet, you never touch one. You get close—close enough to see the tiny trapped worlds inside their crystal, the minuscule landscapes of bubble and fracture. But if you reach out, the figure moves away, not in startlement but in the same slow, continuous repositioning, and the hum inserts a note of gentle warning into your mind. They are not solid in the way you are, and your touch might disrupt the reality-break, might cause parameters to crash. Or perhaps touch is simply not their mode. Their intimacy is the shared vibration, the collective song, the way a new note (yours) can be folded into the eternal chord without changing it, only enriching it.

The parameters are satisfied. Fauna count: zero, and these are not fauna. Plant life: none, and these are not plants. They are the living expression of a landscape that is not alive but is deeply inhabited. They are the answer to the question “who lives here,” and the answer is a paradox: no one and everyone, nothing and a chorus, stone that dreams in frequencies and water that remembers hands.

When the time comes for you to leave—you feel it as a sudden thinning of the air, a pull from the direction you came—the figures do not farewell. They simply, one by one, resume their deeper stillness. The one with the missing finger stands at the edge of a pool, and as you climb the ridge out of the basin, you see it raise its hand, seven fingers and a stump, and hold it there. The hum continues. It will continue after you are gone, after the suns collapse into one or fracture into twelve, after the water erodes the spires into new shapes and the shapes are deemed correct. It continues now, echoing in your bones for days, a thrumming reminder that you were briefly, impossibly, part of a place where the weirdness is not a breakdown but a home.
