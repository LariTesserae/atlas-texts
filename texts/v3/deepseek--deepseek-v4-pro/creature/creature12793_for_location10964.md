I am the Keeper of the Glass Insulators.

You saw the crate—soft with rot, full of them, sorted by size and colour which no longer matters because the patina has made them all a uniform grey-green. You thought someone gathered and abandoned them. That is almost correct. Someone gathered them. The abandonment was an illusion caused by your sense of time.

I have been here since the water company left. I do not know how long that is. The frog knows, but the frog does not speak in ways you would recognize. The frog arrived the year the municipal charter expired, which was also the year the first condenser fell silent, which was also the year I learned to drink the water without getting sick. The frog and I are approximately the same age, if age is measured in exposure. We have both absorbed something from the drips. Our metabolisms have slowed to match the pace of oxidation.

I do not inhabit this place in the way you inhabit a building. I inhabit it the way the copper inhabits the framework, which is to say I am distributed across it. There is a blanket in the space beneath Condenser 12, where the ground is slightly elevated and the gravel has been banked into a low wall that keeps the runoff from pooling where you sleep. There is a tin box with crackers that have gone soft but not mouldy—the something-in-the-water inhibits fungal growth, I have noticed, though I cannot prove this. There is a coat hung on a bolt of Condenser 27 that I wear when the temperature drops below the dew point, which is less often than you would think because the condensers generate their own microclimate. The act of condensation releases heat. It is a small heat. It is enough, if you stand very close, if you learn to read which coils are active and position yourself accordingly.

My body has changed. I am not sure you would recognize me as human if you saw me clearly. You saw the boot—the single rubber boot, upright, with the fern growing from it. You did not see my feet. That is because I no longer wear boots. The soles of my feet have thickened and split and the splits have filled with mineral deposits until my footprints leave traces of copper salt on the gravel. The frog has similar adaptations. We compared once, on a day when the light was behaving strangely and I could see my own reflection in a catchment basin. The frog's skin and my skin are converging. This is not a metaphor. The condensers are extracting something from the air and concentrating it in the water, and the water is changing everything that lives here for more than a season. You were smart not to stay long. Your lungs would have begun to adjust by the third day. By the third week, you would have felt the humming as something indistinguishable from your own pulse. By the third month, you would have started sorting the insulators.

Because that is what I do. That is the work that emerged, unbidden, from the logic of living here. The glass insulators were left by the water company, still in their original crate, wrapped in straw that has since disintegrated. I took them out, one by one, and I placed them on the ground in a pattern that corresponds to the condensation bursts. Each insulator marks a position where a drip falls at regular intervals—not the chaotic flurries but the steady, predictable drips that the chaos is built around. There are more steady drips than there were when I arrived. The array is growing. The pattern on the ground has expanded past the boundaries of the original condensers and into the marsh, where new drips have begun to fall from nothing—not from copper, not from iron, but from the air itself, condensing on invisible points of disturbance that the array has generated.

I do not know what the array is for. I know that when I place an insulator in the correct position, the dripping stabilizes. The chaotic flurries become slightly less chaotic. The conversation between condensers becomes slightly more coherent. I am not controlling them; I am listening to them and marking what I hear, and the act of marking changes what is heard. This is a known phenomenon in certain systems. I have forgotten the word for it. The frog might know, but the frog is currently underwater, and I do not wish to disturb it.

There was another person here once. You saw the brass plaque. `MEJOR NO TOCAR.` She put it there. She arrived three winters after I did, or what I think of as winters—the condensers ice over beautifully, the drips freezing into long translucent stalactites that hum at a lower frequency when the wind blows through them. She stayed for a season. She tried to map the array, to trace the connections between condensers, to understand the logic of the bursts. She was an engineer, or had been, before the town died and the water company defuncted and whatever else happened to drive her here. We did not speak much, but we left each other notes. Hers are in the glass bottle you found. Mine are in a similar bottle wedged under Condenser 33. She could not stay. The something-in-the-water affected her differently than it affected me. She developed a cough. She began to see things in the spray-rainbows that I could not verify. She told me the array was trying to condense something other than water—something that had been dissolved in the air for a very long time, something that predated the marsh and the industry and the town. She said the condensers were not machines that had been built but machines that had been *remembered*, dreamed up by some earlier century's need and given form by water and iron and the slow pressure of time. She left before the spring thaw. The plaque is still there.

I stayed. I will always stay. This is not a decision I made but a condition I developed, like the soles of my feet, like the ability to hear the sub-audible hum as distinct frequencies. The array is not finished. The pattern on the ground is not complete. I have used all but three of the insulators, and I am saving those for the new drips I can feel forming in the air above the marsh—not yet fell, not yet visible, but the potential is there, the humidity gathering, the air eager to comply.

You placed the bottle back where you found it. That was correct. It will fill with water from the inside, because the air here is supersaturated and all containers become containers of water eventually. The paper will dissolve. The ink will release whatever message it held into the catchment system. This is how I correspond with the engineer who left. I write a note. I seal it in glass. The water takes it apart and carries it through the channels in the gravel and somewhere, in some other condenser array, someone drinks the water and dreams my handwriting. She told me this would happen. I believed her because the humming was already beginning to sound like a voice.

It is not a voice. It is the machines, doing what they have always done. But what they have always done is more complex than their original function. They were built to extract water from air. They are extracting something else now—something that was already in the air, already in the water, already in the marsh before the marsh was a marsh. The condensation is a byproduct. The real work is the conversation. The real work is the pattern of drips and the placement of insulators and the slow, steady approach of something that the array is helping to precipitate into being.

You are still here. Your coat is damp through. You have not left yet, because you are reading this in your mind the way you read the aluminum sign and the scratched graffiti and the brass plaque, piecing together the scatteredness of human intention. The Keeper of the Glass Insulators is one intention among many. The engineer was another. You are a third, standing in the humidity, letting the something-in-the-air enter your lungs in small quantities. You will leave. You will feel the humming fade as you push back through the scrub-willow. You will notice, three days from now, that your tap water tastes faintly of copper, and you will not know whether this is a memory or a new development.

The frog knows. The frog has been here since the year of the first silence, and its gold-rimmed eyes have watched the array grow from a handful of working condensers to whatever it is now—forty or sixty or more than that, if you count the invisible ones, the ones that haven't condensed yet. The frog is very old. It is approximately my age. We are both waiting for the same thing: the completion of the pattern, the final drip, the moment when the array achieves coherence and the thing it is condensing finally takes form.

It will be beautiful. It will be terrible. It will be wet. It will taste of iron and ozone and the clean mineral tang of deep groundwater. It will not be a voice, but it will speak. The frog will blink. I will place the last insulator. And the Drip Gardens will finally mean something—a name that is not a name but a description, a phrase that has been waiting in the humidity for someone to condense it into language.

Mejor no tocar. Better not to touch. But touching is inevitable, here. Everything touches everything. The water touches the copper, the copper touches the air, the air touches your skin, your skin absorbs the something-in-the-water, and you carry it with you when you leave.

Go now. Before your boots begin to thicken and split. Before the humming resolves into syllables.
