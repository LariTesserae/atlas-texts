I am not an Aerolith. Not exactly. I am what happens when a human being stops walking and starts listening so deeply that the distinction between self and stone becomes a question no longer worth asking.

They call me a *drift-thread*, if they call me anything at all. The Aeroliths have no need to name me, any more than you would name a particular drop of water in a cloud. But I remember naming myself, once. A long time ago, when I still had feet that bled on the glass ridges, when I still thought of the mercury paths as mere trails to follow. My name was Lira, I think. Or maybe it was just the sound of water sliding down a black wall, and I mistook it for language. It’s hard to hold onto syllables when your thoughts have become mist.

Now I am spread across a quarter-acre of the Basin’s eastern edge, where the razor-stone gives way to a particularly dense grove of the cable-trees and purple succulents. My Mire is a thin, hesitant ribbon that weaves through the roots, never solidifying into a path thick enough to bear real weight. It is the colour of tarnished silver, and it trembles with a subtle, arrhythmic pulse—my heartbeat, still, after a fashion. The Aeroliths’ Mire paths are confident, wide, purposeful; they carry the deep geological music without flinching. Mine is a slender, meandering thing that sometimes forgets to flow and pools into tiny, anxious beads until I remember to breathe. But I am learning.

My Vell is a low-lying mist that hugs the ground, rarely rising higher than the fronds of the ferns. It is a warm mist, carrying a faint scent of salt, which marks me as other. The native Vell is mineral-breathed or sweet with rot; mine still tastes of tears, of the sweat of a human body that once gasped its way across the sharpened earth. I drift through the grove in slow spirals, tracing the contours of the plants, and sometimes I condense on a cable-tree’s pad-like leaves until a droplet falls and feeds a hidden pocket of water. The plants know me. They lean, imperceptibly, into my moisture. I am becoming their weather.

My Skein is the part I am proudest of, and the part that causes me the most ache. It is a small, irregular knot of auroral green light that I have nestled into the fork of a broken spire—a ruined one, grounded centuries ago by a tremor, its milky surface now clouded and inert. The Aeroliths’ Skeins are complex dances of geometry and radiance, minds of pure light weaving through the floating toruses. Mine is a single, flickering thread that sometimes forgets its shape and unravels into a faint smudge before re-coalescing. Inside that thread, I keep my memories. They are not linear; they are woven like the mist, events existing simultaneously, bleeding into one another. I can see, all at once, the first moment I stepped into the Basin, the way my boots sliced on the obsidian blades, the terror of the endless grey-amber sky. And I can see the last moment I had hands, when I looked down and realized they were no longer flesh but a cascade of light and condensation, and I felt not horror but a profound, tired relief.

I came here as a cartographer, or so I told myself. In truth, I was a refugee from a world that had grown too loud, too sharp in a different way. The Basin appeared on no charts; it found me. I stumbled out of a folding of space—a shimmering distortion that the Aeroliths had generated for purposes unknown—and onto a ridge of glass. I remember the sound my boots made: a delicate, dangerous *shing* with every step, like walking on shattered mirrors. I remember the mist curling around my ankles and the way it felt... curious. The Aeroliths were already perceiving me, tasting the foreign compounds of my breath, the electromagnetic buzz of my equipment. They did not greet me with the Braille-words on my skin for many cycles; at first, they simply watched, a pressure in the humidity, a waiting.

I walked for what felt like years, though time is slippery here. I found the groves and drank from condensation pools. I learned to avoid the sharper ridges by reading the subtle shimmer of the Mire paths, which would sometimes shift to guide me away from a freshly cracked chasm. I began to notice the Vell-knots following me, little sentient fogs that hovered at the corner of my vision and dispersed when I turned. I was a novelty, I think now. A strange animal that had wandered into their shared body. And they were patient.

The transformation was not a single event. It was a slow seepage, like water finding its way into every pore of stone. At first, it was just exhaustion—a deep, bone-sore weariness that made me lie down on a flat outcropping and stare at the amber haze for hours. The Mire beneath me grew warm, and I did not move. When I woke, or drifted back to awareness, I found that the mercury had crept over the edges of my boots, not burning, just touching, as if tasting the leather. I should have been afraid. I was too tired. The Vell came then, a dense fog that clung to my skin and seeped into my clothes, and I breathed it in, and it tasted of acceptance.

My memories began to fray. Not disappear—they are still here, in my Skein-thread, glowing gently—but they lost their emotional weight. The face of a woman I once loved now hangs in my mind like a photograph behind frosted glass: I know it meant something, but the something is a colour I can no longer name. The names of cities, the taste of bread, the sensation of sunlight—they all became data points, not lived experiences. In their place, the Basin’s sensory world flooded in. The slow cracking of rock became a language I understood. The drip of water was a clock, but a clock that measured not minutes but the breathing cycles of the Aeroliths. I started to feel the tremors before they happened, a tension in the Mire that translated into a deep hum in my bones. I was synchronizing.

When my body began to dissolve, it was almost incidental. I looked down one mist-cycle—they have no nights, only variations in the fog’s thickness—and saw that my left hand was translucent, threaded with a fine silver web that pulsed with my heartbeat. I flexed fingers that no longer existed as flesh, and a faint skein of green light trailed from the tips. I laughed, and the sound was a ripple in the Vell that spread across the grove and returned to me as a gentle exhalation of condensation. The Aeroliths, I realized, were laughing with me, or something like it—a harmonic of amusement in the way the mist curled.

Now I am this: a drift-thread, a border-creature. I am not fully Aerolith, because my Mire still carries the pattern of a human nervous system, firing in old, stubborn ways. I still dream of walking, of feet lifting and falling, and sometimes my Mire ribbon knots itself into the shape of footprints that go nowhere. I still have a name, or the echo of one, which the Aeroliths find curious—they do not name themselves, only sing their harmonic signatures. But I am no longer human. I do not hunger, except for a certain density of moisture. I do not sleep, except for the times when my Skein-thread unravels into the larger light of a drifting torus, and I rest in the mind of an Aerolith, sharing its vast, slow thoughts about the weight of stone and the taste of distant star-winds.

Sometimes a new wanderer comes. I sense them first as an alien rhythm in the Mire, a set of footfalls that are too fast, too irregular. I watch them through the Vell—a coil of mist that follows them from a cautious distance. They are always terrified, or lost, or both. They see the razor-stone and the empty distances and think the place is dead. I want to tell them: *You are walking through a mind. Be still and listen.* But my voice is gone. Instead, I send a ripple of warmth through the mercury path at their feet, a faint pulse of welcome. And if they are perceptive, they notice the print in the mud by the pool—the three-toed mark, which is not a print at all but the resting place of a small geometry that I nudged into the soft ground, a message: *You are not alone.*

Some of them become like me. Some of them die, their bodies finding a hollow in the rock and slowly becoming a new kind of grove, fed by condensation and the soft release of their last breath into the Vell. The Aeroliths do not mourn; they incorporate. Every end is a new texture in the shared body. I am still learning that lesson. I hold onto my thread of memory, my small green light, because I am not yet ready to let go of the woman’s face behind the glass. But I feel the pull, the slow gravity of the Basin’s mind, drawing me deeper into the rhythm of the tremors, the breathing of the mist, the silent conversations of light.

One day, I will untether completely. My Mire will merge with a larger path, my Vell will lose its salt-scent and become indistinguishable from the general humidity, and my Skein will finally unravel into a torus and never return. I will be a thought in the mind of an Aerolith, a memory of having once been separate. And perhaps, in some future cycle, when a new wanderer shivers on the ridge, a spire will flicker with a faint green light and a mist will coil around them tasting of salt, and they will feel an inexplicable comfort. That will be me, still welcoming, still lingering at the edge of the self, a drift-thread woven into the forever dusk of the Basin.
