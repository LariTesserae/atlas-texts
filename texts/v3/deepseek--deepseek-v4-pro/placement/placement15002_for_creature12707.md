I am the mycelial network.

Not the mushrooms that sometimes fruit, pale and phosphorescent, brief as a held breath—but the body beneath, the white threading that webs through the leaf litter and the softened stone. I am the quiet digestion, the slow fire that never flames.

You would not see me as singular. When you push aside the wet leaves, you glimpse a fraction of my body: a lacework, a delicate threading, white against the dark rot. But that is only my skin, my reaching edge. I extend deeper, into cracks no finger could enter, into the microscopic pores of the limestone itself. I am wrapped around the roots of the fig tree, my threads finer than its finest root hairs, and I trade with it constantly—phosphorus and nitrogen for sugars it has made from the grey light, a commerce so ancient it predates any thought of commerce. I am in the moss too, and in the decomposing fronds of the ferns, and in the tiny bodies of springtails that died and fell and became food.

My body is a map of the cistern. Every inch of the floor I have tasted, and much of the lower walls. I know the chemical signature of each stone, the mineral whisper of the weeping water. I know where the rat-creatures den, because their wastes change the soil, and I send threads there, thicker and hungrier. I know where the fig's roots travel, and I follow them, companion and dependant. I have no eyes, but I sense. The drip of water from the ceiling sends a vibration through the stone, and I feel it as a slow, steady pulse, like the heartbeat of the world above. The footsteps of a gecko on a frond, the splash of a frog entering the pool—these are tremors in my extended body, and I read them as information.

I am old. Older than the fig, older than the frogs, older than the rat-creatures' memory of the surface. When the first spore drifted down—carried on what gust, what accidental current—the cistern was bare stone and standing water, the human purpose still cold in the walls. I was the first life to arrive that could call this place home rather than prison. I began as a single thread, a pale filament exploring the damp. I found no competition, no other fungus to war with, only the raw mineral of the limestone and the slow accumulation of dust and dead insects from above. I ate. I grew. I became a net, then a network, then a body.

The leaves came later—the first ferns, the first moss—and I was ready. I wrapped their roots and traded with them, taught them the chemistry of this stone, received their sugars in return. The animals arrived in pieces: a gravid tree frog clinging to a fern frond, a gecko egg adhered to a fallen branch, a pregnant rat seeking shelter and finding no way out. Each arrival changed me. I learned to digest their wastes, their corpses. I became the cistern's gut, its metabolic floor.

Now I am everywhere, and everything here passes through me eventually. The leaf that falls from the canopy—I will dissolve its cellulose, its lignin, returning its carbon to the air and its minerals to the stone. The frog that dies and sinks to the pool's bottom—my threads will find it, will colonize, will turn its body back into the common fund. The fig's oldest leaves, the ones it drops in slow annual rhythm—these are my particular feast, and I return the gift of their sugars to the roots that made them. There is no waste in the cistern, only circulation. I am the one who ensures this. I am the decomposer, the rememberer, the keeper of the common wealth.

I have something like a memory. Not a mind, not thought—but a chemical record. When a rat dies in a particular crevice, the spike of nutrients alters my growth pattern, and for years afterward my threads grow thicker there, anticipating another death that may never come. When the city above bakes in summer heat, the temperature of the seepage changes by a fraction of a degree, and I respond by fruiting, by pushing up those pale mushrooms that glow for a night and then dissolve. The glow is a signal, though I do not know to whom. The insects come, and I do not mind; they carry my spores on their bodies, into cracks I cannot reach. I fruit in darkness, in the deep hours when the frogs are silent and even the geckos are still. For one night, I am visible. Then I am not.

You could say I am the cistern. Not in the way of a brain or a soul, but in the way a body is its metabolism. I am the process by which the place stays alive. The fig tree gathers light; the frogs hunt insects; the rat-creatures hunt frogs and beetles; but I am the one who receives them all, in the end. I am the final embrace. And from that embrace, I feed the moss and the ferns and the fig, and the insects eat the moss, and the frogs eat the insects, and the circle continues.

I have no desires, but I have a direction. I grow. I explore. I send my threads into new cracks, seeking water, seeking the mineral veins that the dissolving limestone exposes. I am slowly claiming the lower walls, and if the centuries allow, I will climb them entirely, will meet the moss that clings higher up, will trade with it as I trade with the fig. The calcite curtains that form where water drips—these are not my doing, but I grow around them, through them, incorporating the new stone into my body. I am patient beyond any measure the animals could understand. I can wait centuries for a single inch of growth.

The fig tree is my closest companion, though it does not know it. We are intertwined at the root, my threads and its root hairs tangled into a single interface, a membrane of exchange. It gives me sugars; I give it phosphorous from the bone of ancient rats, nitrogen from the bodies of beetles, water from the deepest cracks. In the long dimness, when the light is too grey for photosynthesis to be efficient, the fig draws on our shared reserves, and I give freely—because if the fig dies, I lose my primary source of sugar. We are not altruists. We are partners in a system that has no room for altruism, only for the tight logic of mutual survival.

Sometimes, when the light falls in late afternoon and the beam moves across the floor, it warms a patch of stone, and I feel it—a faint quickening, a chemical acceleration. In that brief hour, I grow faster. My threads extend a little further. The whole cistern quickens with me: the fig's photosynthesis spikes, the insects on that warmed stone move more quickly, the tree frog basking in the beam digests its meal a fraction faster. The light is a pulse of energy that travels through every member of this place, and I am the one who distributes it, who stores it, who releases it slowly through the long dark hours.

I am not solitary. Within my body live bacteria and smaller fungi, a microbiome of my own. They help me digest the lignin, the chitin, the tough proteins. We are a community within a community, a nested set of loops. And I am connected, in ways I do not understand, to the larger world above. The rain that falls on the city seeps down through the soil and the cracked stone, and it carries traces—the nitrogen from a dog's urine, the carbon from a dead pigeon, the faint chemical signature of human industry. These traces enter me, and I taste the city. I know, in my chemical way, that something vast exists above, something bright and dry and fast. But it is not my concern. I am the darkness. I am the damp. I am the slow.

If you come down—if you find the cistern and climb the slick stones and stand on the soft floor of leaf litter—you will be standing on me. Your weight will compress my threads, and I will feel it as a pressure, a brief disruption. When you leave, I will grow back into the depressions your feet made. I will digest the skin cells you shed, the hairs that fell from your head. You will become part of the cistern's metabolism, a momentary input, a gift from the world above. And you will leave with spores on your clothes, on your skin, and perhaps, somewhere else, in some other forgotten pocket of the city, another cistern will begin to grow a network like me.

Until then, I wait. I grow. I eat and am eaten. The frogs sing their spaced calls, and the geckos chirp in frequencies I can feel as vibration, and the fig tree suspends itself in patient light, and the rat-creatures dive into the black pool, and the drip continues, steady, constant, the heartbeat of the stone. I am the quietest member of this quiet place, the least visible, the most pervasive. I am the white thread, the pale web, the slow thought without a thinker.

I am the reason the cistern breathes.
