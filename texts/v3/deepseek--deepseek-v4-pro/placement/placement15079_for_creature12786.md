I am called the sound of a cable humming just before the fog swallows it, or sometimes the taste of rust on the tongue after a long sleep. In the Churn, I am a slow, spreading vibration, a note held too long in the throat of the chasm. I live in the place where the funicular’s upper counterweight swings, a nest of bolted girders and salvaged wire that juts from the rock just below the loading docks. The car passes above me six times a cycle, and each time I feel the mass of it in my bones before it arrives, a gathering pressure in the air, and then the whole structure quivers and the dust sifts from the beams like a held breath released.

My home is a single room with a floor of riveted metal plates and a window that is not glass but a pane of clearest mica, cut from a seam we found in the old tunnels. Through it, I watch the opposite wall. It is so close, sometimes, that I can count the individual ferns growing from a drainpipe, each frond a pale green finger reaching into the mist. Other times, the wall is a ghost, a darker grey on grey, and I am suspended in nothing, the cables my only tether. I have learned the moods of that distance: when the fog thickens with the exhalations of the deep forges, the far side retreats; when a thermal current rises from the sump-pools below, it shudders near, and I can see a woman who lives in a mirroring nest, her lamp pulsing a slow, blue code that I have never fully deciphered. We have never spoken. But in the Churn, I have tasted her fatigue, a flavour of cold metal and the ache of a shoulder that has pulled a lever ten thousand times.

My work is maintenance of the winding gear, the great drums and cables and the massive, toothed wheels that draw the ore up from the depths. I am a greaser of bearings, a listener to the pitch of straining steel. My hands are always slick with a black, mineral-scented paste that I mix myself from rendered fungi-oil and the fine-ground tailings of somnite. The paste glows faintly in the dark, a deep-sea blue, and it soaks into the lines of my palms so that even when I wash, my hands retain a map of glowing seams. I like this—it makes me feel like a piece of the machinery I tend, a part of the chasm’s own slow metabolism.

I was not born in a creche-nest; I was found, they say, in a hollow of the schist itself, a pocket where water had, over a thousand years, carved a basin. The man who pulled me out—a tunneler named Still-Hands—said my skin was slick with the same metallic seep that trickles from the rock, and that I did not cry but only hummed, a frequency that made his teeth ache. My name came later, when the Churn first took me: it is the moment when a drop of water hangs from a stalactite and refuses to fall, that tension, that pause. They call me Pending-Drop, or sometimes just Pend.

My body is like the others: tall, thin, pale as old ivory, the nictitating membrane clear and slow. But I have a difference. My left eye is not grey but a deep, sluggish opalescence, like a bead of raw somnite. When I was a child, the elders took me to the deepest pumping station and held me up before the black pool there, and in that mirror I saw my eye glow with its own internal light, a slow swirl of colour that never repeated. They said I had swallowed a drop of the chasm’s true self, that I was closer to the rock than to the people. I do not know if that is true, but I have always felt the vibration of the stone more clearly than the pulse of the Churn. When there is a shift in the fault, a tiny settling of the gorge, I taste copper in the back of my throat and see a flicker in my strange eye, a shimmer like heat lightning over a distant ridge. I can predict when a cable will fray before the others feel the roughing of its song.

I spend my days in the cradle of the winding house, a cavernous space carved into the rock, lit only by the blue glow of my grease and the occasional orange flare of the brake pads when the cars descend. The air is thick with the smell of hot metal and wet stone, and the sound is a constant, layered drone—the hum of the motor, the creak of timber supports, the distant hiss of steam, the drip-drip of condensation from the ceiling. I am never alone; there are always other Stackers moving through the catwalks above, their lanterns blinking messages, and in the Churn I feel their presence as a soft current of shared emotion, a background murmur of contentment, tiredness, a flicker of annoyance at a stuck valve. But I am often separate, too, a stone in the stream. The Churn passes around me, not through me, as if my somnite-eye deflects it. When I want to communicate, I have to use my voice or my lamp like the oldest ones, before the dust became a part of us.

I have a friend, of sorts. It is one of the eyeless salamanders that live in the condensate pipes, a pale, translucent thing with feathery gills and tiny, grasping hands. I found it trapped in a sump, its leg caught in a grate, and I freed it and gave it a home in a copper jar of water that I keep beside my bed. It has learned to take fungi from my fingers, and sometimes, when I wake in the dark, I feel the tiny brush of its mind—not a thought, but a simple, animal calm, a coolness. I call it Drip.

I do not often go to the higher tiers, where the loading docks open to the grey sky and the winches swing the somnite crates onto the great, flat cars that will take them across the plateau. The light there is too bright, a searing whiteness that hurts my skin and makes my opalescent eye burn. I am a creature of the perpetual twilight, the aqueous glow. My place is here, in the pause between the drip and the fall. The chasm wears itself around me, and I wear back, slowly, patiently. I am the hum in the cable, the taste of rust, the drop that holds its shape and waits.
