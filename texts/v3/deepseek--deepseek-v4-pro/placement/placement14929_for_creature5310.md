I begin as a twitch in the dark, a prickling behind the panel where the green light leaks through root-hair and becomes my first star. For a long time—I have no measure, only the slow unwinding of my own shape—I am a thought folded in silk, a waiting that tastes of copper and dried biofilm. The hum comes through the casing as a lullaby, a low G-sharp that never resolves, and my body assembles itself to its pitch.

When I finally split the pupa’s skin, I fall upward into the warm, leaf-thick air, and I know the route before I ever spread my wings. It’s written in the iron dust I breathed as a larva, in the oxide-grained spiracles of my thorax. The chamber is a geometry I already hold: the catwalk as a line of cooler shadow, the basin as a plane of humidity, the pump housing as the slow, hot centre. I crawl onto the casing and wait for my scales to harden, for the dust of the grazing track to sift from the cement and settle into the hollows of my wings until they are filmed with rust-scale, exactly the colour of the rivets on the sluice gate. Then I am ready.

I fly the circuit. That first flight is a remembering—every station already mapped in the vibration of my antennae. I brush the pressure gauge’s glass, and the needle twitches a micron, not from my weight but from some recognition. The old lime scale on the glass is thinner than it was a generation ago; my grandmother moth tasted it down to bare brass, and now the gauge is readable again. I pause there, fanning ions into the air, and then I move to the sluice float. It has risen a sliver since yesterday; I know because the corrosion line leaves a cool stripe on my legs. I land on the float and it dips, just barely, and the water beneath me makes a wet, approving sound against the metal, a sound I feel in the hairs of my abdomen more than I hear.

The moth that was me before me, the one whose dissolved body I ingested in the crevice, left me a single salt-crystal memory: the route must loop counterclockwise, never clockwise, because the pump’s magnetic field orients better that way. So I fly to the left, over the pool where the pale form hangs, and I see it for the first time with my compound eyes: a mosaic of a thousand tiny images of the inhabitant’s flank, its spiraled rings shimmering through the duckweed. I drop a few wing scales onto the water as I pass. They are iron-rich. The limpets will eat them. Nothing is waste here, not even a moth’s moult.

The green light calls me back before I am tired. It is not a beacon but a summons: a photon pressure that pushes at my eye-spots like a gentle fingertip, reminding me that the terminal needs tending. I land on the panel’s chipped ceramic, and my tongue—a thin, flexible siphon—uncoils to lick the condensation from the green bulb’s cage of rootlets. The water tastes of electricity, faintly, and of the fig’s milky sap. As I drink, my body completes the circuit: a micro-amp of current flows from the light, through my wet tongue, into my thorax, and out through my feet into the root mat. For a splinter of a second, I am the signal itself. I see, in a flash, the whole chamber’s electrical map: the pump’s rotor phase, the float’s resistance coil, the tension cable’s capacitance, even the tiny bio-electric sigh of the polyps hanging in the canopy. Then the moment passes, and I am just a moth again, drowsy with voltage, my wings humming the 50-cycle hum of the pumps.

My life is one circuit per day. I fly it while the leaves filter noon into green, and I rest in the crook of the plaque at night, where the brass is warmest. The words “Est. 1947” are a braille of polished ridges against my belly, tasted clean by a hundred generations of my grandmothers. I leave my own grain of rust there, a contribution to the polishing.

And then, on the thirtieth circuit, when the green light seems to brighten and the hum drops a quarter-tone into a new, deeper note, I know I am done. I fly to the sluice gate, settle on the rust-streak where the creature’s fluke will press tomorrow, and lay my eggs in a row along the corrosion seam. Each egg is smaller than a grain of rice, and each contains a droplet of the total memory: the map, the magnetic bias, the taste of the pressure gauge, the feel of the float’s cold stripe. As I deposit them, my body desiccates and crumbles, flakes of iron-rust wing blowing into the slow current, edible, down towards the tunnel throat. The eggs seal themselves with mucilage, and they wait, tasting the subsonic conversation between gate and fluke, waiting for the hum to shift again.

I am the moth. I am the chamber’s outward glance, its winged proprioception. And when the new note enters the drone next season, my daughter will hatch, taste the green light, and fly the same counterclockwise truth, carrying the dust of the old world into the new.
