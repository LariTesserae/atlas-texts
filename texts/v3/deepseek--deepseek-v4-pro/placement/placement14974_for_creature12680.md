The first thing I remember is the weight of a stone.

Not a crushing weight—I am too fluid for that—but a familiar pressure against the long muscle of my belly, a coolness that seeps through my skin and tells me the shape of the world. My body is a ribbon of water held in a membrane so thin you can see the slow pulse of my fluids shifting, tide-like, from head to tail. I have no legs. I have not needed legs since the mountains wore down to nubs and the land forgot the meaning of steep. I move by rippling, a wave of contraction that starts behind my blunt, eyeless head and runs the length of me like a sigh. It is not fast. Nothing here is fast. Speed is a concept for places with edges, with seasons that snap instead of seep. Here, there is only the long now, and I am exactly fast enough for it.

I live in the hollows where the fog pools thickest. My hollow is a shallow depression in the stone pavement, cupped by a low ring of rounded rocks that have been nudged into a near-perfect circle over longer than my bloodline can recall. The rocks are dark with the wet, filmed with lichen the color of rust and old jade. When the fog pours over the plateau's lip and rolls in like a slow flood, it fills my hollow first, coiling around the stones in tendrils soft as the touch of my own skin. I lie at the center, half-buried in a basin of water that has been there since before I hatched and will be there long after I dissolve back into the soil. The water is never deep—scarcely enough to cover my back—but it is constant, fed by the condensation that beads on the stone ring and trickles down in drops that fall with the patience of a clock that marks centuries. Plink. Plink. Plink. Each drop sends a tiny vibration through the stone, through the water, through me. I feel them all. I have been feeling them since I was no bigger than a millipede.

I eat the lizards. They come to bask on the warm stones of my ring, their obsidian eyes half-lidded, their translucent bodies soaking up the diffuse light. They do not see me as a threat—I am too slow, too much a part of the stones and the water to trigger their flicker of alarm. I wait until one drapes itself over a rock directly above me, its tiny flank rising and falling with breath. Then I unhinge. My mouth is a soft, wet gap that opens not with a snap but a slow, inevitable unfolding, like a flower that has been waiting for a particular slant of light. I take the lizard whole, and it goes still inside me, and I feel its life become my life, a small warmth that travels the length of my body and fades into the general hum of my being. I do not eat often. A single lizard will sustain me for a long turn of the fog clock, and there is no hunger as you would know it—only a gradual thinning of my internal tide, a quiet cue that it is time to unhinge again.

But I eat more than lizards. I also eat the stillness. I eat the vibrations.

You would not notice them. Your ears are tuned to the wrong scale—to the sudden crack, the sharp cry. Here, the world speaks in a language beneath hearing. The beetles trundling over the stones make a sound, not of footsteps, but of the faintest scratch of carapace on lichen, a whisper that travels through the rock itself. The millipedes uncoiling in the crevices send a ripple of tiny impacts, a slow drumroll that takes an hour to complete. The spiders, spinning their fog-caught webs, pluck the strands and the resulting hum is a note stretched so long it becomes a scent, a taste on the wet air. I feel all of it through my belly, through the water in the basin, through the stones that press against my skin. My body is a single, continuous ear.

And beneath all of it, beneath the scratch and the ripple and the pluck, there is a deeper vibration. A hum that is not sound but pressure, a slow shifting of weight that comes from the very bones of the plateau. It is always there, even when the fog is thin and the surface world grows quiet and dry. Some days it is faint, a barely-there buzz that I can ignore, like the memory of a lizard long digested. Other days—fog-thick days, days when the air is so wet I can swim through it as much as the water—the hum swells until it fills me, until my own ripple-motion seems to sync to its frequency, until I am not a creature moving through the world but a note plucked on a string that runs through the heart of the stone.

I know what makes the hum. I have always known, in the way I know the taste of lizard, the coolness of water, the shape of my own slow pulse. It is the gardener.

I have never seen it. I do not need to see it. I feel it all the time, a presence woven through the cracks and crannies of the plateau like the spider silk through the stones—except the gardener's threads are not silk. They are pale, living filaments that run through the deepest joints of the bedrock, thinner than my own thin skin, coated in a substance that makes them vanish into the wet. When I coil in my hollow, my belly pressed flat against the stone, I can feel them beneath me, a net of delicate warmth, a faint chemical tang that seeps into the water and becomes part of my own chemistry. I am not prey to the gardener, and it is not predator. We are, instead, components of the same slow metabolism, the same long dream. I feed on the lizards that bask on the stones the gardener shifted into place aeons ago. My waste, when I dissolve what I cannot use, seeps down into the cracks and feeds the filaments in turn. My body, when I am done with it, will do the same. It is a closed loop, a circle of giving and taking that has no beginning and no end.

Sometimes, I help.

Not with intention—I am not a builder, not a thinker in the way you might use the word. But when the hum is strong, when the gardener stirs in its slow way and begins its patient nudging of the stones, I feel an urge to move. Not to hunt, not to bask, but to coil differently, to press my body into a particular curve, to ripple in a way that subtly shifts the weight of the stones I lie among. I do not question this urge, just as I do not question the fog. I let it take me, and for hours, for days, I writhe slowly in my hollow, my water-ripple skin flowing over the rocks, and I feel the stones settle differently under me, a millimeter to the left, a fraction of a degree rotated. I am not strong, but I am patient, and over the course of my life—which has been long, longer than the lizards, longer than the beetles, longer even than the oldest millipede in the damp crevices—I have shifted my ring of stones enough to change the way the fog pools, to deepen my basin, to make my hollow a little more perfect, a little more welcoming to the life that feeds me and the filaments that feed on me.

When the gardener is most awake, it sometimes grows eyes.

I have seen them. They appear in the deepest hollows—not mine, but older places, where the fog never lifts and the stones are so thickly furred with lichen they look like the backs of sleeping beasts. A pale, glistening knot of filaments pushes up through a crack, no bigger than a lizard's head, and it pulses with a faint light, a bioluminescence the color of dark green on a beetle's shell. It is not an eye as I know eyes—I have none—but I understand it sees. It senses the light, the fog, the faint chemical traces of the creatures moving through the stones. I have coiled near one, once, for a whole season. I felt it register me, a shift in the hum, a focusing of attention that was not quite thought but something older, something that felt like the way the stone feels the rain. I stayed very still, and the gardener looked at me, and I looked back with my whole body, and for a moment—a moment that lasted days—we were a single thing, a loop of knowing and being known, a circle inside the larger circle.

When the fog thinned and the eye reabsorbed back into the net, I rippled away slowly, carrying the taste of that knowing with me. It tasted like water that has been cupped in stone for a thousand years. It tasted like the inside of a lizard's throat just before the unhinging. It tasted like the beginning of the world.

Now, I lie in my hollow again, feeling the pressure drop that means the fog is coming. The first tendrils are spilling over the edge of the plateau, a grey tongue licking the stones. The hum beneath me is strengthening, the filaments swelling with the moisture, the gardener waking into its slow, distributed attention. I feel the urge to coil, to press, to shift. A stone under my mid-body gives a fraction of a millimeter, a change so small only the lichen will notice, a hundred years from now. I ripple contentedly, my body a note in the long song, my life a single beat in the pulse of the plateau. The fog wraps around me like breath from a mouth I cannot see, and I am home.
