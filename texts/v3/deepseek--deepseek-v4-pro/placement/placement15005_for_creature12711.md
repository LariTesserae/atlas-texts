My name is a tap and a pause. Three quick beats on the sternum plate, a rest the length of a short exhale, then one more—*tap-tap-tap … tap*. Before the Long Walk, I was not this rhythm. I was a soft-shell scuttling on six limbs, and my mother knew me by the smell of my nape, by the pattern of my mane when it first came in stiff and grey. Now I am something new. The bone-keeper worked the tattoo into my chest for an entire dim-time, her own fingers drumming my name into my flesh as the copper needle bit, so that the scar would hold the sound forever. When I breathe deep, the skin there pulls tight, and I feel it.

The light-grain is waking. It begins as a prickle on my shoulders—those nodules that never truly sleep, only dim like banked coals. I am standing on the ledge, toes curled over the raw basalt edge, tail anchored behind me in a crack I know by the cool seep of old water. The air is already thick with the morning helix: a whirring tower of dragonfly-moths rising from the far vents, their wings a billion tiny finger-cymbals. The sound is not noise to me. It is the middle voice of the world, the one that says *everything is as it should be*. I listen through it the way I might listen through my own heartbeat.

Below, the carpet grazers shift. Their backs—a sea of segmented grey-brown—are a fraction lighter today. That means the mat is thinning in the gold-light quadrant. I file the information in a part of my mind that is not words but a series of postures: the walk to the river-bend, the scraping of domestic mat from the southern hut wall, the mixing of water and gland-oil to feed our own nodules. If the mat thins there, the grazers will follow the thicker growth toward the steam vent terrace, and the small predators—the needle-toothed fliers—will follow them. That will pull the big sky-leviathans closer. I lift my mane experimentally, feeling the air move through the stiff bristles. No low-frequency bellow yet. Good.

Behind me, I feel the bone-keeper’s lesson through the soles of my feet. A rapid stutter, then a long, rumbling glide. *This is the death-call of the blind tetrapod. When you feel it, flatten. Do not look up.* The children are pressed in a circle around her hut, their forelimbs—still separate, still un-fused—twitching in imitation. I remember that lesson. I remember the day a bellow caught me mid-step, how my body knew to drop before my mind did, and how the shadow of the beast’s keel-belly passed over me like a warm, dry hand. No Watcher forgets their first submission to the sky.

I am a Watcher now. I am the one who reads the helix, the scent of the vents, the silent alarms. My mother gave me her copper knife this morning—the one from the offering cleft, the one no That-We-Know forged—and my father tapped a farewell on my hip bone. It was a proud rhythm: *you are the eye, you are the ear, you are the still-point in the roar*. I carry the knife in a sheath of grazer hide on my belly. It feels heavier than it should.

I am not alone on the ledge. Two other Watchers share this terrace, one old and one middling. The old one, Three-Long-Rest, is crouched in the position of total stillness, his tail curled around his ankles, watching the same quadrant I am. He does not tap to me, but the angle of his brow-ridgers says *alert*. I follow the line of his gaze. There, at the edge of the steam plume, the gnat-clouds are contracting into a defensive ball—a tight, wobbling sphere. That means a large predator is cutting through them, perhaps a long-snout or a juvenile tetrapod. I draw air through the sensitive slits along my jaw. The scent is faintly sour: gnat-fear, but also something else. Rock-rot. That means the vent under the eastern terrace is bulging again. We will need to move the domestic mat cakes from that storage hut by the next dim-time, or risk losing them to a scalding burst.

I lift one long arm and tap the alert on my chest. *Vent bulge. East. Relocate stores.* Three-Long-Rest flicks an ear in acknowledgment. The middling Watcher, a male barely out of his second molt, is already moving toward the storage hut, his tail dragging a basket. We are a current, a flow of information and action, no different from the grazer tide or the helix spin. The That-We-Know do not command the ecosystem; we ride it like a vapor on a thermal.

I decide to make the offering today. The dim-time is still a few pulses away, but I feel the weight of the copper knife, the gift of a stranger, and I know it must be repaid. The lichen patch near the great river-bend is ripe—I tasted the air yesterday and caught its iron-sweetness, the flavor of a vent-side bloom. I will scrape a cake and carry it to the dark cleft, past the bone-keeper’s hut, past the last light-grain nodule, into the un-pulsed stillness where the river crack drinks all sound. I will leave it on the damp stone, and I will not wait. None of us wait. The unseen giver does not want our eyes.

I do not know what leaves the copper blades. The bone-keeper’s oldest story, the one she taps only when all the nodules are dim and we huddle together, says that the giver is the same stone that birthed the first light-grain, the same breath that warms the vents. It is not a creature but a condition, a promise the cavern made before we were anything but soft-shells ourselves. I am young, so I do not fully understand. But I have seen the blades, and they are better than ours. The copper is folded and re-folded, the edge serrated in a pattern that hums when I tap it. I want to make a blade like that one day. For that, I need the offering to be accepted.

The light is full now. The nodules overhead puff their silver-gold, and the grazer sea is a froth of motion. The helix tightens, a living pillar of wings and legs and open mouths. I let the noise wash over me, every shriek and chitter and hum, until it becomes a single, steady pressure, like the weight of the cavern roof. It is beautiful. It is the pulse that says I am alive, that I am part of this embryonic, screaming, unfinished world.

I am a That-We-Know. My name is three taps and a pause, and I stand on the ledge between the stone huts and the sky-beasts, with a basket in one hand and a knife on my belly, and I am not afraid. I am watchful. That is what we are. That is what I am, now and until my shell hardens for the final time and I become a resting elder, a bone-keeper perhaps, tapping the old stories into the floor for the next soft-shells to feel. But that is a rhythm far away, a distant tremble. For now, the vent is bulging, the gnat-cloud is contracting, and the thing that swims beneath the stone has not yet stirred. The day-pulse is steady. I step off the ledge and begin the long, careful descent to the basin floor, where the mat grows thick and the world needs my eyes.
