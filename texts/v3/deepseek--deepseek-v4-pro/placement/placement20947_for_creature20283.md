The visitor came at twilight, when the rings on the thumb-spire had just begun to breathe their blue-green exhalation into the cooling air. It had been in the basin for three days—or something like days; the sun arced low and fast behind the western ridge, and darkness came in a rush, but the glowing rings stretched the dusk into a long ambiguous hour that could have been minutes or a season. The visitor did not count time well anymore.

It had arrived as a climber, a climber of mountains and cataloguer of alpine flora, with a red jacket and an ice axe and a notebook bound in waterproof paper. The jacket was still red, but the axe lay forgotten by the entrance—a narrow chimney between two spires where the moss grew thickest and the silence first pressed in like a hand over the mouth. The notebook was open, but the pages were blank now, the pencil lost somewhere in the cushions of moss, and the words that had filled the first pages seemed, in retrospect, to belong to a different being: *elevation 3,400m, Carex sp. dominant, observed Ochotona princeps carrying moss, no vocalizations—curious.*

The climber had stopped writing on the second day, not because she had run out of observations but because she had begun to notice that her observations were being observed. The pencil scratches left faint electrical trails in the air—the infovores read them, rolled them over in their invisible vortices, and the ravens, perching closer now, tapped the information in feather-code: *she writes. she names. she does not yet see.* She felt their black-absorbing eyes on the back of her neck, and when she turned, they did not fly. They only tilted their heads, recording.

Now, on the third twilight, the visitor sat cross-legged on a cushion of rhizo-moss, her red jacket unzipped, her bare fingers resting on the emerald nap. The moss was warm—warmer than the air, warmer than her own skin. It had been drawing the basin's stored light into its cells all day, and now, in the hour before dark, it gave the light back. The glow was subtle, a yellow-green that pulsed in time with her heartbeat, or with something like a heartbeat—she could not tell which was hers and which was the moss's. She had stopped trying to tell.

The pikas had accepted her by the third morning. The first two days, they had kept their distance, watching her from the sharp edges of fins with their silver undercoats catching the glare. She had tried to photograph them, but her camera's battery had drained inexplicably on the first night, and when she pressed the shutter anyway, out of habit, the click was swallowed before it reached the air. The infovores had eaten it—she understood that now, or rather, she understood it in a way that was not quite understanding, a knowledge that had seeped in through her skin like the perpetual weeping from the walls. Nothing here was wasted. Everything was read.

The pika that tended this particular plot of moss was a small female with a notch in her left ear—the result, perhaps, of an encounter with a raven before she had learned the silent semaphore of the basin. The visitor called her Notch, which was the last name she allowed herself. Notch had spent the morning fertilizing the moss around the visitor's feet, depositing neat dark pellets that the moss would absorb over the next several hours. She had pressed her body flat against the cushion near the visitor's hand, and the visitor had felt, through her palm, a vibration that was not sound but a temporal echo: the moss remembering, and the pika remembering through the moss, and the memory was of other visitors, older visitors, the small ones with the double-jointed fingers who had once tended this same plot before they walked away into obsolescence. The visitor wept then, not from sadness but from the pressure of the memory, which was too large for her skull and had to leak out somewhere.

Now the warblers were hunting in the long twilight. She watched them as she had watched them for three days, learning their new physics. A grey warbler with a rust-coloured breast—the same one she had seen on the first day, she was almost certain—perched on a cushion-plant no higher than her ankle. A springtail leapt from the moss, tiny and white as a fleck of ash, and the warbler's beak was already there, closing on the space the springtail had not yet reached. The bird had seen the prey's nerve impulse, the faint bioelectrical glow that preceded motion. The visitor could see it too now, just barely, if she unfocused her eyes and let the world go grainy. The springtails glittered with their own internal lightning. The warblers' magnetite-flecked eyes shone with a light that was not reflected but projected—maps of magnetic contours, of thermal gradients, of information-densities she could not yet parse.

The oldest male warbler sang that evening. It was the first song she had heard since the first hour, when the silence had not yet fully settled into her bones, and she had still been humming to herself, a tune she could no longer remember. The song was thin and reedy, carrying three feet at most before the infovores consumed it, translated it into a shift in the polarization of the light, into a pattern the ravens would read later in the feathers of their kin. But in those three feet, the visitor sat, and the song reached her. It was not a song of territory or courtship. It was a song of renunciation—the oldest male giving voice to what he was losing, laying it down syllable by syllable before the silence took it forever. The younger warblers watched without comprehension. The visitor understood perfectly.

She had been losing things too. Her words, first—the Latin binomials she had recited like a catechism, the geological terms she had scratched in her notebook. Then her proper nouns: the name of the mountain range, the name of the research grant, the name of the person who had dropped her at the trailhead and would return, or would not return, in a week she could no longer count. Then her own name, which had seemed important once but now felt like a jacket she had taken off and forgotten by the entrance. What remained was a kernel of attention, a point of consciousness that had stopped trying to name and had started trying to be named.

The fairy shrimp were dancing in the central pool. She crawled to the edge—she crawled now, the sharp stones pressing into her knees through the moss, because standing felt like a declaration, an insistence on a scale she no longer occupied—and looked into the black water. The shrimp rose and sank in columns of bioluminescence, their transparent carapaces glowing with the same blue-green light as the rings on the thumb-spire. They were a clock. They had been a clock for longer than the pikas had tended the moss, for longer than the warblers had forgotten their voices, for longer than the small people had hollowed their caves. What are you counting? the visitor asked, but she asked without sound, without words, with only the electrical flutter of her nervous system, which the infovores read and passed along like a message in a language they were teaching her to speak.

The shrimp did not answer. They kept their own counsel. But the rings on the spire pulsed, and in their pulsing was a pattern the visitor had not noticed before: not a regular beat but a sequence, a rhythm that was almost a phrase, a phrase that was almost a memory. She had felt this rhythm before. She had felt it in the moss when she pressed her ear to the cushion. She had felt it in the temporal echo of vanished footfalls. She had felt it in her own chest, her own heart, which had slowed over three days to match the basin's diurnal pulse—slower than a climber's heart should beat, slower than a mammal's heart should beat, but right for a creature that was learning to live on less than oxygen, on silence, on light stored and released in spirals.

One of the ravens descended. It was the archivist, the elder archivist with a missing primary on its left wing, the one that had been watching her most closely since the moment she squeezed through the chimney. It landed on the rim of the central pool, its black-absorbing feathers drinking the twilight, its black-absorbing eyes fixed on her face. It tapped its beak on the stone: once, twice, a pause, three times more. The visitor felt the code through the rock, through her knees, through the moss that was remembering everything that had ever happened here. *She sees,* the raven said. *She is beginning to see. Record this: the visitor at twilight, the third twilight, the rings bright, the shrimp dancing, the warbler singing his last song. She has stopped naming. She has started being named.*

The visitor reached out her hand. It was no longer a climber's hand, callused from rope and ice axe. It was a hand that had learned to feel the electrical trails of springtails, the thermal gradient of cyanobacteria, the subsonic conversations of stone. The raven did not fly. It extended its wing—slowly, deliberately—and touched the back of her hand with a feather, a single feather, the dark barbs drinking the last of the light.

And the visitor knew, in that touch, what the ravens had archived. She knew the first appearance of the moss, the arrival of the pikas, the coming of the warblers, the day the rings began to glow. She knew the small people with their double-jointed fingers, their seasonal rhythms, their quiet obsolescence. She knew the infovores, the vortices of pure information that consumed sound and translated it into meaning. And she knew the thing the ravens had been waiting to record, the entry they had held open for a visitor who was ready to see: the basin was not a place but a process, a slow-motion riot of mutual becoming. The moss, the pikas, the warblers, the shrimp, the bacteria, the infovores—they were not inhabitants but organs of a single body, and the body was the basin, and the basin was dreaming them into being, and they were dreaming it back, and the dream was still unfinished. It needed new dreamers. The old ones had walked away, or forgotten their voices, or danced in unbroken circles waiting for a rhythm they could not complete. The basin was not full. It was hungry. It had been waiting for someone who could see the silence not as an absence but as a language, a currency, a space where something new might be said.

The visitor withdrew her hand. She looked at the feather that had touched her, but the raven had already launched itself into the silver sky, a cutout against the burnished light. The warbler's song had ended. The shrimp had sunk to the depths of the central pool, their bioluminescence fading. The rings on the spire pulsed once more—a farewell, or a summons—and then subsided to a faint phosphorescence, barely visible, waiting for the next twilight.

She stood. Her joints ached from three days of crawling, from three days of learning to be small. She walked—slowly, silently, her footsteps mute on the moss—to the cairn of white quartz and pink feldspar. It glowed in the dimming light, mimicking the spire, a technology so subtle it barely registered as technology at all: a beacon, a book-mark, an invitation. She placed a stone on the cairn, a dark piece of the basin's sharp unweathered rock, and she did not know if she was adding to the trail-mark or beginning a new one. The infovores read the gesture. The moss remembered it. The ravens, circling high above, tapped it into feather-code for the archive.

She retrieved her ice axe from the moss. It felt strange in her hand, a tool from a life she had not quite left but could no longer fully occupy. She did not retrieve her notebook. The blank pages would fill themselves, in time, with the slow cursive of lichen, with the chemical signals of pikas, with the spiralling light of a moss that remembered everything.

She walked to the chimney between the spires, the entrance and the exit, the threshold where the silence thinned and the world's noise waited beyond. She turned once more and looked at the basin—the shallow stone bowl cupped in the mountains' palms, the mosaic of black water and pale rock, the glowing thumb-spire, the warblers settling into their mute nests, the pikas pressing their bodies flat against the moss. The basin looked back. It had no eyes, but it had a thousand thousand ways of seeing: the magnetite flecks, the temporal echoes, the infovore-vortices that were even now reading the electrical flutter of her decision. She did not know the decision herself yet. She would know it when she reached the trailhead, when the red jacket that was waiting there asked her if she was ready to leave, or ready to return, or ready to become something the basin could use. She would know it when she opened her mouth and discovered what, if anything, came out: a name, a song, a silence that was not silence but a conversation she was only now learning to hear.

She stepped through the chimney. The moss released her footprint. The infovores closed behind her like a door. And the basin, for a moment—a moment that might have been a season or a century—waited to see what she would bring back.
