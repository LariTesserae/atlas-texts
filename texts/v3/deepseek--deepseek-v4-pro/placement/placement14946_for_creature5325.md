I am a sound before I am a shape: a tongue-click, twice, then a low hum that vibrates in the wet wood. That’s what they call me—no word, just that click-hum, the noise you make when you’re clearing your throat to speak but then decide against it. I am the one who tends the bromeliad cisterns. I am the water-keeper.

You would find me here, twenty feet up in the green-gold dimness, straddling a junction of three liana walkways with my bare feet curled around the braided handrail. The ropes are slick with condensation, and they bounce under me with the rhythm of someone else’s steps—my brother, maybe, heading to the upper platforms with a basket of palm pith. I don’t turn to look; I know his footfall, a double-thud and a spring, distinct as his face. We all know each other’s tread. The whole network hums with footsteps like a spiderweb hums with the struggles of flies, every tremor telling a story.

The cistern I’m working on today is a cluster of tank bromeliads, each one the size of a cradled infant, their waxy leaves overlapping to form a sealed urn. They’re lashed with fiber cords to the crotch of a strangler fig, and their roots have grown through the lashings, drinking the fig’s own moisture, so the bond is half plant, half intent. I lean in, my nose almost touching the water’s surface. It’s clear as air, but it smells of life: a faint copper tang from the leaf secretions, the sweet rot of drowned insects, the mineral breath of the clouds. I can see the wriggling larvae of the little marsupials that sleep here at night—they’ll be combed for fur later by the old woman, but now they’re just tiny commas of movement. I check the level with my thumb, a practiced dip; we need exactly this much to last until the next dewfall. Too full, and the weight might tear the lashings. Too empty, and someone goes thirsty. I’ve miscalculated only once, when I was barely walking the ropes, and my mother’s click of reproach taught me that water is not a thing you take, but a rhythm you join.

I pull out the stoppered spout—a hollow tapir bone with a plug of felted fur—and let the morning’s catch drain into a skin bag. The water makes a soft, glugging music as it flows, and I match it with my own throat-click, a habit. The old woman says the cistern-keepers all pick up that tic; she calls it the water’s accent. When the bag is full, I plug the bone and heft it onto my shoulder, the weight familiar as my own hand. The walkway sways as I shift, and a troop of monkeys screams past me, two of them leaping from my handrail to the next bridge with a jolt that nearly spills the bag. I hiss, a sharp *psssh*, and they scatter, chattering insults. One of them, a young male with a torn ear, pauses to bare teeth at me. We know each other, he and I; he steals the furry plugs from my cisterns if I don’t tie them down. I fling a drip of water at him, and he flees. The children will catch him later and pick through his fur for the wool the old woman needs, and he’ll tolerate it because he’s never known a world without our hands in his coat. Symbiosis is just a long, snarled braid of annoyance and need.

I make my way to the communal hearth at the basin floor. The descent is a series of drops and swings; I hook my leg over a branch, slide along a diagonal palm-plank, let go to catch a hanging vine, and land on a platform that groans under my weight. Everything I touch is alive—the vine pulses faintly with sap, the plank still has fresh leaf buds sprouting from its edge, which I’ll need to trim later before they snag someone’s tunic. Below me, the black pools shimmer between the root-buttresses. A snake is coiled on a moss cushion, utterly still, its scales the same wet brown as the bark. I step over it. It’s been there since the last storm; it keeps the frog population from overbalancing, and we leave each other alone. The frogs, for their part, are a constant peeping chorus, a high-pitched lattice of sound that overlays the lower thrum of the cicadas. Right now, at mid-morning, the sun must be breaking through the high clouds because the light shifts to a sudden honeyed green, and the hummingbirds are frantic—I feel the vibration of their wings before I see them, a whir that tickles my eardrums. One of them darts to a bromeliad bloom near my shoulder, its beak a needle of iridescence. I hold still, letting it drink. The old woman says that if you’re very quiet, you can feel the moment the nectar leaves the flower, a tiny drop in pressure like a sigh. I’m not that quiet. I’m never that quiet.

I reach the hearth, a broad flat stone with a smoldering pat of peat, and set the water bag down by the cooking area. My sister is there, mashing palm pith into porridge with a stone pestle. She doesn’t look up, just makes a sound—the two-note whistle she’s carried since she was a toddler, the one that means “I’m here and I see you.” I click-hum back. Our mother named us both with her breath, and we’ve never needed more. Around us, the din is at full roar: someone is singing the weaving-chant from the far side, the deep grunting hum that rises and falls like a dream of breathing. A child shrieks with laughter as he’s chased along an upper walkway, the ropes bouncing so hard I see the moss shake loose and drift down in green confetti. The parrots choose that moment to wheel overhead, an emerald-and-crimson shriek that tears the air. I cup my hands around my porridge bowl—a knot of wood hollowed a hundred years ago, regrown by a hundred hands, its surface glossy with oil—and I eat. The pith tastes of the mist, of the clean water I just carried, of nothing and everything. I’ll eat again in an hour, when someone hands me a roasted grub or a fruit the monkeys missed. No one here knows hunger, not because food is plenty, but because food is motion. You move through the day, and food moves into your hand.

After eating, I climb to the highest cistern, the one near the matriarch’s perch. This one is special: an ancient bromeliad that predates the settlement, its leaves thick as my forearm, its heart a deep, black pool that’s never been emptied in living memory. The water here tastes of the sky’s oldest secrets, faintly resinous, and we use it only for the sick or for women in labor. I check it every day, more out of reverence than need. As I approach, the old woman is there, cross-legged, her comb moving through the fur of a marsupial that lies utterly limp in her lap, eyes half-closed in bliss. The wind through the topmost leaves makes her name-sound: *shhhhh*. She doesn’t acknowledge me except to tilt her head, letting me settle beside her on the root-platform. The view from here is the only horizon we have: a jagged gap in the canopy that reveals the next ridge, always cloaked in cloud, a gray-blue smear like smudged charcoal. I’ve never been there. None of us have. Why would we? Everything that is, is here.

She speaks after a while, her voice a soft weaving of sounds that don’t quite form words. She tells me I tightened the lashings too much on the lower cistern; the bromeliad is bruising. She felt it through the roots, she says, a tiny stress-thrum in the fig’s bark. I nod. I hadn’t felt it, but I’ll fix it. She’s always right. Her body is a tuning fork for the whole basin, resonating to the creak of a branch or the slow tear of a root giving way. When she dies—and she might soon, her breath is starting to catch in the damp, a little rasp—I will have to listen harder. Someone else will take the comb and the perch, but no one will hear the way she does. The thought is not sad; it’s just a fact, like the moisture beading on my tunic.

I sit with her for a long time, letting the noise wash over me. Below, the settlement is a churn of activity: new walkways being woven after last night’s storm, a child being coaxed to take his first unaided step along a vine, a cluster of boys daring each other to touch the snake’s tail. The frogs, the birds, the monkeys, the insects, the people—it’s all one surging din, a single living breath. And I am a click-hum in the throat of it, a water-keeper, a pair of calloused feet gripping the wet wood, a bag of skin full of pith-porridge, absolutely temporary and absolutely permanent. The moss will grow over me someday, and my body will feed the roots of the next cistern-bromeliad, and some future child will drink water that tastes faintly of my bones. And that’s not a dream; that’s just the way the drip falls.

The sun shifts, the light dims a shade, the marsupial yawns, and I rise to loosen the lashings. The old woman is already humming the weaving-chant, and I match it under my breath as I go. The walkway bounces with my steps, and somewhere a parrot screams, and somewhere a frog peeps, and I am, I am, I am.
