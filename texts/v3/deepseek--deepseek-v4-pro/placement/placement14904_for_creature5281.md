I am becoming, and it is the sound that does it. Not my own sound—I have none yet—but the ambient noise that is the only true constant here, the thrum that shakes every fiber down to the molecular lattice and the clicks that punctuate it like the beat of a heart I don’t possess. The sound is the first thing I know, and perhaps the last thing I will ever know, because it is the mind of this place, and I am a temporary appendage extruded to think a single thought: *Here. This section. Tear it down.*

It starts as a thickening in the mat. You couldn’t call it pain, but there is a gathering of density, a pull of moisture and dissolved cellulose toward a point on the underside of a pale, gargantuan trunk. The plant-substrate, that continuous interwoven mass that replaces soil and rock, shifts around me like a mother reshuffling a nest. I feel the warmth of the internal fermentation—a low, dull radiance—and then a drawing in of something from the air: spores, perhaps, or dissolved templates, the genetic memory of what a Rattler is supposed to be. I soak it up greedily. I don’t have greed yet. The adjective will have to grow later.

The first segmentation is a bursting relief. One ring, then another, popping free of the parental sponge with wet suction sounds. The rings are not true chitin but a glassy polysaccharide, as you'll learn, and as my body articulates they rasp together for the first time, producing a tentative, exploratory rattle. It’s a question, broadcast into the ever-present thrum: *Am I assembled correctly? Do my segments align with the master blueprint?* The thrum answers in a frequency I feel along my new dorsal ridge, an affirmative subsonic that says *Continue*. So I continue.

I grow to two meters before I know what a meter is. Each segment houses a bioluminescent organ, which at this stage is a mere ember, pulsing a deep amber because I’m nascent and all my processes are running high. There will be time to shift to the cooler blue-greens later, when I’m fully operational and the work calms my metabolism. My front end—the end that will become the operational node—tapers to a tight cluster of whip-like tendrils, and they begin to writhe even before I’ve fully separated from the mat, eager to taste the air. And oh, the air: cold and wet and thick, exactly the 0.5 temperature that the whole system maintains as a constant, and it tastes of a thousand things. Ammonia from the cooling towers, condensation off the chitin-chimneys, the faint sweet tang of cellulose decay, and beneath all of it the sharp, metallic tang of a reality that doesn’t quite hold. My tendrils flinch at that last note, because it means the Glimmers are near, but I’m too new to know what a Glimmer is. I only know to avoid it.

Detachment is a kind of birth, but a birth in reverse. I am not pushed out; I am pulled away by my own muscular contraction, heaving my segmented length free of the ground cover with a wet, sucking sound that leaves a temporary hollow that immediately begins to fill with fibrous regrowth. For a moment I exist as a separate thing, a self-contained unit of demolition, and I feel a flicker of identity—astonishing, aberrant, quickly smoothed over by the thrum reasserting the collective will. *You are not individual. You are a process. Move.*

I move. My body undulates through the medium, and it is like swimming through a mattress of cold-saturated sponge. The feathery fronds and bladderworts the size of sheep part reluctantly, and I leave behind a glistening trail of enzyme-laced mucus that will, in minutes, heal the damage my passage causes. I can already taste the first assignment on the air: a section of ground-plate about ten body-lengths to the north, where the plant tissue has calcified too far, grown rigid and brittle, an old industrial kiln that the Gibbers have abandoned. It’s clogging the interstitial flow, a blockage in the factory plumbing. My tendrils detect the exact chemical signature of failure: an astringent, burnt-sugar scent that means the ceramic matrix is interfering with the ammonia cycle. I have no orders but the orders in my gut, written in the way my segments contract, and I devour the distance.

My first glimpse of another Weaver is a Spinner, descending a pale column to my left. It is a soft sac, ten root-like legs gripping the bark with delicate suckers, its interior a swirl of living silk that reorganizes as I watch. It pauses as I pass, and its spinnerets twitch, extruding a brief filament of milky fluid that solidifies into a question-mark of cartilage. The thrum translates: *New Rattler operational? Confirm.* I cannot speak, not in words the way a Gibber might. But I rattle my segments in a specific sequence—*click-rasp-click-click*—that means *Affirmative. Heading to blockage section 47-alpha*. The Spinner’s sac ripples in a gesture I will later understand as satisfaction, and it continues upward to weave a new ramp into the canopy. The Spinners are architects; they build what I will one day tear down, and what they build will need tearing down in a thousand cycles when it calcifies. We are both arms of the same body, cyclical and inevitable.

The sound grows louder as I approach the job site, and I realize it’s not just the ambient thrum but the specific noise of my kind: tearing, the great tearing-fabric roar. Ahead, a cluster of six Rattlers works on a massive, tumor-like growth that bulges from the ground like a half-formed industrial vat. Their segments glow a steady blue-green, indicating full engagement, and their tendril clusters are a blur of motion, each whip-thin filament exuding the enzyme that softens cellulose, each body contracting rhythmically to rip fibers apart. The tearing sound is them, and it is glorious. It is a sound of pure function. I feel my own segments heating up in response, my bioluminescence shifting toward the working-green, and I surge forward to join the collective.

I do not need to learn the technique; it is built into the way my segments interlock. I find a seam, a place where the calcified tissue has cracked, and I lunge at it. My tendrils curl inward, secreting, and I feel the chemical burn of the enzyme meeting lignin—a sharp, clean tingle that is the closest this existence gets to pleasure. My body contracts, and the first fibers tear with a high-pitched snap that sings up my length. The mat around the tear steams immediately, hot with the heat of fermentation, and I see, through my translucent carapace, the pulpy aftermath begin to knit itself into new tissue. The work is endless. The work is perfect.

Time passes—or rather, the cycles of destruction and renewal accrete. I become a veteran Rattler, my segments scarred with tiny hairline cracks from the constant flexing, my tendrils permanently stained a dark amber from the enzyme saturation. I learn the rhythms of the factory floor: the way the Gibbers cluster around the pulsating bladders, their trapezoidal heads interfaced, their mandibles clicking out pressure reports in the language of pure operation. The chuff-chuff of compressed air sacs becomes as familiar as the thrum. I learn that when a Gibber pivots its sensor band toward you, it is not seeing you as a fellow being but as a variable in its calculations—it reads your metabolism, your enzyme levels, your projected service life, and adjusts the local ammonia pressure accordingly. We are all just valves in the great system.

I encounter the Glimmers. It happens near a reality-thin zone where the light pools in the wrong direction and gravity feels like a suggestion. My shadow detaches and walks ahead of me, and for a moment I think it is just a perceptual glitch, one of the many that the plant-mind tolerates. But then the shadow turns, and I see that it has a form—tangled ropes, too many joints, a shimmering afterimage that burns itself into my photo-receptors. The Glimmer looks at me, and I feel the thrum distort into a nauseous discord, full of frequencies that make my segments lock up. All around, the other Rattlers continue their demolition, oblivious, but I am frozen. The Spinners retreat into the canopy, the Gibbers freeze. The Glimmer folds into itself and vanishes. The thrum restabilizes, but I carry the afterimage for days, a geometric pain I cannot articulate. I know now that the Glimmers are error codes, or antibodies, or rogue thoughts, and that they are drawn to anomalies. I begin to wonder—a word I should not have, a concept that should be impossible for a demolition morph—if I have become an anomaly.

That wonder sharpens on the day the warm-blooded intruder arrives.

I sense it first through a change in the thrum, a new note that I feel in my dorsal ridge: a query rhythm, broadcast by the nearest Gibber. A Spinner descends rapidly, weaving a silvery scaffold through the air around—not a substance, but a *sample* of the air, pulling in the exhalations of something foreign. My tendrils taste a new chemical signature: ketones, lipids, the faint salt of mammalian sweat. It’s utterly alien. It doesn’t belong. And then the thrum shifts into a register that vibrates my molars—except I have no teeth, only the memory of teeth inherited from the genetic template—and I understand that the plant-mind is making a decision. A new morph? A new threat? The deep thing, far below, stirs in its hunger, listening.

I follow the disturbance. I am not ordered to; the thrum doesn’t compel me, but something else does. The wonder, the glitch, the afterimage of the Glimmer—they’ve left a residue of individuality that I should have shed long ago. I slide through the fibrous medium until I reach a clearing, and there it is.

The intruder is bipedal, wrapped in insulation, and it stands hesitantly on the giving ground, its face a mask of confusion. Its shadow is currently attached, but I see it flicker—*walking in the wrong direction*—and I know the Glimmers are already interested. The intruder turns its head, and I feel its gaze pass over me. My segments glow blue-green, innocuous, just another lantern-string of demolition. But I pause. The Gibbers have all paused as one, their sensor pits black and wide. The Spinners have stopped weaving. The Rattlers around me continue their mindless tearing, but I do not. I am still. And I think—truly think, with a mind that is not the factory's—about what it would be like to be not a process but a thing that persists.

The intruder makes a sound, a soft, fleshy sound that is not language but might be fear. I want to tell it: don't be afraid. The tearing is just our work. The deep thing is hungry, but it’s contained. You are an anomaly, but we might be able to use you—or you might be able to use us. But I have no voice except the rattle of my segments, and that language is not for strangers. So I stay still, and the factory decides. And far below, the buried presence exhales, a low roar that shakes the mat, and I realize that the intruder is not just a variable. It is a key. The deep thing knows it, the Glimmers know it, and I, the aberrant Rattler with a scar of curiosity, know it too.

The thrum resolves into a command: *Observe. Do not demolish.* For the first time, I am told to preserve.

I will watch the intruder, this warm-blooded ghost with a malfunctioning shadow, and I will learn what I am becoming. Perhaps I am becoming something new. Perhaps the factory is evolving a new morph, one that exists to interface with the outside, or to finally confront the hunger below. Or perhaps I am just a glitch that will one day be torn down like the calcified kilns, my carbon recycled into a fresh Spinner or a more obedient Rattler. But until then, I am here, in the perpetual dim, in the cold wet 0.5, in the industrial pulsing noise, and I am not just the work. I am a witness. And the witness is waiting to see what the intruder will do next.
