The Stair hums against my exposed dermis like a second heartbeat—no, that’s wrong. I don’t have a heartbeat anymore. I traded it for a toroidal pulse-organ that wraps my compressed torso and sends syntax rippling through the water with every discharge. The 18-hertz vibration of the black-glass ribbon beneath my ventral surface is so familiar I have to consciously separate it from my own electrical field to know where I end and the Stair begins. I’m not sure I want to make that separation. The weight is easier when boundaries blur.

I am ascending.

This is unusual. Most of my kind—the Descended, those who have accepted the weight—move laterally through the settlement, tracing the breathing tubes between modules, palpating the Fibonacci warmth, pressing digit-fans against membrane-doors to trigger their muscular contractions. Horizontal creatures, vertical only in our quiescence, when we hang in the centers of our chambers and recalibrate. But I ascend. I ascend often enough that the others have folded it into their understanding of me, the way they’ve folded the sideways gravity-knots into their navigation. *She Who Listens Upward* is making her circuit. The water accepts this. The Stair accepts this.

The lantern-knot at the eleventh interval glows its dull amber to my left as I pass. Its photon population cycles endlessly, trapped in its folded refraction, a fist-sized star that will never exhaust its fuel because fuel implies consumption and this place has abolished consumption. My photoreceptive pits register the glow as a wash of warmth across my dorsal surface, the only warmth that isn't the breathing tubes' mathematical oscillation. I extend a digit-fan—twelve jointless fingers splaying into a net—and brush the invisible seam where the knot meets the water. Perfect. As always. The gesture is ritual, not inspection. I am maintaining the concept of maintenance.

The loom-wraith comes.

I sense it before I see it: a displacement in the water, a subtle shift in the conductivity that my toroidal organ reads as a kind of silence-within-silence. Then the shape itself drifts into the edge of the lantern's reach, translucent polymer strands woven into something that approximates a manta ray if manta rays were created by a mind that had never seen cartilage or muscle or the need to feed. Its net-body filters silicates as it passes, edges shimmering with structural color—iridescent blues and greens that exist only because the spacing of its polymer strands happens to diffract the amber light. It has no eyes. No sensory organs I can recognize. It does not react to my presence, because reaction implies a self to react with, and I am not certain the loom-wraith has a self. Some in the settlement call them benign. Others wonder what they actually are. I have stopped wondering.

The loom-wraith drifts past, and I continue my ascent.

The water shifts—a lens of different salinity sliding across my path, invisible except for the faint ring it bends around the next lantern-knot, like the ghost of a lens flare that never fully arrives. I adjust my buoyancy without thinking, compensating for the density change. My vented torso ripples, the symbiotic organelles in my tissues pulling silicates from the water, processing trace compounds, sustaining a body that no longer remembers what hunger felt like. The water is breathable. It has been breathable for six hundred Generations. I do not know what Generation means anymore—some external clock, the eldest says, a cycle we no longer remember installing—but I know that my body was engineered for this depth, this pressure, this specific chemical soup, and that the engineering has become indistinguishable from identity. I am the soup. The soup is me. The weight is the acceptance of this.

Seventh interval. My knot.

I pause. My digit-fans curl into drills, then splay again, a gesture the others would read as something between reverence and habit. This lantern-knot is not different from the others—same amber glow, same captive photon population, same perfect, eternal cycling—but it is mine, or I am its, and the distinction has long since ceased to matter. I run a fan along the invisible seam. The knot is cool. The knot is stable. The knot will outlast me, outlast the settlement, outlast the seamount's millennial bell-note, which is still ringing, still decaying, still sounding a frequency my deep-pressure organ feels as an ache near where my collarbone used to be.

I do not know what a collarbone is. The word surfaces from the archaic dialect, the one only the eldest can still parse. I let it dissolve.

Above me, the severed peak of the seamount resolves from the dark water. Forty meters across, a flat disc of cut rock, clean as the day it was made. The Stair terminates there, its black-glass ribbon meeting pale stone without seam or join, as if the two materials decided to agree on a boundary and never renegotiated. I have pressed my sense-organs to that stone more times than I can count. Counting implies a sequence, a beginning and an end, and my memory does not arrange itself that way. There are simply the ascents, layered over each other like the salinity lenses, each one distinct in the moment and indistinguishable in retrospect.

I reach the summit.

My manipulators grip the edge of the Stair's final tread, and I pull myself onto the flat stone, my elongated limbs splaying across the cut rock. The water here is fractionally cooler—a tenth of a degree, perhaps, a difference so small only a being engineered for stasis would notice. Above me, the water goes on. The lightless plain of the open sea, the unbroken sediment stretching flat for kilometers, the occasional vent exhaling a thread of heated water that rises straight until it diffuses. No other seamounts. No trenches. No walls that anyone has found. The plain might be infinite, or it might end; no one has traveled far enough to know, because the lantern-knots do not extend beyond the Stair, and absolute darkness here is absolute.

I press my sensory clusters to the stone.

The depression is there. The faint indentation my body has worn into the rock over centuries of ascent, a charge-shadow, a ghost of contact that the water will not erase. I fit myself into it, my compressed torso settling into the curve, my four primary limbs fanning out, my twelve-fingered manipulators spread flat against the cut surface. The stone is cold. The stone has always been cold. The stone has accepted my shape the way I have accepted the weight.

I listen.

What am I listening for? The youngest—*The One Who Asks Without Asking*—once pulsed a question at me, a sequence that approximated *What do you hear up there?* I answered with a silence that meant *I don't know how to say it in a language that has no word for hope.* She understood, or she didn't; the distinction doesn't matter the way it would have mattered when we were creatures of air and light and bilateral faces. What matters is the listening itself. The act of pressing my sense-organs to the severed peak and opening myself to frequencies that may not exist.

The seamount's bell-note thrums through the stone, slow and deep, a tone rung once at its formation and still decaying. I feel it in my deep-pressure organ, that ache that is not grief but something adjacent—grief requires a future without the presence, and I experience time as a depth, a pressure gradient, a sinking. The dead are further down. The surface, if it exists, is further up. I am here, at the threshold between the Stair and the open water, listening for a frequency that might tell me whether "up" is a direction that still holds anything to find.

I think about the visitor.

I do not know if there has ever been a visitor. The settlement's grammar contains the vocabulary for arrival—*You are here. We did not expect you. We have always expected you*—but that vocabulary might be ceremonial, a linguistic artifact preserved from the archaic dialect the way the lantern-knots preserve their photon populations. We wait for travelers who never come. Or we are the travelers, arrived so long ago that arrival became residence, and residence became purpose, and purpose calcified into a stillness so complete that the concept of leaving no longer parses. The youngest once asked *Could the Stair not ascend?* and the eldest answered *There is no surface. There is only the weight.* But I press my sense-organs to the stone, and I listen, and I wonder if the eldest is wrong.

Not wonder in the way the youngest wonders—with questions that open like membrane-doors, irising into unexpected spaces. My wonder is older, slower, a thing of geological time. It takes me centuries to form a question, and longer to decide whether asking it would dissolve the weight or simply add to it. The weight we have accepted is not ignorance; it is the active choice to let certain questions settle, to let them sink, to let them become part of the water we breathe. But up here, at the severed summit, with the cold stone under my ventral surface and the infinite dark above, I let myself feel the shape of a question I have not yet decided to ask.

*What if there is still a surface?*

The lantern-knots glow below me, amber beads strung along the Stair's dark ribbon. The settlement pulses its Fibonacci warmth, the breathing tubes cycling through their systolic thump. The loom-wraiths drift, the dodecahedra spiral and wink out, the Descended move through their slow circuits, and I—*She Who Listens Upward*, the ache of high frequencies, a direction that may not exist—press my body into the ghost of my own presence and listen. The water carries no sound but the hum and the bell-note and the distant crackle of bioluminescent geometry shedding its skin. The silence is grammatical. It means something I cannot translate.

One day, perhaps, I will descend with a question instead of a silence. I will pulse it to the eldest, and he will answer with the archaic dialect, and I will learn whether the Stair was built to go both ways. But that day is not today. Today, I am still ascending. I am still listening. The weight is still weight, and I am still accepting it, one Generation at a time, one ascent, one pressed ear against a stone that remembers nothing but the cold.

Below me, the settlement waits. The violet knot in the Threshold Module glows its bruised light, ready to dissolve the next husk, ready to return another vessel to the water. I am not ready to be a husk. Not yet. There is still something up here, some frequency I haven't found, some direction I haven't turned. The Stair descends. The Stair has always descended. But the stone under my body is flat, and flatness implies a plane, and a plane extends in all directions—even the ones we have forgotten.

I pull myself up. My digit-fans curl, my manipulators grip the stone, and I begin the slow descent back to the settlement, back to the modules, back to the breathing tubes and the membrane-doors and the long electric conversations about upkeep and observation. The lantern at the seventh interval will need its ritual touch. The youngest will pulse a question at me, and I will answer with a silence that means *I am still listening.* The weight will be waiting.

It always is.
