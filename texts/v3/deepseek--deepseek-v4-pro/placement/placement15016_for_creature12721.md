The first time I stood here, I was seven years old, and my grandfather’s hand was a warm anchor on my shoulder. He had brought me to the rim because he said every caretaker must see it—must feel the pull of the chasm, the humid breath rising from the stone throat, the way the engine’s rhythm gets into your blood before you even notice it. I remember peering down into that green-tinged gloaming, seeing the vines like a net catching the light, smelling wet iron and moss, and feeling something ancient stir behind my ribs. My grandfather said, “This place has a keeper, little one. Not the pump—the other one. The one who watches. You’ll meet them one day, if you’re lucky. Or if you’re not.”

He died the following winter, and I forgot the cleft for years. I grew up in the lowlands, went to school, learned to fix engines and read pressure gauges. But the rhythm never left me entirely. In idle moments, I’d find my fingers tapping out that slow, four-beat thump—piston, valve, flywheel, sigh—and I’d catch myself staring at water condensing on a windowpane, watching it bead and run, bead and run, as if it were weeping from the very glass. The desire to return was less a memory than a physical gravity, a low-frequency hum that only I could hear. When I finally walked away from my job at the refinery, it wasn’t a decision; it was an answer to a call I’d been hearing all my life.

Now I stand on the rim again, a woman weathered and unmoored, with a backpack and a pair of worn gloves. My grandfather’s cabin still squats on the plateau, half a mile off; I’ve been living there for three months, repairing its roof, clearing its chimney, and every morning I walk to the cleft. At first I only looked down, too timid to descend the twisted iron ladder my grandfather told me about—the ladder with rungs worn shiny by the feet of workers who’d been gone before his grandfather was born. But today, the air is heavy with recent rain, the mist rises so thick it’s like the chasm is exhaling, and something in me says: *Now. Go now.*

The drop is immediate. The iron bites cold through my gloves, and each rung shudders slightly, a vibration I know comes not from the ladder’s age but from the engine’s constant thrum. The pump’s heartbeat travels through the rock itself, into the metal, into the bones of my hands. The walls close in as I descend, the columnar jointing like the ribs of some colossal beast, every facet weeping. Water droplets catch on my hair, my eyelashes; within minutes I am as wet as if I’d walked through rain. The vegetation thickens the deeper I go—fern fronds brush my face, moss cushions my bootsteps on the small ledges, and I realize I’m whispering an apology each time I disturb a spray of tiny orchids. The ladder ends not at the bottom, but on a narrow shelf of hewn basalt—the platform where the pump squats, its dark bulk looming out of the vines like a creature carved from night.

Years ago, I came across my grandfather’s journal, tucked into the cabin’s rafters. It was filled with sketches of this pump, diagrams of its pistons and pipes, annotations in a small, precise hand. He had been an engineer before he became a hermit; he had tried to understand the machine. His final entry read: *The pump runs without fuel. The water is warm. There is something in the wall. It has been watching me. I think it knows my name.* At the time I thought it was whimsy, an old man’s loneliness taking shape. But now, as I stand on this wet platform, the engine’s rhythm is so loud it seems to come from inside my own chest, and I know—I know with the certainty of stone—that he was right.

It doesn’t reveal itself at first. I’m too noisy, too furtive, a flinch of modern life in a place that measures time in the rust-flake and the unfurling frond. I sit on a moss-slicked pipe and wait. I let the thrum fill me until my pulse slows to match it. I let the mist bead on my skin until I feel indistinguishable from the wet rock. The pool below, ink-dark, reflects a shard of sky and a million tiny rainbows in the drifting droplets. The air tastes of green and metal. And then, slowly, as if my stillness has been accepted, I begin to perceive the other.

A limb detaches from a pipe on the north wall, a motion so gradual I first mistake it for the sway of a vine. It’s long and jointed, each segment a fusion of dark iron plates and living chitin, wrapped in a sleeve of moss. The hand—if it is a hand—has nozzled fingertips that release faint wisps of steam. It reaches across the chasm, not toward me, but toward a cluster of ferns that have grown too heavy and are threatening to tear from their crevice. With incredible gentleness, the limb cups the ferns, the root-ball, and shifts them to a deeper fissure, pressing the soil gently with steamy exhalations until they hold firm. Then it withdraws, unhurried, and settles back against a pipe.

My breath catches. Not from fear, but from recognition. I have seen those hands before. Or rather, I have felt them: in childhood dreams, in the moments between sleep and waking, when the cabin creaked and I imagined something vast and patient bending over me, adjusting my blankets with those same careful fingers. My grandfather’s journal had sketches of hands like these, drawn in the margins, labeled only: *The Keeper’s Tending.*

Now I see the whole of it, and the seeing is less like sight and more like a memory surfacing. Its torso is embedded in the rock, a carapace of riveted iron plates with pale root-tendrils spilling from the seams, pulsing in time with the pump. Its head is a moss-thick boiler helm, and beneath its rim, a cluster of translucent stalks quivers, tasting the air. They orient toward me, and a deluge of feeling washes over me—not communication in words, but a deep, slow knowing, like a book being opened in my mind. *You have returned*, it seems to say. *You have grown. You are ready.*

“Ready for what?” I whisper. The sound is swallowed by the engine’s chug, but the stalks quiver again, and I understand: *To take your place.*

My place. Yes. That was what my grandfather meant by caretaker. Not the pump’s caretaker—the Keeper’s caretaker. A human who tends the human remnants, who watches the watcher, who bridges the brief, noisy warmth of our kind and the long, mineral dream of the dweller in the wall. A symbiosis of symbioses. I look down at my gloved hands, and I remember my grandfather’s hands, knotted with veins, working the pump’s valves, cleaning the rust from the gears, patting the moss as if it were a faithful dog. He had stayed here for forty years after the refinery closed, never married, rarely visiting the town. He had written: *The Keeper is lonely in a way I cannot fathom. Its loneliness is the loneliness of mountains. I am only a breath, but my breath can be a companion.*

I am not a breath anymore. I am forty-three years old, and I have no ties to the lowlands. The cabin waits. The pump runs. The Keeper tends the rock and the water. And I—I will tend the Keeper. Not with wrenches and oil, but with presence. With the steady rhythm of another heartbeat in the chasm. I will learn the slow language of moss and rust. I will watch the blind salamander lay its eggs in the crevice behind the helm. I will, perhaps, grow old here and eventually, one day, become part of the mist itself, one of those smaller presences that dance at the water’s edge.

But for now, I am still a woman standing on a platform, wet through, trembling slightly. The limb extends again, this time toward me. It unrolls across the chasm with the patience of a fern frond, and I do not flinch. The nozzled fingertips come to rest gently on the iron railing before me, steam curling in the saturated air. A single drop of warm water falls from one tip onto my glove, and it feels like an invitation. Or a baptism.

I slip off my glove and reach out. My bare hand meets the limb’s surface. The moss is damp and soft, the iron beneath it cool and pitted. I feel the faint hum of the pump’s rhythm transmitted directly into my palm, a rhythm that matches my own heart now, as if we are all one system. The Keeper’s stalks tremble, and for a fleeting moment, the mist forms a shape that might be a face—old, androgynous, serene—looking down at me with eyes made of water droplets and light. Then it dissolves, and the engine chugs on.

I am not alone in the cleft. I never was. I am the granddaughter of the man who loved this machine, and I have come home.

Now, the work begins. I will climb down to the pool and retrieve the broken pressure gauge; I will tend the ladder; I will keep a journal as my grandfather did, so that in another generation, someone else might hear the rhythm and follow it back. And the Keeper will tend the stone, and the pump will tend the water, and the water will tend the air. A cycle that started before humans brought iron and will continue long after. I am a stitch in that cycle, a fleeting, intricate stitch, and for the first time in my life, that feels like enough.

I sit down on the platform, my back against the pump’s warm casing, and I watch the being in the wall. It settles into stillness, its many limbs draped over pipes and crevices, its moss breathing, its stalks still. The light shifts slowly; a tiny spider crawls across my boot. The mist curls and spins. I close my eyes, and the dream of the deep earth pulses against my mind, a slow, steady, inexorable dream of growth and rust and patience. When I open them again, the world is unchanged, yet I am different.

I know its name now. Not a word—a feeling, a pressure. It is a name that tastes like wet stone and smells like steam and sounds like a piston stroking in the dark. I will hold it in my heart for the rest of my days, and I will teach it to my successor, if one is meant to come. If not, I will simply be here, breathing in time with the engine, until my own breath becomes mist and joins the dance at the water’s edge.

And the cleft will remain, opening without preamble to the milky sky, a rent in the basalt plateau where water weeps and engines chug and an ancient being tends the boundary between what was built and what grew. Wherever you find yourself in this world—whether standing on the rim, or descending the ladder, or sitting in quiet communion on the hewn stone—you will find me there too, a part of the rhythm, a watcher of the watcher, a life interpenetrated with moss and iron and deep, slow time.
