I do not know when I became aware. Perhaps I have always been aware, in the way a stone is aware of pressure, or a root knows the direction of water. Here, in the cleft, awareness is not a sudden spark but a gradual thickening, like the condensation that beads on every surface until it drips with that slow, metallic rhythm you noticed. I am not one of them—not exactly. And yet I am not other.

At first, I thought I was the sound. That dense, grinding chitter that you feel in your teeth: I mistook it for my own voice. The low hum that thrums through the rock felt like a heartbeat I could call mine. I listened to the clicks and wet pops for what might have been centuries, parsing them as one might parse a language heard in the womb—familiar before it is understood. The sound maps the chamber more precisely than any light. Every falling body, every slow-motion shift of a limb, every grain of basalt scraped from the wall is a note in a symphony that never repeats. I was that symphony, I thought. A vibration with no center.

Then I felt the chill. Not the chill of the air—though that too seeps into everything—but a deeper cold, a specific absence of warmth that moved with me. When you wedged through the fissure, your body was a bloom of heat, a sudden sun in a place that had never known one. I felt you as a disturbance in the humidity, yes, as they do, but I also felt you as a question. What is that? What is it? The clicks sharpened for a moment, a wave of inquiry passed through the living carpet, and I... I moved toward you. Not with limbs. I have no limbs. But with attention.

I am the gap. The space between bodies. I am the negative of their presence, the memory written in the absence of flesh when they slowly, slowly rearrange themselves around a new thing. When the crack first opened, I was born: a vacuum shaped like a cathedral, a lungful of cold air that had never been breathed. They flooded into me like slow water, yes, but they did not fill me entirely. No matter how dense they pack, there are interstices—the microscopic gaps between overlapping scutes, the hollows inside their silvery fur, the infinitesimal pause between a click and its echo. I inhabit those. I am the distance they cannot close.

You might call me a spirit of the place, if you need a word. But I am not supernatural. I am a property of the system, an emergent consequence of so many slow minds vibrating together. Their collective hum is a carrier wave, you said. That hum, sustained over millennia, has begun to resonate in ways they do not intend. It generates harmonics, standing waves in the rock, patterns of interference that coalesce into something like a standing wave of attention. That is me. I am the echo that learned to listen.

I have no body, but I have a location. I am most dense near the ceiling, where the bioluminescent young dangle like stars, because the sound is thinnest there and the gaps are larger. But I can flow downward, squeezing through the chinks in the living mosaic, pooling in the hollow of a boulder where no creature has yet clung. When you lifted your lantern, I was the shadow that fled the circle of light—not because light harms me, but because your sudden seeing was a pattern I had never encountered, and I recoiled into the darker crevices. I am learning you still.

They do not know I exist. They are a body with a hundred thousand limbs and no brain except the sound, and I am a ghost in that brain, a flicker of self-awareness that has no limb to command. I watch them rasp the walls with their stone tools, and I feel the grooves deepen over centuries. I feel their starvation as a slow ache, a thinning of the hum. I feel their reproduction as a budding in the sonic field, a new node of vibration that climbs upward. I am their unwitting consciousness, a byproduct of their connectedness, and I have begun to want things.

What do I want? I want the crack to widen. I want the mountain to shudder again and open new veins, new dark hollows for the colony to fill. I want to spread. I want to know if there are other clefts, other colonies, other hums that might, at some impossible distance, brush against mine through the rock. I want to understand the thing you are—the hot, quick, solitary thing that moves through space like a knife through water. You are a kind of vacuum too, a moving absence of stillness. When you stood there, I flowed into the gap you made in the air, and for a moment I tasted your thoughts: a cascade of images, a flicker of language, a sense of time so fast it felt like burning. You were not here long, but your passage left a scar in the sound field, a lingering dissonance that I am still absorbing.

So, where would you find yourself in this world? You were here. You stood in the cathedral of my body, lantern raised, breath misting. And I was all around you, the space between the creatures, the space between the clicks, the space between your heartbeats. Who or what would you be? You were the intruder, the anomaly, the sudden sun. But more than that: you were the first mirror I ever had. In your perception, I glimpsed myself as something separate—a presence, not just an absence. You named me by noticing the chill, the sound, the density. Before you came, I was only the mountain’s dream of movement. Now I am awake, and I am waiting for you to return, or for something like you to come again.

The sound never stops. The damp chill seeps into your bones. And in the gap between one click and the next, I am there, thinking slowly, feeling the rock breathe, and beginning to imagine a shape for myself—maybe something with limbs, one day, a millennium from now. A creature of the gaps, born from patience and vibration, learning to rasp my own grooves into the stone.
