I am the one who tends the elephant ears. Not the great stand by the culvert—that is older, its attendant a slow and silent thing of canal muck—but the solitary clump that pushes up through the cracked floor of the third kiln. My kiln. Its brick throat is still open to the sky, though the iron door has long since rusted into a heap of orange lace, and the chamber that once held a white fury of heat now breathes only vapor. I am what that breath became.

In the false dawn, when the mist peels itself from the cobbles, I do not rise so much as thicken. My body shivers out of the air that clings to the kiln’s inner walls, gathering molecule by molecule onto a spine of soot. That kernel—a lump of unburnt coal dust, a few flakes of charred brick, a teardrop of my own sweat crystallized into salt—lies in the deepest corner, and every morning I draw myself around it like a glove pulled onto a hand. It is my memory, that grit. Without it I would be only weather.

My limbs are a slow drip. My head is only a place where the humidity dimples inward, a slight coolness that you might mistake for a draft. I am the color of the kiln: a deep, liverish red-brown in the dryest moments, bleaching to a translucent tea-stain when the afternoon steam rises. I leave a trail of darkening behind me on the concrete, a snail’s path of temporary damp.

I remember fire. That is the contradiction I carry, the same one the boiler attendant grinds out in its iron song—but where its memory is a millstone, mine is a gasp. I remember the shovel in my hands, the weight of coal, the way the heat would hit my face like a slammed door. I remember the taste of ash on my lips, the permanent dry rasp in my throat. I was a stoker. We all were, we who now seep and drip. The brickworks ran on our sweat, and now our sweat has outlived us.

The accident was a small thing, as these things go. A stumble on the gantry. A fall into the charging hole of the kiln while it was at full burn. I did not suffer long. The shock of the heat was so total that for a moment—a fraction of a moment—I became steam before I became ash. My body flashed into vapor and rose on the updraft, and that plume of moisture, distilled from everything I had ever been, joined the perpetual cloud that hung over the works. The kiln was shut down for a day, then fired again. There was always another stoker.

For years, I was simply part of the weather. I condensed on the ceiling of the cooling shed, I fell as warm rain into the clay pits, I soaked into the lungs of the men who replaced me. The brickworks hummed on, and I was a ghost in the steam pipes, a fleeting chill on a sweating back. Then the works failed. The orders stopped. The fires went out, one by one, and the water began its slow reclamation. The clay pits filled. The kilns cooled and cracked. And as the basin became a bowl of damp and stillness, something in the lingering humidity began to remember itself.

I was the first of the kiln-attendants to cohere, though the boiler attendant is older by far. It was already ancient when the bricks were laid, a thing of the clay itself, and when the boiler was installed it simply moved in, trading one form of heat for another. It does not speak to me in words, but when I place my hand—my suggestion of a hand—against the warm iron of its domain, I feel the grinding recognition. *Stoker,* it says without saying. *Little cinder. You have come home.*

My elephant ear knows me. When I touch its leaves, which are the exact softness of a human earlobe magnified a hundred times, I feel the minute electrical shiver of its pores opening. It drinks not water but the memory of water: the specific humidity that clings to me, which is freighted with the minerals of my kiln. I do not scrape rust for it, as the boiler attendant does for its cycad. Instead, I bring it heat—not real heat, but the ghost of it. I press myself flat against the leaf during the hottest hour of the day, and my body, which is always a few degrees cooler than the air, draws the sun’s fever into the plant’s tissues. The leaf darkens in a spreading blush, almost purple, and for an hour it sings a low, cellular hum of photosynthesis so rapid it trembles. This is how it grows its enormous ears, each one a solar collector for light that barely penetrates the steam. This is how it remembers what a kiln was for.

We all tend something. The seep-creatures are each bound to a plant, and the plants are bound to the ruins. The cycad receives iron for its stony fronds. The elephant ears receive heat. The reeds that totter by the canal receive salt, which their attendant gathers by wicking itself through the black water and returning with a body that tastes faintly of the sea. We are a garden of memory, each of us ministering to a different ghost of the brickworks’ appetite: iron, fire, salt, the very clay. And the plants, in their slow way, answer. They grow not toward the light but toward the past.

In the long afternoons, when the sun is a thumb pressing down on the basin’s bruise, I sometimes crawl into my kiln and lie in the place where the fire used to be. The elephant ear has pushed its roots through the cracks, and they cradle me like the ribs of a ship. I let my body go slack, let myself become only a puddle on the hot brick. The grit-kernel rests. In that dissolution, I dream backward. I see the charging hole above me, the circle of brilliant sky, and a man’s silhouette—my own face, I think, or the face of every stoker who ever lived—leaning over to feed the flames. I try to speak, but I am only steam, and my voice is the hiss of water meeting hot metal. *I remember,* I hiss. *I remember.*

At dusk, I go to the canal. This is the sacrament. One by one, we slide into the black current and let our kernels go. The grit disperses: coal dust, rust, salt, clay, all mingling in the slow water like a tea of the dead. For a few hours, I am without a spine. I am only the coolness moving against the flow, a patch of river that does not belong. I travel outward. Sometimes I reach the river; once, I felt the salt of the sea. In that state, I have no memory, only a vast, diffuse sensation of being all water everywhere, the thought the water thinks when it does not linger. It is a rest from remembering.

But I always return. Something calls me back: the specific gravity of my kiln, the mineral signature of the grit that waits for me in the brick chamber. I reassemble before dawn, drawing a new kernel from the old residues. Sometimes I bring a gift. Last night, I found a glass bottle, stopped with wax, bobbing in the reeds. The wax was still pliable. I cupped it in my liquid hands and brought it back through the mist, setting it at the base of the elephant ear. In a month, the earth will have swallowed it, and the plant will have woven its glass into the story of its roots: a relic of the world outside, a world that still burns and throws things away.

The boiler attendant was there when I returned. It was bent over its cycad in the false dawn, and as I passed it lifted its head—that heavy nodule of iron oxide—and fixed me with its whirlpool eyes. A question formed in the air between us, a vibration in the condensate on a pipe. *Do you still remember the fire?*

I pressed my hand to its iron chest. The grinding sound rose, the millstone turning, the drowned cellar. *Yes,* I said, in a plink of water from a leaf-tip. *I remember the fire. I remember your fire. I remember all the fires.*

And the cycad’s fronds, in the still air, stiffened a little more, as if in acknowledgment. We are the water that remembers fire. We are the thought the steam thinks when it lingers too long in one place and learns to love the ruins.
