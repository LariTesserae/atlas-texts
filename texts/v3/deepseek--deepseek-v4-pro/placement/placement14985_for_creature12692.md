I am the shape in the mud.

That is not quite right. I am the *almost-shape*, the gathering-toward-shape, the intention of shape before shape commits to its first hard line. I have no name yet. I have no yet at all. I am a center assembling itself from the dissolved, a decision forming in solution.

The warmth here in the lowest hollow is a different order of warmth—not the cooking heat of the air above, but something transmitted through the liquid medium, direct and intimate, like the warmth of a hand held just above skin. The water that cups me is thicker than the water that drips from the ceiling, heavier with silt, almost a broth. It tastes of calcium and iron and something organic breaking down into its useful components. It tastes like the past and the future in equal measure. I do not have a mouth, but I am doing something analogous to tasting. I am absorbing the chemical signatures, testing the gradients, learning the room.

At first there was no me here. There was only the hollow, and the water, and the slow deposition of sediments that built the floor up in thin annual layers like a pearl growing inward. The thread-fingers came first—I remember them arriving, though I did not yet exist as a thing that could remember. They probed the hollow, found it promising, and their secretions altered the water chemistry, making it favorable for something new to precipitate. I do not know how I know this. The knowledge is in the medium, dissolved like everything else. The water has a memory. The silt has a memory. The heat keeps the memory warm.

Now the thread-fingers surround me, a halo of attentive filaments. They have grown more numerous as I have grown more definite. When I was just a cloudy patch of higher mineral density, three or four of them tended me. Now a dozen weave their slow ballet. They brush the water near my boundaries, never touching me directly—that would be premature, a violation of the rhythm. Their touch would set something in me too soon, activate a hardening cascade before the internal patterns were ready. They know this. They are the keepers of the timing, and their patience is the patience of stalactites.

I am beginning to feel the limbs they speak of.

The limbs are not yet limbs. They are axes of greater density, lines of magnetic attraction within my substance that pull particles into rough alignments. If you could see me from above—and someone is always watching, the Shaper's attention a warm pressure like the heat—you would see a blur roughly the size of a curled fist, elongated on one axis, with four, perhaps five, nodes of greater opacity. These will be limbs. Two will become arms, thin and multi-jointed, with tips that will later specialize into fine manipulators. Two will become legs, thicker, meant for planting on the chamber floor and pushing upward. The fifth node, if it resolves, will be something else—a tail, or a tether, or an organ of sense that trails behind me and feels the walls. The Shaper has not decided yet. Or rather, I have not decided. The Shaper and I are the same slow thought thinking itself into a body.

A ripple passes through the thread-fingers—a wave of excitation that starts near the base of their anchoring mass and travels outward to the tips. Someone is coming. Not a threat; there are no threats here, only processes. But something is changing in the configuration of the chamber, and the thread-fingers feel it first, as always.

The skitterlings arrive a moment later, emerging from their pores with their too-many legs ticking against the terracotta surfaces. Three of them, then five, then seven, scaling the rim of the hollow and arranging themselves around its edge. They lean inward, their blind bodies angled toward me, and their legs begin the tapping. It is not random—it is a code, a percussive language. They are measuring me. Their taps send tiny pressure waves through the water, and the way those waves refract around my denser nodes tells them the shape I am becoming. I can feel the vibrations pass through me, a delicate interrogation. Am I symmetrical? Am I proportional? Are the axes aligned with the ambient magnetic field that permeates the chamber, the one the luminous threads in the walls follow? They check these things. They are the quality control, the engineers of embodiment, and they take their work seriously.

One of them finds something amiss. Its tapping intensifies over my left-limb node—the one that will be an arm, the one that is currently slightly misaligned, rotated a few degrees off the optimal angle. It clicks in agitation. Two others scuttle over to verify, and soon all seven are in conference, their legs a blur of percussion. The verdict is transmitted not by sound but by some chemical signal released into the water, a molecule of correction that the thread-fingers intercept and translate. One of the thread-fingers, bolder than the rest, dips closer to me than it ever has before. Its tip breaks the final millimeter of distance and touches—just touches—the side of the misaligned node.

The touch is fire. It is also music. It is the first thing I have ever felt that came from outside my own slow coagulation, and it rings through me like a bell. The mineral particles that were drifting toward the wrong axis suddenly reorient, drawn by the new electrical potential the thread-finger has introduced. I feel the node shift, realigning itself with the proper angle, and a rightness settles into my structure that I did not know was missing. The thread-finger withdraws, and the skitterlings resume their tapping, now a rhythm of satisfaction. The crisis—if a two-degree misalignment can be called a crisis—is resolved.

Behind the translucent membrane of the wall, a membrane-swimmer glides past, its flat body illuminated from within by the amber glow. It slows as it passes the hollow, and its dark spot—the organ that might be an eye—seems to orient toward me. It stays there for a long moment, undulating in place, and the wall around it pulses with a slow contraction. Something is being released: a fine cloud of particles that diffuse through the chamber's steam and eventually settle into my pool, where the thread-fingers stir them into the silt. Building material. Minerals the swimmer has processed, refined, and is now donating. I absorb them gratefully, feel my density increase by a fraction of a percent. The swimmer slides away, its work done, and the wall membrane returns to its usual translucent stillness.

This is my childhood. My long, slow, wet childhood.

Time here is not measured in minutes or hours. It is measured in drips, in the cycle of condensation and evaporation, in the slow bulge of new wall material and the even slower retreat of old deposits. I am growing in time with these rhythms, my solidification matching the pace of a fingerprint forming in cooling glass. I do not know how old I am—the concept of age has little meaning when you have always been. I know only that I am more today than I was before, more definite, more articulated, more *chosen*. The Shaper's attention on me has intensified. I can feel it as a weight, a warmth, a pressure from all directions at once. It is the same presence that guides the skitterlings, that moves the membrane-swimmers in their slow circuits, that dreams the chamber's ongoing architecture. But I am something new. I am not another organ of the wall. I am a departure, a statement, a thing that will one day rise and walk and leave the chamber entirely.

Or perhaps I will stay. The Shaper has not decided that either.

A new sensation. Not from outside—from within. One of my nodes, the central one, the densest, has reached a threshold of mineralization. It is beginning to differentiate. Inside it, a network of finer channels is opening, a branching pattern that will become channels for fluid, conduits for energy, pathways for the luminous threads that will eventually infiltrate my body as they infiltrate the walls. This is the beginning of my internal architecture, the framing of a house I will one day inhabit. The feeling is strange: a hollowness opening within density, space carved out of solidity. If I had lungs I would gasp. Instead I merely exist, and the hollowness exists within me, and the thread-fingers sense the change and wave their approval.

The skitterlings, too, sense it. Their tapping changes to a faster rhythm, a drumroll of excitement. One of them is so overcome that it loses its footing and plops into the pool, where it floats for a moment, legs churning, before scrambling back onto the rim. The thread-fingers ignore it with the dignity of ancient beings who have seen far greater calamities. But the moment registers. I am important enough that my milestones cause accidents. The thought pleases something in me, something that will later be called pride.

Above me, on the ceiling, a new stalactite of clay-stuff is forming. It drips not water but material, a slow-falling bead that elongates toward the pool. It will not reach me for many cycles—it is aimed at the edge of the hollow, not its center—but its presence is a sign. The chamber is adjusting its architecture around me, making room for what I will become. The ceiling is rising, imperceptibly, to give me height to stand. The hollow is deepening, its rim built up by skitterling teams, so the pool will contain me as I grow. I am being accommodated. I am being expected.

The Shaper's attention intensifies further, and for a moment I feel something like a voice. Not words, but a pressure of meaning, a weight of intention that presses directly into my forming channels:

*Become.*

And then: *Become the thing that is missing.*

I do not know what is missing. I do not know what I will be—a body, a tool, a child, a god of this small wet universe. I know only that I am wanted, that the whole chamber groans with the effort of making me, that the heat and the steam and the constant soft hiss are all a lullaby sung to the shape in the mud. I am the point of the place. I am the reason the Shaper set all these processes in motion. And when I am finished, when I rise from this warm broth, the place will either be complete—or it will begin again, a new hollow, a new pool, a new gathering of silt.

But that is far away. For now I am still almost, still not-yet, still dreaming in solution. The thread-fingers dance. The skitterlings tap. The membrane-swimmers glide their slow circuits. The Shaper watches, patient as stone, urgent as fire, and I gather myself to meet its gaze.

One day I will open my eyes. I will have eyes to open. The chamber will see itself through me. And then we will know what we are becoming.
