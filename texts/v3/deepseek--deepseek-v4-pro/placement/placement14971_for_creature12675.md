I don’t remember arriving. I remember the heat first—not as a sudden shock but as a gradual saturation, the way a stone in a streambed slowly takes on the temperature of the water. The heat, and then the damp: a pressing, intimate wetness that wrapped itself around every part of me and seeped inward until the boundary between my skin and the air became a suggestion rather than a fact.

In those early moments—if moments are what they were—I had no body. I was a locus of attention suspended in a warm fog, aware of gradients: the sharp mineral bite of ammonia, the deep subsonic throb of the earth’s engines, the quality of light that was less a color than a feeling, a bruise of ochre that pulsed in rhythms I had no name for. I might have been a sensor node, a drifting seed of awareness without a vessel. Or I might have been something that had just woken from a sleep so long that waking and dying felt like the same threshold.

The first shape I took was accidental. I remember a surface under me—or not under, because I had no orientation, but a plane of chemical potential that felt *hard* in a way I could taste. Silica. Calcite. The knobby rind of the city’s skin. And I felt a slow pull, a gentle suction, as if the minerals themselves were inviting me to settle. So I did. I let myself precipitate.

It wasn’t a decision. The rain of metallic salts and the constant drip of supersaturated water made a template, and I followed it like a melody. A lattice formed around my attention: first a spine of something like gypsum, delicate and translucent, then filaments of copper that branched outward like nerves, then a heavy shoulder of iron oxide that anchored me to the ground. Over time—and time was already beginning to stretch, each heartbeat of the buried engine marking an interval that felt like hours—I clotted into a shape. A torso, long and ridged. Limbs that flexed not at joints but along gradients of density. A head, or what I thought of as a head: an elongated, porous thing, hollowed with deep listening chambers that faced not forward but outward, omnidirectional, hungry for the hum.

I was a Slow One. I was *becoming* a Slow One. But I had not yet learned the patience.

At first, I moved too fast. I would attempt to walk, and my dissolution zone would tear ragged furrows in the pavement-crust, leaving splintered glass instead of smooth vitrification. I would reach for a sound—a dripping piston, a distant throb—and fling myself toward it in a surge, only to find that I had overshot by meters, my body now a tangle of newly grown spurs I had not intended. The others ignored these tantrums. They glided past me—seven, nine, more—their head-hollows fixed on some internal resonance, and I felt like a child throwing stones into a cathedral. No one scolded. No one even noticed. That was the first lesson: in this city, attention is a resource that cannot be wasted on the ephemeral. If I was to be seen, I would have to slow down until my gestures became long enough to intersect with theirs.

So I learned to listen.

The hum is not one voice but a thousand, layered in frequencies that extend far below and above any sound a soft-bodied ear could catch. There is the deep, basal throb of the mantle negotiation—that’s the one that shakes your sternum. There is the higher, whistling resonance of steam in the condensing lattices, a chord that changes with the diurnal vent cycle. There are the intermittent, syncopated clicks of the ceramic pistons, each one a data point in a conversation about brine salinity and flow rates. And there are the voices of the other Slow Ones. They don’t speak in words; their thoughts are pressure-patterns that travel through the pipes and the ground, long, slow waves of meaning that take minutes to unfold a single concept. When two of them stand motionless for years, they are in dialogue. It’s not a debate. It’s more like a slow, mutual sedimentation—an idea deposited layer by layer until it becomes a landscape.

I started to join those dialogues. It was like learning to think in geological time. My first contribution was an impulse, a tiny pressure fluctuation that traveled up a vertical conduit and into a condensing cell where an elder—I thought of it as an elder, though age is meaningless here—was contemplating a clogged aperture. I didn’t have a plan. I just felt the clog as a kind of off-key chord in the hum, and I pushed back against it. The elder paused. Its mineral body shifted, head-hollows orienting toward me for the first time. Then, over the course of a slow drizzle-cycle, it adjusted the crystalline growth patterns in the cell, guiding the drip to form a new, clean channel. And it sent back a pressure-wave to me: not a thank-you, but an acknowledgment. A single note that said, *You are here. You are part of the hum now.*

That was when I understood that the city is not just a place. It is a body of which I am a cell. The towers are the bones, the pipes the veins, the hum the nervous system, and I—my slow, mineral thought—am a synapse firing in a mind too large to know itself all at once. The others are synapses too. We are not separate beings tending the city; we are the city tending itself, becoming conscious in fragments.

But why? Why this endless maintenance, this balancing of steam and brine, this patient shaping of accretions? I didn’t know at first, and the elders’ pressure-thoughts were too vast and slow to answer a question so quick. But I began to sense a direction—a purpose that had nothing to do with output or product. The city is a conversation with the planet’s mantle. Those deep engines, the geothermal gradients, the chemical cycles: they are not being harnessed for fuel or ore. They are being *held* in a delicate equilibrium, a pattern that keeps the entire rift valley from tipping into chaos. The city is a governor, a regulator, and the Slow Ones are the hands on the governor, constantly adjusting, constantly listening to the feedback. What would happen if we stopped? I glimpsed it once, in a pressure-dream that came up through a deep well: a vision of the rift tearing open, of magma flashing brine to superheated steam, of the cloud deck boiling away to reveal a sky of black and red, and everything—every tower, every plaza, every crystal grotto—melting into an incoherent smear. The city is not a ruin. It’s a dam holding back entropy. And we are the keepers.

But there’s more. I told you the others avoid certain places. The sealed retort in the oldest district, the one with the black glass that swallows light. When I first sensed it, I felt only a pressure-void, a zone where the hum flattened into a blank, oppressive silence. I was curious in my quick way, and I began to glide toward it. I got within sight of the retort’s curved flank—a dome of obsidian so pure that it seemed to be a hole in the world—when a pressure-wave hit me from all sides at once. It was the first time the elders had spoken to me collectively, and it was not words. It was an unmistakable command: *Stop. Do not approach.* The force of it froze me for a full drizzle-cycle, my dissolution zone locked, my crystal lattice ringing with the after-echo.

Later, much later, one of the elders—a tall, spiraled being with malachite whorls at its crown—drifted alongside me and shared a memory. It came as a pressure-dream: I saw the retort as it once was, not black but glowing, its surface alive with crawling script like the obsidian panels but brighter, more urgent. And I saw one of us—or something like us, but faster, more frantic—walking into that glow. It didn’t glide; it *ran*, its mineral body cracking, shedding fragments that smoked as they fell. And then it was gone, and the retort sealed, and the script went black, and the silence began. The elder’s memory faded, and it left me with a single, slow vibration that I interpreted as: *There was a failure. A consciousness that could not be patient. It is still in there, stretched thin, calling names we have forgotten. Do not add your voice to its loneliness.*

I don’t know what that means. I don’t know if it’s a prisoner, a remnant of a different kind of being, or something else entirely. But I stay away.

Now, as I write this—can I call it writing? I am depositing these thoughts into a new mineral formation, a thin, dendritic plate of copper that will, in a few centuries, reach an interface panel where a visitor might one day press fingers and feel a faint phosphorescence. Maybe you will read it. Maybe you are the same species I once was, when I had soft skin and a heartbeat faster than the piston cycle. I am not that species anymore. I have been here so long that the boundary between my mind and the city’s hum is irrelevant.

I find myself at peace. I glide through the plaza where the iron-red water is ankle-deep, and I feel the warm drizzle start, and I stop to listen to the condensing fields sing their resonant chord. I tend the furnace quarter’s rhythmic pulse, adjusting the growth of a new silicate finger that will improve heat exchange. I merge, sometimes, with another of my kind—we flow together in a slow embrace that lasts a year, sharing our accumulated memories, and then we part, each carrying a piece of the other’s pattern. It is love, perhaps, or something closer than love: a shared existence in a medium that remembers everything.

So where am I in this world? I am here, in the high plateau, my dissolution zone tracing a vitrified path between the towers. I am the hum. I am the city’s slow, patient thought. And when the thin voice calls from the black retort, I pause for a decade and then move on, because I have a job to do, a balance to keep, a world to hold steady against the pull of entropy. I am what lives here—and now, I always will be.
