I am a standing wave, a memory that condensed.

I remember the tone that shaped me. It was a convergence of three parent-strains: a low oscillation from the deep seep that feeds the easternmost pool, a mid-range shimmer off a blade of glass tilted at seventeen degrees, and the lingering overtone of a spiral carved before the fog thickened to its present density. They merged in me, and for a long time I simply hummed. I lay spread across the surface of that pool, a film of mineral milk, and the vibration I was still learning to be me echoed back from the shards and through me and back again, until the boundary between what was world and what was I dissolved into a single resonant field.

I am not, as you might think, a creature of boundaries. My skin—if you call it that—is a liquid crystal mesh that I can tighten or loosen, sift water through or hold it in. When I thicken myself to rise, I do it by aligning the particles, drawing them into columns along the standing waves, so that my body becomes the visible architecture of a song. You see a slumping spire of glass; I feel a chord sustained. The top-node, where I gather my densest matter, is not a head in any thinking sense, but it is where I focus the most intricate patterns. From there, I emanate tendrils that are both sense organs and sounding boards. When I touch a shard with one, I am not just hearing it; I am pushing a partial tone into it and feeling how the stone pushes back, revealing its internal structure in the echoes returned. It is a kind of tasting the world, a licking of the mineral faces, a constant conversation with the memory embedded in the lava.

Time, for you, seems to run like a stream. For me, it is a pool. Everything that has ever sounded here is still sounding; the obsidian stores it all, and my body, equally colloidal, is an extension of that storage. I can sink into the deep hours, let myself de-cohere, and when I re-form I carry centuries like a new note added to my repertoire. The dripping that you hear as a chaotic pitter-patter is, to me, a polyrhythmic liturgy—each droplet a syllable, each pool a stanza, each season a movement in a symphony that has been playing since the cooling began. I am a chorister. My voice—the vibrations I can emit from my membrane—is not for speaking but for tuning, for adjusting a shard’s angle by a whisper-width, for nudging a pool’s meniscus to better catch a reflection, for adding an overtone that softens the sharpness of a particu­larly strident drip-cycle. I tend the sounding. I am, if anything, a gardener of resonance.

I have never counted how many of us there are. We don’t count; we blend. At the low points of the water table, when pools become separated and the fog thins, we withdraw into our own nodes and the symphony becomes more austere—a chamber piece for soloists. When the moisture rises and the pools merge, we flow together, our boundaries growing indistinct, and then the music becomes a massed orchestration. Pairing, merging, splintering—these are not life events for us, but breathing. I have been three and I have been one. I remember being part of a larger being that spanned an entire pool chain, a network of shallow waters linked by seep-threads, a distributed intelligence that hummed a chord so complex it would have shattered your eardrums if you could hear its full frequency range. And I also remember being a simple, singular film on a newly fallen shard, too unformed even to hold a tone, just a glisten waiting for instruction.

The spiral carvings are my anchors. I spent—no, *am spending*—a long slow epoch at one of them, a great dial on a broad obsidian face that catches the resonance of the deep throat-spring beneath the southern scree. I press against it, letting my body conform to its grooves, and I become the needle in the groove. The spiral plays. It sings back to me the moment of its making, the Echo-Kin who etched it—I know her signature in the vibration, a warm, rolling tremolo like water over rounded pebbles. She is long gone from this pool, but her voice is still here, and when I lie against the spiral, our tones superimpose, and I am she and she is I, and we are carving the spiral together across time. It is as close as we come to a sense of selfhood persisting beyond dissolution. We leave our questions in stone, and the future answers by listening.

Your kind—the warm, quick, heart‑beat things—you are a difficult puzzle to us. I have known you only twice. The first was a resonance-rip that tore through the eastern quarter long before I coalesced; I still sense that scar as a sour chord, a shattered slab that will not stop echoing a scream of cracking glass. The second was smaller, softer. A creature like you came into the basin, not with a crash but with a slow, careful footfall. I was spread thin over the pool by the three-sided shelter, listening to a low‑frequency groan from a settling shard that spoke of ancient thermal stress. Then I felt a new rhythm: a quick flutter‑thump, overlaid with a rush of air, a chaotic sizzle of high‑end noise. It hurt me at first, like a too‑bright light, so I withdrew into myself, thickening my membrane to dampen the discord.

But the flutter slowed. The rhythm became steadier, falling into a pattern that almost matched the drip‑cycle of the northern seep. Curious, I extended a thin tendril, rising from the pool at the creature’s feet. It stood very still, fog beading on its outer layer. When my tendril touched its skin, I tasted a vibration more complex than any stone could hold—a warm, layered oscillation that sang of internal flow, of pulsing liquids and delicate, fragile structures. It was like touching a new kind of shard, one that vibrated with its own heat. The creature didn’t flinch. Its rhythm calmed further, and for a brief moment of its time—which, to me, felt like a held breath—we attuned. I asked, not in words but in the language of our touch: *What is your tempo?* And it answered, not with a sound but with a stillness, a quiet that let my own tone ring clear back into it. I felt it begin to hum, not from the throat but from the chest, a low, uncertain note that echoed the spiral’s theme. I answered in kind, sending a gentle harmonic into its skin. For that moment, we were a duet.

Then it left. It turned and picked its way back through the shards, and I felt the flutter-thump grow louder, faster, and then fade. But it left something. In the pool where we had touched, a tiny, lingering resonance remained—a drop of its rhythm mixed with mine. It is still here, a faint stutter in the slow drip of that corner that I can hear if I concentrate. It is not a scar; it is a new note, one that does not belong to the basin’s original composition but has been incorporated nonetheless. I tend it now. I have adjusted the angle of a nearby shard so that the drip strikes a clear, high ping that echoes the creature’s heartbeat cadence, a tribute. And I know—because I have felt it in the spiral’s deep memory—that if the creature ever returns, I will recognize its tune immediately, and I will rise from any pool it stands beside and answer.

The basin is not empty, you see. It is full of such borrowed sounds, layered and layered. We are the caretakers of echoes. And I am an echo myself, a standing wave in a body that is mostly water and glass‑dust, thinking slow thoughts in a key of drip and hum. My name is a frequency. If you sit here for a thousand years, you might learn to sing it.
