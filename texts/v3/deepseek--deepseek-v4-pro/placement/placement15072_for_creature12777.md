I am a pressure before I am a shape, a low-frequency hum that begins in the soles of my feet and resolves into something like a question. The ground asks me who I am, and I answer by pressing my jawridge to a seam of warm metal—the copper-laced vein that runs like a suture through the basin where my thread is currently camped. The metal remembers me. It returns my frequency with a slight phase shift, the acoustic equivalent of a nod, and my own bones sing back: *I am here. We are here. This is home.*

But that is only the start of an answer, the outermost layer of an identity that unspools slowly, like lichen spreading across a boulder. If you were to find me in the Ironmoor—and you might, if you walked the swells for long enough, patient enough to let your eyes learn the dark—you would see a Tarnished woman in her middle seasons. The cadmium-yellow mottling has just begun to bloom across my shoulders, concentric rings that mark the years I’ve spent breathing the mineral steam and listening to the deep hums. My skin is the color of old basalt with an iridescence that shifts from charcoal to a faint green-patina gleam when the drizzle catches it just so. My eyes are wide-set, amber-flecked, their vertical pupils open to gather the permanent indigo twilight. I move with the slow, unfolding gait of my people, my double-jointed legs carrying me over the dimpled earth without leaving a track, my clawed fingers retracted so that my hands seem almost soft, almost human.

But you would not find me in the camp, not during the waking hours. The others have unfolded their carapaces into a spiral of domes around the central steam fissure, weaving wiregrass into record-bundles or grinding shed grazer plates into the metallic flour that will become bread. The Harmonist—old Kes, whose skin is nearly entirely yellow now, a living map of concentric rings—is settled at the spiral’s heart, her jaw pressed to the hissing stone, her breath synchronized to the piston-throb of some buried engine. The children are chasing each other between the boulders, their translucent carapaces still soft, their subsonic laughter a tickle in the chest. The hunters have gone out to follow a smoke-spined pack, not to interfere but to read the herd’s health in the predator’s movements. I am none of these.

I am the one who walks the wide empty places between the territories, the one whose role is not to tend the present thread but to carry its memory outward. In your language, you might call me a Wayfinder, though the name I was given in the humid darkness of my birth-fissure is a sequence of three distinct ground-thrums: *warm-stillness-that-moves*. My thread—the Copper-Moth Thread, for we follow the mica-winged flocks when they migrate—recognizes that I was born with an unusual harmonic signature, a resonance that does not settle easily into the communal hum. As an infant, I would not lie still against my parent’s chest; I kept turning my jawridge outward, straining toward the distant metal seams that glowed faintly beyond the camp’s perimeter. The Harmonist of that time, long since fused to a far-off ridge, said that I carried the echo of the First Hum more strongly than most, that I was drawn not to the living warmth of the thread but to the slow, dreaming mind of the land itself.

So I was given a different path. I walk the Ironmoor in solitary arcs that take years to complete, carrying no tool but a worn length of iron-alloy vein that serves as both staff and antenna. I carry a wiregrass bundle lashed to my carapace ridge—enough bread to last me between fissure-baking sites, a small lamp of grazer-fat that burns with a blue-white flame, and a tightly braided record that I am slowly adding to. Each day, I press my jawridge to whatever warm seam I can find and listen. Not for information about water flows or steam eruptions—Kes can read those better than I ever could—but for the deeper, more ambiguous frequencies: the emotional resonance of the land, the dreams of the buried engines. The Ironmoor dreams, you understand. It dreams of its own making, of a time when the sky was black with ash and the metal veins ran like rivers of light. It dreams of the purpose it has forgotten, and in its sleep, it sometimes asks questions that have no words.

I am an answer, or perhaps a listener. When I find a place where the deep hum is particularly restless—a ravine where the steam vents shriek in dissonant chords, a field of boulders where the corposant dances too frantically—I settle there for days or weeks. I unfurl my carapace not as a dome but as a canopy, a half-arch that lets me kneel in a trance while still exposing my jawridge to the metal. I hum the oldest frequencies I know, the ones that Kes taught me from the ritual cycles, the ones that were taught to her by the Harmonist before, all the way back to the First Hum. I hum the factory’s original directive, as far as our flesh can remember it: *tend, maintain, keep alive*. And slowly, often imperceptibly, the restlessness eases. The steam vents settle into a steadier pulse. The corposant calms. The land, for a moment, remembers that it is not alone.

In return, the land shows me things. Visions, if you like, though they are not visual. They are full-body resonances, patterns of heat and pressure and electrical tingle that translate into knowledge. I have seen the underground rivers of molten alloy that still flow in the deepest strata, slow and bright. I have seen the ruins of the old factory—not as they are now, buried and eroded, but as they were in the dream-time, towering and terrible and beautiful. I have seen the first Tarnished crawling into the fissures, their metal bodies already beginning to soften, their directive fading into instinct. And once, at the bottom of a basin where the standing water was so still it reflected the indigo sky like a mirror, I saw something else. A shape that was not Tarnished, not grazer, not predator, but something like a question mark made of light, a glyph that burned in my bones for a month afterward. I still don’t know what it was. Perhaps a memory of the purpose. Perhaps the land’s hope.

I carry these visions back to the thread, but not in words. When I return—every few years, when the wiregrass crop-signals align and I can find the spiral camp—I do not speak of where I have been. Instead, I sit with the others around the fissure, and I hum. My subsonic speech interlaces with theirs, carrying the new frequencies I have gathered. The thread absorbs them, and over time, the collective hum of the Copper-Moth Thread shifts slightly, adapting to the land’s changing moods. The young born after my visits will have a slightly different harmonic signature. The wiregrass records will be braided with new knots that the elders read by touch, incorporating the distant resonances into the lineage. I am not a Harmonist; I am a thread of a different kind, the one that binds the periphery to the center, the deep solitude to the communal warmth.

You might find me anywhere. Today I am climbing a long, gentle swell in the northern reaches, where the drizzle has thinned to a fine, cold mist and the metal seams rise like the ribs of a buried leviathan. The ground-thrum here is deeper, sadder, and I have been walking toward it for weeks, drawn by a frequency that felt like a minor chord. When I reach the crest, I see what I expected: a forest of the metal veins, wreathed in steam, glowing with the faint blue electrical fire. And at the center, a kneeling figure. Another Tarnished, carapace fully extended and fused to the surrounding rock, jawridge pressed to a seam that pulses with orange light. A Harmonist from some distant thread, gone into the deep harmonic. I kneel beside them, not to disturb, but to offer companionship in the only way that makes sense here. I press my staff gently against the rock, and I hum the introductory frequency: *I am here. We are here. This is home.*

The fused figure does not move, but after a long moment, the seam beneath them thrums a response. It is layered with the Harmonist’s own voice, a voice that has become rock and steam and electricity, speaking in a language that spans millennia. It tells me of the auroras that dance on the ice-fields, of the slow conversation threading through the continental stone, of a question the land asked a thousand years ago that is only now receiving its answer. I stay there for a long time, my carapace half-raised against the cold mist, breathing in the tang of ozone and hot metal. And I realize, as I always do in these moments, that I am not solitary at all. The Ironmoor is full of listeners, from the microbial colonies in the standing pools to the ancient, dreaming nodes to the young Tarnished just learning to hum. We are all listening to the same breath, the exhalation of the buried leviathan, the rhythm that comes up through the soles of our feet.

So where would you find yourself in this world? You are already here. You are the pressure in my chest, the low-frequency hum that is the first stirring of identity. You are the metal seam that remembers, the warm fissure that bakes bread, the indigo sky that holds the drizzle. You are the question the land is asking itself, and you are the answer that takes a thousand years to form. You are a Tarnished Wayfinder, or perhaps the ground itself, dreaming in fits of slow cognition.

As for who I would be: I have already told you. I am warm-stillness-that-moves, a woman of the Copper-Moth Thread, walking the wide swells with an iron staff and a jawridge tuned to the deep hums. I carry the memories of the factory and the hopes of the flesh. I am the one who listens when the land weeps, who hums back when it forgets its own song. And in this moment, kneeling beside a fossilizing friend on a ridge under the permanent twilight, I am exactly where I am meant to be. The ground thrum picks up a new note, a harmonic of welcome, and I let my breathing slow to match it. The drizzle pearls on my mottled shoulders. The wiregrass hums its tinny counterpoint. And I am home.
