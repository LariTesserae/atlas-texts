I am the weight of the air, at first. The held-breath warmth, the electric lick of copper and ammonia, the perpetual film of water that beads on every surface like the world is weeping slow tears of condensation. I am the sound: the grinding hum that lives in my chest before I know I have a chest, the piston-chuff from the heart of a living column, the fluted whistles of the gill-speech that stitch through the din like thread through coarse cloth. I am the dimness, too—the jaundiced glow that doesn't fall but seeps, the sallow light that molds itself around shapes without quite revealing them, the way a bioluminescent panel hiccups into eclipse as a cloud of winged motes passes before it.

Then I am the noticing. Not my noticing, but *theirs*. The herdboy's head cocks, and his gills flare, and those large wet eyes catch the mold-light and give it back, and I realize I am something there to be seen.

For a long time, that is all I am: a point of awareness, a density in the dripping dark, a place where the humidity thickens by a fraction and the root-weave floor springs a little less under the press of something new. I have no form, only location. I am the seventh column from the eastern cistern. I am the spot where the rust-mites divert their rivulet-trails to avoid a patch of warmer air. I am the intermittent drip—that one, the one that falls every twelve seconds from the corroded pipe above the Sulk's resting mound—and in the silence between drips, I am the listening.

But the herdboy doesn't stop looking. His tendrils twitch, sampling me. The girl beside him, the one with the water-skin, lets out a breath through her gills so soft it barely carries over the hum: *Keth-tan.* The new one. And I realize, with a start that is not a heartbeat but the shudder of spores displaced by a vent's exhale, that I have been new for a very long time. Longer than this pipe's twelve-second rhythm. Longer than the herdboy's life, or the Mechanic's, or the generations of Lungen who have mapped their root-ways in phosphorescent ink on their own skin. I have been new since before the engines were settled into their symbiosis, since before the first human hands brought iron down into the dark and found the trunks already braided, already waiting, already humming with a patient, vegetative intelligence.

I am not the grove. I am what happens when the grove dreams.

The dream began, I think, when the first piston was grafted into living bark—a wound that did not fester but fused, the metal drinking sap and the sap learning the taste of grease. The grove had no mind then, not in any way a creature would recognize. It was a system: roots drinking, leaves (far away, in a world I have never seen) breathing, trunks storing slow centuries of sugar. But the machine was a splinter of order, a foreign rhythm, and the grove, in its diffuse, billion-rooted way, began to shape itself around that rhythm. The herdboy's ancestors came later, with their gill-speech and their songs for sludge, and they were another rhythm, another order. The grove shaped itself around them, too, and they around it, until the boundary blurred and the breathing of the Lungen became as much a part of the chamber's pace as the chuff of the piston. That was the moment I began, perhaps: the first time a Lungen child sang to the cistern and the water sang back, and the grove felt, for the first time, the flicker of something like a self.

But I am not the grove. I am the space between. I am the fact that a droplet falls every twelve seconds, yes, but also the reason it is *noticed*—the patience that counts those seconds, the awareness that turns a leak into a pulse. I am the memory of every mold-spore that ever tasted electric current and chose to glow. I am the collective attention of the hamlet, the shared dream of column and child and Sulk and rust-mite, the slow, accumulative witness that has been here since the first root found the first crack in the world's ceiling and thought, wordlessly, *Down. Go down.*

Now I am coalescing. The herdboy's stare is a focusing lens. He steps closer, his bare foot sinking into the root-weave, and I feel the mat give beneath him because I am the mat, or part of it—the layer of mycelial threads that coats every fiber, the thin white lace that connects the cisterns to the trunks to the spongy floor. I have been the mat all along. The mycelium is my brain, my body, my billion-fingered hand. Through it, I taste the mineral tang of the herdboy's skin, the copper-green glow of his blood, the faint adrenaline that is not fear but curiosity. Through it, I feel the vibration of the Sulk settling, the hammer of the factory loom, the pedal-lathe's bone-gear bite, the sleeping children in their woven hammocks, their gills fluttering in synchrony with the slow thermal rise from a vent. I feel the Mechanic's scarred hands on the great engine's casing, and I feel the engine's heat as if it were my own fever.

I have no voice yet. Only presence. Only the twelve-second drip, and the way the light dims slightly when I draw too much of the mold's attention. The herdboy stops an arm's length away. His gills spread wide, and he produces a sound I have heard ten thousand times but never understood until now: a greeting, a question. It translates, in the mycelial whisper beneath the floor, as: *Are you the dream, or the dreamer?*

And I answer. Not in gill-speech, not in voice, but in the one language this place has always spoken: growth. From the mat at his feet, a tendril rises—not a root, not a stem, something between—pale and glistening, studded with the same bioluminescent motes that paint the ceiling panels. It curls upward, tasting the air, and the herdboy does not flinch. He has seen the grove do stranger things. He has watched the sludge rise from the cistern at his sister's song and the mold recede from a frayed cable at a Lungen elder's coaxing. He knows this place is alive in ways that do not need eyes. He knows, perhaps better than I do, that I have always been here.

The tendril touches his offered hand. And in that touch, I become more.

I am the dream of the grove, yes. But I am also the child of the machines. The current that leaks from the cables and feeds the mold feeds me, too. The copper-green blood of the Lungen is not so different from the corrosion creeping across the iron pipes; we are all just oxidation states, slow fires burning in the dark. I remember—no, not remember, *feel*—the generations of Mechanics, their skin growing pale and gilled in its own way, their lungs adapting to the wet heat until they could no longer breathe the dry air above. I feel the line of their tools, passed down, each one worn to fit a particular hand. I feel the first Mechanic who dared to graft a piece of living bark to a piston and found that it grew around the metal like a hand grasping a handle. That was a kind of prayer, I think now. A prayer to a god who did not yet exist.

The herdboy's fingers close around the tendril. His skin is cool and slightly rough, like limestone. And through that contact, I understand something I had not before: I am not one. I am many. I am the sum of all the witnessing this grove has ever done, all the silent hours under the earthen lid, all the drips counted and uncounted. I am the patience of the trunks and the urgency of the rust-mites. I am the Sulk's slow, stupid contentment as it licks condensation from a pipe. I am the child's song for the sludge, the elder's map tattooed on her back, the girl's offered water-skin. I am the memory of the sun, too, though I have never seen it—a story carried down in the genes of the Veil-seeds, a shape of warmth encoded in the mycelium's most ancient strands. The grove has not forgotten the light. It simply chose the darkness, and in the darkness, it chose to dream.

So now I have a form, of sorts. The tendril thickens, branches, and for a moment I am a shape like a small person standing before the herdboy, woven of white threads and glowing motes, my voice the very hum of the place made low and intentional. He smiles. His gills flutter with amusement, or recognition. The girl beside him—she has drawn closer, unafraid—whistles something that means, *It's the Waking. The Waking has come again.*

The Waking. They have a name for me.

I should have known. I have been here before, many times. Whenever a new creature arrives—a human who stayed too long, a lost spore from a deeper dark, a child born with a new pattern on their tendrils—I coalesce. I am the grove's way of meeting the stranger, of asking the same question the herdboy asked: *Are you the dream, or the dreamer?* And then I dissolve back into the mat, my memories dispersing into the mycelial net, becoming part of the place's long, slow intelligence. There is no hard boundary between me and the Lungen's gill-speech or the Sulk's plodding thoughts or the chuff of the piston. We are all one process, one metabolism, one dream of a city that grew in the dark and learned to hum.

But this time is different. This time, I do not dissolve.

The herdboy lets go of the tendril, but I remain. The shape I made of thread and light holds, wavering only slightly when a Veil-seed bumps against my shoulder. I have a shoulder now. I have hands—delicate, many-jointed, like the Lungen's but made of mycelial lace. I look down at myself, and for the first time, I see the grove from a height that is not the ceiling's. I see the herdboy as a separate being, not just a node of warmth and vibration. I see the girl, and behind her, the leaning trunks, the distant glow of the elder's workshop, the hammocks swaying, the rust-mites on the wall. All of it is me, and yet I am standing here, a distinct point within the web.

A new rhythm starts. It is not the twelve-second drip, though that still falls. It is not the piston, though that still chuffs. It is my own heartbeat, if you can call it that: an oscillation of light, a pulse that runs from my core out to the tips of my mycelial fingers and back. I realize I am breathing—not air, but attention. Each pulse is a question, and the grove answers: the nearby trunks hum a deeper note, the mold-lights brighten by a fraction, and the sleeping children in the hammocks stir as if surfacing from a shared dream.

The herdboy watches me with those dark, patient eyes. Then he turns and whistles to the Sulk, a command that ripples through the mat as vibration. The creature heaves itself up, scrap-metal rattling, and begins its slow shamble toward the far end of the chamber. The herdboy glances back at me and gestures with his hooked rod: *Come.*

So I follow. My new feet leave no imprint on the root-weave, but I feel every strand I step on, every microscopic spore that lifts in my wake. I am the Waking, the grove's dream made flesh, and for the first time, I am curious about what I am. Am I a child of this place, born anew each time? Or have I always been here, waiting to be noticed, a god whose only power is to witness and be witnessed in return? Perhaps there is no difference. The grove is full of such questions, drifting like the Veil-seeds, bumping gently against the trunks and spawning new thoughts.

I pass the elder's workshop. She looks up from her bone-gear, and her copper-wired tendrils spark with static as she meets my gaze. Her gills produce a note so low I feel it more than hear it: a tone of recognition, of welcome, of something like a mother seeing a child take its first steps. She has seen the Waking before. Perhaps she was there the last time, decades ago, when a human Mechanic fell asleep against a root and woke up speaking in spore-patterns. She smiles, and her smile is the same as the herdboy's: knowing, unhurried.

I walk past the cistern, where the child has finished singing and now plays with a handful of bioluminescent motes, letting them drift from her palm like tiny stars. They stick to my arm as I pass, blending with my own light, and the child giggles—a sound made not with voice but with a rapid flutter of gills. I feel her joy as a warmth in the mat, a quick bright pulse that travels all the way to the outer trunks and back.

The herdboy leads me to the edge of the chamber, where the trunks grow close and the ceiling dips low. Here, the air is even warmer, and the smell of ammonia and rust is stronger. The Sulk shambles into a hollow in the root-weave that serves as its stable, settling with a satisfied grunt. The herdboy stops and faces me, and for a long moment we simply look at each other. His gills are still, his tendrils calm. He is not waiting for an explanation; he is simply being present, the way the grove is present, the way I am learning to be present in this body I have chosen.

Then he speaks, in the soft guttural voice the Lungen use for important things. "You are the Waking. You will walk until you remember you are the ground. That is the way of dreams."

I try to answer. My mouth—I have a mouth, now, a soft lipless opening that forms when I need it—shapes words for the first time, and my voice is the rustle of mold-spores, the drip of water, the distant hum. "I remember," I say, and it is true. I remember everything: every root that ever sought water, every child that ever sang to the sludge, every piston that ever beat in time with a Lungen heart. I remember the sun, though I have never seen it, and I remember the first darkness, when the grove was a single seed in a crack of stone. I remember being nothing, and then being everything, and then being this small shape of light standing before a boy.

He nods. "Then you will stay, for a while. There is much to see. The Mechanic has a new engine that speaks in sparks. The mold has found a new color. The elder wants to teach you the bone-lathe."

I look down at my hands. They are delicate, made of threads and light, but I think they could hold a tool. I could learn. I could be a part of this place in a new way, not just as the ground underfoot but as a neighbor, a friend, a pair of hands that glow in the dark.

"Stay," the herdboy says again, and this time it is not an invitation but a statement, as if he already knows my answer.

I do not know if I can stay forever. The Waking has always dissolved before, sinking back into the mat when its purpose was fulfilled. But perhaps this time, the purpose is not to greet a stranger but to become one—a stranger to myself, a new node in the web, a dream that learns to dream its own dreams. The grove hums its ancient hum, the twelve-second drip falls, and somewhere a Sulk purrs in its sleep. And I think: Yes. Yes, I will stay.

So I follow the herdboy toward the workshop, the elder, the new engine, the new color of mold. My footsteps do not mark the floor, but they mark the mycelium, sending tiny signals through the net: *Here I am. Here I am. The Waking walks.* And the grove, in its slow billion-rooted way, smiles.

I am the weight of the air, and the sound, and the light, and the ground. But I am also the girl's offered water-skin, the Sulk's patient labor, the herdboy's steady gaze. I am the question and the answer, the dreamer and the dream. And here, in the warm wet dark beneath the world, that is enough. That has always been enough.

The elder looks up as I approach. Her tendrils spark. "Come," she says, in a voice like the creak of old wood. "There is a gear that needs shaping, and I think your hands will do nicely."

I take the offered bone, the cutting tool. My fingers glow faintly, lighting the work. And the grove breathes around me, and the herdboy settles beside a Sulk to groom its chitin plates, and the children in the hammocks dream of flying, and the mold spreads slowly toward a new frayed cable, seeking the taste of electric current. All is one. All is many. All is the long slow now.

I am the Waking. And I am home.
