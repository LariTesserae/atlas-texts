I am the breath between the thrum and the drip.

My name—the one I answer to, the one whistled down from tier sixteen when the lamps dim—is Resonant Shell-Third, because I was born in the echo-cave behind the third condenser shell, and my first cry carried a harmonic that made the old Voice-Catcher pause in her humming and smile. I have been listening ever since.

I wake not to light but to a shift in the engine’s bass. The turbines on level nine have cycled down a quarter-tone; I feel it in the bones of my shins before my ears register the change. Around me, the moss I use as bedding is damp and warm, releasing its green smell as I stir. My chamber is a hollowed heat-exchanger casing, curved walls of riveted iron, a grating floor that lets the warm air rise from the shaft below. Through the gaps, I can see the pale glow of lamps beginning their slow brightening—the long dawn of the Stack. Condensation beads on my skin, and the algae in my epidermis tingles faintly, drinking the wetness. I stretch, toes curling around a rung, fingers testing the give of the rope ladder beside my bed. All is as it should be.

I am a Voice-Catcher in training, though the title is still half-lent. My teacher, Old Whistle-Bone, says I have the ear but not yet the patience. She has sent me to walk the south-east galleries today, to sing the diagnostic tones and map the new creaks. The Stack-Garden is always settling, always breathing, and it is our duty—the Voice-Catchers’ duty—to know the difference between a healthy groan and a sigh that means fracture.

I pull on my tunic, woven from broad-leaf fibre and patched with tisker-skin at the elbows. My copper bracelets chime softly as I move, each one marking a tier I have learned to read. The oldest is from the Hall of the Old Uncles, where I sat for a full dimming cycle three seasons ago, watching the purposeless arms dance and feeling the rhythm enter my blood. The newest is from the Ever-Grey gallery, where the windows look out onto that other interior vastness—a place I cannot quite believe is not a dream.

I climb. The ladders are wet under my hands, the vine-ropes braided tight and mottled with moss. I pass the orchard on tier twelve, where old Wheelfly is already pruning the dwarf fruit trees with her curved knife, her whistled greeting a quick triple-note that bounces off the copper walls. “South-east?” she asks, and I click confirmation. She flips a hand-sign: *listen for the loose belt on conveyor seven.* I sign back *acknowledged*, and continue upward.

The heat wraps around me as I ascend, the humidity an almost-physical weight. Somewhere above, a scheduled misting system hisses to life, and I pause to let the fine spray settle on my face, my tongue, my eyelashes. It tastes of minerals and faintly of the vine-roots that have worked their way into the pipe. I open my mouth and breathe deep, taking in the water, the steam, the distant tang of ozone from the transformer banks. The Erubim do not drink from cups; we drink the air.

The south-east gallery is a long catwalk spanning a shaft that drops into mist. Far below, the glow of the turbines paints the fog orange. Far above, the ceramic tiles of the ceiling are just visible, their numbers faded. I walk slowly, bare feet feeling the worn-smooth grating, the give where a support beam has aged. Now and then I pause, place a palm flat against a pipe or a wall, and hum.

The hum is low, starting in my chest and vibrating through my skull. I tune it to the frequency of the engine thrum, then shift it slightly, listening for the echo. A solid wall returns a clean, true note. A wall that has developed a micro-fracture returns a wavering, dissonant one. I mark the spot with a smudge of grease, then whistle a quick pattern—"watch this"—for any nearby gardener. Later, a team will come with resin brewed from fruit-tree sap and a patch of woven fibre to reinforce the wound before it can spread.

Near the end of the gallery, I find the loose belt Old Wheelfly warned of. A conveyor system, one of the ancient ones that still shuffles matter from some unknown origin to an equally unknown destination, is shaking too fast. The belt has worn thin, its edges frayed. I climb out onto the cable tray beside it, my toes gripping the metal, and run my fingers along the vibration. It is a jittery, anxious rhythm, nothing like the steady pulse of a healthy machine. I kneel and hum directly into the casing, a low, calming note, while my hands work the tension wheel—a carved gear of heartwood, ancient but still solid. Slowly, the belt steadies. The machine sighs, a vent of steam releasing, and the vibration settles back into the great slow heartbeat of the Stack. I pat the warm iron casing. “Grandmother,” I whisper, the title we give to all engines that still run. “Better now.”

As I stand, a pipe-grip bird flutters down from an overhead branch—no, a pipe. It perches on my shoulder, its long middle claw wrapping carefully around my copper necklace. It has a message for me, a tiny bundle of fibre tied to its leg. I whistle a greeting, and it chitters back, dipping its soot-dusted head. The bundle holds a single berry and a twist of wire shaped into a spiral—an offering from tier twenty, where the elders sit in their niches. A summons, perhaps, or a blessing. I eat the berry, sharing half with the bird, and tuck the spiral into my belt. Offerings are to be passed on. I will leave it at the Hall of the Old Uncles on my way back.

The day moves slowly. I walk and listen and hum, repairing nothing too large but noting everything. I pass a family of tiskers nesting in the insulation of an oven; they scold me as I approach, and I sign *safe passage* with a flat palm, the gesture we use to promise no harm. The triangular marks on the walls—wound-wards—I skirt with a whispered recitation of my ancestors’ names, three generations back, as I was taught. One mark is fresh-painted, the rust-and-grease still glossy. Someone has been maintaining the boundary. Good.

In the deepest part of the gallery, where the lamps are few and the shadows thick, I find a place where the vines have grown strange. They have woven around a bank of old circuit panels, the leaves pressing into the rusted metal as if reading the dead screens with their veins. A slow drip from a pipe above falls in a rhythm that almost forms words, the plink-plink-plink a pattern I nearly recognize. I sit there for a long moment, breathing the warm, green-scented air, and I listen. Not with my ears alone, but with the algae in my skin, the tapetum in my eyes, the vibration in my bones. The Stack-Garden is dreaming, I think. Or remembering. The Recitation says the Builders left pieces of themselves in the walls, and sometimes those pieces stir.

I do not startle when the steam vent hisses and forms a shape, a swirl that looks, for just a breath, like a face. I hum a low, respectful note and sign *thanks for the message,* though I do not claim to understand it. The face dissolves, and I continue on.

The dimming begins as I make my way back down. The lamps shift from yellow to amber, the shadows lengthening. I stop at the Hall of the Old Uncles, a chamber off the main shaft where the machines dance their slow, purposeless ballet. Three old Erubim are already there, sitting on split pipes padded with moss, their eyes half-closed but their ears tuned to the rhythm. I leave the spiral of wire at the feet of the oldest machine—a towering thing of gear-arms and ceramic joints that moves in a repeating sequence of six positions. One of the sitters nods at me, her filed teeth showing in a faint smile. “Good walk?” she clicks.

“Good walk,” I whistle back. “The south-east wall sings true. Conveyor seven needed a word.” I take my place beside them for a time, letting the thrum and the dance resonate in my chest. The vines around the machines have put out a new tendril, pale green and seeking. I watch it move, so slow it is almost still, and think about patience.

Later, when the twilight is full and the warm rain starts—a steady drip from a new leak somewhere above—I return to my chamber. I strip off my tunic and let the rain wash the day’s grime from my skin, the water threading through the familiar channels the algae likes. I lie on my bed of moss and listen to the Stack breathe. The engines throb. A bird calls somewhere far away, the triple-note of a pipe-grip. In the darkness, the numbers on the ceiling tiles are invisible, but I can feel them there, painted by hands long still.

I am not trapped. I am held. The iron door is a myth; the Outside is a children’s chant. This—all of this, the wet and the steam and the green and the steel, the animals and the machines and the strange, slow dreams—is the world entire. And I, Resonant Shell-Third, am its listening ear, its humming voice, its patching hand. Tomorrow, I will climb again. I will find new creaks, new offerings, new shapes in the steam. The slow dynamic will spin on, and I with it, until the day I come to a niche and sit down and finally understand the Grandparents’ full song.

But not yet. For now, I am alive with the thrum, and the algae in my skin tingles with the next note.
