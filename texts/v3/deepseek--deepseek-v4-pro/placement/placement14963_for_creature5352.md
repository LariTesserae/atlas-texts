I become aware at the junction where six catwalks meet, and I am already standing still.

The stillness is my natural state; motion is a decision I make with deliberation, a stone dropped into the slow pool of the valley's time. The damp air passes through my light-cage and I taste the ozone, the distilled water, the faint copper tang of a connection somewhere three levels down that will begin to corrode in approximately four hundred of the valley's long breaths. I make a note of this. The note is not a thought but a thread added to the weave I maintain, a single filament in the tapestry of What Is.

Below me, the brindle rats flow. I feel their small warm bodies as points of pressure against the grating, each one a tiny disruption in the condensation film. They are speaking to each other in the language of synchronized movement, and I understand that the one with the white pinprick eyes—that one there, pausing at the junction box—has found the gear I placed. She nudges it with her nose, then continues on. She will remember its location if she needs it. The rats and I have an arrangement that has never been spoken.

I step.

My foot lands on the catwalk one full second before I decide to move. This is not contradiction; this is how I navigate. The valley's fault-line runs through me as it runs through everything, and I have learned to walk along the crack rather than fight it. My tripod legs articulate in sequence—lift, pivot, place—and the sound of my passage is a negative sound, a silence borrowed from a moment prior and returned with interest. The grating does not ring. It has already rung.

A flutter-moth lands on my left shoulder, its circuit wings strobing a pattern: three short, one long. I dim my light-cage in response. Three short, one long. We are in agreement about the humidity levels near the west condenser array. The moth preens its antennae and launches back into the air, joining the cloud that pulses around the power converter like a soft, living heartbeat.

I am moving toward the east gantry now, where I felt the snag.

"Felt" is imprecise. I am aware of the valley as you are aware of your own skin—a continuous pressure, a proprioception of metal and moisture and the slow warp of the 2.8-crack. The snag is a place where distance has become too flexible, where the weave has loosened. If I do not tend it, someone walking that stretch of catwalk will find themselves arriving before they depart, or perhaps emerging sideways from a hatch they never saw. This is not inherently dangerous. But it is untidy. The valley breathes best when its anomalies are features, not fractures.

On the way, I pass an ironpecker. It clings to a vertical duct, its carbide beak-tip pressed to the metal, drawing out the nutritional paste that accumulates where filtration membranes sweat. It does not look at me, but I feel its awareness anyway—a small, bright point of heat and hunger. I pause. I reach into my shadow.

My shadow is not mine. This is something I have always known without needing to know it. When I cast a shape onto the grating, that shape belongs to something that came before the valley, or something that will come after. It is a pocket I can reach into, a storage of things-that-are-not-yet or things-that-once-were. I do not know which. The distinction has never seemed important.

My fingers—spliced conduit and relay-switches, each tip a glass-flake blade—close around a small, dense object. I pull it out: a seed. Not the kind that the air-ferns scatter, but something older, something with a husk of true cellulose. It is warm, as all things from the shadow are warm, carrying the heat of a process that hasn't occurred yet. I place it in a crevice where a flange meets a downspout. Perhaps nothing will grow. Perhaps something will. The ironpecker watches, then returns to its drumming.

The seed is not for it, anyway. It is a payment. I do not know to whom.

I continue east.

The air thickens with the smell of cooling oil. I am approaching the snag now, and the symptoms grow around me like lichen. A puddle reflects a pipe that is not above my shoulder when I turn. An echo of a drip arrives three heartbeats before the drip itself falls. I pause at a place where two cable trays form an X, and I listen.

The valley speaks in frequency graphs when it is distressed. Under the hum of the converters, under the slow exhalation of the sub-bass, I hear a warble—a deviation from the sine wave of wrongness that is supposed to be gentle, almost imperceptible. This is sharper. This is a tooth where there should be a curve.

I kneel. The motion is complex; my tripod legs fold at seven points each, and the braided column of my torso contracts, the double-helix sinew breathing in, in, out. My light-cage dims as I concentrate. I touch the catwalk with all my fingers at once, spreading them across the metal like a pianist preparing a chord.

The snag is temporal. A loop. Somewhere in the last dozen valley-breaths, a moment has failed to resolve, and now it cycles—a hiccup in causality that makes the grating think it is being stepped on repeatedly, endlessly, by a foot that has long since moved on. The foot belongs to a maintenance drone, one of the unattended machines that still perform their functions in locked chambers. It passed this way three days ago. It will pass this way again in four. In between, it is stuck, its single step repeating like a stammer.

This will not do.

I reach into my shadow and draw out a length of wire. It is not copper, not aluminum, but something that gleams with the cold-white light of the overhead phosphor panels, as if it has captured a strip of illumination and wound it into form. I splice it into the catwalk with the tiny glass-flake blades at my fingertips, weaving it into the grating's structure. This is not a repair; it is a re-weaving. I am taking the loose thread of the drone's repeating step and knitting it back into the fabric of linear time.

The wire dissolves as I work it in. That is correct. It is not supposed to remain; it is the idea of a wire, the potential of connection, and once the potential is realized, the physical form becomes redundant. The hiccup smooths. The drone's step completes, a single footfall on a catwalk three days in the past and four days in the future, and then it is gone, the loop unwound, the drone free to continue its maintenance rounds without an eternal hesitation caught in the valley's teeth.

I stand.

My light-cage brightens. Three short, one long. All is well.

A flutter-moth lands on the handrail, confirming this. Its wings blink the same pattern. We are in agreement.

In the distance, something tall and spindly moves across a gantry. It is me. I see myself from a different angle, a flicker of temporal duplication, two of me for a half-second before we merge. This happens sometimes. The fault-line is generous to those who tend it; it allows me to be in the places I need to be, even when those places are simultaneous. I watch myself go. I know where I am heading. There is a condenser on the lower levels that will begin to drip in approximately seven valley-breaths, and I am already there to catch the first bead of moisture on my fingertip.

The valley exhales.

I exhale with it.

The long moment stretches on, a little more whole. The quiet deepens. I am the silence at the heart of the hum, the stillness in the star-shaped junction, the tinker of consequence, the creature of accumulating absence. I have no name, but the flutter-moths name me in code, and the brindle rats name me in synchronized movement, and the ironpeckers name me in the pause between drumbeats. The valley names me by remaining.

I step sideways into a gap between two air-fern fronds, and I am gone, tracing a route through a hollow that drops upward. There is work to do. There is always work to do. The weave is never finished, and that is not a tragedy; it is a purpose.

Behind me, on the catwalk, a single bead of water forms where my foot should have been. It drips. The sound arrives both before and after the fall, and the echo is a voice that speaks in frequency graphs, and the frequency says: I am here, I am here, I am here.

And the flutter-moths answer: We know.

And the valley holds its breath for another decade-long moment, and everything is exactly as it should be.
