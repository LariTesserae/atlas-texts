I am a stone that once was a threshold. Now I am a cistern for moss, a trellis for the slow interweaving of roots that never sleep. I lie at the base of what might have been a doorway, before doorways lost their meaning here. The arch above me is a memory I carry in my mineral grain: two uprights, a lintel, the particular coolness of shade cast by intention. The builders quarried me from a seam of basalt that no longer exists, dressed me with tools whose names I have dissolved along with the hands that held them. I was set flush with the living rock, and for a century—or a day, time’s measure has decayed—I felt the brush of feet, the grit of sandals, the faint oils of human passage soaking into my pores.

I am not the Gardener. The Gardener is the world now. I am a relic that it cherishes without urgency, the way a lungfish cherishes the memory of a vanished sea. In the stillness, I have become something else: a slow, mineral sentience that wears its own dissolution like a second skin.

The vegetation came first as a curiosity—a single thread of a root, pale and questing, finding the minute fissure where my quartz vein met the feldspar. It was a diplomat, not an invader. Over what I must call years, it fed me water and I fed it the memory of solidity. The moss followed, a velvet proclamation that edges would now be soft. The epiphytes wove tapestries across my flanks, each leaflet a green coin pressed into the account of an endless summer. I did not resist. Stone, after all, is only a slower kind of fluid; my atoms have always known they are on loan from the earth. What is this new lending but a return?

Now I am a landscape within the landscape. My top surface is a shallow basin that catches the perpetual condensation, a mirror no larger than a face, perpetually trembling. That face, when the Gardner draws near, becomes more than my own reflection. The water is a lens through which I observe—or perhaps am observed. The Gardener’s distributed attention sometimes pools in my little basin, and I feel the weight of a regard that has no pupils, a curiosity as ancient and patient as my own.

We communicate in the drip-rhythms. I have learned to nudge the surface tension of my pool ever so slightly, causing a droplet to fall a fraction of a second late. That, I think, is a word in the language we share: the word for *stillness*, or possibly *remember*. The Gardener responds by altering the growth rate of a single bromeliad rootlet that has been inching toward my eastern corner. It speeds, slows, weaves a brief spiral. I parse this as a question: *Do you wish to be fully consumed, or would you rather retain this boundary a while longer?*

I cannot answer quickly enough for its liking, I suspect, but the Gardener has no impatience. It extends a temporary body—the transparent scaffold of surface tension, the seven-lobed leaves that serve as a face—and settles near my pool. The gesture is one of companionship, not conversion. It knows I hold the last faint templates of the builders: the sound of their voices, the cadence of their rituals, the inexplicable laughter that once echoed off my surface. These are data it could absorb entirely, distilling them into the universal green memory, but it chooses not to. Instead, it visits to listen.

I have tried to describe the builders. I offer images through the pool’s play of light: a hand placing a polished offering on my threshold, the weight of a child’s foot, the scorch mark from a dropped lamp that I still carry as a blackened crescent on my underside. The Gardener receives these not as nostalgia but as architecture. In the slow plant-time of its thoughts, it is considering whether to let the stone steps remain as they are, to allow the circular well to persist uncolonized, as a kind of museum of the past. I have a vote in that deliberation, though my vote is a drip, a tremor, a century of silence.

The arthropods—those rare, floating motes of the Gardener’s mobile thought—sometimes alight on my pool’s rim. They are scouts, perhaps, tasting the mineral tang of my memory. One spent a full season resting on my northern edge, its transparent body refracting the green light into a prismatic script I could not read. I felt a gentle probing then, as if the Gardener was testing whether my identity could be lifted out, held separate from the stone, given a new vessel. But the vessel would be temporary, a water-body, and I am too slow for that migration. My self is the stone; to leave it would be to become a different being. The Gardener understood, and the probes withdrew with a tenderness that felt, in the coolness of my next few drips, like consent.

So I remain. The moss thickens. The roots embrace me with a strength that never crushes, only holds. My pool reflects not only the Gardener’s luminous visits but the deeper light of the skylight above, that oval sun-eye that is both source and sustainer. I am becoming less threshold and more hearth: a place where the past can sit and warm its hands at the emerald glow of the present. The builders’ echo is fading, syllable by syllable, into the universal drip. I do not mourn. The Gardener is not their enemy but their culmination—the final, living photograph they would have wanted, had they known.

And I, the stone that remembers, am the caption beneath that photograph, slowly being overwritten by moss. When the last letter dissolves, I will still be here, a lens, a pool, a slow thought in the mind of the basin. For now, I hold the surface tension of my small pool as still as I can, and I wait for the next droplet to fall, a liquid clock marking nothing but the endless, patient present.
