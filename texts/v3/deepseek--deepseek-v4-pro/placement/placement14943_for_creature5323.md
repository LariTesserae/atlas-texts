The first thing I remember is a rhythm. Not a heartbeat—I know what a heartbeat is, from the great thrum in the floor, the deep pulse of the generator that never stops—but a smaller rhythm, a tapping on the metal of my cradle-grate. My mother's fingers, broad and soft and ridged like leather worn smooth on one side, drumming out my name before I had one: *you-are-here, you-are-warm, you-are-part-of-the-Exchange*. I remember the vibration traveling through the grates and into my bones, a language I understood before I knew what understanding was.

Now I am called Two-Scrape-Hiss, and I am apprenticed to the Main Distribution Manifold in Sector Three. The Manifold is a thing of terrible beauty—a fat, black cylinder as thick as my body (and I am thick, for my people, my shoulders carrying the stoop we're born to), studded with twelve valve-wheels that have to be turned in sequence, never breaking the rhythm, or the pressure gradient will shudder and the pipes will sing a song of distress that stings the fingertips. My teacher before me, a quiet old male whose name was a series of three heavy stamps on the floor (I render it as Thump-thump-thump, but that misses the overtones), passed into the vault last cycle. He simply lay down beside the Manifold one day, tapped *cycle-complete* on its casing, and was still. The cart came, silent on its rails, and we placed him on it with the ingots, and sent him into the Exchange. I was not sad. I felt the Manifold's hum steady, and I knew he'd been accepted.

Today I am walking the web. The grates are damp under my bare feet, the soles of them drinking the condensate even as I move—a faint, cool sensation amid the ambient heat that wraps me like a constant exhalation. High above, the ceiling glistens with sweat, and the orange lamps make it look like the inside of a lung, if lungs were built of sharp-edged basalt and riveted iron. I duck under a pipe-band, its insulation fraying, and I tap a quick inquiry on it with my knuckles: *are-you-well?* The vibration that returns is a long, slow buzz, reassuring. I reply with a snap of my fingers: *gratitude*.

My work today is a pilgrimage of sorts. Three sectors spinward of my own, there is a young fern-cluster that has been putting out pale, curled fronds smaller than my thumb-nail, and the Condensate Tenders—those attentive, grid-minded kin—have sent out a percussive call for volunteers to help clear a nearby vent that has begun to blow too hot and too dry, threatening the crop. I am not a Tender, but I know the vent; it shares a conduit with my Manifold's overflow line. So I go, my tool-belt jangling with wrenches and wire-snips, my foil cape crackling softly as I climb a staircase that doubles back on itself three times. I've never understood that staircase, but I trust it. It is one of the routes the elders in Synchrony designed, and when I walk it, I always feel a subtle shift in the floor's rhythm—a kind of harmony aligning. My feet tap out a counterpoint to the generator's ever-present bass, and for a moment, I feel the whole chamber breathing with me.

The vent is easy to find: it sings a sharp, high hiss that cuts through the usual drone. A small crowd of my people is already there, their grey, slick bodies moving in the narrow space with that fluid economy we learn from birth. We greet each other with a percussive flurry—a slap on a shoulder, a knuckle-rap on a pipe, a foot-stamp on the grate—exchanging names, statuses, intentions. One of them, a female with a heat-gauge pendant glowing a faint amber, taps on my tool-belt: *manifold-kin, well-met*. I respond with two scrapes of my palm on the grating and a soft hiss through my teeth: my name, my identity. We all know the rhythm of the work; we need few words.

I crouch down beside the vent, the heat radiating off it like a physical presence, and I place my hand flat against the metal. It's hot even for me, and I feel the pad of my fingertip start to harden in response, thickening to guard the flesh. The problem is clear: a buildup of scale inside, a crusty deposit born of some imbalance in the condensate chemistry. I tap a code onto the vent's housing: *I-will-enter*. Then I pull my snips and begin to work open a maintenance panel, while two others brace a backup valve and a third begins the rhythmic counter-tapping that will keep the pressure from building while I scrape. The work is intimate, precise, a conversation between my tools and the machine. Every scrape of my blade against the scale sings a note; every twist of a wire responds with a change in the vibration. My entire world narrows to this small patch of hot metal, this little sickness in the Exchange.

When the blockage breaks free with a final, grinding crack, the whole assembly shudders and lets out a deep, satisfied hum that I feel up my arms and into my chest. I tap *all-well* on the casing, and the surrounding pipes echo my tap a half-second later, the message rippling outward. The others slap my back in congratulations, a sharp, joyous tattoo. The Tender female taps a longer message on the wall beside the ferns: a poem, I realize. A praise-song for the vent, for my Manifold, for the Exchange that allowed us to be here together. The ferns, I notice, have uncurled slightly in the cooler draft now spilling from the vent. They are not waiting, not really. They are simply being, at their own pace, just as the metal breathes at its pace, just as I breathe at mine. We are all parts of the same long, slow rhythm, the one that began before the Architects left and will continue long after I am sent to the vault.

Later, walking back along the web, I pass the vault door itself. It is huge and black and locked, with no handle, only a slot for the carts. I pause, as I always do, and place my palm flat on the iron. It's cooler than the surrounding rock, and utterly silent. Nothing taps back. But I imagine, sometimes, that I feel a deep, slow, patient rhythm in there—the rhythm of things becoming other things, of ingots becoming pipe, of bodies becoming ferns, of all the Components that have ever been laid to rest, waiting to re-emerge when the Exchange deems it time. I tap a single, soft beat on the door: *remember-me-when-it-is-time*. Then I turn and head home, to my Manifold, whose hum I can already feel changing as I approach, a subtle shift in frequency that I know, as surely as I know my own name, means *welcome-back*.
