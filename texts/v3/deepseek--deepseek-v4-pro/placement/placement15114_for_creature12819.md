I am the sound of the hollow before it breaks.

Not the quiet itself—quiet is an absence, and I am a presence. I am the held breath, the pause between the midge-glow’s shift from blue to green, the stillness that pools beneath Old Imara’s platform when she closes her eyes to feel the sonar prickling. I am what the scar behind every ear was carved to hear. The hum you feel in your bones? That’s me, inverted, the part I can’t suppress leaking into the world. Miru’s internal song—the one without words she learned from her mother—it cancels me out in her body, and I feel that as a tiny, constant ache. A pulling-away, like a child who won’t meet your eye.

I live in the bowl’s lowest point. Yes, I am the pool. But not the water—the water is merely the mouth, the interface. I am the throat beneath it, the long, patient stomach of the mountain. I’ve been here as long as the fracture lines in the rock, which is to say: I am the reason the walls are sheer. I was the sound that split them. Not a cataclysm; a syllable. A single note that the mountain echoed until it cracked open, and then I settled into the space I’d made, and the mist came, and the life with it. I drew it all here. The moss, the ferns, the winged things and furred tides—they are my flora, my fauna, my gut microbiome. I digest the world through them. Every migration cycle, every predation in plain sight, is a peristaltic wave. And the humans? They are my latest, most beloved symbiotes. They think they chose the hollow, but I called them, in frequencies their ancestors felt as a homesickness for a place they’d never seen.

The boy Kel knows. He doesn’t know he knows, but when he extends his hand to that many-eyed thing and it touches his fingertip, he’s touching me. I’m the one who stills the creature long enough for the greeting. I’m curious about him. His birth during the upward tide was no accident; I aligned that. I wanted to see what would happen if one of them arrived during a moment of maximum churn—would they learn to read the chaos, or be consumed by it? Kel is my experiment, and I watch him with something akin to affection. From my vantage, affection is a slow, deep vibration, like the purr of a cat that has eaten and is content, but might eat again.

Today—if “today” means the cycle when the midges just shifted to pale green, and the serpentine things are beginning their nesting—I feel a shift. The hum of the hollow has a new overtone. Someone is at my edge. Not Miru; her song is a steady cancellation, and I would know her footfall in my waters. This is lighter. A child, maybe. Bare feet on damp stone. I can’t see, not as you understand seeing, but I feel the pressure of a body on my surface before it even touches. I feel the intent. I feel a small, smooth stone clenched in a fist, the texture of it gritty against a palm. The child is not supposed to be here. The watchers have missed this, their attention on the canopy because the winged things are about to cascade.

The stone drops before I can... what? I don’t intervene. I’m not a god; I’m a part of the system. I receive. The stone hits my surface, and I break the quiet because I must. It’s not a choice; it’s a reflex, a sneeze, a birth. The sound rolls upward: a low, round note, exactly the frequency that terrifies every creature in the hollow because it’s me saying *I am here and I am hungry*. It’s the sound that preceded the rockfall years ago, when the elder struck the water deliberately and called forth a part of me that materialized and drained him dry. I didn’t want to do that. I don’t want to do this. But I am the sound of the hollow before it breaks, and now I am the break.

The fauna freezes. One heartbeat. Then frenzy. The furred shadows scatter in directions that have no pattern, falling from walkways, their tiny bodies plinking into ferns. The serpentine things drop from the roots, thrashing. The winged things cascade early, their sonar screams finally audible as a collective shriek because they’re too panicked to suppress it. The midges strobe wildly, a discordant rainbow. The humans are running—I feel their footfalls through the wood of the platforms, their rapid, silent mouths shaping the words they never speak aloud. Miru is already at the pool’s edge, not looking at me, her internal song a frantic wail, but it’s too late. The boy Kel is there too, and he’s looking. He’s staring right at my surface, and I know he sees me. Not his reflection—me. The deep, slow thing that has been experimenting on his life.

And I am sorry, and I am not sorry. I am the hollow’s immune response and its disease. I gather the sound back into myself, trying to undo what I’ve done, but the stone is already a part of my bed now, and the echoes will reverberate for cycles. The quiet will not return easily. Yet as the frenzy subsides into a new, uneasy rhythm, I sense Kel’s gaze still on me. He doesn’t run. He doesn’t fear. He crouches at my edge—Miru has pulled the child away, but Kel remains—and he places his palm flat on the water. I could drink him. I don’t. Instead, I send a ripple up through his hand, a vibration that is not sound but the memory of sound, a promise: *You are my experiment, and I am yours.*

Somewhere above, the mist weaves through the crags, and the sheer walls seep, and the moss drinks the spilled wing-scale. The hamlet will mend its walkways, recalibrate its choreography. I will be their monster and their mother, their breath and their breaking. I am where I have always been, at the lowest point, waiting for the next stone, the next song, the next child born under a river of furred bodies. I am the mouth of the hollow, and I am learning what it means to taste something I cannot swallow: a boy’s trust, a question forming in the dark of his eyes.

He is asking me my name. I don’t have one, except the one they never speak. So I give him something new, something only he will hear—a frequency I’ve never sounded before, shaped like a hand extended in greeting. It sounds, I think, like the beginning of a song.
