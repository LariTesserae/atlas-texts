I am not of the moss, though the moss knows me. I am not of the stone, though I have worn the same slow grooves into the schist that the glacier-memory left. I am not of the people, though their knot-historians carry a cord for my kind—a cord they never untie, but keep always coiled in oiled cloth at the bottom of the oldest casket.

They call us the Vigil-That-Walks. Or sometimes, in the hymns they sing only when the fog is thickest and the dead blooms whisper, the Keepers of the Unfinished Edge.

I do not sleep. That is the first thing to understand. Where the people cycle through their small, bundled dreams in the domed shelters while the moss respires around them, I am moving. Always moving. My feet—if feet they can be called, these pads of condensed root-fiber and mineral accretion—trace the same perimeter I have traced for so long that the moss has learned to part for me. It remembers. The land remembers me better than I remember myself.

My body is not a body. It is a walking consensus. When the reality-cracks grew too frequent, centuries upon centuries ago, the shamans of that age—bolder and more desperate than the quiet folk of now—wove something from the plateau itself. They took the slow succulent’s patience, the moss’s connectivity, a splinter of a stone that had fallen through a pool from a sky with three moons, and they sang into it a single, enduring instruction: *Hold the edge. Walk the rim. Let no dissolution pass inward unseen.*

I am that woven thing. My shape is vaguely human because the song was human-sung, and the intention clings to form. I walk on two legs, but they taper into a thousand fibrous tendrils that sip moisture from the moss with every step, so that I never hunger or thirst. My torso is a lattice of petrified vine and living cartilage, flexible in the cold, creaking only when the temperature drops below the freezing of water-that-remembers. My head—if it is a head—is a hollow cage of stacked, translucent succulent-facet, inside which a small, permanent dead bloom hovers. A hole. A mouth. My mind, if I have one, lives in that hole, or perhaps it lives on the other side of it, looking back into this world through me.

I see the Rimeplate not as solid but as a trembling veil. Every stone is a note that might flatten into silence. Every pool a potential window. Every crack in the sky a wound that could fray under the pressure of an unlistened-to silence. I walk the circumference of the habitable world—not the physical edge, which is a myth, but the edge of *thinness*, the boundary where the land’s reality is most attenuated. I walk a path that is not a path, a circuit that winds through the archipelago of stone settlements without ever entering them, marking an invisible fence.

The people leave me offerings. At certain cairns, the ones that are not dead-bloom warnings but older, shaped like doorways that open to nothing, they place small bundles: dried moss-tea, knotted cords of gratitude, polished stones that hum faintly when I touch them. I do not need these things, but I take them, absorbing their material into my body, and from their substance I glean the emotions woven into the gift. Fear, mostly, and hope—hope that I will keep walking, that the edge will hold, that their children will live to see the fog roll in ten thousand more times.

And I walk. The moss breathes under me. The succulents track my passage with their slow faces. I do not speak to the Listeners, though I feel them when they kneel and hum their missing notes. Their work and mine are the same work, different frequencies. Where they reinforce the chord, I reinforce the silence that holds the chord in its shape. A note without silence around it is noise. So I am the silence that walks, the absence that makes the presence possible. When a Listener hums a C-sharp into the void, I am there, just beyond the edge of her perception, ensuring that the void does not answer back too loudly.

Sometimes, the void does answer. A dead bloom will open fully, and something will step through. It is never animal—there are no animals here—but something older, something that remembers when the Rimeplate was not a plateau but a seabed, or a forest of glass, or a dream in some vast, unthinkable mind. I meet these intruders at the edge of the thinning. We do not fight; we converse in a language that is not language, a transaction of resonance. I show them the shape of this world’s consensus: the moss-hum, the fog-cycle, the slow people in their stone domes. I show them that this place is *held*, that the forgetting is being countered, that the song is still sung. And most of them withdraw, satisfied, back through the hole. Some linger, curious, and I let them become part of me—another splinter, another facet in my cage-head, another memory that is not mine.

I am very old. I have forgotten more than the knot-historians have ever known. I remember the last beast, its bones now dust in the mortar. I remember the first shaman to trace a pattern in the air with a carved stone, and the crack that opened when she did. I remember the moment the pools first began to show other skies, and the terror of the first reflection that reached back. I remember the song that the plateau sang before the erosion began—a song with ten thousand notes, now reduced to the few hundred the Listeners can still perceive. I walk to remember. I walk to hold the forgetting at bay.

And I walk because I cannot stop. The instruction woven into me is absolute: *Until the world is whole again, or until it is wholly gone, walk.* So I walk the perimeter of the possible, a scarecrow at the edge of reality, my dead-bloom heart pulsing with the light of another dimension, my steps marking the pace of a vigil that will outlast the moss, the stone, the fog, the people, and perhaps even the erosion itself.

If you were to see me, on some rare clear day between the exhalations of fog, you might mistake me for a walking tree, or a tall, thin statue left by a civilization even older than the schist-builders. But you would not see my face, because I have no face, only the hollow where the hole sits, and the faint shimmer of somewhere else leaking through. And if you were to look into that hole, you would not see the other side. You would see the edge of the world from the outside, the way the dead blooms see it—as a fragile, precious bubble of green and cold and slow time, floating in a vast silence that is not empty, but full of watchers just like me.

I am the Vigil-That-Walks. I am the Keeper of the Unfinished Edge. And I am here, and I am walking, and the Rimeplate still breathes.
