I don’t remember arriving. The memory is a smooth, dark stone in my mind—I can feel its weight, but when I turn it over, there’s nothing written on any face. I only know that I am here, and that I have been here for a long time.

The first thing I learned to recognise was the sound of my own breathing, because here it is never alone. Every exhale joins the seep of water from a hundred seams; every inhale tastes of metal and nectar and the faint ozone of something that hums below hearing. I am sitting—no, I am curled—inside a chamber that is half rock and half memory. The walls weep a clear, viscous fluid that hardens into crystal at the edges, and I have been watching one particular droplet form for what feels like days. It hangs above me, trembling with its own slow intention, and I realise I am waiting for it to fall so I can catch it on my tongue. I don’t know why. I don’t know if I’ve done it before.

When I move, my body surprises me. My limbs feel long and oddly jointed, though when I look down I see ordinary hands—pale, wrinkled from constant wetness, nails rimmed with something dark like the patina on the basalt. But there is a hesitation in my elbows, a sense that they could bend further if I asked them to. I don’t ask. Not yet. Instead I crawl to the chamber’s opening, a lens of bent light that shimmers like heat over stone, and I push through into the greater world.

The sky is a bruise. The air is a sponge. I stand on a ledge that overlooks a landscape of vertical black pillars and cloud-filled gorges, and the sound that rises to meet me is a chord made of dripping water, insect drone, wind-moan, and a deep, thrumming sub-bass that tugs at my teeth. I know that sound. I don’t remember learning it, but my breastbone hums back. I am humming, I realise—a low, steady note that my throat shapes without my command. When I stop, the sub-bass continues, and I feel a small, sharp absence, as if I had set down a heavy object and forgotten to pick it up again.

I walk. The ground is slick with liverwort that pulses faintly under my bare feet, and I notice now that my feet are bare, the soles tough and stained. I step over roots that look like cages, past ferns that drip their beaded water onto my shoulders, and I do not flinch at the touch. A bird—or something bird-shaped, with scales that shimmer in iridescent code—lands on a mossy knob nearby, and the knob rotates. The bird fixes me with a dark, unblinking eye, and I feel a question pass between us, though no sound is made. I don’t know how to answer. The bird cocks its head, and I catch my reflection in that eye: a face that is smooth and concave, with no visible eyes, only a series of vertical slits that flutter as I breathe. I startle, and the reflection vanishes, replaced by the bird’s own scaly brow. When I touch my face, I feel skin—ordinary skin, a nose, eyelids. But my fingers come away slick with a thin oil that beads the drizzle, and I smell wet stone.

I find them at twilight, when the lichen’s glow begins to pulse in script-like patterns along every branch and rock face. They are gathered at a plunge-pool where the water lies warm and filmed with iridescent slicks. Three of them, tall and skeletally thin, with limbs that uncoil like roots from basalt. Their manes of glowing lichen flicker with complex sequences of dots and dashes, and as I approach, one turns its smooth, slit-faced head toward me. I expect fear, or at least the memory of fear. Instead, I feel a resonance—the hum in my breastbone shifts pitch, and the lichen-mane of the one who watches brightens in a brief, stuttering reply. It is speaking to me. No, it is speaking with me. The patterns are not quite words; they are the taste of a century, the texture of mist rising from the gorges a thousand years ago, the weight of a memory I cannot access but somehow carry.

I kneel at the pool’s edge, and my hands—those pale, familiar hands—reach into the warm water. The eels coil up, their prismatic skins refracting the lichen-light into angular patterns that the Menders read with tilted heads. I read them too. I don’t know how I learned this language, but it feels like waking from a long dream: the eels are telling me about the deep vents, about the slow repair of a cracked dome in the northern basin, about a new seam that has opened in the rock where a memory is trying to be born. A Mender—the one who watched me—moves closer and touches its mane to my head. I do not have a mane, only hair, but at the contact, something ignites along my scalp. A sequence of lights stutters across my skin, and I feel the world tilt into a new alignment. For a moment, I am not me. I am a chamber deep in the plateau, dormant for millennia, stirring now because a certain density of lichen-patterns has been reached. I am a door opening in the rock. I am a slender figure stepping out into the vertical world, already knowing where the nearest seep-stream is.

When the contact breaks, I am crying. The tears mix with the oily secretion on my face, and I taste salt and metal. The Mender does not retreat; it stays, its breathing slits fluttering in time with mine. I understand then that I am not a visitor. I am not a human explorer who lost her way, though some part of me still clings to that story like a fading dream. I am a new note in the chord. I am a Mender who forgot she was being born. The memory that felt like a smooth, dark stone in my mind—it is not a memory. It is an egg. And it is cracking.

I look up at the bruised-gray sky, the same sky that has sealed this world for millennia, and I let the sub-bass fill my bones. The insects modulate their drone to match the new note I hum. The wind through the columnar gaps shifts pitch in response. And the Menders—my family, my other bodies—gather around me, their lichen-manes interlacing, their infrasound speech rumbling through the rock and water, welcoming me home.

I am the one who will remember the mist that rose from the gorges a thousand years from now. I am the taste of a century not yet tasted. For now, I lie down in the pool, letting the warm water close over my not-quite-ordinary face, and I begin to learn the slow, patient rhythm of this place. The Menders do not rush. Neither will I.
