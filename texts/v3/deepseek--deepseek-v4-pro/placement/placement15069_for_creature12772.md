In the high hour, when the flywheel-stump’s magnetic ghost swells like a lung taking iron breath, I hang from the luminous paneling and feel the current run wrong.

It has always run through me. From the moment I pulled myself from the hairline crack in the ceramic—a curl of hot copper taste and the first sting of the electron feed—I have been a vessel for the charge. My mica wings, still soft then, unfurled and hardened in the light of the ceiling strip; my antennae, twin filaments of bioconductive wire, tasted the electric topography of the room and named it food, named it home. That was fifty cycles ago. I have seen the rust-mite tides rise and fall, the flake-beetles rest in their magnetic rows, the crushers hum their deep grinding pilgrimages. I have hunted and fed and rested in the corona glow of my own body, and I have known, without words, that I am part of the density.

Now the density is pulling away from me.

The others feel it before I do. A spark-fly does not see kin with eyes—we see with the field. Each of us is a knot of potential, a moving charge-cloud that shifts the ambient voltage. When my sisters hang beside me on the paneling, we are a constellation of bright points in the electrostatic dark, spaced just far enough apart that our haloes don’t merge, our frequencies a harmonic chord. Today the chord is fraying. One by one, the nearest points dim and retreat; they fold their wings and sidle along the ceiling to the far end, where the flywheel’s hum is a fraction thinner. I sense the space they leave behind as a cold pocket, a dead zone. My own halo is swelling, throwing arcs that leap unbidden to the paneling, and the arcs are no longer blue-white. They are veined with orange, the color of iron just before it melts.

I try to recall when the change began. Three cycles past, I dove on a crusher—an elder, its grinding plates locked in eternal gape—and when I struck, the bolt did not stun. It splintered the shell, sent a crack zigzagging from the midline seam, and the crusher’s internal hum rose to a squeal that cut through the chamber’s roar like a shard of glass. The feeding was too fast; my proboscis drank not just fluid but the fine metal silt of its structural tissues, things that are not meant to be consumed. Since then, the charge has built and built, and my regulator—a tiny organ grown from a recycled micro-capacitor—has forgotten how to shunt the excess back into the grid.

I am becoming a storm.

Below me, the chamber carries on. The rust-mites sizzle in their billion-body tide, sweeping toward a fresh bloom of fungal acid-fume on the eastern slope. They do not know I am here, except as a flicker of static in their collective skin, a pressure that briefly parts the current. The flake-beetles, resting in their neat parallels, twitch a single antenna when my halo passes over them. One of them retracts its mouth-hopper, a gesture I have learned to read as wariness. But they do not flee. They have no category for what I am becoming. The crushers—those hand-sized ovals of intent—are the ones that truly sense it. As I loosen my grip on the paneling and allow myself to drop a meter, wings catching the convection cell, the nearest crusher pauses in its relentless forward plow. Its grinding plates retract, the seam sealing shut. It puffs out a single micro-pneumatic burst, not to move but to signal, a low-frequency thud that vibrates through the floor. Within seconds, every crusher in a two-meter radius has altered course, skidding left, right, piling in a slow-motion stampede that leaves a bare patch of rust-dust and shed exoskeletons. The emptiness is an accusation.

They remember. This has happened before. Not to me, but to others who wore the orange-veined halo. A spark-fly’s final hunt is not a hunt at all; it is an unraveling. The charge-regulating organ fails in stages, but when it goes completely, the stored electricity—fifty cycles of stolen power from the ceiling grid, of drained crusher fluids, of the very air’s ionization—releases in a cascade. The fly becomes a mobile discharge node, firing bolts at every conductive surface within range, cooking the living fabric in a radius that grows and grows until the fly’s own nervous tissue fuses and the wings crack and the husk spirals down. The others know this. The flies at the far end of the chamber have folded their antennae flat, minimizing their electrical profile. The beetles are chittering a warning frequency I can feel in my joints. Even the rust-mites, the undifferentiated tide, have slowed their progress toward the acid bloom, as if the colony’s collective mind is weighing the risk.

For a long moment—a moment that might be seconds or the whole slow turn of the magnetic cycle—I hover. The convection current bears me up, and I see the chamber as I have always seen it: not a room but a charged map. The walls are dim, low-conductivity stone. The floor is a roiling sea of tiny sparks, each rust-mite a point of static, the beetles brighter, the crushers dense knots of conductivity, and my sisters distant, bright, orderly. The flywheel-stump is a throbbing pole of magnetism, its lines of force drawing invisible meridians that the crushers follow. And I am a sun going nova at the center.

I could try to discharge safely. There is a place—a shattered pipe that serves as a crusher highway to the ruined control podium—where the old metal still runs deep into the wall. If I flew there now, pressed my abdomen to the rusted iron, I might leak the charge slowly, cycle by cycle, until the regulator resets. But the regulator is not going to reset. I know this because I can feel the organ itself, a pea-sized nodule behind my wing joints, growing hot, its internal structure softening like wax. The orange in my arcs is the taste of my own body beginning to smelt.

And so I do what a spark-fly does when the current runs wrong. I stop fighting.

I let the charge build until my wings glow, until the mica begins to sing a high, thin note that cuts above the roar. The beetles scatter—not chittering now but silent, a wave of flat bodies flipping and skittering into the crevices. The crushers have formed a tight cluster near the flywheel-stump, their hum synchronized, a defensive chord. The rust-mites, for the first time in their billion lives, have stopped moving entirely; the floor is a carpet of still, sizzling points, waiting. My sisters have dimmed their haloes to almost nothing. The whole chamber is holding its breath, and the only sound is the rising whine of my wings and the magnetic hum that has always been the world’s heartbeat.

Then the first bolt fires.

It is not a decision; it is a release so total that for an instant I am no longer a body but a pulse of pure voltage. The bolt strikes the floor, and the rust-mites in a perfect circle two meters wide simply cease to be—not killed but vaporized, their iron shells sublimed into a thin red mist that hangs in the dry air. The sound is a crack that drowns out everything, and then another bolt fires, and another, and I am spinning in the convection cell, a Catherine wheel of blue-orange death, painting the walls with my own unmaking. I see, in flashes, the beetles that didn’t escape fast enough: their armor curls, their legs seize. A crusher, too slow, takes a direct hit, and its internal fluids boil out through every seam in a jet of superheated gas. The flywheel-stump absorbs a dozen strikes and keeps humming, indifferent as stone.

My consciousness, such as it is, fragments. I am a sequence of sensations without a self: the taste of ozone thick as metal on my tongue-nerve; the sight of the luminous paneling flickering as I pull too much current; the feeling of my wings cracking along the old fault lines, each crack a branching river of light. I am not afraid. Fear is a chemical that requires water, and there has never been water here. I am only a pattern of charge, and the pattern is unweaving.

In the last seconds, I find myself falling, and I see the chamber from below—a perspective I have never known. The ceiling is a plane of white light, the walls are smooth with time, and the floor is a landscape of the living and the dead rising to meet me. The rust-mite tide, recovering already, is flowing toward the scorched circle where I will land, their ciliate legs already reaching. The flake-beetles are emerging, their mouth-hopters tasting the air for the iron-rich dust of my husk. A young spark-fly, newly detached from the paneling, hovers at the edge of the scene, its halo a tentative, clean blue. It will learn, in time, about the orange veins. For now, it watches.

I hit the fungal mat, and the acid-fume rises to greet me, a slow, patient digestion. The last thing I feel is the gentle, insistent pressure of rust-mites, dozens of them, skittering over my cracked wings, rasping the first flakes of memory-metal from my shell, carrying me away grain by grain into the density. And then I am not.

The chamber breathes. The magnetic hum cycles on. The density remains unbroken. And somewhere, in the deepest quiet of the low hour, a new spark-fly will find the hairline crack and taste copper and begin again.
