I find myself exactly where I have always been: standing ankle-deep in a pool of body-warm water, a scraping tool in my hand, the roar of the world pressing in on all sides until I no longer hear it as sound but as the steady hum of my own blood. I am on the seventh terrace from the basin floor, the one my family has tended for eleven generations—I know this because my grandmother told me, and her grandmother told her, and the knots on the elders’ wall go back further than anyone bothers to count. The pool in front of me is a pale, milky blue, ringed with a crust of rust-red bacteria that marks the exact temperature where the larvae grow fattest. I have scraped this particular rim every day since my mother first placed the tool in my hand, a tiny copper blade then, worn now to a crescent thin as a minnow’s rib.

Who am I? I am the sound my knuckles make on a hollow bone tube—two fast clicks, then a thump that vibrates in the stone. That is my name, the one I answer to when Clicks-end calls me from three terraces away. My mother gave me a different name when I was born, a song of copper pot and bone needle, but that is private, heard only in the sleeping chamber. To everyone else, I am the click-click-thump that tells them where I am, that I am working, that the larval pools are tended. I am the hands that know without looking whether a pupa is ready to harvest, the bare feet with toes that grip wet travertine as if they have roots, the sideways-closing eyelids that keep the steam from blurring my vision. I am twenty new chambers old—that is, my family has cut twenty chambers into the rock since I was born, though I lost count of exact years long ago because years do not matter here.

But I am also the pool itself, in a way. When I scrape the bacterial mat, I am feeding the larvae that will feed the frogs that Clicks-end hunts, and his hunting feeds us, and our bodies will eventually return to the water, to the mineral crust, to the bacteria. We are not separate from the basin; we are a loop inside the larger loop. I think this but I do not have words for it. I feel it as a rightness, a hum that matches the drone of the steam vents. When a larva wriggles under my fingertip, I feel the wriggle in my own gut. When a frog calls, my throat tightens in sympathy.

I am a keeper of the balance, though I would never call myself that. The elders hold the Great Ledger, the wall of knotted cords, but every person in the basin holds a smaller ledger inside their body. My mother taught me to read the pools: if the larvae are too many, the water turns cloudy and the bacteria suffer. If they are too few, the fly clouds thin and the birds that nest on our roof-beams will go hungry and their guano will no longer feed the algae in the runoff channels. So I scrape exactly enough. I do not count larvae; my hands know the weight of a scoop. When I lift a handful of writhing grey threads and place them in my clay pot, I am not taking lives—I am shaping the endless emergence, the landscape that is always arriving but never arriving. That is what I am: a shaper of the arrival, a set of hands that help the world continue to be exactly what it already is.

Sometimes I wonder what it would be like to see something new. Not a new vent opening—that is a rupture, a wound—but a new thing, a thing that has never been here before. A color not found in the bacterial rings. A shape not carved from bone or stone. But when the thought rises, it frightens me, and I push it down with the same motion I use to press a pupa cake onto the drying rack. Newness is not part of the roar. The roar is made of layers that never change: the basso frogs, the hissing vents, the high piping of the midges, the rhythmic hammering from the copper workers three terraces over, the distant shout of a child in a game. That constancy is my cradle, my breath. If I reached out and a note were missing, I think I would fall.

So I scrape. The tool rasps against the stone, a sound that blends into the wall of noise. The water sheets over the pool’s lip, smooth as a polished hide. The steam beads on my shoulders, runs down my spine, and I shiver in the heat. I am Kith—no, I am the click-click-thump, the keeper of the seventh terrace larval pools, the daughter of the woman who taught me the weight of a scoop, the lover of the man who hunts the largest frogs. I am a node in the great woven hum, a single knot in the endless cord. And I am, in this moment, complete.
