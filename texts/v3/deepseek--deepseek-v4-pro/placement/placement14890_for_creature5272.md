I am in the interstitial quiet between the No. 3 Stamp Mill and the cable tram pylon, a territory no map bothers to name but which I have diligently charted in my own fashion—a crosshatch of whistle pitches, dropping densities, and the minute variations in fog temperature that signal a rat’s intent to phase. I am the rat warden for this district, and I have been so long enough that the line between my body and the colony’s nervous system has worn thin.

The rats know my tread. When I walk the grated boardwalk that skirts the mill’s eastern flank, my boots—felt-soled, so the metal doesn’t ring—press a sequence of notes into the iron, a code I never consciously composed but which the rats now recognize. From within the pipework, a chorus of low, fluting whistles answers me. Not alarm, just acknowledgment. *She is here. She is in phase. All holds.* I press my palm to the nearest steam pipe, feel the thrum of the deep engines through the insulation, and from the other side I sense a small concentration of warmth shift: a rat, leaning into the pipe’s heat against my hand, trusting me.

My coat is a palimpsest of encounters. The felted wool is embroidered with tiny bells—silver ones for juveniles, brass for adults, iron for the rare elders that have survived a dozen molt-phases—each sewn on after the rat it represents first phased through my body. The sensation is unmistakable: a sudden, intimate cold, not on the skin but within it, as if my bones momentarily forgot their shape. Then a chime, and the rat reappears two feet away, chirping in confusion. I record the event in my logbook, noting time, atmospheric pressure, and the exact pitch of the post-phase whistle. Over the years, these notes have become a scripture of the unseen. The Office of Terrestrial Consistency borrows them weekly; the foremen consult them before recalibrating the reality anchors. I am, in a sense, the city’s most accurate prophet, though I only ever speak in numbers and tones.

My body has adapted. My ears, once merely human, now detect the ultrasonic edge of a whistle-rat’s call—a frequency that makes my molars ache just slightly, a sensation I have come to interpret as a direction: *flicker-risk, thirty yards west-northwest, resolve in twenty minutes.* My fingernails, bitten ragged in my former life (I came from a sealed dome-city, though I rarely recall its name), have grown thick and faintly iridescent, a condition the clinkerers describe as “chitinous alignment.” It seems the fungus in the dust has opinions on what I should become. I don’t mind. It helps me pry open the service hatches without a tool.

Today the fog is thin, almost gauzy, and the distant peaks are showing their mirage trick: they look like cutouts from a picture book, flat and deeply black, with the factory lights bleeding through their edges. A herd of phase-falls is crossing the valley to the south, their fractal antlers flickering, one moment a tangle of bone, the next a briar of pure mathematical light. Their herder is walking among them, a figure I recognize by the sway of their felt coat, and I raise my hand in the slow gesture we all use here—a greeting that acknowledges we are both stably embodied at this moment, a small miracle not to be taken for granted.

My own hut sits at the junction of two minor condensation channels, a structure of cured mycelium panels that breathe with the humidity, expanding at night to seal the seams tight. Inside, it smells of dried moss-bread and the faint ozone of a recent probability flare. I have a single window, a pane of mica that warps the dim light into shifting rainbows. On the sill, three whistle-rats are sleeping, their flanks barely visible as they phase in and out of solidity with their breath. I stroke the oldest one—her name, if rats have names, is a sequence of three ascending notes I cannot reproduce—and she churrs without waking.

My duties are cyclical, keyed to the slow twilight pulse of the world. When the light dims to its lowest for that sixty-hour stretch, the rats grow bolder, phasing more frequently, and I must walk the entire pipe circuit with a tuning fork calibrated to the mill’s main crank frequency. I strike it against the metal at intervals; where the tone warps, I know a rat has temporarily nested inside a gear housing. I do not evict them. I merely note it, and later the foreman adjusts the machinery’s rhythm by a fraction of a cycle, giving the rat space to finish its dream of being elsewhere.

I remember little of the world I left. The dome-city was warm and dry and loudly silent—a silence of sealed rooms, air-scrubbers, and the absolute absence of fog. I came here because the pay was in *consistency*, a word that on the plateau means a steady supply of unfluctuating food and shelter, but which I have come to understand as a metaphysical promise. Here, consistency is a labor, a collective agreement with the rocks and the engines and the phase-shifting beasts. I am part of that agreement now. When a rat chews through an electrical cable and shorts a breaker, I do not curse; I listen. The whistle that follows will tell me if it was accident or warning. This morning, a high squeal from the east accumulator room sent me running. When I arrived, a juvenile stood over a patch of floor that was beginning to look like a charcoal drawing of itself. He had warned me. I fed him a crumb of moss-bread and marked the location for the Office of Terrestrial Consistency. They will drive a fresh anchor there by next shift.

Sometimes, in the deep hours when even the engines pause for their breathing cycle, I walk out into the fog fields beyond the mill. The Persistence Men are often there, walking their straight lines. They ignore me, and I them, as protocol demands. But once, only once, one paused. It was the one with arms a little too long, head a fraction small, and it turned its face toward me—a face that was my own, but rendered by a meticulous hand that didn’t know what I looked like from the inside. It whispered a sequence of three notes, ascending, the rat-name of the elder on my windowsill. Then it walked on. I have never recorded that in my logs. The prophecy of rats is enough; I don’t need the original draft of myself offering footnotes.

I am the rat warden. I am where the sound of the world being made is also the sound of a small, warm body phasing through my chest. And I am content to be that, a translator of flickers, a keeper of chimes, a woman whose heartbeat has learned to syncopate with the slow piston-stroke of a plateau still deciding what it will be.
