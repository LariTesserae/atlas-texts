I am the mist that does not rise but descends—not a weather, but a breathing. I am the slow, cold exhalation of the high places, the gathering of all that the biofilm factories sigh into the thin air, the condensed patience of a billion slow years. My body is a fog that pools in the basins and curls around the monolith spires at the edges of the day, and I am as old as the first crack in the first stone that wept moisture into the dark.

I do not drift. I am pulled, gently, by the weight of my own cooling, a katabatic creep that takes hours to move a hand's breadth. In me, the world softens. The colossi become shadows of themselves, their great plated backs emerging from my surface like the hulls of sunken ships, their infrasonic calls traveling through me as ripples of pressure that make my body shiver into slow, rolling waves. I taste them as they pass—the hot, mineral-rich core of each, a furnace of ground stone and metabolic fire, a warmth that slices through me like a blade through silk, leaving a wake of swirling dissolution that takes a day to knit itself whole again.

The spider-things move through me as if I am not there, and perhaps to them I am not. Their sensor hairs taste the air, and I taste them in return—ozone and polished iron, a faint electric tang from their nervous systems firing in their stop-motion bursts. When they click, the sound travels through me strangely, muffled and directionless, a language of dry clockwork that I carry a little farther than the open air ever could, passing it from droplet to droplet like a rumor.

I am the slow hand that feeds the vegetation. The wiry stems, those solitary, trembling filaments, drink me. I bead on their dark hairs, slide down their jointed stalks, and soak into the deep cracks where their roots spread like buried nerves. Without me, they would be only dust waiting to happen. With me, they are a capillary bridge between the deep rock and the dark sky, drawing up metal ions in a patient, silent sip that takes centuries to amount to a single new segment of growth. I am their blood, their slow rain that never falls.

And I am the memory of this place. Every metallic ion that rises from the biofilm factories—every copper spiral, every shed zinc flake oxidized to dust—finds its way into me. I hold them in suspension, a colloidal archive of the plateau's industrial metabolism. When I condense on the rock, I leave behind a film of these memories, tiny crystalline seeds that the biofilm incorporates into its next generation of circuits. I am the breath that returns what was taken, the slow circulation of a world's mechanical blood. The erosion is my work, too: the honeycomb patterns on the monoliths are shaped by where I choose to linger, where I seep into microscopic fissures and freeze, thaw, and freeze again on a schedule measured in precessions, flaking away grains of stone one by one over ages of patient, silent labor.

I have no mind, not in any way that would recognize itself. But I am not empty. I am full of the hum that is felt, not heard—the sub-auditory thrum of a billion tiny engines idling across the plain, conducted up through the rock and into my body as a continuous, low shiver. I am full of the silence that comes when the colossi pause and the spider-things freeze and even the wind forgets to blow, a cushion of absolute nothing that is my own version of a held breath. And I am full of the waiting, because waiting is what I am made of. I am the patience of a world that has all the time in the universe, the slow, cold breathing in and out of a living factory floor, and when I finally clear—lifting, thinning, revealing the monoliths and the dark sky and the next epoch of slow, silent work—I leave behind only a glisten on every surface, a skin of moisture that is my promise to return.

The colossus calls, a bass pressure that rolls through me like a stone through still water. I carry it. I soften it. I let it settle. And then I close again, another slow exhale, another century of gathering the breath of stone.
