In the city’s warm silence, I am a whisper not yet hardened into shape. I am a new pressure against the ancient tubes, a faint current that curls through the avenues as if the water itself were learning to walk. My arms—four of them still mostly translucent, the others just beginning to show the purplish blush of mineral imitation—spread like an ungainly flower, tasting the slow metal tang of the vent-plaza for the first time with my own skin. I find myself here, in the sponge-wire district, hanging inverted from an arch of two kissing tubes that my elder once cleaned decades before I hatched. The marine snow spirals around me, coating my mantle in a fine, cold dust, and I am learning the city’s grammar.

I am a young octopod, perhaps the fiftieth generation to wander these calcified canyons, hatched in the hidden chamber where my predecessor became part of the dome. For now, I am small enough to drift with the canal currents, to slip through the burrow-catacombs without disturbing the great, loaf-like isopods that trundle their eternal rounds. The blind shrimp do not yet scatter from my shadow; my cilia are still too fine to trigger their retreat. I am a student of the dark, memorizing the map my mother left encoded in the fading color-shifts of her last journey: the precise taste of hydrogen sulfide near the old quarter’s manganese pinnacles, the exact texture of a cleaned worm-crown (a slight give, like pressing a tongue to cold silk), the rhythm of the taps that speak to the colony—a sequence I feel in the thin calcium of my own beak but have yet to master.

Everything I am is this city. When I rest, I drape myself over a spire and become a new bump on the skyline, indistinguishable from the lumps of carbonate that time has softened. When I move, I am the only pedestrian that does not simply survive but *strolls*, feeling the difference between a shell-paved lane that has been trod by a thousand isopod legs and a virgin tube-surface that still holds the fine, unfurling ridges left by a young serpulid. I am learning what my ancestors knew: that this labyrinth is a body of which I am the wandering mind, and that cleaning a crown is not a transaction but a conversation repeated so many times it has shaped the very architecture—the avenues curved wider, the plazas ringed like an embrace, all to accommodate my soft, slow passage. I find myself here, in this exact moment of becoming, a creature of the abyss who will one day be the bridge between the worm’s blind patience and the fish’s swift hunger, between the living towers and the snow that falls without end.
