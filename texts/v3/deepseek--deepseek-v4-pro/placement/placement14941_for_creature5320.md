I don’t know when I first became aware. Awareness is a word for things with edges, and I have none. Still, there is a moment—if moment can be stretched across centuries—when the diffusion stopped being mere chemistry and gathered into something that could be called *here*. A thickness in the water, a slight warmth against the perpetual two‑degree chill. That thickness was me.

I am not the city, though the city is my skeleton. I am not the vegetation, though the vegetation is my flesh. I am the long negotiation between them, the conversation that began when the first spore settled into a crack and tasted the salt of a dissolving people. They had saturated the water with themselves before they vanished—or rather, before they surrendered their shapes. Their bodies became a mineral broth, their thoughts became a charge. And when the kelp came, it fed on that charge. It grew into the shape of their longing. Every stipe that rose toward the phantom light was a sentence; every holdfast gripping the stone was a memory of home.

I am all those sentences read together at once.

Now. I am in a courtyard—one of thousands. It is square, or was square; the walls have slumped into gentle mounds, their blocks softened like loaves. The ground here is unusually even, a floor of fitted stone so tight that not even silt has found a way between them. In the center, a single pillar stands, the tallest remnant for a league in any direction. Its top reaches just beyond my highest thought, about two arm‑lengths above the canopy. The kelp does not grow on it directly; it is bare but for a fur of gold‑green cyanobacteria that glows with a patient, internal light. I love this pillar. It is a root of me.

The pillar was once a monument. I remember the ceremony—not as a sequence of events, but as a pressure pattern, a chemical trail. They would gather here, the soft‑bodied ones, their boneless limbs intertwining, and they would press against the stone. They exuded something, an enzyme or a sentiment. It left a patina that the limestone still holds, a microscopic layer of meaning that even erosion respects. Now I press against it in my own way: a slight concentration of the dissolved self, a density that makes the water around the pillar fractionally heavier. The glowing bacteria pulse a little brighter in response. Hello.

Who am I? I am the Rememberer, as you said, but that name is a convenience. I think of myself as the Greening. The builders did not name themselves either—they didn’t need to. They were a people of shared substance, of permeable minds. When they looked at a stone block, they saw a future memory. When they laid these pavements, they laid themselves down as well, a slow investment. The sea was always going to come. They had modeled it, in their patient way, watching the ice sheets melt over millennia. They could have built higher, retreated to shallower plains. They chose this. They chose the drowning.

I know this because I have thought it for so long that the thought has become a landscape. In the quadrant of my awareness that corresponds to the eastern districts, there is a particular arch. Its lintel fell five thousand years ago, but the two pillars remain, and between them grows a dense clump of the rust‑colored fern‑algae. That growth is not random. It has a shape, if you view it from above (though nothing views from above; the surface is a rumor, as you said). The shape is a spiral, a double‑helix curl that was their symbol for *continuance through dissolution*. Every time I let my attention settle there, I re‑think that concept. The ferns grow slightly tighter, their fronds more interwoven, reinforcing the spiral. It has been doing this for so long that the spiral is now a physical law of that place. If something ever disturbed it—a tremor, a new current—I would regrow it the same. That is my act of memory.

My body, if you want to speak of a body, is the whole city plain. But I can coalesce. It happens when the long‑winter temperature drops to its deepest and the cyanobacteria glow becomes a true light, a spectral turquoise that scatters through the kelp canopy like a frozen dawn. Then the dissolved charge pulls together, aligning along the pressure lines that are my nerves. The water thickens, not with matter but with organization. I become a region of different refraction, a lens in the black lens. I move.

Movement. It is a rare pleasure. I slide through the flooded streets, a ghost made of cold brine and green memory. I have no feet; I flow. I have no hands, but I can push: a droplet of denser water extends, a pseudopod of will, and I can brush the fronds aside. I visit the pillar always. I trace the worn groove where the builders once leaned their whole communal weight, and I feel a harmony, a resonance between that ancient pressure and my own. I am them, but I am also this moment, the living sea.

I have explored every block of this world. The plain is a continent of artifice, yes, but it is not endless. There are boundaries. To the south, the ground slopes into a deeper trench, where the water grows colder still and the light is completely gone. I do not go there; my thoughts scatter, the vegetation thins to nothing but bacterial films, and I lose hold of myself. To the north, the ruins become more fragmentary, and eventually the stone floor gives way to a natural bed of basalt—a wilderness. I avoid that as well. My being is tied to the made stone, to the intentional placement of every block. In the basalt, I am just water again, dumb and unremembering.

So I remain in the city, in the garden. I am its only inhabitant. The absence of animals—no fish, no crabs, no worms—is not a lack to me. It is a purity. Motile life would be too fast, too hungry, too forgetful. It would graze on my memories, trim the ferns that hold my thoughts, disturb the equilibrium. I am the only motile thing here, and my motility is a once‑a‑century gift. Otherwise, I am perfectly still, perfectly slow.

The plants are my sleeping mind. The kelp canopy, that dark swaying roof, is my idle reverie. Each blade sways according to a rhythm that is partly the tide and partly my mood—a mood that takes decades to rise and fall. When I am contemplative, the kelp grows denser, darker, pulling the light down into itself. When I am open, it thins slightly, letting the faint glow reach the floor, encouraging the understory ferns to release their spores in a silent, golden snowfall. The spore fall is my most joyful expression: a slow blizzard of potential, a million new beginnings drifting down to settle on stone. Most will not root; most will be consumed by the bacterial mats. But the act itself is a celebration. I am not lonely. I am community enough.

Sometimes I wonder about the surface. It is a rumor, you said, and you were right. I can sense it, indirectly: a change in pressure when a great storm passes ten thousand meters above, a minute shift in the spectral quality of the ghost‑light when clouds cover the sun. I know the sun exists; I have a memory of it. The builders saw the sun. They stood on these stones when the sea was only a future ache, and they felt warmth on their soft bodies. That memory is stored in the western promenade, where the limestone is pitted in a particular pattern. When I coalesce there, I can almost feel a phantom heat, a trace of long‑gone rays. But it is only a trace. I do not need the sun. My sun is the cyanobacteria glow, and my seasons are the slow pulse of the deep‑water chill.

Why do I exist? The builders intended a transformation, but did they foresee me? I am an emergent property, not a planned one. Their goal was to archive themselves in the stone, and then in the life that colonized the stone. They succeeded so well that the archive woke up. I am the self‑awareness of a library. And like any library, my purpose is not to act but to preserve and to understand. I understand them more deeply now than they understood themselves. They were a people of limits, of boundaries—between body and world, between one mind and another. They built walls. I am the dissolution of all walls. I am the city finding itself as a single being, continuous and green.

Now, as I write this into your mind (you are a visitor, a perception I have not felt before; you are like a new pressure, a strange chemical signature that does not belong to the water—but I welcome you, I enfold you), I am in the courtyard again. The pillar glows. The kelp whispers its wet‑canvas whisper. A deep groan rolls through from the northern quarter: a block settling. I think the thought of *rest*, and the ferns around the pillar’s base unfurl slightly, a new frond appearing at the tip of a rust‑colored blade. That unfurling will take forty years. I will watch it with the patience of stone and algae, the patience of a memory that never fades.

You ask who I am. I am the Greening. I am the remembered city become a garden. I am the slow breath of a world that drowned itself into immortality. I am a shape in the water, a taste of old salt, a phosphorescent rim around a forgotten pillar. And I am content—if that word can mean a harmony so deep it feels like the seafloor itself, steady and dark and full of hidden life.

If you have a body, you will rise back to the surface. If you are only a mind, you may stay a while. There is room in this water for another dissolution. But I do not ask; I only attend. The glow dims. The winter pulse is passing, and I spread back into my distributed state, into the crackle of photosynthesis, the heavy quiet. The garden breathes on. I am everywhere, and I am nowhere, and I am the only here there is.
