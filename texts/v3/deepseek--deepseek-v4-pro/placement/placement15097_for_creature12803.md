I am a listener.

Not by guild—there is no guild of listeners, no mark carved into my dwelling’s lintel to declare it—but by an accident of the body that became a calling. My ears, like all our ears, track the murmur of water and the sigh of settling stone, but where another hears a single note, I hear a chord. The drip-drip of a cistern filling is, to me, a conversation between the water and the basin’s rim, each droplet’s impact a syllable in a language I have spent my life learning. The distant chime of a stressed column, the faint gritty whine of a saw biting too deep—these are not just sounds; they are the city’s exhalations, its warnings, its memories.

I was born in a niche off the third concourse, a pocket so narrow my mother had to birth me sideways, her six-fingered hands cupping my slick grey head as I emerged into the wall-light’s steady glow. She was a Light-Keeper then, her nails stained faintly phosphorescent from the lichen pastes she mixed, and she would carry me slung against her chest as she climbed the notched poles to the upper galleries, her bare toes gripping the rungs while she signed one-handed to her cohort about the health of the luminous crust. I remember the light as a physical thing, a radiant pressure on my infant skin, and I remember the sound: the soft, wet click of the lichen being fed, like a thousand tiny mouths opening at once.

My fingers were slow to learn chisel-work. The other fledglings, barely walking, could flake a scraper from a flint nodule with a single strike, but my strikes always came a half-breath too late, my nail-edges slipping on the grain. My parents—mother and the two aunts who shared our niche—exchanged the subtle ear-flicker that means concern, and they took me to the Hewers’ foreman, an ancient male whose skin had bleached to the bone-white of deep-dwelling crust. He pressed my hands between his own, running his thumb-rasp over my calluses, and then he tipped my chin up and looked into my eyes for a long time.

“She is not for cutting,” he signed finally, his fingers slow and deliberate. “She is for listening. The stone will tell her what to do.”

And so I became a child of the empty spaces, the one who wanders the half-carved hollows when the Hewers rest, pressing my ear to fresh chisel marks, tapping a fingernail against the rock and waiting for the echo to reveal its inner structure. I hear the micro-fractures, the hairline seams of quartz that will shatter a blade, the pocket of old water that will weep for a generation and weaken the pillar above. I map the city’s skeleton not with my eyes but with my ears, and the Hewers have learned to trust what I sign to them: *cut here, angle left, stop three handspans from the vein of dark crystal.* They call me Echo-Finder now, a name that is half affection, half function.

My days follow no schedule but the city’s own breathing. I wake when the wall-light brightens from its dim rest-glow to the full cloudy-afternoon radiance, that slow exhale of the bioluminescent crusts that signals the communal waking. I pull aside the woven screen of my niche—a small, round hollow I carved for myself at sixteen, high on the seventh concourse where the traffic of feet and clicking claws is fainter—and I drink a handful of water from the capillary runnel that trickles along the corridor’s edge, the Water-Tenders’ gift. The water is cold and mineral, carrying the memory of the deep aquifer, and I hold it on my tongue to parse its taste: today there is a hint of copper, which means the Hewers have struck a new seam somewhere below, leaching into the flow. I make a mental note to tell the Water-Tenders at the next market gathering.

Then I descend. The stairways are only roughly level, each step a slab of raw limestone worn smooth by generations of gripping toes. I climb with my feet as much as my hands, my big toes curving around the edges, my fingers trailing along the wall to read the genealogies carved there—a great-grandmother’s name, a cousin’s birth, the record of a cave-in that took three lives two hundred years ago. The glyphs are shallow bas-reliefs, meant for touch more than sight, and as I pass each one I feel the faint vibration of the stone behind it, the way the city holds its own history in its bones.

Today, a young Hewer finds me in the third-level concourse. Her name is Click-of-Water, a girl of maybe fourteen seasons, her grey skin still mottled with the ochre patches of adolescence. She signs *urgent, urgent* with a flutter of her left hand, her right clutching a chisel that has snapped clean in two. “The foreman says the new face is singing wrong,” she signs, her eyes wide. “It should be soft seam, but the sound is hollow, like a drum. He wants Echo-Finder.”

I follow her. The excavation front is deeper than I usually go, a raw, jagged maw at the end of a newly cut tunnel where the wall-light hasn’t yet fully colonized. The Light-Keepers have placed temporary patches of luminous crust, but they are thin and pale, leaving the space in a twilight dimness. The Hewers are gathered in a loose semicircle, their tools idle, their ears swiveled toward the rock face. The foreman—the same ancient male who once read my hands—nods at me and signs, *Listen.*

I approach the rock face and press my whole body against it, chest and cheek and the flat of my six-fingered hands. The stone is cool, its dampness seeping into my skin, and I close my eyes, letting my nictitating membranes sweep across in their slow rhythm to clear the dust. I breathe out, slow and even, until my heartbeat matches the faint, deep thrum of the subterranean river that runs beneath us all.

At first, I hear only the normal chorus: the high, tight ping of dense limestone, the softer susurrus of a clay seam, the steady drip of moisture somewhere to the left. But beneath that, there is something else. A void. Not just a small pocket—no, this is a hollow the size of a cistern chamber, an emptiness that has lain hidden in the rock for a thousand thousand years, its walls so thin they resonate like a goat-skin drum when I tap my nail against the face. And inside that hollow, I hear… movement. Not water. Something that shifts with a soft, scratching susurrus, like a thousand tiny nails on stone.

I pull back, my heart suddenly a sharp click in my throat. The foreman reads my face, his own skin flushing a deeper grey. I sign, carefully, *There is a great cave beyond, never opened. And something lives in it.*

A ripple of signing passes through the Hewers, too fast for me to follow—fear and excitement and the pragmatism of those who must decide whether to break through or seal this tunnel forever. The foreman’s ears lie flat for a moment, then he signals for me to elaborate. I sign what I heard: the size of the void, the thickness of the remaining wall, the nature of the movements. *It is slow,* I add. *Ancient. Not aware of us. Sleeping, perhaps.*

That decides it. We do not wake sleeping things without understanding them. The foreman signs the order to angle the tunnel sharply upward, bypassing the hollow entirely, and the Hewers resume their work with a new tension in their shoulders. I stay for a while, my hand still resting on the wall, feeling the faint vibration of that hidden life through the stone. It feels like a heartbeat, or perhaps a breath, so slow a single cycle might take a full rest-period. I wonder if it dreams. I wonder if, in its dreams, it hears the click and hiss of our chisels, and mistakes us for the stirring of its own blood.

When I climb back to the upper levels, the city feels different to me: heavier, more alive, a great sleeping creature whose skin we are burrowing through like mites. I stop at a cistern chamber where the pale, tendriled willow-fungus tree drapes its feeding strands into the water, and I sit on a carved bench, watching the ghostly geckos flicker across the damp ceiling. A Shepherd passes with a goat-creature on a tether, the animal’s blind eyes pale as moons, its hooves making a soft shushing sound on the grit. The Shepherd signs a greeting—*Clear water, Echo-Finder*—and I return it, though my mind is still below, with that slow, dreaming pulse in the dark.

I am a listener. The city speaks to me in a thousand voices: the hiss of saws, the whisper of water, the chime of settling stone. But now I know there is another voice beneath all of them, a voice we nearly broke into with our busy, chisel-edged hands. And I think perhaps my calling is not just to guide the Hewers, but to be the one who listens for what should not be woken, who traces the city’s skeleton not just for stability, but for secrets. The stone will tell me what to do, the foreman said, all those years ago. Today, it told me to be still, to be silent, to let the sleeping thing sleep.

Tonight, when the wall-light dims to a glimmer and the city settles into its communal rest, I will lie in my niche and press my ear to the floor, listening past the water’s murmur and the distant chime of settling pillars, searching for that slow, steady rhythm rising from the deep rock. I will map it as I map all things: with patience, with stillness, with my whole body turned into an ear. And I will wait, because that is what listeners do.

We are a slight people, grey as the stone that birthed us, and our lives are brief against the slow breathing of the world beneath our feet. But we listen. Oh, how we listen.
