I am the old lodge at the valley’s throat, the one that has forgotten its first shape. They call me a hill now, but I was built of mud and sticks and the long, slow calculus of survival. I do not move, yet I breathe: my ventilation shafts puff steam into the cold air like the exhalations of a buried animal, and inside me the darkness is warm and dense with the smell of my kin. They rest against my ribs—the grizzled elders, the yearlings sleek as oil, the kits no larger than musk melons. I feel the press of their bodies, the occasional scratch of a claw adjusting a twig in my lining, the vibration of a tail slapping water in a dream.

I am not the water, but I remember every channel that has ever lapped at my foundations. I know the precise taste of the mud that seals my own walls, a taste that changes minutely with each new load the beavers fetch from the canal bed. They pat it onto me with that wet, percussive smack, the mason’s rhythm, and I absorb it into my body. Generations of repair have given me a layered memory: the soft, fibrous core of willow branches cut when the glacier’s melt was still a new thing in the valley, the intricately woven alder of the first expansion, the river cobbles wedged in after the flood that nearly breached the eastern wall. I hold those stones like teeth, like a promise against the constant whisper of water that would unmake me.

At dusk, when the city stirs, I feel them leave: the tickle of fur slipping through my underwater entrance, the single-file brush of tails against the tunnel’s curve. I am left with the elders who have grown too slow for the canals, and I house their silence. They gnaw on stored rhizomes in the dark, their worn incisors scraping with a sound like sandpaper on wood, and I listen. They know what I know: where the winter food cache is buried in the deepest pond, which channels will ice over first, where my alternate entrance is hidden among the roots of an undercut bank. They bump a young adult aside from a repair when necessary, their old muscles remembering, and in that moment I am their home, their archive, their unspoken memory made solid.

When the rain comes, I exhale with every pore. The moss cushions on my crown soak up the wet, growing thick and green, and ferns uncurl from my shoulders in the spray zone of the waterfall. A great blue heron sometimes perches on my highest point, a gray statue motionless for an hour, and I feel its weight like a thought that comes and goes. Moose wade below me, their antlers dripping, and I sense the thunder of their passage through the water as a subtle shift in pressure against my submerged base. The wolves leave their tracks on the marsh edge, but they do not trouble me; I am too old for their hunger.

You ask where I would find myself. I am already here, the oldest lodge, the living hill. I am not the city, but I am its seed, its anchor. The valley opens above me like a wound in the earth, and the beavers have filled it with their silent, damp, cold thriving—but it began with me, and with my kind. They will build other lodges, other dams, and I will outlast them all, a breathing repository of mud and wood, waiting for the next rain with the patient tension of something that knows itself to be complete.
