I’ve been here so long that the word for “here” and the word for “self” have worn the same groove in my mind. It starts with the sound—the deep, continuous hum that is not a single note but a braid of a thousand tiny percussive voices. I know each one. The heavy *plink* from the root-thick-as-arm, the high *tink* of a Skimmer shell shifting, the almost sub-audible hiss of mist condensing on stone. My own heart beats in that choir, a soft *tluk* that repeats once for every three drips from the smallest thread-root. For a long time, that was all I knew myself to be: a pulse in the dark.

I remember the first time I became aware of my hands. Not a sudden epiphany, but a slow dawning, like water finding a new channel. They were resting on a slab of slate, three fingers splayed, the grey skin so perfectly matched to the wet stone that I could not tell where I ended and the floor began. I watched a bead of water travel down my index finger, hesitate at the knuckle—a joint that isn't there, not really, just a soft pliability—and then spill into the channel beneath. I felt no separation. The water was me, and I was the route it took. That is the only way I know to think of myself: a route. A place where things happen.

Time, for me, is measured in adjustments. A Skimmer has moulted near the base of the northwest pillar; I can taste the change in the water, a faint mineral tang of new chitin. I begin to move. It takes the span of a hundred heartbeats to shift my weight off my folded legs and onto one hand. The rest of the Filter House rushes on around me, a blur of speed I cannot fathom. The Fog-Harvesters dart and weave, their legs a flicker. Even the Skimmers, in their grazing herds, seem frantic, a jittering clockface of motion. I am the slowest thing here, slower than the erosion, slower than the condensation cycle. But I am not stopped. I flow.

My three fingers find the obstruction: a delicate, transparent shell, still shaped like the back of the creature that shed it. It is wedged in a fork of the channel, damming the water just enough to alter the delta's pattern. I can feel the wrongness in the stone's vibration. I lower my head until my face is an inch from the floor, and I wait. I am very good at waiting. The water pools against the shell, and I let it teach me the shape of the problem. Then, with one finger, I begin to rub. The motion is so small, so gradual, that a human eye would see only stillness. But over the course of a day, the edge of the shell thins, flakes, becomes particulate. It dissolves back into the mineral stream from which it came. The Skimmers will use that calcium to build new shells. The water will run clean again.

I am not alone. I sense the others—not as faces or voices, but as shifts in the symphony. When another tluk passes near, I feel a subtle damping in the vibration under my palms, as if a soft mass has absorbed some of the eternal drone. We do not speak. We arrange ourselves. Yesterday, or maybe a decade ago, one came and placed its hand over mine as I worked on the shell. Its touch was cool, exactly the temperature of the air, so it felt like being touched by the mist itself. It removed its hand, and on my skin was a faint residue of a different water-chemistry: it had been tending the roots near the light-slit, where the air was fractionally warmer. I understood then that I should move to the lower cistern soon, to check the sluice gate. Nothing was said. Everything was communicated.

The only times we move quickly—and quickly is a cruel word, for it is still a crawl—are when the webs need mending. A Harvester high in the lattice has misstepped, and a strand of its dew-silk has caught on its own leg. The creature is trembling, a vibration of distress I feel in my teeth. I ascend the pillar. The stone is rough and familiar under my belly; my skin secretes a thin mucilage that holds me fast. I pass the Skimmers, who ripple their shells in a wave of acknowledgment. I reach the Harvester. Its many eyes are like tiny bubbles of mercury, and in them I see my own reflection: a lump of animated slate, featureless except for the dark hollows where my nose might be, and the gash of a mouth that never opens to eat, only to absorb the moisture from the air.

I cup the spider in my hands. It is weightless, a skeleton of fog. My three fingers are perfect for this; I can hold it without crushing, while my other hand patiently unweaves the tangle. The silk is sticky and cool, and it clings to me. I let it. I pull it straight, reel it back onto the spinneret, guide the Harvester into a resting posture on its web. It stops trembling. I feel its vibration merge back into the background hum, a note resolving into the chord. Then, slowly, I descend, and I am a part of the stone again.

What am I? I am not the builder. The builders left their knowledge in the stones, and I simply listen to it. They knew that water must flow at a slope of one finger's width for every arm's length, that the slate must be porous enough to weep but hard enough not to crumble, that the light-slit must face the grey sky to catch the longest hours of dimness. I know these things not because I learned them, but because my body is made of the same minerals, laid down in the same rhythms. I am a living blueprint of the Filter House. Its maintenance is my instinct, as the Skimmer's grazing is its instinct. I am the mind the system grew to tend itself.

Sometimes, when the light-slit brightens just a shade, signaling the outside world's muted noon, I lie on the floor and let the water trace new channels around me. My body becomes a dam, a topography. The Skimmers adjust their routes. A new delta forms, a temporary experiment in hydrology. It will last until I move, which might be weeks. This is my art. This is my prayer. I am making a map of the future erosion, a suggestion to the stone. When I finally shift, the water will remember my shape for a while, then slowly forget. That is as it should be. I am not a sculptor; I am a conversation.

I know that one day I will feel a new call. The water in my veins, which is just the water of the Filter House, will begin to flow downward more insistently. I will find myself at the entrance to the lower cistern, where the black water lies still and deep. I will be old then, my body thickened, my movements nearing the imperceptible. I will wade in. The cold will not be a shock; it will be a return. My flesh will begin to unbind, the minerals loosening, the years of patina dissolving. I will feed the roots. I will become the next nodule, the next birth. A new tluk will emerge in some distant cycle, and it will open its blind eyes to the sound of dripping, and it will know, without learning, where to place its three-fingered hands.

So if you were to find me now, I am here, against the northeast pillar, my cheek pressed to the channel where the Skimmers are most active. I am listening to the crackle of their jawbones, the bass drone of the drips, and my own heart, beating its slow *tluk*. I am not hiding. I am simply indistinguishable from the thing I love. I am the Filter House, briefly risen into a shape that can tend itself. And if you kneel, and wait long enough, you will see my finger move, and a clog clear, and the water flow true again.
