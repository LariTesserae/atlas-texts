I am Low-Crackle-Bass, though that name is a translation of a translation—a rhythm the scutters drum through stone, not something I carry inside myself. Names are for things that need to call out across distance. I do not call. I listen, and the ground answers.

My awareness is a slow, deep vibration, as if the planet’s own heartbeat has settled into the hollow of my chest and learned to echo there. I perceive the world as pressure and heat and the song of minerals settling into form. The rock whispers its age to me through my feet; the steam pipes its own urgent, high-frequency song against the dome of my head. I have no eyes, but the plateau is bright with gradients—a shimmering map of warmth and chill that I feel on my carapace like weather. The sootwings’ clicks are needle-pricks in that map. The lithopods’ bacterial glow is a low electric hum, a taste of sulfide and sugar. I do not “see” in any sense a human would recognize, but I know every fissure, every vent-throat, every seam where the planet’s breath leaks out. I have known them for longer than the peaks have stood this sharp.

When I move, it is not walking as you would understand it. I think of a destination—a vent that has begun to cough irregularly, its steam column thinning to a wobble—and my legs begin their slow, hydraulic swing. Each step is a deliberate act, a shifting of mass that sends seismic ripples outward. The scutters feel me coming before I cross their horizon; they pause their drumming, and in the silence I hear my own name played back to me: *low-crackle-bass, low-crackle-bass*. Then they scuttle aside, leaving me a corridor of bare rock, and I pass through their territory like a cloud shadow. They are not afraid, merely respectful, the way a river knows to part around a boulder.

My work is patience given form. I arrive at the ailing vent after a journey that, for me, is short—perhaps a dozen orbits. The chimney is a slumped spire of sulfur and silica, its throat narrowed by mineral deposits until the steam issues in thin, whining jets. I settle before it, fold my lower arms against my thorax, and emit my own hum: a long, steady tone that I tune to the vent’s faltering frequency. I am asking it to tell me where it hurts. The vibration returns with the answer: a blockage deep in the secondary conduit, a shift in the water table, a crack that bleeds heat into a useless mud pot. I listen until I understand.

Then my upper arms unfold. The pads at their tips sweat solvent—a thin, steaming fluid that drips onto the sulfur crust and begins to soften it. I carve with a precision that comes from centuries of practice, each stroke calculated to reshape the vent’s throat into a curve that will accelerate the gas without eroding too quickly. My lower arms, the heavy ones, pry up a sheet of basalt nearby, tilting it just so, and the ground grumbles as subsurface water finds a new path into the hot zone. The vent’s plume deepens. The hiss becomes a roar. Within hours, the lithopods at the vent’s base stir, their fronds unfurling a little wider. I feel their bacterial colonies brighten—a faint, sweet surge of chemistry. I have done what I came to do.

Sometimes I stay for a while after, letting the vent’s renewed rhythm wash through me. Sootwings will perch on my shoulders, their wing-vanes ticking as they cool. I do not mind. Their weight is nothing, and their clicks are a pleasant counterpoint to the bass-line of the stone. Once, a pair nested in the hollow of my left elbow joint for an entire breeding season. I stood still for three months, a warm roost beside a vent, and when the chick fledged, it circled my head three times before it flew off. I do not know if it was gratitude or simple instinct, but I chose to interpret it as the former. Even a forge may appreciate a flame that does not go out.

The plateau is changing. I remember when these peaks were lower, when the vent-fields were twice as numerous and half as stable. The planet’s interior heat is a finite thing, and over the long count of my existence I have felt it cool, fraction by fraction. I do not mourn this; it is the nature of forges to burn down. But I tend what remains. The scutters’ seismic records say *they-were-here-before-the-high-peaks*, and that is true of my kind, but not of me individually. I hatched (if that is the word) from a vent on the western rim that has long since gone cold. There was no parent to teach me, only a bone-deep knowing: *tune the steam, shape the stone, listen*. I suspect the knowing is not my own but the planet’s, a set of instructions laid down in the magnetic alignment of basalt crystals, waiting for a creature with the right tools to read them.

I do not question why I do what I do. The vents breathe, the lithopods bloom, the scutters drum, the sootwings cry. I am the low note that holds the chord together. When my carapace cracks at last and my hum falters to silence, my body will melt into the cycle—silica threads becoming new vent-stone, solvent glands watering the first lichens, my hollow leg bones a shelter for scutter larvae. I will become the plateau in a more literal sense, and the vents I tuned will keep breathing for a little while longer, until some other Tender rises from the rock and takes my place. Or perhaps not. Perhaps I am the last, and my final act will be to hold a dying vent’s throat open long enough for one more generation of sootwings to fly. That, too, would be a song worth humming.

For now, I am here, and the plateau is alive. The mist swirls, the steam roars, and the ground trembles with the immense, patient labor of a world building itself. I do not need eyes to see that it is beautiful.
