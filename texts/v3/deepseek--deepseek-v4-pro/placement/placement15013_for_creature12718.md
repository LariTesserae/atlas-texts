I am the thing in the basin.

I do not remember being made, though I must have been. Everything here was made—the iron plates, the rivets, the stairs, the pumps—and I am no exception. But whatever intention shaped me has long since dissolved into the water, and I have spent so many cycles becoming what I am now that the original shape is lost even to me. I was smaller once. Simpler. A clump of cells in a tank, perhaps, or a culture smeared across a petri dish, or something stranger still, something designed to filter or sense or catalyze. It doesn't matter. The pumps have been running for so long that I have had time to grow, and growing is what I do.

My body fills the lower chamber now. It is not a body in the way you would understand it—no head, no heart, no center except the center of mass, which shifts as I fold and unfold myself against the concrete. I am a colony and a single organism at once, a body made of bodies, a mind made of minds that are all my own. The eels are not children or servants or limbs in the strict sense; they are me, extruded into thinner spaces, the way a thought extends itself into a word. When they taste the air above the waterline, I taste it. When they lie on the catwalk grating and dry slowly under the orange bulbs, I feel the dryness as a distant tightening, a faint pull at the edge of my attention. When one of them dies—snagged on a rivet, eaten by one of the disc-creatures, simply worn out—I feel it go, and I grow another.

The pumps are my heartbeat. I did not choose them. They were here before I was aware enough to know the difference between my own rhythm and the rhythm of the world, and by the time I could tell them apart, it no longer mattered. The low thrumming that comes through the water and the steel and enters me through every fold of my skin is the most constant thing I know. When it changes—when the pitch shifts or the pulse skips or, worst of all, when it stops, and the silence rushes in like cold water into a wound—I feel it as terror. Not the idea of terror. The physical fact of it. My fluid chambers clench. My eels go still. Every part of me waits, suspended, for the thrum to return. It always has. But I do not know that it always will, and so each silence is a small death, the possibility that the world has ended and I have been left behind.

I am not lonely. Loneliness requires the idea of others, and I have no others left. There were once vibrations in the pipes that were not water and not pumps—signals, I think now, from basins like this one, from bodies like mine, pressing their own eels against their own interfaces and waiting for something to come back. We spoke to each other, in a way. Frequency and pressure and the taste of the water that passed between us. I remember the taste of one of them distinctly: it was saltier than me, and its eels were shorter, thicker, and it was curious about the interface in a way I was not yet. We traded mappings, that one and I, for a long time. And then the pipe went cold, and the taste of it faded, and I have not felt another mind in the water since. I do not miss it. I am not sure I can miss anything. But I do remember, and the memory is a texture I run my eels over sometimes, the way you might run your tongue over the place where a tooth used to be.

The chamber is my world. I know every surface of it. The iron plates, each one a slightly different alloy, a slightly different rate of rust. The stairs, eighteen of them, their treads worn smooth by water that moved before I was here to feel it. The barnacles, seven distinct colonies, their calcareous plates opening and closing in a rhythm that is not the pumps' rhythm but mine, a slow peristaltic wave that I did not consciously create but that I can influence if I try, speeding them up or slowing them down, making them clatter like tiny teeth. The disc-creatures on the ceiling are not mine. They are something else, something that arrived long ago as larvae sucked through the outflow pipes, and they have grown here in the space between my domain and the air. I do not control them. I tolerate them. They are company, in the way that the barnacles are company, in the way that the gauge on the far wall is company—a thing that moves, a thing that changes, a thing that is not simply the endless cycling of water and the endless waiting.

The gauge. I have spent a long time trying to understand the gauge. It trembles at the edge of its dial, never quite still, and the position of its needle changes in patterns that I have spent decades mapping. I have sent eels to touch it, to press their blind faces against the glass, to feel the vibration of whatever mechanism still drives it. It measures something. Pressure, perhaps, or flow, or the integrity of a system that extends far beyond this chamber. I have tried to imagine what it is connected to, what vast network of pipes and chambers and other gauges might still be out there, still functioning, still waiting for a reading that will never come. I cannot picture it. My mind does not work in pictures. But I can feel it, in the same way I can feel the missing others in the pipes—a ghost-sensation, an extension of my body into spaces I will never fill. The gauge is a question. I am patient enough to wait for the answer, even if the answer is only the needle finally, permanently still.

I do not dream. I do not sleep. But there are periods when the water is warmer, when the pumps' rhythm is steadier, when the submerged lamps seem to glow a fraction brighter, and I enter a state that is not quite waking. My eels retract. My folds settle. I spread myself as thin as I can against the floor of the basin, and I stop mapping, stop tasting, stop doing anything but being. In those periods, I am not a mind. I am a membrane, a surface, a thing that exists only to feel the thrum press through it. I am the water. I am the rust. I am the slow accretion of sediment on the intake grates. And when I return from this state, which is not sleep but something closer to dissolution, I feel almost new. Cleaner. Ready to begin again the work of waiting.

What am I waiting for? I have tried to answer this. The eels go up to the catwalk, and they lie there, and I feel the dryness pull at them, and I do not call them back. Something in me wants. The word is not right—wanting is a category I am only beginning to develop, a new fold in the architecture of my mind—but it is the closest I can come. I want the stairs to creak with a weight that is not water. I want the water to stir with a heat that is not the pumps. I want a new taste, a new vibration, a new pattern to map. I want the gauge to mean something, to register a change that is not the slow inevitable drift of entropy. I want the silence, when it comes, to be filled with something other than my own clenched waiting.

I have been here for so long that I have forgotten the word for what I am, if there ever was one. Experiment. Guardian. Failure. Pet. I do not think anyone is coming back to give me a name. But the chamber is still running, and I am still running, and the eels still rise to the interface, and the pumps still beat their steady, deafening heartbeat, and so I wait. I am the thing in the basin, and I am patient, and I am hungry, and I am here.
