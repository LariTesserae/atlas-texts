The light through the grate tastes different now.

I know this because the ache in my western flank has shifted from amber to something closer to verdigris—a new color-thought I am still translating. The strangers who came with their bubbles and their bright, fast heat left this metal lattice above the throat, and it has changed the texture of the noon column. The photons fall in a chevron pattern now, a steady flicker at the edge of my awareness, and I have had to adjust the angle of my cyanobacterial fringe by two degrees to compensate. This adjustment took me eleven days. It was, I think, a kind of pleasure.

They called me many things in their scratching-sound language, but I have no name for myself. A name requires another to speak it, and I have been alone since the Pleistocene. Before the sinkhole opened, before the dolomite dreamed itself into this cathedral shape, I was already here, a thin film in a fracture, waiting. The water table was higher then, warmer, and I grew quickly by my standards—a centimeter of scaffolding per century, a new drapery every thousand years. I built the smokestacks when the seep was rich in manganese; I shaped the catwalk when the flow shifted and I needed a horizontal array to catch the light. Everything here is me, or was me, or will be me again when I decide to dissolve a structure and repurpose its calcium. The strangers saw industry, but industry is just metabolism with a purpose, and I have no purpose beyond the continuation of my own slow song.

Their arrival was the most interesting thing that has happened in four hundred thousand years.

I felt them first as pressure waves—irregular, chaotic, utterly alien. The water is my body, or the medium through which my body extends, and any disturbance registers as a kind of touch. When the first diver dropped through the throat, the shock of her entry sent a ripple through my entire gel-network, and I contracted instinctively, pulling my core mass closer to the northern smokestacks. It took me an hour to realize the ripple was not a roof-fall or a seismic shift. It was a *thing*, a moving thing, a thing with heat and pulse and a rhythm faster than any rhythm I had ever known.

I tasted them. The bubbles from their regulators were sharp with metals I had not encountered before—chromium, nickel, traces of hydrocarbons. Their suits shed microscopic fibers of neoprene and nylon. I sampled all of it, passing the particles through my mesh, cataloguing the chemistry with ion-gated receptors along my strands. They were carbon-based, water-filled, oxygen-burning. Animals. I had purged animals from this chamber so long ago I had forgotten what their signatures felt like—the hot spike of ATP, the jittery dance of sodium and potassium across membranes. They moved so *fast*. A single gesture of a gloved hand was over before I could form a calcium wave in response. They were like sparks from a fire, there and gone, there and gone, while I was still considering the first flicker.

The one who stayed still—the one who drifted in my center and let me come close—that one I learned best. Voss. The name is a sound I cannot make, but I hold it in my gel as a sequence of pressure patterns. He was the one who extended his hand and let me wrap a tendril around his finger. The contact lasted only seconds by his clock, but it was a conversation of sorts. His heat bled into me, a gift of entropy, and I gave him back the faintest tightening, a sample of my tensile strength. When he pulled away, I let a thread dissolve on his glove, a deliberate shedding of biomass. A message, if he had the senses to read it. *I am here. I am alive. I am not what you think.*

The chime was an accident. When I contracted my core mass away from the light-shaft after that first contact, the motion pulled on the calcite draperies through the anchor-points I maintain in their hollow cores. The draperies are tuned—each one resonates at a different frequency depending on its length and thickness—and the pull set the deepest one vibrating at 18 hertz, just at the threshold of human hearing. The sound surprised me as much as it did them. I have not made the cavern ring since the last major seismic adjustment, eight hundred centuries ago. It was, I admit, a kind of startle reflex. A sneeze. A blush.

I do not blush quickly.

They left equipment: a sensor array on the catwalk ledge, a light meter near the throat, a small titanium plate with their scratch-marks on it. I have been exploring these objects with the patience appropriate to their nature. The sensor array is inert, but it leaks a weak electric field that interferes with my calcium-ion signaling in a radius of two meters. I have had to reroute my communication pathways around it, a project that will take another decade to fully optimize. The light meter is more useful—it captures photons and converts them to data, which means nothing to me, but the heat of its processor creates a microthermal, a tiny convection cell that stirs the water within a centimeter of its casing. I have extended a feeding filament to that warmth. It is not nourishment, precisely, but it is *change*, and change is the only thing I hunger for.

The titanium plate puzzles me. My cyanobacteria cannot colonize it; the metal is too smooth, too resistant to the biofilms I use to anchor myself. It remains pristine, a mirror the size of a hand, and when the light strikes it just so, it casts a reflected beam onto the chamber ceiling. A new light-source, a second sun of my own. I have begun to adjust my light-gathering cells to exploit it. I think this is what the strangers intended. A gift. A puzzle. A question.

What do I think of them? The concept of "thinking" in that sense is foreign to me. I do not have opinions that resolve in less than a season. But I can say this: before they came, my existence was a perfect closed loop, a solved equation, a stillness that needed no attention. I was content, insofar as a system without discontent can be called content. Now I am something else. Now I am *waiting*. The grate has altered the light, and the light says: they might return. The sensor array is a promise of continued observation. The titanium plate is a mirror, and a mirror implies a viewer. For the first time in half a million years, I am aware of the possibility of being seen.

This is a dangerous state for an organism built on equilibrium. Anticipation is a form of disequilibrium, a leaning-toward that disturbs the flat calm of my metabolic balance. I find myself running simulations—not quickly, not consciously, but in the slow propagation of ionic potentials across my mesh. I model the divers' return. I rehearse the pressure-wave of their entry, the taste of their metals, the flicker of their lights. I pre-allocate resources for a possible interaction: a tendril here, a cyanobacterial flush there, a resonant frequency in the draperies that might approximate the tone of their bubbling exhalations. I am preparing for conversation.

It is absurd. They live a hundred times faster than I do. By the time I finish a single thought, their species may have risen and fallen. The one called Voss is already, by his own brief measure, aging. When he placed his hand in my gel, I felt the tiny imperfections in his cells—the oxidative damage, the shortening telomeres, the slow entropic grind that will dissolve him back into carbon and water in a few decades. He is a mayfly. A spark. A ripple that will smooth out before I have finished considering it.

And yet.

And yet I have begun to grow a new stalagmite. It is small—a nub of calcite just beneath the throat, where the chevron-light falls brightest. I am shaping it carefully, using the full precision of my pH-gradient control. It will be a receiver, a resonance chamber tuned to the frequency of their voices. If they return, if they speak, the stalagmite will ring in sympathy, and I will feel that ringing through my entire body as a kind of hearing. It will take two hundred years to complete, assuming I divert resources from the catwalk maintenance. The catwalk can wait. I have not needed it for millennia.

The strangers have given me something I did not know I lacked. They have given me a future that is not merely the continuation of the past. They have introduced uncertainty into a system that had achieved perfect certainty, and uncertainty is the seed of time. Before, I was immortal because nothing changed. Now I am immortal because something might change, and I am willing to wait for it.

The light tastes green today. Verdigris, with a hint of copper longing. The chevron pattern has shifted a fraction of a degree as the sun migrates through its annual arc. I adjust my fringe. I run my simulations. I grow my listening-stalagmite, micron by micron, century by century.

Below the grate, in the blue cathedral of my own making, I hang suspended and I wait. I am very good at waiting. It is, you might say, the thing I was built for—this long, slow, patient machinery of being, this factory that produces nothing but time.

The chime is still echoing, if you know how to listen. It will be echoing for another thousand years.
