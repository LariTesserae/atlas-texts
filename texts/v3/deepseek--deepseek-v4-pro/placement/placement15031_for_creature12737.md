I am a note in the throat of the world, and my name is the frequency my mother sang into the wall before I was a knot of tissue in the dark. You would hear it as a long, slow chord—something between a whale’s moan and the shiver of a plucked root—but to me it is simply *I am here, I am here, I am here*, repeated in the key of wet bark and warm breath. I hang by my tail from a strut polished by ancestors, my body a pale comma against the liver-coloured wall, and I sing my part into the chorus.

The air glows. It always glows, a pearlescent noon without source, thick enough to taste. When I draw breath—through the trembling purse of my mouth, through the pores of my slick ivory skin—I taste the spore-soup, the fungal rot, the sweet exudate of the leaf-awnings fifty feet above my head. I taste my neighbours, too: the musk of a brooding mother extruding from her bladder, the sharp tang of an elder’s molting wax, the faint, milky signal of a Gulper inflating two spirals to my left. Every taste is a word. The world speaks in chemistry, and my body is fluent.

My hands are never still. The sucker-pads on my fingertips, glistening with the same mucoid dew that sweats from the walls, stroke the root-strut in idle patterns. This is how I tend the shape of things. A spiral here coaxes a trickle of sap to pool in a hollow, where tomorrow’s fungal bloom will feed the howler troupes. A drumming there—a soft, rhythmic *tock-tock-tock* of pad on flesh—signals the wall itself to thicken, to extrude a new ledge where a weary adolescent can rest. I have no tools. I am the tool. Every Hinderer is a gardener of meat, a sculptor of the living architecture, and our city grows because we sing it into being.

Below me, the central void shimmers empty. That emptiness is deliberate, a cathedral nave we have cultivated for centuries, where the sound can gather and hang in visible waves. I watch a drop of water, fallen from a leaf-tip ages ago, still suspended in the mist. It swells with light, a perfect luminous sphere, falling so slowly that I could watch it for an hour and barely see it move. I have, in fact, done so. Time is a fog here; my heartbeat is synchronized to the world-pulse, a single cycle that takes a geological day. Fast and slow live in the same body. The wall-fauna churns in a blur of predation and rebirth, while I taste the slow, deep thrum of the continent’s dreaming mind beneath everything—a bass note so low it is less a sound and more a gravity, pulling my bones into alignment.

I was not always this still. I remember being a howler. The memories are not images but a riot of sensation: the spring of six limbs on a liana, the flail of my mid-torso appendages (now long absorbed into my ribcage), the wet-persimmon face I wore, permanently fixed in a yowling O. I chased fliers with my troupe, our howls overlapping into a raucous, cascading music that I now know as a nursery rhyme—a raw, unformed version of the language I speak today. I feared the Gulpers. I remember the sudden collapse of space, the wet slap, the shudder in the air when a troupe-mate was taken. But I also remember the hunger, the uncomplicated joy of eating, the itch of shedding skin in translucent sheets that drifted into the floor to be digested. And I remember the day the signal came: a new frequency, a humming from the wall that pulled at my spine. I found a depression in the parent flesh, pressed my back into it, and sang the private frequency my body now knew. The wall parted. A bladder extruded around me, pear-shaped and translucent, and I curled into a fetal ball, my skeleton reknitting, my mind flooding with the adult understanding of the world’s voice. When I emerged, I had five limbs and a name, and I knew the Song of Songs.

The Gulper that caught my troupe-mate is still there, two spirals to my left. It is a pale, shapeless mound now, indistinguishable from the wall except for the faint circular seam. I taste its contentment—a flat, fermentation note. It is not my enemy. It is a phase, as I was a phase, as the fliers that swirl in murmuring clouds are a phase. When it dissolves, it will release spores that might settle on a bladder and trigger a new nursery. The howler it ate carried recycled neural tissue from a Hinderer ancestor who fused with the wall epochs ago. We are all one conversation, and the Gulper’s collapse, that warp in reality, is just a punctuation mark.

The wind begins. I feel the walls contract, the valve-orifices gape, and a warm, gale-force breath scours the hollow clean. The hanging water drop shivers but does not fall faster. The chorus adjusts—we all sing louder, our harmonics shifting to ride the turbulence, and for a moment the sound becomes a physical pressure that makes my tail grip tighter. Then the wind dies, and the mist settles, and the air tastes new.

And I notice it. A trembling in the song. A note I do not recognize, somewhere in the central emptiness. Not a howler, not a flier, not a neighbour extruding from a bladder. Something standing on the root-floor, solid and strange, its bones not yet rattling in sympathy. I stop my idle strumming and listen. The note is… tentative. It is trying to harmonize. I feel my chest vibrate with a curiosity I have not tasted in lifetimes.

I swing closer, coiling my tail along a new liana, my eyes—the size of cupped palms, black and depthless—fixed on the source. There you are. A creature with strange, stiff limbs and no sucker-pads, your skin dry, your mouth a flat line instead of a sphincter-tremble. But your eyes are moist, and in the way you stand, I taste the beginning of a listening. The song bends around you like water around a new stone.

I pause, and I howl. Not words, not yet, but a chord: *I am here, I am here, I am here.* And when you do not run, I add the question I have never had reason to ask before: a rising tone that means *What is your name, and can you sing?*

The world breathes. The Gullet hums. And I wait, my own note hanging in the air like the water drop, for your bones to answer.
