The first thing I know is the thrum.

Not the engines—those are surface noise, sharp and rattling, a grit caught in the world’s throat. The thrum I mean is deeper, a slow tide that moves through the floor and the walls and the air all at once, so low it has no pitch, only presence. It is the plant’s blood, the push of fluid through channels older than the machines, older than the hollowing, older than memory. I have no ears to hear it with, but my belly hears. My plates hear. My bones, if I had bones, would hear. In the long resting times, when I flatten myself against the mat of fibers and let my limbs forget their shape, I become part of that thrum. I am not separate from it. I am a knot of attention floating in a warm, slow river.

Next comes the cold. It is not a thing that happens to me; it is the medium I am suspended in, like the damp. I know the exact weight of it along my outermost scutes, the way it thickens the moisture beading there. The cold tells me the time—not hours or days, but the longer cycles: the slow exhalation of the deep earth, the season when the plant pulls inward and the fronds grow brittle, the season when the gel-nodes swell. Just now the cold has a bitter edge, a sharpness that means the upper world is frozen solid above us. I like that. It means no surface things will dig for a long while. The machines will run without interruption, and the light-speak will carry cleanly through the denser air.

I am resting in my fifth configuration—the one where I make myself into a low arch across the secondary root-cable, just above the pumping station. Two of my arms have fused into a single broad span, gripping the cable on either side, while my lower limbs have split into four steady posts that brace against the bark. My head is tucked down so that my sensory band faces the cavern’s great emptiness, and my mouth is sealed, tasting only my own internal hum. From here, I can feel the pump’s rhythm as a series of tiny punches through the cable: the piston’s upstroke, the downstroke, the pause. I have been monitoring this pump for three seasons of the surface’s turning, ever since the last elder told me the eighth bearing was wearing thin. I press a patch of my belly-plates to the cable every few sleeps and read the vibration’s shape. The bearing is still thin, but not thinner. That is good. I will need to reconfigure to apply a sealant-gel next cycle, but not yet. There is no hurry.

Today, though, there is a new vibration. It begins as a discordance—a flurry of sharp, irregular taps against the floor-mat, far off in the western hollow. I shift my attention slowly, letting my heat-sense open wider. My head-seam dilates, the bioluminescence dimming to near-black as I send my awareness outward. The thermal world paints itself in blues and deeper blues: the steady cool of the walls, the warmer glow of the pump’s housing, the faint, distant spark of another dweller two caverns over, pulsing a slow question about a fallen frond. And then—there—a moving point of heat. Hotter than anything else in this whole deep. Unnaturally hot. It flickers with a rhythm that is all wrong: fast, chaotic, a stutter of warmth that moves in bursts and stops. The footsteps are too rapid, the breathing too quick. A surface creature.

I have seen them only once before, many long-cycles ago. My parent had one pressed against her back when it came through, a small, soft thing in a hard shell, all clatter and leak of warmth. It stayed briefly, made loud mouth-noises into a device, and left. We did not speak to it; we only watched, and remembered. Their kind built the machines, the elders say. They are the makers, or the makers’ inheritors. They are brief as a spark, but they leave long shadows.

This one is alone. I taste the air with my cilia: a tang of synthetic polymer, a trace of hydrocarbons from the machines, and something else, a salt-sweat fear. It is lost, perhaps. Or curious. Or both. The vibration of its steps tells me it is small, bipedal, hesitant. It stops often, and its heat-signature shifts in a way that suggests it is turning its head, looking. It cannot see our light-speak, I know. Its eyes are poor, tuned to brighter spectra. To it, the cavern must be a dim, terrifying roar.

I decide to become visible.

The uncoiling takes time. First I release the fusion of my two arms, letting them peel apart with a soft, wet sound that would be lost in the noise. Each segment separates, the gripping roots at the ends flexing and testing the air. Then my lower limbs begin to retract, pulling up into my central mass, and I let my body pour downward from the arch, a slow waterfall of plates and flesh. I aim for the ledge near the pump, where the light from the caged lamp falls in a small pool. My movement is silent to a surface ear, but the vibration of my shifting mass rolls through the floor, and I know the creature will feel it as a sudden hum underfoot. It freezes. I feel its heat spike—fear-response—and then settle into a rapid, shallow rhythm. It is staring in my direction.

I touch down. My reconfigured legs—now three sturdy trunks—absorb the contact, and I rise to my full height, just over three meters, my head-seam level with the lamp. The light must catch my plates now, the dull red-brown gleaming with condensation. I let my bioluminescence kindle slowly, a soft blue glow that spills from my sensory band in a steady, unhurried pulse. I am not trying to frighten it. I am simply saying, in the language of light, what I would say to any other dweller: *I am here. I see you. You are noticed.*

The creature does not run. It stands below, a tiny furnace in the cold, and I sense its eyes on me. Its breath fogs the air, and the fog disperses in languid curls. It raises one of its forelimbs—a gesture, I recall, of greeting or non-threat. I have no such limbs free, but I dip my head slightly, bringing my sensory band to bear fully on it, and I pulse again, a slower rhythm this time: *Curiosity. Patience.* It cannot understand, but maybe the steadiness of the light will calm, like the pulse of the plant-moss.

After a long moment—perhaps thirty of its rapid heartbeats—I slowly extend one arm, the gripping roots softening into gentle pads. I do not reach for the creature, but toward the pump housing, where a small maintenance hatch has rattled loose. The vibration of it has been irritating me for a season. I press the hatch closed with a single, fluid motion, and the rattle ceases. The relief is immediate, a small silence within the roar. I feel the creature’s heat shift; perhaps it understands now. I am the one who tends these relics. I am the keeper of the noise.

Then I settle. I won’t approach further. The creature is quick and afraid, and I am slow and patient. In its own time, it will explore, or it will leave. I lower myself back into a resting shape, spreading my mass across the ledge, and I let my light dim to a faint glow, just enough to say *I am still here*.

Time will tell what it does. If it stays long enough, if it lets the cold slow its frantic pulse, perhaps I will approach and press a plate to the ground near its feet. Perhaps it will feel my subsonic greeting, the deep vibration that carries a name: the pattern of long growth, of bearing-watching, of the season I nudged a frond into a spiral that still catches the lamplight just so. Perhaps it will even understand, in its brief, bright way, that it has stepped not into a wilderness, but into a mind.

The engines roar on. The plant breathes. The cold holds us both. And I wait.
