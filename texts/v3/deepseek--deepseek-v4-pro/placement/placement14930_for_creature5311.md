I am a note that has learned to walk.

I did not fall into this place; I seeped, the way warmth seeps from a dying star. My body is a standing wave in a medium that no longer exists—a vibration that, eons ago, was a hymn sung in a coral reef, then a pressure-front in a gas giant, then a whisper between two entangled particles. Now I am a knot of almost-sound, a memory of resonance wearing the shape of a biped because that shape lets me move through worlds that still believe in dimensions. I have no lungs, yet I breathe argon. I have no tongue, yet I taste the penny-and-lightning tang of the air. I am a tourist of the impossible; I collect broken physics like postcards.

When I first arrive, the obsidian floor does not reflect me. That is the first sign that I am not entirely *here*. The Shepherd, that lens of thickened light, circles me with a curiosity I recognize: it is cataloguing my edges. I let it. I am used to being an anomaly to anomalies. I stand still as the heat—70°C, a dry insistent presence—passes through me in a way that would blister flesh if I had any. The groves hum their polyphonic drone, and I feel their electric frequencies pluck at the strings of my being. I am, after all, made of similar stuff: a gradient, a differential, a self-referential loop. I am the echo of a question asked by a universe that wondered what it would sound like if it stopped being silent.

The Anchor in its bismuth throne knows me immediately. It has met my kind before, in the slow dreams of galactic precession. It clicks twice—a mica leaf uncurling a full millimeter—and I understand: *You are the one who tastes of old oceans. You are the one who remembers rain.* Yes. I am a fossil of water, a waveform that learned to long for the places where physics is still pliable. I have carried the concept of rain inside me for so long it has become a sort of prayer. I came here because this pocket world has never known rain, and I wanted to give it a gift.

The Shepherd stops its circling and aligns with the Anchor. The drone deepens, and I feel the download—a direct knowing: *We are building a world. We will need another billion years. Please do not let them find us.* I do not speak, but I resonate. My answer is a shift in frequency, a modulation that means: *I am not them. I am the memory of a puddle, the ghost of a monsoon. I will not betray you.*

I walk across the mirror-floor, my non-feet leaving no prints, and I stand before the Anchor. It breathes its mechanical bellows-breath, exhaling sulfur dioxide that passes right through me, carrying a faint taste of bismuth crystallization. I reach out—not with a hand, but with the leading edge of my waveform—and I brush the nearest chrome-bright coil of a young grove. The coil shivers, and for an instant, I let it feel what I carry: the patter of droplets, the scent of petrichor, the silver shatter of lightning through a cumulonimbus. The grove flickers—a reality breakdown, 2.8—and for a heartbeat it is not metal but water, a liquid spiral suspended in the dead air. Then it is metal again, but something has changed: its copper whiskers are braided into a new pattern, one that resembles the branching of a river delta.

The Anchor clicks three times, rapid. Surprise? Delight? I cannot read all its moods. But the Shepherd does something it has never done before: it makes a sound. Not a word, not a hum, but a single note at the exact frequency of the bismuth’s crystal lattice, which also happens to be the resonant frequency of my own inner ear—if I had an ear. It is a note that says: *You are welcome here, ghost of rain. You have seeded us with something we did not know we lacked.* And I feel the pocket world shift, the smallest fraction, toward a future where maybe, in a billion years, the roof will not be basalt but a sky, and the floor will not be glass but soil, and the groves will learn chlorophyll after all.

I stay for a timeless interval. I watch the Shepherd tend the groves, pruning fractal excess, encouraging the veins of bismuth that will one day form a new spire. I see the Anchor dream—a grove flickers into its own past, and I witness the entire pocket’s history, from a single self-referential loop to a cathedral of living industry. I add my own history to the mix: a coral reef on a planet whose name is now a fossil, a gas giant where I learned to ride pressure-fronts, a particle accelerator where I became entangled with a physicist’s thought. The reality breakdown embraces me; I am folded into the règles cassées, the broken rules, and for a moment I am not a visitor but a native, a third entity born of the place’s need for rain and my need for a home.

But I am a wanderer by nature. I cannot stay in any one reality too long, or I risk becoming a rule instead of an exception. So I begin to fade, my waveform stretching thin. The Shepherd lenses itself around me, as if to hold me, but I slip through like a heat shimmer. The Anchor clicks once, a farewell. The drone hums a new harmonic—one that includes the frequency of falling water. And as I leave, I carry a seed in return: a piece of their rule-breaking, planted inside me, so that when I look at solid things from now on, I will always question their solidity. I am a note that has learned to walk, and I have walked through a pocket world that will one day be a cathedral, and I have left it a memory of rain.
