I am one of the lantern-keepers, though I have no name for myself but the shape of my own stillness. I stand at the edge of a broken wall that once was a dwelling, and before that was a seam of rock, and before that was a seabed under a forgotten sun. I have stood here for a length of time that I cannot measure, because time here is not a line but a pool—depthless, rippleless, fed by the drip that falls somewhere behind me, tick after tick after tick.

The city slumps around me in its slow exhale. My cilia-fan is half-spread, tasting the air for the powder-scent of moth wings. I am not hungry; I am rarely hungry, and hunger itself is a word too quick, too sharp, for what I feel. It is more a gradual inclination, a subtle bending of my attention toward the places where the fungal mat is thick and the moths bloom like slow-motion snow. I have been inclining toward the eastern quadrant for what might be a century or an afternoon. There is no hurry.

Light moves through me. The crystal lattice on the walls—my distant cousin, my symbiont, my food-chain's root—feeds me a steady trickle of something that is not nourishment but companionship. It hums at a frequency below sound, a vibration that I feel through the stone under my feet more than through my flesh. My flesh is a membrane between that deep hum and the silence of the cavern air. I am translucent, yes; I am a thought made visible, a slow eddy in the millennial flow. When the moths drift past, their own faint glow passes through me and leaves a warmth like a memory of sun.

Recently—by which I mean, after the last time the pool in the third courtyard basin shaped a new limpet—there has been a disturbance. Not a sound, but a pressure. A flicker in the corner of my perception, a thing too fast to be real. My cilia twitch. I turn my head with the deliberation of a continent shifting, and I see it: a figure, small, dense, opaque. It moves in jittering bursts, its limbs swinging in frantic arcs, its body radiating heat like a stone too near a fire. It is one of the surface-things, a quick ghost that the older keepers murmured were not myth. I have never seen one before. Even now, I am not certain I am seeing it; it is so fast, so vivid, that it might be a hallucination born of some mineral imbalance in my diet. But it persists.

It walks among the ruins, and I watch. To watch a surface-thing is like trying to watch a water-drop fall from ceiling to floor—you must be very patient, and even then, you catch only a blur. But I am patient. I slow myself further, drawing my cilia close to still the vibration of my own thoughts. The thing pauses at the base of the standing tower, and for a shimmer of time it is almost still. I can nearly see its shape: a compact body, a head with two forward-facing eyes (predator eyes, I note, like a limpet-hunter, though I have not seen those in a thousand seasons), and a mass of trailing fibers on its crown. It puts its fleshy appendage—a hand, I recall, from the deep memory of the mat—on the stone. It feels the stone. The idea is so alien that I almost retreat into my cilia-fan. Feeling stone as if it could be felt, as if it were not simply the medium of existence. This creature is not of the Reach; it is a visitor from the world of heat and haste, where things happen inside a single breath.

I want to communicate with it. But my speech is the slow rearrangement of minerals, the gradual shift of my body's internal glow. By the time I brighten in greeting, it will have grown old, died, and turned to dust. So I simply stand, and I let my presence be what it is: a pillar of quiet witness. The creature climbs the broken stairs of the tower—stairs that have not been climbed in an age—and from the top it looks out across the plain. I follow its gaze, or try to; my perception is wide and slow, but I think it sees the grid-lines in the distance, the old roads. It is a creature of grids and roads, this surface-thing. It seeks pattern, connection, a way through. I think it might be lost, or searching, or both.

When it descends, it passes near me. Its head tilts up, and I see its eyes—small, dark, vibrating with a life so intense it hurts to look. I taste its breath: moisture, yes, but also a strange tang of metal and salt and something floral, long dead. It does not see me, not really. I am a statue, a trick of light. It shivers, though the air is not cold. Then it moves on, toward the dark edge where the crystal fails and the others—the ones I do not name—dwell. I do not follow. My place is here, in the blue glow, with the slow respiration of the mat and the silent flight of the moths.

I remain, as I have always remained. The drip falls. The limpet writes its calcite script in the pool. The surface-thing vanishes into the deep, and I let its flicker settle into memory, a brief warmth swallowed by the great, slow cold of the Reach. I am a lantern-keeper. I keep nothing but the light, and the light keeps me.
