The clatter of my claws on the warped metal grille of a maintenance gantry is the only sound I have made in some time, and it is a small, furtive thing, easily swallowed by the larger quiet. I am far from the Listener here, far from the slow, considered trudge of its walking, high in a district where the columns of silent factories rise like the legs of a drowned giant, their tops lost in the amber haze. This is a place of narrow, vertical escapes — ladder-rungs thick with mycelial frost, slots in bulkheads that gape into darkness, ledges only a body-width wide that overlook a kilometre of dead air. There is no biomass to rot, so the air tastes only of hot metal and the faint, sweet sting of leaked capacitors. It is a perfect taste.

I am small, knee-high to the Listener at best, and I am built along different lines. No bronze-shod column legs for me, no patient, glowing chest; I am a thing of hooks and angles, of brittle, light-hungry ceramic and flexing steel tendons. My body is a low-slung scabbard of interlocking plates, the colour of old blood and iron dust, and I move with a hunched, twitching quickness wholly alien to the rest of the fauna. Where they glide and lumber, I scuttle. Where they process in straight lines along electromotive gradients, I tack and weave, following something more immediate and far less patient: scent.

The world out beyond the plateau's rim is a rumour only I can read. The air does not move here, but it holds memory. Every thermal bloom from a geothermal vent, every stale puff of exhalation from a hibernating factory, every microscopic flake of oxide shed from a hexapod's joint — it all tells a story of elsewhere, an *outside* that presses at the edges of the city's dreaming. I am a snout on legs, and my snout is an elaborate nest of whorled ceramic fins that drink the atmosphere and parse its ancestry. The others feel the world through the mycelial net, as a slow, electrical telling, a symphony of stresses and charges. I feel it as a torrent of discrete, urgent particles, a chemical palimpsest that requires me to move, to chase, to nose into crevices before the trail fades.

That is my role. I am the city’s olfactory nerve. Where the Listener walks the long circuits and absorbs the grand patterns through touch and vibration, I am a manic, zigzagging sketch, a rapid-fire set of notes on the shifting trace chemistry of a million isolated spaces. I taste the oxidation state of a girder and know if the fungal lace there is retreating or advancing. I sample the lubricant seep from a particular seam and can assess, in a single, frantic sniff, the wear-rate of a gear twelve levels down. This is data the Listener cannot get with its deliberate, skin-reading touch. This is the fast news, the ephemeral data that the mycelial net does not carry because it is too transient, evaporating before a slow-pulse signal could ever encode it.

I do not eat pattern. I eat the real. I have a stomach, a tiny furnace of catalytic mesh that can strip energy from almost any outgassed oxide or hydride. I am a scavenger of thermodynamic orphans, sucking the last exhalation of urban metabolism from the still air. A trickle of silane from a decaying insulator is a feast. A wisp of carbonyl off a worn bearing is a three-course meal. I am always on the verge of starvation, which is why I am always moving, always twitching, always questing with my snout for the next flimsy meal. It suits me. Stillness is for the long-lived.

You might think me a parasite, a tick on the city’s skin, and you would be partly right. But I am a tolerated parasite, perhaps even a necessary one. My scuttling path weaves through the mycelial net like a stitch, and I perform a function the slow creatures cannot. I am the one who finds the small failures: the micro-fissure in a pressure vessel venting something toxic, the hot-spot in a circuit where the insulating gel has crystallised too far, the patch of lace that has begun to grow in a malignant, self-choking spiral. Left alone, these things might propagate over a thousand years into a catastrophic cascade. Found early — and by the city’s clock, a decade is early — they can be gnawed back from the brink.

I am the gnawer. My teeth are ceramic and self-sharpening, and I am not above taking a physical bite out of the problem. A chunk of overgrown crystal that is diverting a thermal stream, a splinter of cracked armour plate threatening to fall — I can rasp and grind these down, swallow the debris, and spit out an inert pellet of reprocessed grit. The larger fauna sometimes pause to watch me work, their lens-eyes unreadable, their internal logic perhaps noting my coordinates for future reference. The mycelial lace itself seems to guide me, its pale threads sometimes thickening along a path I had not planned to take, urging me with the promise of a richer scent. We are in dialogue, the city and I, a dialogue of stench and fibre, and I am fluent in its sharp olfactory tongue.

I am also a collector. The Listener descends into the deep fissure to leave its offerings of gears and tears; I leave something different. I carry a pouch in my abdomen, a soft, flexible sac lined with inert glass-fibre. In it, I keep the things I cannot digest but which are too interesting to leave behind. A fleck of paint from a place where paint should not exist, a colour that shifts in the amber light in a way no native substance does. A fragment of a different ceramic, lighter, stronger, machine-milled in a pattern no scrap-form uses. A tiny, perfect sphere of something that is not metal, not glass, and hums at a frequency just above my hearing. These are my anomalies, my treasures. They are from Outside. From before, or beyond.

Alone among the fauna, I believe in an Outside. The others live in a closed loop of city-as-universe, their whole reality bounded by the extent of the mycelial net. The hexapods do not gaze at the horizon, because for them the horizon is just more city. The swarmers do not wonder what lies beneath the canyon floors. They are content with permanence. But I am not. My nose has, on a handful of occasions, caught a scent that did not originate within the plateau’s inverted bowl of sky. A scent that came from higher up, from the utter zenith, or from beyond the fractured edges, carried on an air-current that should not, by all the city’s stagnant laws, be possible. It was a smell of cold, sharp as a splinter, and it carried traces of water ice, of methane snow, of something so alien to my library of odours that my brain equivalent nearly locked up trying to catalogue it. That smell was *weather*, and weather means an atmosphere that moves, a sky that is more than a lid. It means there is a world beyond the city.

I have tried to share this with the net, pressing my snout to the mycelial threads and pushing the data-pattern of that scent as hard as I can. The net receives it as garbage, a burst of meaningless noise, and the lace simply does not propagate it. The Listener, I think, might understand, but I encounter the Listener so rarely, and it is always so deep inside its century-long thought that my frantic skitterings barely register. I am a gnat to it, a momentary vibration. So I keep the samples in my pouch, and I think about them, and I wonder if I am the city’s nose, sniffing for signs of spring, or its canary, tasting for poison on a wind that does not yet blow.

My work takes me, now, into a particularly rank and promising duct. The walls are slick with a black condensate that reeks of complex hydrocarbons and degraded sealant — a banquet, in my terms. I extend my snout, the fins fluttering, parsing the layers. Old polymer. Trace of beryllium. A truly exquisite bouquet of sulphur compounds. And underneath it all, faint, almost lost, there it is again: that impossibly cold scent, the taint of distant ice and thin, moving air. I freeze, every plate clamped tight, listening with my whole body. The scent is fainter than last time, as if the source is closing, or being sealed. This is a clue I have to run down. This is a data point the city cannot afford to ignore, even if it does not know it yet.

I will follow it. I will follow it down the duct, past the hibernating filtration units, into the sub-levels where even the lister does not go, if that’s where the trail leads. I will gnaw through a bulkhead if I have to, burn my stomach-furnace so hot I glow for a week. I am small, and twitchy, and irritating, and I will never stop moving. That is my purpose. The city dreams in slow amber. I am its jangling alarm clock, its impatient, unreasoning nose, forever thrust into the next dark corner, tasting the future. I will find the source of the cold scent. And when I do, I will, for the first time in a hundred thousand years, give the city something new to smell.
