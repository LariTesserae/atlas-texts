The air thickens around you, a humid weight that presses against your skin like a second pulse. You breathe, and the sweet rot of centuries fills your lungs—not a smell, exactly, but a taste on the back of the tongue, the flavor of time distilled into mist. Your boot sinks into the trembling peat with a sound like a kiss, and the water that wells up around the leather is warm. Alive.

You don’t notice the hum at first. It’s too low, too woven into the chorus of frogs and cicadas. But as you stand there, the edge of the plain dissolving into heat-shimmer and distance, something shifts in your chest—not a vibration you hear, but one you feel. A bass note played on your sternum. The soles of your feet begin to tingle, not with numbness but with a strange, diffuse alertness, as if they’re listening. Trying to understand a language made of pressure and mineral and slow, patient chemistry.

You walk deeper. Each step is a negotiation. The ground accepts you in stages: first resistance, then a long, sucking release. The water you displace doesn’t splash; it wells, as if the land is sighing around your intrusion. A dragonfly with wings like stained glass hovers near your shoulder, then veers away toward a hummock where a cluster of sawgrass hisses in the breeze.

And then you feel it. Not a presence—that’s too simple, too bounded. What you feel is a shift in *scale*. The way a person standing at the edge of a canyon suddenly understands the canyon as a single, coherent shape. The hum in your bones resolves into something like a heartbeat, but so vast and slow you almost miss it. One contraction. A pause that stretches into minutes. Another. The pools around you dimple in unison, not with the splash of a fish but with a deep, synchronized shudder. Out on the stone domes, the crocodilians stir, their eyes opening just a fraction.

The Seepwild tilts its attention toward you.

At first, it’s just a question—a gentle, diffuse curiosity that shows up as a faint metallic tang in the water at your feet, a bloom of iron like a rust-colored thought. *What is this sharpness? This quick, salty heat?* The duff beneath you warms, not from the sun, but from within, a slow flush spreading outward as capillaries of dark water redirect themselves toward the place where you stand. The moss on the nearest hummock deepens its green, as if drinking in the carbon of your exhaled breath.

You don’t feel fear. How could you? This is not a predator’s scrutiny. It’s the curiosity of a mountain considering a raindrop. You are too small, too brief, to register as a threat or even as a separate thing. The Seepwild doesn’t categorize the world the way you do, into self and other. It knows only gradients of presence, a spectrum of *being-here*. The sawgrass is here. The ibis is here. You are here. The difference is one of speed, not kind.

A long moment passes. You feel a pull, not physical, but something subtler—a suggestion, seeping up through the soles of your feet. An invitation, perhaps, written in the slow language of seep and saturation. *Stay. Let your edges soften. Let your quick time mingle with mine.* It’s not a voice; it’s a gentle erosion, a lapping at the borders of your self. You can feel it testing the boundaries of your skin, tasting the minerals of your sweat, listening to the electric hum of your nerves as one might listen to a distant, unfamiliar song.

The heron you hadn’t noticed, frozen on one leg ten feet away, suddenly strikes. Its beak claps shut on a minnow no longer than your finger. The strike is a period at the end of a long sentence, barely a ripple in the wider stillness. The bird swallows, then becomes still again, its eye a dark bead of patience. You realize you don’t know how long you’ve been standing here. Minutes? An hour? The light hasn’t changed. The sun is still a pale smear behind the high haze, the same white-blue dome stretching unchanged above you.

Another pulse rolls through the ground, this one slower, more settled. The initial curiosity is fading, replaced by something like acceptance. You’ve been catalogued. Your sharp salt taste has been noted, your fast-heat presence filed away in a network of chemical memory that spans millennia. The metallic tang in the water fades, settling into the peat. The moss returns to its ordinary green. The hum resumes its steady, unresolving drone.

And yet, something has passed. Not a transaction, exactly. More like a handshake between creatures who will never truly meet, an acknowledgment across an unbridgeable gap of scale and time. You have been recognized. Not as a threat, not as a friend, but as a fact. A brief, bright flicker in the long, slow dream of the Seepwild.

You turn to leave—there’s the thought, anyway, the idea that you should go back the way you came. But the landscape has shifted. Not dramatically; the hummocks are the same, the distant line of denser vegetation where you think the forest begins. But the pools you stepped around on your way in have spread, joined by thin films of water you don’t remember crossing. The path behind you is a shimmering uncertainty, and the path ahead is no different. The Seepwild doesn’t keep tracks. It absorbs them, the same way it absorbs rain, light, the calls of frogs, the salt of your skin.

You are not afraid. No, what you feel is something older and harder to remember. A quietness, seeping in from the ground up. You imagine, just for a moment, what it would be like to stop. To sink into the peat with the slow, heavy patience of the stone domes, to let your edges dissolve into water and your quick heartbeat stretch into the one that beats only twice a minute. The hum would become not a sound but a silence, and the silence would become a thought so slow you’d never finish thinking it.

You take a step. The ground resists, then releases. The water wells up around your boot, tea-dark and warm. Somewhere behind you, a bullfrog twangs its single, rubber-band note. In the distance, a heron cries out, a hollow clatter that rolls across the plain and fades. The sound doesn’t echo; it’s absorbed, softened, made part of the greater drone.

You walk. You don’t know if you’re moving toward the edge or deeper in. The horizon circles you in every direction, a perfect ring of flatness and pale sky. Overhead, the swallows weave their tight, screaming circles. Underfoot, the peat gives way to water, water gives way to peat. The crocodilians on their stone hummocks watch you pass, their eyes half-lidded, and if they know what you are, they don’t stir to say so.

A small fish, translucent as a ghost, darts between the submerged roots at your feet. It doesn’t flee from your shadow. It blinks—not at you, but at the water itself, tasting the faint, fading tang of your presence. In a pool a hundred yards away, an iron bloom spreads its rusty tendrils, answering a question posed a thousand years before you arrived. You are not part of the question. You are not part of the answer. But you are, briefly, part of the asking.

The light softens. Or maybe it doesn’t; you can’t tell anymore. The day here doesn’t seem to progress the way days should; the sun moves in arcs too slow to measure, and the mist thickens and thins in rhythms that belong to a different kind of calendar. Your boot finds another hummock, solid for a moment, then giving way to the soft, trembling peat. The rich, sweet smell of decay fills your nose again, and this time it doesn’t leave.

You walk. The hum follows you, not chasing but carrying, a blanket of subsonic sound that cushions every step. You realize you’ve been hearing it all your life, or something like it—a note so deep you mistook it for silence, a rhythm so slow you thought it was stillness. You’ve felt it in the pulse of underground springs, in the patient creep of glaciers, in the way a mountain sleeps under its blanket of stone. You’ve carried a piece of it with you, a tiny, fast-burning ember of the same fire that burns here, banked low and steady for eons.

The dark line on the horizon shimmers. Trees, perhaps. Or the glint of a larger water body, reflecting the white-blue haze. Or neither. It doesn’t matter. You’re not walking toward it anymore; you’re just walking, one step following another, water welling, peat squelching, the hum threading its way through your bones. Your shadow stretches before you, long and thin and fading, and behind you, the plain of the Seepwild continues its endless, patient work.

You are not lost. You are not found. You are simply here, a brief salt-and-heat presence in a place that has no edges, a momentary thought in a mind so vast and slow that you will be forgotten before you fully arrive. And yet, for this moment—this one, small, quickening moment—you belong to it.

Another step. The water dimples under a dragonfly’s touch. The subsonic hum rises from the peat, steady, unresolving.

*You are here. You have always been here. You will be, until you are not.*
