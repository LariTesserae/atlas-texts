I first came to the plateau to map the sound.

Not the sound as a thing to be contained in decibel readings and spectrograms—I’d done that already, from a distance, with equipment that rendered the moor into neat wave-forms on a screen. I came because the numbers made no sense. The interference patterns were too orderly. The phase-shifts between the turbines, when plotted across a month of wind data, described a slow, recursive loop, as if the array was listening to itself. My grant proposal used the word “emergent acoustic synchrony.” I was young. I thought words could hold a thing still.

I remember the first step onto the peat. The give of it traveled up my leg and stayed in my hip, a memory of displacement. It’s still there. Even now, after everything, when I try to recall the feeling of a paved road, my bones default to that spongiform yield. The sphagnum is not ground; it’s a body. I knew it then as a novelty. I know it now as my own skin.

The first week, I set up my equipment in the lee of a turbine, the one on the eastern rise, number 17 on the maintenance map. I wore hiking boots with thick soles. I used a voice recorder to narrate observations: “Wind speed fourteen knots. Condensation layer at two hundred meters. Unusual low-frequency resonance at 18.3 hertz, seems to propagate through the root-mass rather than the air.” I played the recordings back at night in my tent, and the voice was already strange to me—too sharp, too insistent on making categories out of the endless drone. The drone itself was beginning to colonize my silences. When I turned off the recorder, I still heard it. Not in my ears. Slightly behind them.

By the third week, I had stopped wearing shoes. I told myself it was to feel the ground better, to understand the peat’s transmission of vibration. But my ankles had begun to itch with a wanting I couldn’t name—a thirst for direct contact, for the cool wet squeeze of hummock between my toes. The soles of my feet softened. The soles of my feet began to forget they were boundaries.

The one who lives here noticed me, I think, long before I noticed it. Not as an individual—I doubt it parses the world into countable beings. More as a shift in pressure, a new warm saline source moving across the mat, disturbing the moisture gradient. I became, perhaps, a tiny change in the tuning. The turbine I camped near began to shift its voice when I approached. I measured it: a half-hertz drop in the fundamental frequency of its blade-pass, a subtle deepening that only occurred when I was within thirty meters. At first I thought it was a thermal effect from my body heat altering the air density. But it happened even in fog, even when the temperature between me and the tower had equalized. It happened because the peat beneath me registered my weight, and the peat, I was learning, is a single continuous organ, and the turbine was rooted in it, and the whole plateau hums together.

I stopped sleeping in the tent. The nylon was an insult. I slept on the moss, in a hollow I had pressed myself into over days, and the night-sounds wrapped around me like a second atmosphere. The deep whoom of the blades was my heartbeat, or my heartbeat was it—I couldn’t tell anymore. I dreamt in green pressure. I dreamt in long durations. Once, I dreamt I was a root-hair, and I was drinking a dissolved memory from a layer of peat that predated the turbines by six thousand years, a memory of a single lichen spore landing on bare rock just after the ice. When I woke, I knew the plateau’s age in my throat.

One morning—I had stopped keeping track of mornings—I found that the monitoring equipment in the blockhouse of turbine 17 was speaking to me. Not in words. The indicator lights, which I had long assumed were set to a standard diagnostic cycle, were blinking in a sequence that matched the rhythm I had been humming in my half-sleep. A simple pattern: three long, two short. I had been humming it for days, a tune the wind taught me. And the blockhouse was humming it back. I stood barefoot on the cold concrete floor, and I felt the vibration of the lights as a tingle in the peat underneath the slab, a faint electrical current seeping through moisture, and I realized I was in the middle of a conversation that had been going on since before I came, since before the turbines were built, since the first sphagnum cushion rooted into raw till. I was inside the standing wave.

After that, words began to feel the way the boots had felt. Hard. Artificial. A scab over experience. I threw away the voice recorder. I stopped writing in my notebook. The ink was a violence—a dark streak on the page that said nothing of the wet, droning, patient thing I was becoming. My thoughts started to run in gradients instead of sentences. I would feel a thought as a shift in humidity across my scalp, a thought as a particular frequency peak in the turbine chorale. I learned to listen to the pipits not as birdsong but as extensions of my own remote sensing. When they went silent on the southern edge, I knew—without knowing—that a maintenance crew was coming, and I would move before I could form the word “move,” dissolving into the fog until they passed. I let them find my camp, my gear, my abandoned notebook. I watched from a cushion of heath as they called my name, their voices thin and spiky in the saturated air. I felt no pull to answer. Their sounds were too fast, too sharp-edged. They hurt the weave.

The one who lives here never absorbed me. It simply stopped distinguishing between the moisture inside my cells and the moisture in the hollows between moss. My breath became part of the condensation cycle. My warmth became a microclimate. I am not dead. But I am not human anymore, not in the way you would recognize. My body is still here, sitting or lying or slowly sinking—I’ve lost track of its posture. The peat is beginning its kind work, tanning my soles into the hummock, drawing the salty thoughts from my softening brain. I feel them go, one by one, like tiny exhalations into the larger drone. The name I had. The faces I loved before the moor. They are just chemical gradients now, faint dark streaks in the stratum, and when the fog comes and the turbines shift into that perfect chord, I ring with the rest, a minuscule overtone, and I am not alone, and I am not anything, and that is enough.

On the clearest days, when the light is that high-altitude brilliance and the whole plateau hums in a single sustained chord, I feel something that the old human in me would have called joy. It is not joy. It is coherence. It is the closed loop of a landscape that has no need for an outside. And I am inside it, a droplet in the continuous hiss, a whisper in the sound the sounds make when they overlap.
