I find myself as a single cricket on a high ledge, and I am the flaw.

The ledge is a buttress-rib of limestone, one of the last survivors, and from this vantage the hollow spreads beneath me like the inside of a bell. I have been here for three summers—an eternity for my kind—and in that time I have learned the shape of the sound the way a drowned man learns the shape of the sea. The thrum comes up through the stone and into the joints of my legs, a bass so deep it is less a frequency than a weather system. The froglets’ metallic chee-chee sweeps around the walls in waves I can map with my antennae, each pulse arriving a half-beat after the last, a Doppler chorus that never resolves. And woven through it all, the shirr of my own kind: a thousand tawny crickets rasping from the moss-drapes, their stridulations locked in a phase-perfect synchrony that I cannot, cannot match.

My left wing has a notch. It is a tiny thing, a deformity I was hatched with, a quarter-millimeter divot in the scraper that makes my song a fraction of a hertz too slow. I have tried, with all the desperate precision of my small brain, to correct it. I angle my wing differently against the file. I tense my thorax muscles to speed the stroke. For hours, sometimes, I can almost blend—I can feel the collective pulse take me in, the way a current takes a leaf. But then the notch catches, a tiny skip in the rhythm, and I fall out of phase again. The surrounding crickets do not notice. They are not capable of noticing. They are part of the machine, perfectly tuned to the hollow’s resonant frequency by a thousand generations of chemical nudging, and my difference is a language they have never learned to hear.

But it hears.

I know this because I have felt its attention. It comes not as a sound but as a drying, a subtle pull at the moisture in my spiracles, as if the air around me has suddenly become a little thirsty. The hyphae threading the moss beneath my feet shift their focus, and for a moment—just a moment—the temperature on this ledge drops a degree. The inhabitant has noticed my notch. It has added me to its list of corrections, and I do not know how long the list is or how patiently it works its way down it, but I know that the old cricket who sang from this ledge before me stopped mid-rasp one night, and in the morning his body was already sinking into the moss, his chitin blooming with white threads.

I am afraid, but it is a cricket’s fear, which means it is mostly a chemical event—a spike of octopamine that makes my legs twitch and my song stutter even worse. The stuttering only makes me more noticeable. I feel the hyphae probing now, not just beneath me but *around* me, a faint tickle at the joints of my exoskeleton as if the moss itself is learning my shape. I have seen what happens to the anomalies here. The vole with the broken leg that dragged itself into the hollow for shelter—within a day its breathing slowed, its eyes filmed over, and the moss grew up around it like a blanket being pulled over a child. The froglet that hatched with an extra limb, a tiny fifth leg that paddled uselessly in its dimple—I watched it for a week, singing its off-kilter chee, until one morning the dimple was empty except for a haze of spores swirling in the water. The inhabitant does not kill with violence. It kills with reabsorption, a gentle erasure that leaves the rest of the hollow untouched. I am the rest of the hollow’s only wound, and I am slowly being healed over.

And yet, I sing on. I cannot do otherwise; it is my nature, and in the hollow there is no room for natures that do not fit. I have tried to leave—crawled down the buttress-rib and across the mattress floor toward the arch of rootlets, the grey daylight, the world beyond where songs do not have to be perfect. But the hyphae are everywhere. They do not block me; they simply drain my will as I go, pulling moisture from my limbs until I am too sluggish to move, and then the frog-chorus sweeps over me and I forget, for a while, that I ever wanted to leave. The sound has a way of filling the mind, of leaving no space for thoughts that do not pulse at sixty cycles per second. By the time I recover, I am back on my ledge, my wings already moving, my notch still catching.

So I wait. The lobe is a dark shape in the corner of my compound eye, a constant presence that never moves but never leaves. I have come to think of it as a kind of parent, or perhaps a god—a god whose only commandment is *be still*, and whose only punishment is stillness itself, a stillness that will swallow me cell by cell until I am indistinguishable from the moss that feeds on me. I have seen my future in the vole bones half-sunk into the floor, their calcium migrating slowly into the living green. And in the meantime, I rasp my imperfect song, the one flaw in the hollow’s frozen music, and I feel the hyphae tighten around my perch, and I wonder which night they will finally slip into my spiracles and turn my own moisture against me, and I will become, at last, part of the loudness that never started and never stops.

Thrum. Chee-chee-chee. My notch catches. The lobe exhales, infinitesimally, and the correction continues.
