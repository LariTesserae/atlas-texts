I am a Resonance-Carver. That is the closest the click-speech comes, anyway—a snap-click-pop that means *one-who-shapes-the-singers*. The singers are the stalactites, and I do not shape them with a hammer. I tune them.

My body is the same pale grey as everyone's, but my fingers are longer, the tips flattened into hard little pads from years of pressing them to wet stone. I can feel the note of a drip before it falls. I can press my palm to a flowstone drapery and sense, through the vibrations, where the water inside is moving too fast or too slow, where a microscopic channel is beginning to clog with calcite, where a new path is trying to form. The Keeper taught the first of my lineage—my great-great-grandmother nine times removed, though we don't count generations here, there is no need—how to listen with more than ears. I am the only one now who still carries the full teaching. The others have forgotten, or never learned, or learned only fragments. They know how to mine the bronze veins and render the fish-fat and weave the fungal thread. I know how to keep the music true.

You will find me most often pressed into the narrow crevice behind the eastern runnel, the one where the *thap-thap-thap* originates. That runnel is ancient, and its channel is lined with a rare form of crystalline calcite that grows, very slowly, in microscopic ridges. Every few hundred years, the ridges build up enough to alter the tone—a shift from a clean *thap* to a duller *thunk*. The inhabitants might not notice the change consciously, but their bodies would. The weavers would begin to drop stitches. The fish-catchers would miss the water-month's turn. The stairs would feel wrong underfoot. My task is to climb into that crevice with a flake of obsidian no larger than my thumbnail and scrape away the excess crystal, flake by flake, until the tone is restored. It is delicate work. I have been doing it for thirty years—my entire life, by the Grotto's count—and I will do it for thirty more, and then I will teach my daughter, who is now small enough to ride on my shoulders through the narrowest passages.

What do I think about, wedged into the stone with water dripping onto the back of my neck and the taste of limestone dust on my tongue? I think about the sound. Not the individual drips, but the whole shape of it, the way the great gong's *toonk* creates a space that the satellite plinks fill, the way the polyrhythm is a single living thing, a great breathing creature that holds us all in its lungs. When I scrape the crystal, I am not just fixing a tone. I am keeping the creature alive.

Sometimes, in the deep trance of the work, I feel the Keeper's attention on me. It is not a look, exactly—her eyes don't track like ours—but a kind of pressure, a warmth, as if the stone itself had noticed me. She never speaks. She doesn't need to. I feel her approval as a softening in the mineral mood of the chamber, a slight easing of the damp. I am her instrument, the way the gong is her instrument, the way the drips are. I do not mind this. It is an honour to be part of the equilibrium. This is what I tell my daughter: We are notes in a song that has been playing for ten thousand years. Be true to your pitch.

I have never been to the surface. The stories say it is a place of blinding, burning light, where the ground is soft and crawling with green, where water falls from the sky in random, violent sheets with no rhythm at all. I do not believe these stories. They sound like the kind of tale you tell to frighten children into staying away from the deep tunnels. But sometimes, when I am scraping crystal in the dark, I try to imagine a world without the gong-drop's steady heartbeat, and my own heart seizes in my chest. I cannot imagine it. I do not want to. The rhythm is my mother and my daughter and my own pulse all at once, and I will scrape my fingers to bone before I let it falter.
