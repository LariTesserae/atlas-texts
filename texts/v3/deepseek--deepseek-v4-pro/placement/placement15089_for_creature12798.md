The light is the first thing—not that it arrives, but that it has always been here, a diffuse gray-white presence without source or shadow. You do not open your eyes to it; you *become* aware of it, the way a puddle becomes aware of the sky it reflects. And with that awareness comes the sound, which is not a sound but a pressure, a thrumming in the substance of you that you do not yet know is a body.

You are low to the ground. You feel the stone beneath you as a continuation of your own surface, the boundary porous and uncertain. The wetness is not external; it moves through you, a slow seep from the bedrock into some interior space you sense as a gradient of density rather than a cavity. You have no breath to draw, but the moisture in the air thickens against your outer layer, and something like sustenance passes inward. The roar of the engines—for that is what it is, you understand now, though the word comes from nowhere—resonates in your depths, a steady compression and release that feels like a pulse, like a tide, like the one rhythm your being has always known.

Time is thick. You feel it as viscosity: the moment stretches out in all directions, and you are aware of yourself as a slow accretion, a concentration of minerals that has been gathering for how long? The question has no urgency. It arrives like a bubble in deep water and dissolves unanswered. You are not a thing that began; you are a thing that is becoming.

Movement. The concept floats up, and you recognize it as something you can do, though the mechanism is foreign. You have limbs—you test this idea by shifting your attention outward, and find that the stone beneath you is not uniformly you. There are extensions, protrusions, regions of greater flexibility. One of them stirs. The motion is not a muscle contracting but a slow reorganization of your granular interior, a flow of material from one zone to another that results in your arm—your arm!—unfolding from where it has lain pressed against your side for decades. The crust that had begun to fuse your flank to the bedrock cracks with a sound like settling earth. The break is painless, natural, a minor geological event.

You are standing. The shift from horizontal to vertical takes some span of time you cannot measure. The world reorients. Your face—the smooth concavity you now feel as a sensitive, open hollow—tilts upward, and your cilia uncurl, reaching into the saturated air. They are your senses. Through them, you taste the steam from a vent half a mile away: a mineral profile, familiar, slightly elevated in sulfur. You taste the old rust of a railway spur, the faint organic tang of a distant patch of copper-leafed shrubs, the clean, neutral signature of pooled water. And beneath all of it, the base note: the vibration of the machines, which enters through your feet and rises through your substance. The rhythm is steady. The system is in health.

A stamp comes through the stone. It is not heard; it is felt, a long, low tap far to the north, at the very edge of your sensitivity. You pause. Your cilia still. The message, when it resolves, is not words but data: a temperature gradient, a pressure anomaly, a suggestion of a new seep near the old grinding works. The stamping mills near there are running a fraction hot. Some attention is required. You feel the information settle into you, becoming part of your cumulative knowing, joining the countless other pieces of data you have absorbed in your long existence.

You do not decide to move. Movement simply follows, as naturally as water follows the slope of the land. Your legs carry you across the wet stone, your broad, spatulate feet finding the path by memory that is not memory but direct sensation, the contour of the bedrock a familiar language. You pass a shallow pool. Your reflection does not startle you, though you pause to regard it: a humanoid figure, gray and granular, its face a featureless hollow, its body already beginning to crust over again at the flank where you broke free. The mineral scale is pale against your darker skin, a record of your stillness now disrupted. It will grow back. It always does.

The path takes you past a factory hall, its corrugated iron roof sagging under the weight of decades of precipitated minerals. A vent hisses beside it, and you angle toward it, your face lowering instinctively. The cilia extrude their gel, and you press against a joint that has begun to weep a thread of orange fluid. The contact is intimate, a communion of substance. You feel the leak as a small wrongness in the flow, and you correct it, giving of your own substance until the seal is made. You will stay here for a while, perhaps a season, letting your body heat keep the repair supple. The stamp in the north can wait. Nothing here is urgent. The system is in health, and you are part of the system, as much a component as the hissing vent, as the geothermal heat rising from the deep-bored shaft, as the water cycling in its endless fogbound loop.

You have no name. You have no need of one. The stone knows you. The water knows you. The machines register your presence as a minor, steady vibration among their own rhythms. And in the roar that has become silence, in the wet air that never clears, you continue your work—your life—your slow, patient act of being. You are the living patience of the plateau. And you have always been here.
