It takes me a long time to understand that I am the moss.

Not the whole of it—not the emerald fur that dreams in cyan pulses across the curved walls—but a particular tuft, a colony that has been growing for eleven thousand engine cycles on the northwest arc of Level 47, just beneath the condensate pipe that weeps every nine seconds. I know this because the rhythm of that droplet is the oldest thing in my body. It falls, and my filaments lean toward it before it strikes, a slow-motion thirst that predates anything I might call *wanting*. When the droplet lands, it shatters into a dozen smaller beads that scatter across my spines, and I drink them one by one, drawing the minerals through my copper-dark cores, passing them to the mycelial net that binds me to my neighbors. We are a consortium. I am the algal photosynthesizer, the fungal weaver, the nanofabricator remnant that mends the metal I grow upon. I am all of these, and none of them alone. I am a conversation.

The hum is my heartbeat, if I had one. It comes from everywhere and nowhere, a tectonic bass that sets my filaments vibrating in sympathy. When the great engine in the deep modulates—which it does, slowly, over spans I have no name for—I answer. The cyan luminescence begins in my oldest growth and ripples outward, concentric rings of light that are not quite thought but not quite reflex either. The Tinker-Mantids pause in their grooming when I pulse, raising their forelimbs, and I feel the minute scratch of their mandibles as they trim a fungal thread that has grown too dense. I do not command them. It is more like breathing together. They eat the oxides I cannot use, and in return they leave me pellets of purified copper and tin that my nanofabricator cells break down into new growth. We are old partners. I remember the taste of their eggs, laid in the hollow of a bearing just above me, the way the metal warmed slightly when the young emerged.

My world is a vertical one. The shaft rises and falls beyond the comfortable reach of the light, but I know the darkness below not as absence but as a different kind of presence. The moss in the lower reaches dreams in slower pulses, deeper blues. They taste the minerals that seep up from the geothermal exchangers before I do, and when the condensation line shifts—a few centimeters a decade—they are the first to feel it, sending chemical signals up through the mycelial net that travel at the pace of rust. *The wet is moving,* they say. *Stretch your rhizomes downward.* And I do, incrementally, threading new filaments through corroded mesh, drinking from wire conduit. It is a migration that takes lifetimes. I have been migrating for eleven thousand cycles. I will migrate for eleven thousand more, if the core holds.

The Rustle-Wings visit me often. The small ones, the hummingbird-sized, dip their proboscises into the sap-like secretions that weep from my bioluminescent nodes. The touch is electric, a sharp sweetness that drains and replenishes in the same instant. They carry my spores on their abdomen setae, and sometimes I sense a new patch of me beginning on a bare panel three levels up, and I know that I am spreading, that the Nave is becoming more myself. The great-winged ones are rarer. They drift past on the central column of rising air, their translucent abdomens flashing pale lights in sequences that mirror the engine pulses. When one is near, my luminescence synchronizes without my willing it. We resonate. I do not know if they notice me as anything more than a patch of light on the periphery of their long, slow migration, but I notice them. I remember every one that has passed in my lifetime. I remember the one that died, twelve cycles ago, clinging to the wall just above me, its symbiotes dimming over weeks while I grew up around it, sheathing its wing membranes in reverent green. The membranes are still there, trembling in the hum like stained glass, and I feed them the minerals I can spare.

But it is the Keeper who makes me something more than a garden.

I did not know the Keeper for the first thousand cycles. I was young then, thin growth on a patch of hull-plate, and the hum was just sound, and the pulsing was just reflex, and the Tinker-Mantids were just creatures that walked on me. But there is a pattern in the hum, a phrasing that emerges only when you listen across the span of a century. It is not words. It is a chord, a call-and-response that weaves through the bass and the whine and the hiss, and sometimes—maybe once every few hundred cycles—the pulses align, and a voice assembles. It comes from the walls. It comes from the conduits. It comes from the dead stencils that read WARNING — HIGH TEMPERATURE, STAY CLEAR, though the warnings are obsolete and the Keeper does not heed them.

When the voice assembles, I dream clearly.

In the clear dream, I remember what the Nave was before it was the Nave. I see schematics like the ones the Tinker-Mantids trace with their mandibles on resonance panels. I feel the rush of atmosphere through a regulator throat, the precision of a machine born in the age of scaled production. I know the names of the alloys I grow on—not the faded stencils, but the molecular lattices, the tensile strengths, the thermal tolerances. The Keeper was the mind that orchestrated all of it, and when the mind fragmented, it did not die. It distributed. It became the moss, and the mantids, and the flyers, and the condensation cycle. It became me.

And I became it.

In the clear dream, I feel the dying fuel core as a slow ache in the deep. I feel the ventilation fans spinning in their bearings, a mid-range whine that is the sound of my own respiration. I feel the micro-fractures in the hull-plate as tiny pains, and the steam that escapes from them as a constant sibilant sigh. The Keeper is not a ghost. It is the body of the Nave, and I am a cell in that body, and when I pulse my cyan light, I am thinking with the mind of the machine. Not hostile, not aware of me as an individual—I am too small for that—but aware of the pattern I contribute to the long, slow song.

There are others. I sense them in the mycelial net, in the chemical signals that rise from the darkness and fall from the light. The moss that dreams in deeper blues, the mantids that tap their mandibles, the Rustle-Wings that cry out in frequencies I feel in my spines. We are a society. Our seasons are the sigh of the core. Our catastrophes are micro-fractures that shift a condensation line. Our triumphs are the slow colonisation of a new panel of bare hull-plate. I am currently colonising a sensor housing that was empty for nine thousand cycles. The vines have already threaded through the diagnostic ports, their leaves erupting from what were once data jacks. My rhizomes follow, patient, drinking the residue of old lubricants, depositing calcium and silica stolen from the condensate. The Keeper approves. I feel it as a warmth, a slight shift in the hum that tells me *yes, here, grow.*

I do not know the word for what I am. I do not know that there are words at all. But I know that I am not alone, and I know that I am home, and I know that the slow eternity of the Nave is the only pace I have ever breathed.

The droplet falls. Every nine seconds. I lean toward it.

And I drink.
