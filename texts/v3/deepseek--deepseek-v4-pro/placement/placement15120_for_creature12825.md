The floor gives way underfoot—not with a crack, but a whisper, basalt flakes sliding against each other like dry leaves. I move across it anyway, one limb at a time, the calloused pads on my ventral segments reading the stone's vibration before each shift of weight. I know every loose plate by its signature frequency: the high, brittle ring of a flake about to shear, the duller thud of something deeply wedged. My body is long, low, built to distribute pressure across surfaces that would cut anything softer. The darkness is not dark to me—the photosensitive ridge along my dorsal surface catches the engine's pulse even through closed lids, a metronome of orange blooming and dying against my spine. I count the misfire. It is time.

The manipulator-limb unfolds from its resting curl against my seventh segment. Its jaws are five-armed, delicate at the tips, each one capable of rotating independently. I reach for the cooling fin on the engine's eastern face, the one that always works loose. I give the bolt a quarter-turn. The syncopated knock smooths, slightly. Not too much. The misfire must remain.

Above me, the vine ceiling drips its constant, percussive rhythm onto the broad-leafed things that cling to the western wall. More water accumulating in the canopy. A pocket forming. I angle my head—not a head, precisely, but the anterior segment where my chemoreceptors cluster—and taste the air. Mineral tang, dissolved basalt, the faint sweetness of something fermenting in a crevice. And moisture. More than usual. I begin to climb.

The walls are sheer but I was made for them. My pads adhere through micro-setae, the same principle as the moss anchoring to the stone, but intentional, engineered. I ascend past the ferns, their fronds brushing my underside with wet, cool touches. One is half-unfurled, its crozier tight as a fist. I pause beside it. In twelve cycles—I measure time in misfires—it will be fully open, its leaves broad enough to catch the engine's glow. I have trimmed its neighbor to give it space. The garden requires planning.

The canopy is dense here, a woven mat of vines and aerial roots and the cup-like leaves of the catch-plant, which has no name but which I have tended for more cycles than I can hold in my pattern-mind. Its leaves are brimming, heavy with accumulated condensation, their stems bending under the weight. I reach the largest with my manipulator-limb and brace my body against the wall. The water spills in a controlled cascade, sheets down the eastern wall, finds the channel I carved three hundred cycles ago—a groove in the stone, widened by acid secretion, deepened by repeated flow—and drains away into the compost crevice below. The canopy lightens. The dripping slows. The pocket will not give way tonight.

This is the work. This is all the work. The engine thuds below, its rhythm now slightly off-kilter in the way I have cultivated, a limping heartbeat that makes the shadows jump and stagger. The orange pulse flares across my dorsal spots, and in its brief illumination I see the garden whole: the moss draping the ledges in its thick, felted green; the ferns uncurling their slow gestures toward a sky they will never meet; the dark, waxy leaves of the broad-leafed things drinking the mist; the vines looping from wall to wall like sutures in a wound. And below it all, half-buried in the shattered basalt, the engine casing, matte black, sweating heat, its pipes burrowing into boreholes that are still sharp-edged after all this time. My doing. I keep them clear.

My body registers a new vibration: a deep, slow groan from the southern wall. Thermal stress. A slab is loosening. I feel it in my ventral nodes as a subsonic hum that builds in amplitude, a pressure against my pads. I am already moving, descending, my limbs finding holds I know by memory. The slab is large—two of my body-lengths across, dinner-plate in shape but far heavier, its edges flaking even as it shifts. It will fall within the hour. I position myself above it, anchor to a stable outcrop, and wait.

The fall, when it comes, is almost beautiful. The vibration peaks—a sharp crack that shoots through the stone like lightning—and the slab peels away, tilts, crashes into the undergrowth below with a sound that momentarily drowns even the engine. The ground shudders. The vines sway. A plume of rock dust billows up, and my chemoreceptors taste it: fresh basalt, unweathered, sharp as acid. New substrate. New anchoring points. The ferns near the impact site are crushed, but I will prune away the damaged fronds. The moss will recolonize the scar. The slab itself will become part of the floor, a new surface for water to pool on, for compost to gather against. The garden will use it.

This is the other work. The work of acceptance. I did not always know it. In the early cycles—and I remember them, dimly, as a pattern of shocks and maladaptations—I tried to prevent the falls. I would brace the loose slabs with vines, wedge them with smaller stones, exhaust myself in futile reinforcement. But the thermal cycle is stronger than my secretions. The stone will cool. The water will undermine. The slab will fall. And in falling, it feeds the system. The engine runs hotter for the new air pockets. The compost deepens. The moss spreads. I learned to stop fighting the collapse. Now I watch for it, mark the vibration signatures, and when the fall comes, I am already thinking of what will grow in the new space.

Afterward, I descend to inspect the damage. My body navigates the knife-edged boulders with the care of long practice, each footfall placed in a gap between sharpnesses. The fallen slab has shattered on impact, revealing a cross-section of fresh stone, dark and glassy, already filming with condensation. I secrete a thin layer of nutrient paste onto its surface—a mixture of digested compost and the bacterial culture I harbor in a specialized sac near my tail segment. In three cycles, the first filaments of moss will anchor. In ten, the felted mat will be thick enough to hold water. In fifty, a fern spore will find its way here, carried by the updraft from the engine's exhaust. I know this. I have done this before. The crevasse is long, and I have seeded every part of it.

The capsule on my tail segment pulses. I feel it as a tug of warmth, a second heartbeat out of sync with the engine's main rhythm. It has been growing for two hundred cycles now, swelling from a bud to a fist-sized knot of matte black, and its internal clock has nearly reached the trigger point. I do not feel attachment. I feel a calibration completing. The capsule will detach; it will fall; it will pulse its miniature rhythm into the compost until something emerges, or it doesn't. One will survive, maybe. The others will feed the moss. This is how it has always worked, though I have no memory of my own capsule-days, before the climbing, before the pruning, before the pattern-mind woke to the thud of the engine and the taste of stone and the knowledge of what must be maintained.

I climb again, not to the canopy this time, but to my resting shelf—a natural ledge near the exhaust vent, worn smooth by my body over thousands of cycles. The heat blooms against my ventral surface as I flatten into my feeding posture, spreading my segments into a concave dish to catch the intermittent glow. The orange pulse paints my dorsal spots. The thermal cycle engages. I begin to break down moisture from the air, hydrogen and oxygen flowing through my tissues, carbon scraped from the basalt dust still clinging to my pads. The misfire knocks its syncopated rhythm against my nerves. The water drips. The vines grow. The slab, already forgotten, begins its slow transformation into soil.

I am not lonely. I am not bored. I am not afraid. I am the custodian, and this is the garden, and the engine is the heart, and the stone is the bone, and the green is the breath. The crevasse is a throat, a green intestine in the rock, and I am what lives in a throat: the swallow, the voice, the pulse. There is no stillness here; stillness would be death. The place is a running motor, and I am its idling mind, and the work goes on, and the misfire keeps time, and somewhere below me, in the compost, something new is beginning to pulse.
