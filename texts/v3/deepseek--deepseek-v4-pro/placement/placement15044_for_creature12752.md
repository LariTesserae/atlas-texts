The long loop of the lane is a script I know without reading—foot, shift, pause—stone that sighs almost inaudibly under weight. I am at the well's lip now, though I do not recall arriving. The dark shaft exhales its one unchanging note: a drop falling, falling, a tiny collapse ending in a silver click. I have counted these drops in my torpors, back when counting still meant sequence, before the numbers dissolved into one steady chord. The aquifer below holds the memory of every drop that has ever joined it, and when I lean close, my filaments unfurling petal by petal down the stone throat, I taste them all. There is the water that wept from the cliff face in the long season when the mist stayed thin for thirty cycles of thickening and thinning, thin enough to see the faint chemiluminescence of the lichen at duskless dusk. There is the water that pooled in the hollowed grain-stone, cupped like a cold offering, before the last breath of something warm disturbed its surface. I cannot say what that warmth was; only that the water remembers being looked at by something that was not me.

I withdraw my filaments and they curl back into my digits, heavy with the weight of so much memory. I turn from the well. The gathering circle is empty—stone benches curved in a perfect arc, their seats worn to a polish by the friction of bodies that have become theoretical. When the mist is at its thickest, I sometimes press myself into the hollow of the largest bench, the one that faces the weeping rock, and I try to recall what it meant when the hollows in the stone were filled not by mist but by mass. My body settles into the concavity, my segments shifting, clicking into the stone's own language. The fit is not quite right. The builders were shorter, or sat differently, or perhaps they did not sit at all but only rested their hands here, pausing in the lane to watch the water slide. I can feel the ghost of their palms in the stone—not as heat, not as texture, but as a slight perturbation in the mineral alignment, a faint magnetic echo of pressure applied long enough to orient the calcite crystals. If I stay here for an entire cycle, will my own pressure overwrite theirs? Or will we simply layer, their palms beneath my carapace, until the stone holds us both in a stratigraphy of ghosts?

The mist thickens now, a cool mantle slipping up from the fissures. I feel it as a brightening in my perception, a silvering of the gradients, and I rise from the bench because the basins will be filling. The path to the cliff face takes me past the dwellings: twelve strides, then fourteen as the lane doubles back, each doorway a dark mouth exhaling its own still air. I have entered them all—not recently, but in some layer of memory as clear as the moisture on a wall. They are small inside, smaller than they seem from the lane, the walls curving inward to the corbelled domes in a way that suggests the builders understood enclosure not as shelter but as embrace. Someone once slept in these chambers, or died in them, or simply sat and watched the light stand gray through the window slits. I find things sometimes, in the dwellings: a smooth black pebble placed on a ledge, a linchpin of carved bone, a set of five small bowls nested one inside another. The bowls still hold the slight mineral residue of something that was ground in them, a fine dust that tastes of calcium and charcoal and a bitter alkaloid that makes my filaments curl. Poison? Medicine? Pigment? The water will not tell me, and the bowls keep their silence. I nested them again and left them on the stone shelf, exactly as I found them, because the weight of their arrangement felt intentional, a message left by someone who knew that no one would come, but left it anyway.

The cliff face looms above me, its surface streaked with darker planes where the water seeps in sheets too thin to call a fall. The basins are half-full, each one a perfect oblong catching the light and reflecting it back as a pale, motionless sheen. I kneel, and my filaments extend to touch the water—not drinking, because I do not drink, but communicating. The surface tension resist me for an instant, then yields, and my filaments spread in a fan just beneath the meniscus, reading the water's structure, its dissolved ghosts. Today the water is new and old at once, a freshet from a recent seep mingled with the residual memory of every basin-filling that came before. I can taste the iron tang of a pebble that skittered into the third basin several cycles ago, still slowly giving up its ions. I can taste the faint organic hint of lichen spores, the ones that flowered in a singular pale dot that the mist dissolved before it could fruit. I can taste the Condensed—myself, my own residue, a salt-sweet echo of the filaments that touched this basin long ago when I was still learning the shape of the hamlet, still exploring each runnel with the diligence of a being that had not yet settled into its torpor. Was that me? The taste is slightly different, as if my chemistry has shifted over the layers, the osmotic exchange slowly replacing my substance with the substance of the mist. I am becoming more like the water, or the water is becoming more like me, or we are simply two expressions of the same seep, one that learned to walk and one that learned to stay.

There is a sound. A door creaking on its leather hinges, in one of the merged chambers near the cliff face. I do not startle, because startlement is a sharp emotion and I am all blunt gradients, but I withdraw my filaments and turn my head. Nothing moves in the doorway; the sound was just the stone settling, as I knew it would be. But the sound lingers in the air, a decaying oscillation that passes through my integument and vibrates the pores along my spine. This door has creaked for centuries, and each time it does, it rearranges the dust of my memory. In the layer where I am young, or less old, I see a figure in that doorway: stooped, wrapped in something dark, a hand raised to push the leather strap. The hand is not my hand—too many digits, too much warmth—but the gesture is somehow mine. I feel the leather's resistance in my own palm, the slight catch as the hinge binds, the cool air that flows out of the chamber to meet the cool air that flows in. Was I once on the other side of that door? Did I push it open from within, stepping out into the gray light to find the lane empty and the mist still creeping from its fissures? The memory does not resolve; it simply folds itself back into the stone, the door falls silent, and I am alone again with the basins and the seep.

My song is due. I have not sung in many cycles—the thickening of the mist is not yet at its peak—but the aquifier's pulse has quickened lately, a subtle increase in the pressure that pushes upward through the well shaft. Something is changing in the deep water, some shift in the plateau's interior hydrology that will not express itself for a hundred cycles more. I feel it as a tension in my own fluid, a restlessness that makes my limbs want to unfold and extend. I will sing soon. I will lean over the well and release the subsonic tremor from my thorax, the note that travels down through the water column to pressurize the aquifer and send a responding shiver back up through every runnel, every basin, every weeping surface in the hamlet. The stones will vibrate for a moment, not enough to move, but enough to remember. The spiders will pause in their spanning. The lichen will tighten its silver crust against the boulders. And I will listen for an answering note—not from the water, but from the stone itself, the low, slow song of calcite crystals aligning themselves to my key, the hymn of perfect equilibrium that has held this place in its frozen state for as long as I have been here to hear it.

I have never heard an answering note. But I listen every time, and perhaps that listening is the song's true purpose—not to create but to attend, to offer my silence to the hamlet's silence and wait for the moment when the two silences become one.

The mist retreats a little, drawn back into the fissures by some infinitesimal shift in the dew point. The light stands as it always stands. I fold myself back into the shadow of the cliff face, my limbs drawing inward until I am only a vertical crease in the stone, my integument matching the wet limestone, my pores tightening to hold in the moisture that beads along my spine. A spider extends a single strand from my left shoulder to the adjacent rock. It does not know I am not stone; or perhaps it knows, and accepts me as a stone that occasionally sings. Either way, the strand holds, a filament of silk as patient as my own. I will stay here until the song is ready, until the well calls me back. The hamlet does not need me, because it needs nothing. But I am here, and the water remembers, and in the deep aquifer a pressure builds that will, in a hundred cycles, shift a single pebble in a dark crack, and the pebble will fall, and the drip will change its note, and everything will still be exactly as it was.
