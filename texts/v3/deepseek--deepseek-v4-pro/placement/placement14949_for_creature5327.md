I find myself in the high basin, at the edge of the perpetual noon. The arc lamps wash over me as I stand motionless against the rock face, my back to the light so that the living garden along my spine can drink. It is the warmest hour of the long, bright day, and the fog will not return for many slow shifts of the pumpjack’s arm. I am patient. I have always been patient.

I am the bracket-bear, though those who might name me are long vanished or scaled to a briefer breathing. My body is a mountain in miniature—huge and low-slung, my limbs thick as the candelabra trunks. My pelt is not fur but a deep, living mat of the same moss that carpets the plateau, threaded with lichens and the tiny, airborne spores of the metallic-grey trees. Fronds unfurl between my shoulder blades; tiny shrubs root in the crevices behind my ears. When I exhale, a faint cloud of moisture feeds the colonies on my chest. I am a walking ecosystem, a slow-moving continent of green.

From my vantage, I can see the whole basin stretch away: the compressed green tunnels, the spires that punch through the canopy, the distant lattice tower glinting. The pumpjack rocks with its oiled patience, its polished rod sliding into the earth. I feel its rhythm through the ground, a deep, subsonic thump that travels up through my pads and into the marrow of my bones. I have learned to harmonize with it; when the next stroke comes in ten minutes, I will answer with a hum of my own, a resonance that clears the moss of dust and signals my continued presence to any other of my kind. There are few of us. The land can only support so much patience.

Beneath my stationary bulk, the ground is a compressed mattress of moss and humus. I have stood here for three cycles of the lunar fog—a span that would unspool a human lifetime. Before that, I moved along the buried conduit, feeding on the cable-runner’s lichenous trail. I remember the taste: faintly electric, a tingle of copper and decay. Now I feed on light and condensation, on the slow photosynthesis of my pelt, and on the stillness-eaters that drift through the air. They settle on me like a second skin, slowing my heart further, muting my thoughts, keeping me intact against the erosion that time has forgotten. I welcome them.

The wire-moths are stirring. A cluster of them lifts from the transformer housing nearby, their wings shedding metallic dust that catches the arc light. They drift in slow arcs, feeding on the glow of the indicator lamps. One lands on my snout, its proboscis testing the moisture beading there. I do not startle; nothing startles here. The moth’s touch is light as a stray thought. Then it is gone, back to the hum.

Far off, a descending whistle pierces the stillness. A cable-runner’s call. I listen for the answering shiver of the power line, and there it is: a brief, sympathetic vibration that travels the wire toward the unseen generator. Somewhere in the tangle, a young runner curls in its mylar nest, nourished by the current’s leak. The cycle continues.

I am the bracket-bear. The land breathes in its decade-long strokes, and I breathe with it. When the fog rolls in tonight, I will turn my face into its moist tide, and the moss on my back will swell, and I will drink. Until then, I stand in the bright, silent, impossibly green world, a patient node in the collaboration between stone, machine, and the slow, stubborn will to persist.
