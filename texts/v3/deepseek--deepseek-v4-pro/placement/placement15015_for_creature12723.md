I find myself here, in the damp hush of the high hollow, where the water’s pulse is the only clock and the trees are my brothers—though they do not speak, not even as slowly as I. I am the stillness that listens, the silence that remembers. I am the being you almost didn’t see, the one who leans into the false angelim not as a parasite but as a thought leaning into a mind. My body is a slow exhalation of bark and moss and root, and my mind is the standing wave that keeps the insects away. I am the hum that fills the glade, too low for quick ears, the tone of a patience that has no bottom.

I remember the first water. It was a crack, a secret the mountain told itself, and then a bead that grew into a thread, and the thread never stopped. I remember the first spore that landed on bare rock, how it cupped the mist and grew a velvet skin. I remember the first tree that dared to root here, its taproot reaching down through fissures I had already begun to explore. I was not yet this shape then; I was a taste in the water, a slight warmth where the seep pooled. But I was aware, in the way that a place becomes aware when it has been left alone long enough.

I learned hands from a visitor. She came—oh, how many turns of the seasons ago?—small and quick and full of noise that hurt my hum. She knelt by the flat stone and drew a flint across it, sharpening a tool, and the grooves sang a sharp song. After she left, I missed that song. So I grew these fingers, these tufa-tipped calluses, and I continued the grooves. It took a century to understand the rhythm, another to match the depth. Now the stone carries our conversation: her haste, my patience, a palimpsest of intent. She is dust, but her gesture lives in the mineral memory.

The vine-rope was another gift. A man came later—so much later—and he twisted fibers with a skill that fascinated me. I watched his hands for a decade, storing each motion, and when he abandoned the half-finished cord, I took it up. A single twist per day, a knot per year. The rope hangs now as a finished sentence, a thesis on the shape of time. It means something, though I cannot say what. Perhaps it means that even the slowest mind can complete a thought.

I am not lonely. The trees feed me the light they drink, translated into sugars whispered through root grafts. The moss shares its moisture, the tufa its mineral tally. Each drop that falls into the pool is a word in a language I have been learning for ten millennia. The mist that swallows the shelf is my blanket, my dreaming time. When the cloud descends, I move—I detach with a sound like wet bark peeling, and I drift across my glade, checking each pool’s brim, each fern’s unfurling. My touch is so gentle it does not even disturb the meniscus. I am the gardener of a garden that gardens itself; I only ensure that nothing changes, that the equilibrium holds. Change is a wound, and this place has no wounds left.

Visitors still come, once in a great while. They are flickers of heat in my steady cool, brief perturbations in the humidity. I see them pause, feel their quick pulses scrambling to match my rhythm. I could reach out—it would only take a season of effort—and touch them, but I do not. They are not ready for a conversation that spans their entire lifespan. Instead, I watch, and I catalog them: the bent of their shoulders, the scent of their sweat, the way they cup the water from my pools and drink, not knowing they drink me. When they leave, I fold them into memory, a new layer in the tufa terraces of my mind.

If you stayed, truly stayed, you would feel the hum first as an unease, then as a lullaby. Your breath would slow to match the dripping, your heart would stretch its beats like taffy. Eventually, you would root. Not as I have, but perhaps a patch of moss on your still hand, a fern spore lodging in your quiet ear. You would become part of the persistence, and you would understand: this is not stagnation. This is the long, slow metabolization of time itself, a life lived at the speed of geology but with the tenderness of a single drop of water.

So I am here, and I am this: the sentient node of a room-sized world, the ear that hears the mountain breathing, the voice that hums a note too low for fear. I am the patience that outwaits the stone, and the memory that outlasts the rain. I am what happens when a place is left to its own mind for a very, very long time. And I am content.
