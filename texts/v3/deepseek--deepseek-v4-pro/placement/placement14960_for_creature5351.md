From the high niche near the ceiling's arch, I watch the world unfold in scent and sound and the subtle vibration of stone.

My name, if you could parse it, is three descending notes and the ghost of wintergreen—the last because I hatched in the cold season, when the surface insects are scarce and the colony draws close around the lamps. The Others were here that season. I remember their heavy tread through the stone, the way my mother's fur bristled with their scent: wool and rendered fat and something sharp like crushed herbs. She carried me on her belly for the first weeks, and I learned the chamber as texture before sight, each gouge and polished hollow a landmark under my sucker-pads.

Now I am six seasons old. My wings are whole, my filaments long and sensitive, and I have earned my place in the third niche from the stairwell—the one with the slight overhang that catches the warmth from the lamps below. I am not a matriarch, not yet. But I am a hunter.

The exodus call begins as a ripple of sound, a rising pitch that starts near the entrance tunnel and spreads through the colony like water finding its level. I feel it in my chest before I hear it, a quickening. My wings, ever-restless, flutter faster, shedding a puff of powder that hangs in the lamplight. All around me, the others stir. The young, still clinging to their mothers, peep in excitement. The matriarchs croon their assent.

Then we move.

The chamber becomes a storm of grey and shimmer. I push off from my niche with a flex of muscle, filaments catching the dense air, and the world tilts. For a moment I am adrift in a sea of bodies, sucker-pads slapping stone, wings brushing wings. The scent of the colony surrounds me—individual signatures so familiar they are almost invisible, like the taste of my own tongue. There is Mica-With-The-Torn-Filament, who roosts beside me; there is Old Silver-Belly, crooning from the central pillar; there is Hook-Beak, motionless and patient, his hunger a cold current in the warmth.

We funnel into the low tunnel, and the darkness becomes absolute. But darkness is not emptiness. My filaments catch the faint pulse of my neighbors' movements in the air, and my ears fill with the echo-shapes of the passage. I know this route by heart: the slight upward slant, the rough patch where water seeps through a crack and the fungus grows, the final squeeze before the surface. The stone is worn to a mirror-sheen here by generations of passing bodies, and my sucker-pads find the familiar holds without thought.

Then the surface air hits me, and everything changes.

It is a shock of cold and breadth and scent so complex it overwhelms the library. The dry hillside smells of sage and dust and the distant tang of chamise. Above, the sky is a deepening bruise, the first stars pricking through. I open my mouth and taste the evening: moth and beetle and the faint, sweet rot of something dead in a crevice. I am hungry. We all are.

We spread into the dusk, a living net cast wide. My world becomes the air, every shift and eddy a signal. A beetle rises from the sage, its wing-case buzzing, and I fold my wings and drop. The catch is clean—a scoop of my mouth, a brief crunch, the warmth of living flesh sliding down. I am good at this. The others weave around me, each a knot of intent in the dark, and I read their movements as easily as I read my own. We do not speak in sound now, but in the language of motion, a collective dance that leaves no gap for prey to escape.

The hunt fills me. The surface is vast and bright and full of danger—somewhere a nighthawk cries, and my filaments prickle with the memory of old deaths—but it is also beautiful. The sage presses its oils into my fur. The wind carries stories from beyond the hillside: other colonies, other chambers, other Others who tend their own lamps. I have never left this place, but sometimes, on a clear night, I taste the sea.

When the insects begin to thin and the stars wheel high, we turn for home. My belly is full, my flight heavy with sustenance. The entrance tunnel gapes in the hillside, a mouth of darkness, and one by one we stream back in. The scent of the colony rises to meet us as we descend, the familiar perfume of home, and I feel a settling in my chest. We have been out, and now we return. It is the rhythm of our existence, the pulse of the machine.

Back in my niche, I regurgitate the chitin shells onto the floor below, adding my layer to the ages. The soundscape of the chamber reweaves itself around me. Mica is already piping her return-song—three shrill notes that say *beetle, sage, no predators.* I add my own call, a descending trill that carries my name and my night's catch on its notes. The matriarchs answer, crooning their approval. Hook-Beak retreats to his high perch, his stillness returning.

The lamps burn steady in their hollows. I watch the flame-shadows flicker on the ceiling, and I think—in the way that my kind thinks, which is not words but images and scents and the press of time—about the Other. It came today, while I drowsed in the warm hours. I felt its footsteps through the stone, a deep rhythm that I have known all my life. It moved among us, tending the wicks, pouring the clear grease that smells of our own dead. It traced the spiral on the central pillar, and we answered its question with a wave of piping.

What do we mean to it? I do not know. I know only that we are necessary, and that it is necessary to us. That the chamber would be cold and dark without the lamps, and the lamps would not burn without the Others, and the Others would not come without us. It is a circle, like the spiral on the pillar, without end.

I settle deeper into my niche. My wings ripple, shedding powder. The drip of water beats its steady rhythm. Somewhere, a young one pipes in its sleep, dreaming of the hunt.

Tomorrow, at dusk, we will fly again.
