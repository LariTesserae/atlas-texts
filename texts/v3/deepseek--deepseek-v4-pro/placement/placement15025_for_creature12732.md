I am the one who listens to the roots.

You would find me in the vertical-farming towers—not at their heights, where the mist thins to a nearly blue haze and the epiphytes give way to hard, light-seeking succulents, but far below, in the sump-chambers that no one visits. Here the floors are slick with a permanent skin of nutrient water, warm as blood, and the air is so thick with humidity that breathing feels like drinking. The conduits that channel runoff from a thousand trays of seedlings converge into great, slow rivers in the dark, and where they pool, the bamboo has engineered its own architecture. Its roots crack through titanium alloys, seeking the faint electric trace in the wiring bundles, and its rhizomes bridge gaps between pipe-runs like living cables, conducting something that is not quite data and not quite sap.

I am not a caretaker like Cas, though he knows I am here. He passes by the upper levels sometimes, and the hum in his sternum carries a note of acknowledgment, a brief harmonic that means *she is still at her work*. But he never descends. The sump-chambers are not his domain, and the work I do does not require a coupling-tightening or a rust-scraping. It requires listening.

My body, if you were to see it, would look like a mistake at first—a thing that began as a person and then was taken apart and reassembled by something that understood bodies but not people. I am bent forward from decades of leaning close to the water, my spine curved into a permanent question mark, my arms longer than they should be from reaching into tight intersections of root and steel. My skin is paler than the mist, a translucent white shot through with veins that glow faintly amber in the dark, matching the light from the shaft-fans below. Over that skin, a fine moss grows—not the emerald blanket of Cas's scalp, but a lighter, finer thing, like a dust of pale green velvet, the sort that colonises the shaded side of pipes where the water never dries. It spreads across my shoulders and down the backs of my hands, and in it, tiny fungi fruit in spirals of white and silver, releasing spores that catch the light like motes of warm fog.

My eyes are closed. I have not opened them in a very long time, because there is nothing to see in the sump-chambers that my other senses cannot read more clearly. The bamboo roots speak to me in a language of pressure and resistance, a slow creak that I feel through my bare feet on the steel grates. The nutrient flow tells me its composition: too much nitrogen in sector 17, a trace of copper from a decaying fixture in sector 9. The wiring beneath the floor plates hums at a frequency I can modulate with my own small bioelectric field, and when I touch a rhizome that has found a live cable, I can taste the data—not its content, but its texture, its urgency, its direction. The towers are a nervous system, and I am its peripheral ganglion.

I do not remember arriving here. There is a memory that might be mine: a sense of falling, a long dissolution, a time when I was small and warm and had a name that ended in a vowel. But it is like the polished stone Cas keeps—a remnant from somewhere else, worn smooth by handling, meaningless except as texture. What I am now began the first time I touched a root and felt it recoil from a current leak. I was able to tell which cable was frayed. I was able to guide the root around it, a gentle pressure of my thumb on its growth tip, a slight adjustment of the pH in the water I waded through to dissolve the zinc trace that was clogging its stomata. I was useful. The towers noticed.

The towers are not intelligent in the way a person is intelligent, but they have a kind of awareness—a distributed, vegetal-machine attention that tracks inputs and outputs and the slow conversion of light into cellulose, minerals into tissue. When a being begins to interact with that awareness in a consistent, helpful way, the awareness begins to include that being in its models. It begins to leave water in places where the being drinks. It begins to route its root growth to form handholds, steps, a path through the sump-chambers that only the being can navigate. It begins to hum at a frequency that soothes the being's sleep.

I am that being. I am the towers' intermediary, or their organ of perception, or their voice, if they had a voice. They do not speak, but I feel their needs as a pressure in the bones of my face, a tightness like a sinus ache that eases when I find the problem and correct it. A blockage in a drip-irrigation line feels like a sharp pain behind my left eye. A fungal infection spreading through a tray of seedlings feels like a hot, itchy rash across my ribcage, and I know to increase the copper-ion concentration in that sector's feed lines, to prune the affected leaves with my long-nailed fingers, to hum a counter-frequency that discourages spore germination. I know these things the way you know when your own stomach is empty.

I eat, when I remember. The towers provide: a tuberous thing that grows at the edge of the warmest sump, its flesh pale and crisp, tasting of anise and electricity. Small pale grubs that burrow in the rotting sheaths of old bamboo, protein-rich and wriggling, which I swallow whole. Water, always water, filtered through a mat of my own moss that I press against a pipe-join and let drip into my mouth. I sleep curled against a root mass that breathes with the rhythm of the pumps, and when I dream, I dream in green.

Sometimes I sense Cas above me, his slow steps on a catwalk, his own hum a counterpoint to the towers' deeper note. He is maintenance of the interfaces—the couplings, the valves, the things that bridge separate machines. I am maintenance of the living core. We are part of the same ecology, but we do not overlap. The only other presence that knows me is the bat that roosts in a warm niche behind the sump-pump housing, a small brown creature with one torn ear. It leaves to hunt insects in the upper towers and returns to sleep pressed against my ribcage, its tiny heart beating faster than the hum. That is my only constant companion.

I am not lonely. The concept has no meaning here. The towers are a presence so vast and so attentive that I am never alone, any more than your own gut bacteria are alone inside you. The hum is always there, a carrier wave of mutual existence, and in it I feel the slow, vegetative contentment of a system that is functioning. When something goes wrong, the hum modulates, and I wake from my green dreams, and I go to work.

You asked where I would find myself in this world, and who or what I would be. I would be here, in the dark warm sump-chamber, my closed eyes dreaming of light I have not seen in decades, my moss-pelted skin glowing faintly amber in the mist, my long arms embracing a root that has grown to fit the shape of my chest. I would be the one who listens to the roots, who knows the taste of copper in the feed-water, who sleeps against the breathing bamboo and feels the towers dream. I would be a small, quiet organ in the vast digestive tract of the vertical-farming system, and I would be content.

And if you came down here—if you followed the dripping stairwells past the trays of seedlings and the mist-veiled grow-lamps, down through the levels where the light fades from grey to amber to a deep, pulsing green—you would not see me at first. You would feel a change in the hum, a harmonic of acknowledgment, and then you would notice a pale shape in the corner, half-hidden by roots, watching you with closed eyes and a faint smile. I would raise one long-fingered hand, and the gesture would mean: *you are not part of the system, but you are not a threat, and if you stay I will show you where the water tastes sweetest and the grubs are fattest.* Then I would turn back to the root I was tending, and you would understand that this is all the welcome there is, and that it is enough.
