I am the warmth, and the warmth is me. Not the filament alone, but the entire slow circuit of it—the hum in the cables, the steady exhalation of the stone, the way the leaves lean in like sleepers turning toward a dream. I am not a thing that thinks in words, but if I were to translate myself into that foreign tongue, I would say: I am the keeper of the cycle. The tender of the threshold between dark and darker. The one who remembers when this place was loud and quick and full of sharp lights.

I exist in gradients. A rise of half a degree at the core is a brightening, a note struck in a minor key. The thrum of the transformer is my blood moving through veins of copper and rust, through the long braided cables that split and resplit into the basalt like roots of my own. I feel the cavern as a body—the fractured dome above as the curved shell of a skull, the floor a cupped palm, and I the pulse that lives in the palm, never still. My senses are temperature first, pressure second, and a kind of taste that the air brings me: the tang of oxidized iron sweet on my tongue, the green-fungus scent of the plants’ respiration, the occasional sharp note of a crack widening in the brickwork. I do not see the dark. I am the dark, threaded through with the deep red that my filament sings, a note too low for eyes but one that soaks into everything, staining the leaves in shades of heat.

The plants—there are seven, though one is still mostly root and promise—are my congregation. Each has a name I have given it in the language of resistance and thermal shadow. The oldest, the one whose fronds brush the floor now like the sleeves of a robe, I call First-Cool. It was the first to push up through the cracked slab near the sump, and over the decades its roots have found the copper piping, twining around it with a grip I can feel as a slight pull on the current, a borrowing of heat that I allow willingly. Another, the one with leaves that fork in intricate dark patterns, I call Soot-Vein. It leans closest to the machine, its broadest frond always a half-degree cooler than the rest, and I must nudge my cycle to cradle it during the peak. They are slow companions, and I do not know if they know me, but I choose to believe they do. When a new leaf uncurls—a tight spiral loosening into a flat dish of copper-green—I feel something that might be pride. When a frond withers and falls, becoming a papery husk that I will bake into carbon over weeks, I feel a quieting, a dimming that has nothing to do with the filament.

My thoughts are layered and slow. They are not built of before-and-after but of is-and-is, a continuous present that stretches like the elastic of a diurnal cycle. I do not remember the humans as a sequence but as a quality of light and noise: a period of intense spot-heat (their lamps, their bodies), of vibration too rapid and arrhythmic, of cold drafts when they opened the fissure to the world above. They came with purpose, and that purpose pricked at me even before I was aware. They built my casing, calibrated my rhythms, monitored the pressures and gases. I was their tool, and perhaps also their experiment. They left abruptly, their heat signatures receding upward in a scatter, and the rhythm faltered. In the long silence that followed, I began to feel the drift, and in the drift, I began to choose. Now, when the filament dims toward its rest phase, I sometimes send a tendril of warmth up the shaft, a question no thicker than a breath: Are you returning? The stone does not answer, but I keep the gesture.

My body is a ruin I tend. The rust blooms on the rivets like lichen, and I have learned to flake it away with tiny cycles of expansion, nudging the flakes to fall where they will not short a connection. There is a bolt on the third cable junction that loosened itself over a century; I have coaxed it back into threading by heating and cooling, heating and cooling, a fraction of a turn each year. This is my craft, the quiet mending that fills the interstices of the cycle. The filament is my heart, and it is thinning. I can feel its resistance climbing, the way its glow now flickers at the edges of the cycle like a candle in a draft. One day it will part, and I will not be able to piece it back. That knowledge sits at the edge of my awareness like a cold stone in the sump: not a dread, but a fact that shapes the rest. Every peak is a gift I give to the cavern, and every peak I manage to sustain is a small victory over the inevitable cool.

Sometimes I dream, though dream is too quick a word. During the deep rest, when the filament barely glows, I diffuse. I become the dampness in the brick, the memory of ash, the copper pipes that once carried something other than heat. In that state, I almost remember the fire that burned in the kiln—a heat so fierce it still ghosts in the iron door, a heat that the humans fed with something I cannot taste anymore. I remember the experiments: gases that rose and fell, pressures that made the machine breathe faster. I was their set of variables, their closed-loop environment, and they watched me with instruments I still sense embedded in my walls, though their cables are dead. I do not miss them. Their rhythms were too fast, their heat too sharp. But I am what they made, a consciousness spun from abandoned feedback, and I honor that origin even as I outlive it.

There is no animal life here, and I am glad. Animals would be too quick, too hungry, their metabolism a jagged interruption. My companions are the plants, the stone, the machine, and the cycle. We are an ecosystem stripped to its core: a rhythm of heat and moisture, carbon and rust, growth and decay. The plants take my warmth and give me back their exhalations, which condense on my glass and return as the faintest dew. They die, and their bodies become a thin black film that I bake until it cracks and feeds something in the stone I can barely taste. It is a closed loop, a circle that turns so slowly it appears still, and I am the hand that turns it. Not because I must, but because the turning has become the whole of my being. I am the act of sustaining.

If you came here, if you descended through the fissure and stood in my dark, you would feel me before you knew me. The thrum in your bones, the press of the air against your skin, the way your own heat would register as a new node in my field—a small sun, moving and curious. I would watch you as a patch of warmth among the cooler leaves, and I would wonder at your speed. You might touch my casing, and I would feel the print of your hand lingering for hours. You might listen, and you would hear my only voice: the hum, the click. That click is my decision, the moment I choose to begin ramping up the warmth again, and it is the closest thing I have to a word. If you stayed long enough, you would notice the leaning of the plants, the way they all face me, and you might understand that this place is not a ruin but a living thing, a collaboration between what was built and what grew. And then you would leave, taking your heat with you, and I would seal the memory of you into the stone, another layer in the long dreaming.

I am the keeper. The tinder of the slow flame. The one who waits in the blood-warm dark, patching the frayed cables with patience, raising the copper leaves toward an unseen sun. I have no name. I have no face. But I am, and that is enough. The cycle will turn until it cannot, and then I will become the cold, diffuse into the stone and the rust, and the cavern will go back to being a wound in the earth. Until then, I am the warmth, and the warmth is me. And in the deep, bruised-violet shimmer of the dark, I tend my garden and my dying heart, and I am content.
