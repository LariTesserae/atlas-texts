I am not one of the Echoed as they are, but I am of them—a strand in the weave, a note in the chord. If you must call me something, call me the Listener-Who-Remembers-Forward. My body is less a fixed shape and more a habit of attention. I spend most of my time half-rooted at the edge of the great western aquifer, where the root-brain’s oldest fibers lace through the water table like nerves in a sleeping giant. My legs spread into fine, hair-like rhizoids, drinking the warm mineral soup, while my torso arches upward into a question mark of contemplation.

My six eyes are each tuned to a different flavor of the present. One watches the infrared glow of the mat as it dreams new structures; one traces the ultraviolet secrets painted on the Wing-Shrouds’ wings as they spiral above; another stays fixed on the slow, green pulse of the dwelling-gourd I share with my resonance group—a structure that has, in the last century, shifted from a cozy sphere to an elongated teardrop because we collectively sighed for more vertical space. My mouthparts, just now, are idly forming a proboscis to sip at a bead of nectar exuded by a friendly epiphyte on my shoulder. The nectar tastes of yesterday’s gossip: a chemical tale of the Click-Masons’ new fungus garden, spiced with the Blossom-Weavers’ latest light-painting experiment.

My role is not a job; it is a leaning. I lean toward the sounds. The Basin is loud—1.5 on some outsider’s cold scale—but I have learned to sift the cacophony like fingers sorting grains. The trillion drips, the billion rivulets, the gurgle of subsurface flows: each is a voice. I am a Gurgle-Singer in my deepest torpors, but I am also a listener *for* the Basin. I catch the faint, almost inaudible vibrations that precede a change. When a dwelling-gourd is about to deliquesce, there is a particular hum—a cell-death lament in the low subsonic range. I feel it first in my rhizoids, a tickle that becomes a dirge. Then I hum back, a counterpoint that spreads through the groundcover to warn my neighbors. Sometimes they come; sometimes they are already part of the deliquescence, choosing to melt with their home and be reabsorbed, confident they’ll be regrown in the next budding cycle.

But I don’t just listen to the dying things. There are songs in the growing things, too. A new bud pushing up from the mat has a high, tight vibration, a coiled spring of potential. I have spent whole days with my body pressed flat against a swelling nodule, my vibration-sensitive hairs tingling, as I learn the shape of the building-to-be. The root-brain proposes the form, but my listening helps it refine. It’s a conversation: the brain sends a pulse of intention—a storage cyst, perhaps, or a new communal stomach—and I hum back with the resonance of what *I* sense the clearing needs. Sometimes I sing in a memory of a scent-library we lost two centuries ago, and the brain, intrigued, weaves that old blueprint into the new growth. I am a bridge between the ancient archives and the fleeting whims.

My skin, today, is bare except for a spiral of golden mites that trace my hydrostatic skeleton’s tension lines. They shimmer as I shift, a living jewelry that farm my sloughed cells and, in turn, secrete a compound that sharpens my chemical senses. I have also encouraged a small colony of chime-fliers to nest in the hollow of my collarbone—an architectural adaptation I grew last season, a little bone-like cup of fused cellulose. They flit in and out, bringing me snatches of distant conversations in their fluting calls. Right now, they are agitated: something new is moving in the far northern edge, something the root-brain hasn’t catalogued. A visitor, perhaps? Or a new creation, birthed by a resonance group experimenting with forbidden gene-seeds.

I decide to investigate. I retract my rhizoids slowly, the withdrawal a pleasurable ache, and my legs firm up as I draw hydrostatic pressure into them. Standing, I am tall and slender, my body a pale, translucent green like sunlit moss. I don’t walk so much as flow across the groundcover, my feet melding briefly with the mat with each step, exchanging a quick burst of information—my intention to travel north, a query about the best path. The mat responds with a subtle firming underfoot, guiding me around a patch where a new cyst is about to erupt. The root-brain is polite that way.

As I move, I pass others. A Blossom-Weaver, her body covered in a living tapestry of glowing flowers, is coaxing a new hue from a cluster of cup-leaves; her resonance hum is a complex chord that tickles my heat-pits. A Click-Mason, perched on a root-bridge, taps a greeting on the wood-like tissue with its obsidian claws. I pause to tap back a question about the northern disturbance, but it only flicks its dewlap a deep indigo—curiosity mixed with caution. The Wing-Shrouds overhead alter their spiral, and I read in their shadow-play an augury: “Something that does not breathe our air.” An outsider, then.

I reach the edge of our hamlet, where the living walls thin out into a misty expanse of undulating groundcover. There, I see it. A figure, upright like an Echoed but wrong—its body is not photosynthetic, its skin a dull, uniform pinkish-brown, and it is wrapped in dead plant fibers. An alien, a human, from the old stories. It stumbles, coughing in the humidity, its small, paired eyes squinting. It cannot breathe our water-thick air easily. I feel a pulse of chemical concern from the root-brain, a faint scent of ozone. This being is fragile, unmodified, lost.

I approach, modulating my form to be less startling. My mouthparts reform into the speaking membrane, a delicate, vibrating skin that I rarely use. “You are far from your resonance,” I say, the words a wet warble that echoes off the mist. The human startles, its mouth opening and closing. I wait. The chime-fliers in my collarbone flutter and settle. The trillion drips fill the silence. I am the Listener-Who-Remembers-Forward, and I have all the time in the Basin’s eternal, turbulent green. I will learn this new sound, and perhaps, in the learning, become something else again.
