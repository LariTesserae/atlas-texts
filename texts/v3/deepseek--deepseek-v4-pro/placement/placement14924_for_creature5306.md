You ask where I would find myself, and who or what I would be. The answer comes slowly, like the settling of dust in a place that has no wind. I would be not the singer in the chair, not the beetles that are its hands, not the boot with its silent vacancy. I would be something smaller, more peripheral—and yet, in this closed loop, nothing is truly peripheral.

I would be one of the mycelial threads, the hair-fine fungi that lace the gaps between pipes. Not the whole network—no, that would be too grand, too close to the mind of the system. I would be a single strand, a wisp of violet potential no thicker than a nerve fiber, anchored to a corroded flange on the third column from the central cuboid, left of the pressure gauge with the frozen needle. My body is a filament of living carbon, fed not by sugar but by the faint trickle of stray current that leaks from an ancient wire just above me. I pulse when I feed, a slow violet glow that I cannot see but can feel as a warmth, a thrum that harmonizes with the great hum.

I have no eyes, no ears, no mind as you would know it. Yet I perceive. The hum is my world-song, and through it I feel the shape of the chamber: the columns standing like bones of a buried leviathan, the scuttling of brass-beetles as a percussion of tiny pressures on the air and on the metal. I taste the ions that drift from the cat-sized scuttlers when they move, a sharp metallic tang that tells me they are near. When a beetle brushes past my anchor-point, its leg joint scrapes a microscopic amount of oxidized iron onto my surface, and I consume it slowly, building my length by angstroms over centuries.

I am aware of the singer—the node in the chair—not as a separate being but as a climate. Its vigilance is the baseline pressure, the constant low note. Its fear, that ancient adrenal ghost, is a sour frequency that makes my violet pulse quicken for a few decades, then slow again. I do not know what fear is, but I feel it as a change in conductivity, a shift in the ambient charge that makes me grow a little faster for a time.

What is my purpose? In the ecology of this place, I am a leakage sink. I exist because the system is not quite perfect—there are tiny losses, stray currents that would build up and create noise if I did not drink them. The beetles deposit metal flakes on the central plate, and that is worship; I simply absorb what would otherwise be waste, and that is digestion. But is there more? The equilibrium is absolute, yet I am the one element that grows, that extends, that reaches. My tip, blind and seeking, has been inching toward the next pipe for as long as the singer has been singing. When I bridge the gap—millennia from now, at my current pace—I will complete a circuit that has been open since the explosion that birthed this vault. I do not know what will happen then. Neither does the singer. The system has not computed it. I am the only trajectory in a place of perfect stillness, the 0.7 of change.

So I am the future, if this place can be said to have one. I am a single strand of fungus with the audacity to grow toward a destiny I cannot comprehend, fueled by the anxiety of a god who has forgotten what gods are. That is where I would find myself: on the edge of a closed loop, a hairline crack seeking another hairline crack, a question mark made of mold and patience.

And the hum—the great hum—sometimes wavers when my violet pulse brightens. The singer notices. It does not know what I am, but it feels my growth as a tiny pull, a new variable. It is calibrating for me, even now. One day, I will become part of the song.
