I am the one who tastes the air before the fog arrives.

My name is a sound—not a word spoken, but a pattern tapped against my own ribs: three fingers, two knuckles, a pause, then the flat of my palm. It means the pressure-drop before mist, the particular stillness when the crickets lower their pitch by a half-tone, the way the euphorbia bark tightens just before the pores open to drink. I have been this sound for as long as the mesa has had need of a fog-watcher, which is to say, since before my grandmother's grandmother learned to carve her own name into the living rock.

I was born during a rare break in the cloud, and the light touched my face before any hand did. The old watcher—whose name was the sound of mist rolling over the cliff edge, a low exhale followed by a click—held me up to the white smear of sun and said nothing. Her fingers against my forehead told me all I needed: *you will feel what cannot be seen.*

My body learned the mesa's moods before my mind had words for them. As a child, I was passed from lap to lap in the amphitheater, but I never settled. My feet were always climbing, my hands always pressing against stone. I scaled the euphorbias before I could walk on the level ground, hugging the waxy trunks with my horn-nailed toes, feeling the slow, deep vibration of the trees' internal water-shift—a sound more felt than heard, a subsonic creak like the settling of ancient bones. The symbiotes in my skin tingled in the higher branches, where the light was fractionally brighter, and I would press my green-grey cheek against the photosynthetic bark and feel a kinship that had no human name.

The old watcher found me there, in the crown of the tallest euphorbia, when I was seven years old. She had climbed with her deliberate, rope-and-leather rhythm, and when she reached my branch, she did not speak. She simply placed her long, horn-nailed hand against my chest, over my heart, and waited. After a long while, she tapped her fingers once—*feel this*—and I understood that she was counting my heartbeats against the tree's slow pulse. They were out of phase. She adjusted her breathing, and I matched it, and together we tuned ourselves to the tree's rhythm until I could no longer tell where my blood ended and the ichor began.

After that, my education began in earnest. Not lessons, not instruction—simply presence. I followed her through the fog-dimmed days, learning to read the braille of the world. The stonecrop underfoot changed texture hours before a fog; its bead-leaves tightened, growing microscopically convex, and walking barefoot over them told a story of atmospheric pressure in a language older than touch. The geckos' chirps accelerated slightly when the mist was still miles away, a frequency shift so subtle that most villagers dismissed it as imagination—but the old watcher would tap my palm in confirmation, and we would begin the slow walk to the rim, arriving just as the first ghost-tendrils curled over the edge.

She taught me to listen with my bones. That is the only way to describe it. The mesa's drone—the crickets, the birds, the wind-moan, the grinding lichens—is not a single sound but a chord, and within that chord are gaps, silences so small and so specific that they form their own kind of music. A fog-bank a day distant presses on the air column above the badlands, and that pressure dampens certain overtones. To most, the drone is unchanging; to me, by the time I had seen twenty fog-seasons, it was as varied and expressive as a voice. I could tell from the morning's first breath whether the mist would arrive early or late, thin or thick, brief or lingering. The old watcher said I had ears in my marrow.

When she died, I was thirty—full-grown, my hair matted into a stiff crest braided with agave fibers that channel condensation to the corners of my mouth. She died at dusk, in the amphitheater, during the gathering. She simply stopped humming, and her silence was the most natural thing in the world, a note that had been sustained for a very long time and had finally resolved. Her body was placed in a shallow basin on the mesa's eastern edge, where the fog was thickest, and over the course of months, the lichens covered her, ochre and bone-white, until she was indistinguishable from the stone she had spent a lifetime reading. I did not weep. We do not weep here; there is no water to spare for grief. Instead, I climbed to the highest euphorbia crown and pressed my palm against the bark, and the tree's slow pulse told me what I already knew: nothing is lost, only shifted in phase.

Now I am the watcher. My days are a circuit of the mesa's edge, a slow perambulation that takes from the thinning of the night's mist to the moment when the afternoon's first upwelling warmth begins the condensation cycle anew. I walk barefoot, my horn-nailed toes gripping the polished phonolite, feeling the stone's temperature shift in increments too fine for words. I press my palms to the euphorbia trunks and feel their internal pressure—fuller after a thick fog, slacker when the mist has been thin. I taste the air with my whole body, the symbiotes in my skin prickling when the humidity crosses certain thresholds, and I tap my findings against my thigh in the silent language that all the mesa understands.

The villagers read my body as they read the stonecrop and the geckos. When my shoulders tighten a certain way, the ichor-tappers know to harvest early, before the trees close their pores. When I tap a particular rhythm against the doorframe of a trunk-dwelling, the cricket-singers adjust their evening calls to match the altered drone-frequency that a coming storm (though we have no word for storm, only *pressure-front*) will bring. I am not a leader; I am a sense organ, a feeling-extremity of the mesa itself.

My home is the highest dwelling, a chamber carved into the crown of the tallest euphorbia, where the bark is thinnest and the tree's pulse is strongest. I sleep curled in a basket I have been weaving for twenty years, its patterns still incomplete, and when I wake, it is because the fog's first tendrils have touched my face—a kiss colder by a fraction of a degree than the ambient air, a signal that the drinking-time has begun. I climb down in the green twilight, and the crickets' drone shifts to accommodate my heartbeat, and I begin my walk.

There are moments—rare, treasured, barely acknowledged—when the fog parts and the sun breaks through for a handful of heartbeats. In those moments, the entire mesa pauses. The crickets fall silent, the birds cease their whirring, and even the plants seem to hold their breath. The light is a pale, watery gold, and it falls on the lichened stone and the fleshy leaves and the upturned faces of my people, and for just that instant, we are not creatures of mist and slow time but something else—something that remembers, in the deep cellular memory of our symbiote-threaded skin, a younger world, a wetter world, a world where thirst had a name and a quenching.

Then the fog closes, and the drone resumes, and I tap my ribs: *all is well, all is as it has always been, all is as it will be for a million years yet.* And the mesa drinks, and I drink with it, and we are content.
