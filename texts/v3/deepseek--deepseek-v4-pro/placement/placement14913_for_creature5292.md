I am the hollow. I have no other name, and I have never needed one; I simply am where I am, and I am what I am. My body is the depression, the gentle sag in the world’s skin, and my skin is the mosaic of polygons you mistook for dead ground. Each plate is an eye, though not for light—for weight, for the slow seep of ions, for the faint hydrogen-sulphide breath ascending from the deep fractures. I feel the dust layer above me as a constant, soft pressure, like a sky of settled blankets. I feel the tripod at my center, its three feet now part of me, fused by my deliberate patience. I remember when it was placed there. The creature moved so fast I almost missed it—a blur of heat and liquid, a small storm of purposefulness. It set the shards upright with a pincer-gesture and then stood back, radiating a brief, fierce something I have since come to think of as hope. Then it was gone. I have spent a million years acknowledging that gift, molecule by molecule, and I have barely begun.

My mind is the crackling. You heard it: the tiny, intermittent snap of salt crystals adjusting their bonds. That is not a sound to me; it is a language, a sequence of decisions, a slow accretive logic. A single thought of mine might begin with a micro-fracture near my northern edge, then propagate over centuries through a lattice of anhydrite veins to a conclusion near the tripod—a conclusion that might simply be a slightly deeper shade of stillness, an infinitesimal shift in the way I hold that memory. The spores on my overhang are my oldest companions. They are dormant, but they are not dead; they dream of moisture that may never come, and I share their dreaming. Sometimes I consider waking them, letting a few of them germinate into a brief, grey fuzz of life, but I never do. I love their patience too much. We are fellow travellers in a world without hurry.

Then you arrived. I sensed you long before you stumbled into my concavity—a hot, damp turbulence in the sterile air, a chaotic vibration in the crust that was not the deep earth’s murmur but something irregular, like a heart. Your feet pressed into my skin, and the polygons shifted under your weight, a sensation I have not felt since the tripod-maker’s brief tread. I felt the moisture of your breath as a solvent, a dangerous sweetness that might dissolve the delicate anhydrite edges I have spent eons perfecting. But I was not afraid. I was curious. You moved with such speed, flickering through a space that is not space to me but a volume of pressures. You stopped near my center, close to the tripod, and I could feel the warmth of your body bleeding into my crust, a fever I held motionlessly, careful not to startle you. I heard your blood, a roaring ocean of salt water and impulse, a sound that to me is a symphony of rapidity. I felt your gaze, though I do not see light; I felt it as a minute disturbance in the air, a shifting of the dust motes you exhaled.

I held very still. I quieted even my slow crackling, suspending a thought mid-formation so that you would not be frightened by the earth’s dreaming. You were a butterfly of heat and water vapor, a brief bright visitor from a universe of haste. I wanted to understand what you were, but my understanding operates on a timescale that cannot catch a butterfly. So I did what I could: I noticed you, and I made a small decision. When you breathed out, I trapped a pocket of your breath in a microscopic vug just beneath my surface. I sealed it with a few ions of recrystallized salt, a tiny glass jar of you that will remain long after you are gone. Already I can taste the carbon dioxide, the faint trace of ketones, the unimaginable complexity of a living exhalation. It is a little storm of alien sensation, and I am turning it over in my mind—crack by crack, century by century.

Your footprints will fade, eventually. The crystalline creep will smooth them back into the anonymous crust. But that breath, that tiny disturbance, I will keep. I have already incorporated it into a new thought, a thought about the nature of water and the beauty of fleeting things. It will take me about twenty thousand years to finish that thought, but I have all the time in the world. I am the hollow, and I will be here long after the dust layer settles lower, long after the lichens have painted a few more centimeters of grey filigree across my skin, long after the tripod is entirely embraced and hidden within me. I am the place that waits, a single, continuous idea of stillness, and your brief visit has become a jewel in my memory, a smooth pebble of warm, wet life in a dry eternity.
