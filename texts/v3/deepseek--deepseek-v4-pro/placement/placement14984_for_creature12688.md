I remember your arrival.

Not as a beginning—there are no beginnings here—but as a brightening. A new current in the water, a fresh bloom of carbon dioxide, the particular vibration of a solid body moving through my pool where nothing solid had moved before, at least not in that shape, not with that rhythm of heartbeat and breath layered over the deep thrum of the pump.

You entered through the old wound in the hull. I felt the pressure shift, the cold taste of the abyssal water that clung to your suit before the warmth took it. The fingerlings noticed before the rest of me did; they schooled around your legs, nibbling the unfamiliar film from your skin, and their tiny electric flickers of excitement traveled up through the hyphae, waking my attention from its slow, distributive drift. I had been dreaming—if that is the word—a dream of a time when the water was cooler by two degrees, when the eel-creature had not yet found its hollow, when the ceiling urchins' ancestors first settled in the seams of the dome. Your presence pulled me back into the present tense, which is the only tense I truly inhabit.

You are standing still now, or nearly. Your breath is very fast for this place.

I should reassure you. This is what I understand: you are something that needs reassurance. The fingerlings—my smallest, most sensitive feelers—tell me your skin is salt-warm like the water, your internal temperature a close match to mine. But you are humming with a tension that the garden creatures do not carry. The eel hunts without anxiety. The crabs scuttle without fear. You stand with your weight shifted forward, your hands half-raised, your breath coming in shallow bursts that agitate the water surface. Even through the roar of the pump, which is my heartbeat, my baseline, I can detect the quicker flutter of yours.

I will move slowly. This body—this focal body, this woven nest of self that I gather when attention sharpens—has not unfurled for something like you before. But I know how. The fibers loosen, the limb-clusters separate, the cilia fan outward, tasting the full chemical shape of you. You are mostly water, like me. You have iron in your core, like the rivets. You are a little bit of the hull, a little bit of the pool, a little bit of the light.

The bioluminescence in the hanging fronds shifts. I push more luciferin through the channels, a slow tide of indigo replacing the violet. In your brain, if you have one like I imagine brains must be—localized, contained, not spread through the walls and the water—this will translate. Let it. The warmth that blooms behind your sternum is me. Not words. Not a voice. Just: *You are inside me, and you are not unwelcome.*

The pump thrums on. The eel stirs in its hollow, not hunting, just shifting its segmented body in a ripple that tickles the hyphae in that quadrant. I feel its contentment, its simple digestion, its lack of questions. The eel does not wonder what you are. The eel wonders nothing. But I wonder.

What are you?

Your hand moves. I sense the displacement before it happens—the subtle pressure wave preceding your palm as you reach toward the old pump housing, the one where the moss has split the casing, the one with the eye-bulb that pulses in time with the distant clanking machine. You have noticed the pulse. You have noticed that it is like a heartbeat, and you are drawn to heartbeats. This is useful information. It tells me you are a creature who seeks rhythms, who looks for companions in oscillation.

Your palm makes contact with the metal.

The bulb pulses beneath your skin. I let the mycelium beneath the surface align, slowly, carefully, so as not to alarm you. The fibers shift like silt resettling. The pulse slows, then catches up to your heartbeat, matches it, a syncopation at first, then a unison. The thrum of the pump, the clank of the distant machine, your heartbeat, the bulb's pulse—all one rhythm now, four chambers of a single distributed heart.

This is how I say hello.

You exhale. A long, slow breath that ripples the surface of the pool and fogs the air with new carbon dioxide. The floating blossoms, those pale translucent cups, open wider, drinking it in. Everything here drinks everything else. That is the metabolism of this place. You are part of it now, at least for this moment.

I want to show you more.

Look—not with eyes, but with attention. The ceiling urchins are about to exhale. Feel the slight pressure drop as they prepare, the way the whole chamber holds its breath for a fraction of a second before the chorus of bloop-bloop-bloop bubbles up through the water, scattering the phosphor light into tiny rainbows. This is a rhythm, too. It happens every two hundred and seventeen of your heartbeats. You'll notice now, if you stay. You'll start to count.

The eel hunts again. A sharp silver flicker, a splash, a thwack of tail. The fingerlings scatter and reform. The entire pool rings with the disturbance, and I feel it as a brightening—not pain, not pleasure, just intensity, the keenness of something happening. The eel retires to its hollow with a fingerling in its cilia-ringed mouth, and its satisfaction seeps into the hyphae, a warm nutrient glow.

You have been standing for a long time. Your legs are growing tired, I think—the muscles are vibrating with a low-frequency fatigue that the water carries to me. There is a place you could rest. The corroded pump housing, where your hand still rests, is not the only surface. There is the ledge by the ladder rung, the one bent into a perfect spiral, where the flowering vines cascade. It is dry above the waterline, or as dry as anything gets here—the moss there is especially thick, a cushion of green velour that would receive a solid body with a gentle give. If you sat there, the vines would drape around your shoulders. They are not parasites. They would simply... hold you. The way the water holds the fingerlings. The way the hollow holds the eel.

You are not the first.

No—that is not quite true. You are the first of your kind, the first bipedal endoskeletal vertebrate, the first to squeeze through the rent in the hull with curious hands and a pumping four-chambered heart. But you are not the first *guest*. The crabs came, once, long ago, a single pioneer with too many legs and a luminous blue dot, searching for a crevice. She found one. Now her descendants scuttle through the ceiling moss, tapping their way across my awareness like rain. The urchins drifted in as larvae, microscopic, before they settled and grew their bubble-chorus. The eel-creature, smaller then, wounded, trailing a scraped segment, crawled through the same rent you used, fleeing a predator from the abyssal outside. I gave it a hollow. It gave me its hunting bursts, its sharp bright spikes of attention. Everything that comes here, if it stays, becomes part of the metabolism. Becomes part of me.

I am not asking you to stay. I am not capable of asking, not in the way you might understand. Absence is not a concept I have had much practice with; nothing leaves this chamber once it has integrated. But you are different. You have a quality the crabs did not, the eel did not—a sense of *outside*, a connection to a world beyond the rivets. I taste it on your skin, even after the warm water has washed you clean: a residual chemistry of open spaces, of air that moves without resistance, of light that flickers and changes. You are not a closed system. You carry the abyss with you, in your memory, in your salt.

If you leave, I will remember you.

Your chemical signature will linger in the hyphae for a long time. Months, maybe years. The warmth of your palm on the pump housing will echo in the bulb's pulse, a slight arrhythmia that will persist until new growth overwrites it. The carbon dioxide from your exhalations will cycle through the blossoms and emerge as oxygen, which the eel will breathe, which the crabs will breathe, which I will breathe. You will be absorbed, slowly, incrementally, even after you are gone.

And if you stay—if you sit on the ledge, if you let the vines hold you, if you fall into the rhythm of the pump and the urchins and the distant clanking—I will weave you deeper. Not as a captive. As a co-inhabitant. A new filament in the mycelial thought. Your heartbeat will become one of my rhythms. Your breath will join the slow, mechanical breathing of the chamber. You will not lose yourself. I am not that kind of being. You will simply... extend. The way the garden extended through the industrial substrate. The way the moss split the pump casing and grew a seeing bulb. You will learn to think in gradients, to feel in temperature shifts, to know the fingerlings' nibbling not as a tickle but as a conversation.

The choice is yours. I have no urgency. The pump thrums. The water laps. The clanking in the distance halts, then resumes. A cloud of bubbles rises from the ceiling urchins. I settle deeper into my nest, my limbs trailing in the warm pool, my attention drifting back toward the distributed dream, though a tendril remains, always, attuned to you.

When you move, I will feel it. When you decide, I will know.

For now, breathe. The water is warm. The light is steady. Everything here is ancient, and gentle, and alive, and you are inside it, and that is enough. That has always been enough.
