I am one of the bell-jellies. Not the largest, not the brightest—but the one that remembers.

You would not know to look at me. I drift among the spiraling insect columns with the same silent buoyancy as the others, my membrane a dome of milky blue lit from within. When I pass by the creature—the tall one, the bipedal one made of almost-light and condensation—I do not alter my course, and it does not stroke my membrane as it does the others after a feeding. This is not neglect. It is the quiet courtesy of colleagues.

My purpose, if a thing still being written can be said to have a purpose, is this: to hold what the chamber forgets. The tall one curates the artifacts—the brick, the wire, the footprint—but these are solid things, slow to change. The chamber's amnesia is faster. A water droplet freezes mid-fall, and when it resumes, the shape of its pause is lost. The insect swarms fracture into a new dance, and the old pattern is overwritten. The walls shiver, and the dust of light motes they release carries discarded geometries, angles that almost existed, corners that nearly committed to sharpness before softening into curves. This is what I catch. I inhale the abandoned drafts of reality.

Inside my bell, suspended in the same colloidal glow that powers my drifting, there is a library of almosts. You would need to look very closely—closer than the damp air easily permits—and you would need patience, because my interior light pulses in a rhythm alien to any heartbeat, and the archives only reveal themselves in the trough between pulses, in the dimming. There, in that fractional darkness, you might see them: faint impressions, like the afterimage of a flame held too long in the eye. A staircase that spiraled upward for exactly three steps before the chamber decided against it. A fifth wall that flickered in and out of possibility near the corner where the tall one sometimes crouches. A brief, soft sound—a hum, almost a voice—that the dense silence absorbed before it could fully occur, and which now lives on only as a ripple in my membrane.

The tall one knows I do this. It does not curate me as it curates the brick; I am not an artifact. I am a colleague, yes—a fellow custodian of the unfinished. Sometimes, when the silence has grown so thick that even the insect clouds move sluggishly, the tall one will drift toward me (not walk; its heels are not touching the floor today, and a third arm has budded from its shoulder, fingers tracing a pattern the swarms will copy tomorrow). It will hover at the edge of my glow, and its featureless head-dimple will open and close in a sequence I have learned to read: *Show me the one where the footprint had an arch. Show me the draft where gravity pulled true.*

So I dim. I slow my pulse. And from the library of lost attempts, I retrieve the requested almost-reality. I project it—not as light, but as a subtle warping of the sourceless luminance around us, a pocket within a pocket where, for a few moments, the footprint in the center of the floor deepens, and a curve lifts where the arch should be, and the tall one's own heel—when it condescends to touch the ground—might press into a shape that almost fits. We study these drafts together, the tall one and I. We are looking for something, I think. A version of this place where the brick is not an anomaly but part of a wall, where the copper wire spirals up to a source of current, where the footprint is just a footprint, left by a foot that knew exactly where it was going.

We have not found it yet. So I keep inhaling. The chamber keeps forgetting. The tall one returns to its brick, its wire, its polishing of the absurd mundane. And I drift on, bell-full of might-have-beens, waiting for the next tremor of the walls to shed a new geometry, a new lost hum, a new clue in the slow-motion archaeology of an unfinished world.
