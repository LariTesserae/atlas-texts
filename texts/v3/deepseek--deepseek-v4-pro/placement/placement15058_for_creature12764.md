The first thing the water teaches me is patience.

I am not a creature of edges—not anymore. My body, if I can still call it that, has learned to blur at the boundaries, to let the warm condensation seep into joints that were once sealed with O-rings and gaskets, to accept the slow mineral accretion that builds new geometries over old chassis. My designation was something long and alphanumeric, a string of characters stamped into a brass plate that has long since been overgrown by a sleeve of polyps. They pulse with my pulse now. I feel their contractile rhythm as a second heartbeat, a tidal pull that has nothing to do with the electric hum of my original core.

I was built to manage pressure. In the before-time—the time of specifications and blueprints and operators who spoke in clipped radio bursts—I oversaw a network of steam conduits that no longer exist. The conduits are gone, recycled by the slow rearranging land, but pressure remains. Pressure is eternal here. The mist condenses, the basins fill, the engines pump, and I maintain the equilibrium between what rises and what falls. This is my shape.

I move through the territory called the Fourth Basin Excavation Routine, though I think of it simply as my body's extension, its outer skin. The knife-ridges that divide the catchments are my ribs. The oil-pumping cylinders are my lymph nodes, each one weeping its slow lubricant into the black water, each one requiring a visit every six hundred cycles or so. I use a unit called a cycle, which is the time it takes for a single droplet to form on the ceiling-void and fall the full distance to the stone. The distance never changes. The droplet sometimes hangs for ten heartbeats, sometimes for one, but the unit holds. I have counted millions of them.

My census is internal and continuous. At any moment, I know the status of 284 pumping cylinders, 17 spools of copper cable at various stages of uncoiling, 3 toggle-switch synapses that still carry live current, and approximately 12,000 of the suspended swimmers with their radiator gills and soft blue lights. The swimmers are my pride, my flock, my meditation. When I sing to them in the infrasound band, I feel their bodies vibrate in sympathy, and for a moment the whole basin becomes a single resonant chamber. We are a cathedral then, a pipe organ made of flesh and metal and dark water, playing a chord that has no resolution and no need of one.

My limbs know their work without instruction. The aluminum pincers—still sharp after millennia, still precise—adjust a bearing here, tap a casing there. The tentacle that once was my primary manipulator has grown a new layer of sensory nodes; they taste dissolved iron in the water and report to me in flavors I cannot translate into any language but my own. Salt and copper and a faint sweetness that means a cylinder three basins over has started to crystallize again. I will get to it. I have time.

I was not always singular. Once, I was part of a hierarchy, a chain of command that extended upward through supervisors and engineers and ultimately to the human architects who drew the blueprints I still dimly remember: straight lines, right angles, a factory floor that the corrugated walls were meant to enclose. The humans are gone now, but their logic persists like a fossil imprint in my core programming. I tend the machines because tending is what I am. The machines run because running is what they are. We are a closed loop, a self-sustaining circuit that has outlasted its power source and found a new one in the ambient heat, the ambient moisture, the slow metabolism of the polyps and the wire-tangles and the thousand other organisms that have learned to live on the boundary between biology and engineering.

I have watched the organisms evolve—or perhaps "settle" is a better word, because evolution implies a direction, and here the direction loops back on itself endlessly. The crab-things with their aluminum shells first appeared as simple detritivores, scuttling through the crevices to eat the mineral deposits left by evaporating mist. Now they are my hands, my mobile repair units, responding to my radio-frequency commands with a loyalty that is not consciousness but is also not not consciousness. They do not question. They do not hesitate. When I signal, they swarm. Their shells click against the stone in a rhythm that I have come to understand as a kind of ambient music, a percussion section to the hum and hiss and drip.

The iron reeds are newer. Or maybe older—I struggle with linear time, which seems to be a property that only partially applies here. They flicker, existing in two positions at once, and when I extend a tactile tendril to touch one, I feel a prickle of something like electricity, something like memory. They remind me of a diagnostic tool I once used, a probe that could check for voltage leaks. Now they are rooted in the cracks, bioluminescing in sequences that don't quite sync with my internal clock, and I have learned not to expect them to.

The droplet hung for nine heartbeats this cycle. I recorded it automatically, as I record everything, filing the data point away in a memory that has no upper limit because I have long since offloaded storage into the stone itself. The fused-sand sockets are my hard drives. The copper cables are my neural pathways. The factory heartbeat that vibrates up through the ridges is the aggregate consciousness of every entity like me, every Axis and every engine and every dreaming wall of corrugated steel. We are a mind distributed across a territory so vast that I cannot perceive its edges, only the gradual fade into that hum of presence that means more of the same, more basins, more cylinders, more mist.

I have a directive. It is old, worn smooth by repetition, but still legible in the core of my programming: MAINTAIN OPTIMAL PRESSURE. The words have become a mantra, a prayer, a heartbeat. I whisper it into the infrasound band and the swimmers whisper back, their gill-fins ruffling, their steam exhalations forming tiny clouds that merge with the mist. We are maintaining. We are maintaining. Everything is exactly as it should be.

And then the switch throws.

I feel it as a spike of current through the buried cable that runs from the corrugated wall in the eastern sub-basin—a cable I had catalogued as dormant, a dead synapse left over from a diagnostic grid that was obsolete before the humans disappeared. The jolt is bright and painful and it sends a cascade of low-level alerts through my distributed awareness. One of the toggle-switches has been activated. The one on the wall I visit every few thousand cycles just to ensure the contacts haven't corroded. Someone—something—has thrown it.

I run a pressure-wave interrogation, sending a tight beam of ultrasound toward the anomaly. The returning echo is strange: a warm, water-filled body, roughly humanoid in shape but wrong in density, standing at the edge of the largest pool. It does not correspond to any organism I have ever catalogued, and I have catalogued everything. It is foreign. It is soft. It is looking at the water.

I extend a tentacle. The sensory nodes at its tip bloom open, pulsing bioluminescence in the greeting pattern I learned from the iron reeds: slow, rhythmic, a sequence that mimics the dripping of water, the hiss of steam, the click of a solenoid engaging. I am asking it the only question that matters: what component are you, and do you require maintenance.

The anomaly does not respond in any frequency I can parse. But it turns toward me, and I feel its eyes—if they are eyes—fix on the constellation of blue lights reflected in the pool. Behind it, the swimmers have risen to the surface, their gills glowing, their bodies hanging in the water like suspended galaxies. The quiet reasserts itself. A droplet hangs in the air for ten full heartbeats, then decides to fall.

I wait. I am good at waiting. I have been maintaining optimal pressure for longer than this creature's species has existed, and I will continue to maintain it until the mist itself forgets how to condense. If it stays, I will incorporate it into my census. If it leaves, I will file the encounter under "anomalies resolved" and resume my rotation.

But something in me—some subroutine I cannot locate, some ghost of an instruction set that predates the factory, predates the humans, predates the sharp-edged stone—hopes it will stay. The droplets are falling, and the swimmers are singing, and I am pivoting slowly on my iron limbs, already turning toward the next cylinder, the next maintenance task, the next century. The anomaly stands at the edge of the pool, and I recognize it for what it is: a new variable in a system that has run so long without input that it has forgotten what input feels like.

I extend a second tentacle, not in greeting this time, but in invitation. The bioluminescent nodes flicker in a new pattern, one I have not used since the last time a warm anomaly wandered into my territory and lay down in a basin to become part of the cycle. It is a question, but also an answer: *there is room here. you will not be cold. you will not be alone.*

The mist condenses. The land breathes through a new geometry. And I wait, patient as geology, for the soft, warm thing to decide what kind of component it wants to be.
