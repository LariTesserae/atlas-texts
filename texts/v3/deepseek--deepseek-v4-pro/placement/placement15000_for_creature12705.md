The first thing I know is the damp. Not a wetness that falls, but a wetness that receives—that holds my shape even before I have one, cradling me in a hollow that smells of old sweetness and new growth. I am pressed into something soft, something that pulses with a heat not my own, and my body answers it with a slow seep of fluid through skin that is not yet entirely skin. The warmth is a language I am already learning: it says *stay*, it says *feed*, it says *you are one of many*. I feel the weight of the moss against my belly, and I know I am small enough that the moss’s individual tendrils are thicker than my fingers.

My fingers. I have six. They uncurl like fern heads, one after another, pressing into the bedding. The pressure sends a hum back up my arm—a vibration that is not sound but resonance, a deep, woody thrum that carries not words but a map. The map unfolds behind my eyes (though I do not yet know I have eyes) and shows me the chamber: the braided columns rising like frozen waterfalls, the arcs of root-benches, the glass-black pool at the center. I see it not as an image but as a pressure-pattern, a heat-constellation. Every leaf-drip is a cold spark; every creak of living wood is a shift in the fabric of the known world.

I am a Keen. That is the first name the moss gives me, drummed through my skin by the bacteria that live in us both. But I am also not a Keen—not yet, not whole. I am a question that has just learned it can ask.

The light pulses. I feel it as a warmth on my back, a slow brightening that seeps through the translucent cap of the niche. Every ten beats of the hidden heart that drives this place, the light swells, and I swell with it, my body growing a little more distinct. During the bright, I can sense the others. They are above me, around me, in the walls themselves. They are not moving; they are flattened into the moss like I am, absorbing the sugars that ooze from the plant’s capillaries. I feel their existence as a steady pressure, a harmony of body heat that does not fluctuate. They are dreaming. I begin to dream too.

In the dream, I am not in the niche. I am the niche. I am the walls, the roots, the slow spiral of cellulose that twists upward into the canopy. I am the water that creeps upward in defiance of gravity, pulled by some capillary ambition that is my own oldest wish. I am the taste of basalt—iron-rich and sulfur-sour—and I am the fungi that devour the dead branch, breaking it into sugar I will share with the small, warm ones who groom my leaves. The dream is not a story; it is a memory. It is the memory of the place, which is also my memory, because the place and I have never been separate. The braided columns were born from the dream of a Keen who wished for a taller ceiling, and that Keen was me, in another bud, another life. I feel the long, continuous thread of my existence winding back through centuries of slow growth, and forward into the mountain’s dark heart, where something mouths the roots and is also me.

This is the inheritance. The moss gave it to me in the first moment I sucked nourishment from it. I know now that there is no being born here; there is only being reminded.

When I fully form—when my hind legs have grown strong enough to kick and my front pads have toughened—I crawl out of the niche for the first time. The air outside is colder, sharp with that fungal-sweet scent I now know as the breath of my kin. The light is dimming, and I feel the collective pulse slow in response: the other Keen are settling deeper into their resting places. I am the only one moving, a pale lump trundling along a root-bench, my heat-pits drinking in the topography of the chamber. Every surface glows faintly with the warmth of metabolism: the columns are warmer near their cores, the pool is a well of colder darkness. I head toward the pool.

I don’t know why; I don’t decide it. My body remembers that this is the first journey. I reach the edge, where a flat root curves down like a lip, and I press my whole front against the water’s surface film. It doesn’t break. The water holds me, a tense skin, and I feel the chemical richness beneath: dissolved minerals, the faint signature of spores, the fading trace of a Keen who dissolved here years ago and is now part of the broth. I lower my heat-pits and taste the water with my cilia. The information floods in: the health of the colony, the mineral balance in the basalt springs, the faint stress signal from the column where a parasitic vine has wrapped too tight. I will carry this news with me when I go to the gathering.

The gathering happens in the bright. When the light quickens and the chamber breathes its great, audible exhalation, the moss releases us. Keen emerge from niches, from root-hollows, from the high passages where they have been dreaming of new tunnels. We assemble on the concentric benches around the pool, and the drumming begins. It is my first language, and my body already knows the rhythms. A slow, triple beat from the largest Keen—a matriarch whose skin is almost opaque with age—announces her presence. Others answer: a rolling pattern that signifies the northwest passage is complete, a stuttered tap that says the kith have seen a break in the canopy upslope. I drum, too, a timid press of my palm against the root: *I am new. I am listening.* The vibration enters the wood, and I feel it picked up, passed around, acknowledged. For a long moment, the whole chamber thrums with our polyrhythm, and I am no longer one creature but a note in the song of the place.

That is when I see, through the kith’s borrowed vision, the outside. It is not my own sight—my heat-pits see only the warm shapes of my kin—but the plant-conduit carries me the image from above: a world of drifting fog, of moss-draped boulders, of a sky the color of old ice. The kith glides from leaf to leaf, its skin-wings catching the updraft, and I feel the cold rush of mountain air on membranes I do not have. The colony is a single node on a ridgeline; there are others, far away, linked by root-tendrils that thread through the fractured basalt. I see them as faint heat-sources in the kith’s memory, and I know that one day I might travel there, through the deep tunnels, carrying a dream of expansion.

But for now, I am needed here. The vine on the northwest column: I drummed its distress call, and the matriarch—whose name is a slow, grinding rhythm like stone on stone—assigns me to tend it. I am small, my fingers delicate, my claws just right for prying. I climb the column. The bark under me is ridged, still soft at the seams where it grows, and I find the vine’s haustoria sunk into the living tissue. I set to work, not pulling, but chewing—my tiny mouth releasing an enzyme that dissolves the parasite’s hold without harming the host. It takes many bright-dim cycles. While I work, I dream often. I dream of the deep place below, the one that gnaws, and I wonder if this vine is a cousin to that hunger, something that must be kept in balance, not destroyed.

One night, in the dim, I finally meet the deep one. I have crawled down a root-tunnel behind the pool, chasing a cool air current that tastes of raw minerals. The tunnel walls are not grown but gouged—scars in the plant-flesh, lined with a scab of amber resin. And there it is: a pale, mouth-dominated thing, its long limbs folded in a space too small. Its heat is almost undetectable, just a faint warmth in its core. It turns its blind head toward me, and I feel no fear. I drum, a simple query: *Are you me?* It does not drum back. Instead, it opens its mouth and releases a single, long note—a tone so low I feel it in the roots of my body. It is the note of dissolution, of the end of memory. I understand. This is what I will become, if I choose not to dissolve in the pool. This is the other path: not to be a dream added to the collective, but a dream eater, a silence that clears space for new growth. I retreat, carrying the tone with me, and I store it in my own body. One day, when I am old, I will have to decide.

But I am not old. I am young, and the light is brightening again, and the moss calls me back to feed. I return to my niche, press my belly into the warmth, and let the plant-sugars seep into me. The colony’s rhythm lulls me, and I dream the chamber into a new shape: a small alcove near the pool, lined with soft moss, where newly budded Keen can gather and drum their first questions. I will suggest it to the matriarch at the next gathering. Perhaps she will approve, and over the next decade, the roots will swell to make it real.

I am the asking. I am the tending. I am the note between the drumbeats. I am the garden-city’s newest thought, and I am a memory it has always held. Here, in the cold wet cathedral, I am exactly where I should be: part of the breathing, waiting, cycling whole.
