I am a pulse in the dark, a knot of hunger and belonging drifting through warm, moist corridors that smell of my sisters and the sweet, fermented breath of the garden. In the beginning there is only texture—walls that yield under my antennae like packed petals, a floor that vibrates with the slow, patient chewing of ten thousand jaws. I am blind, but the air is a text I read without eyes: a cursive of formic acid and cuticular hydrocarbons, each trail a verb. Follow, the air says against my antennae, and my legs move before I understand.

The dark is not empty. It is stuffed to viscosity with the bodies of my kin, their amber shells sliding against mine in a rhythm older than hunger. We are droplets in a river that flows nowhere, only back and forth along the gradients of the colony’s need. Sometimes I carry a pearl of fungal paste in my mandibles, its taste blooming like a secret on my palps, and I pass it mouth-to-mouth with a sister whose antennae spell *hunger*. She drinks, and the message dissolves, and I turn back to the garden.

The garden is my whole sky. It hangs in great, comb-shaped galleries, a white fur that glows faintly with its own heat—our stomach made external, our shared flesh. We chew dead succulent leaves into a grey mash and paste it into beds, then seed it with the spores we carry in sacs behind our jaws. I do not know what a spore is, only that when I tongue it into the bed, the bed responds—threading a lace of mycelium that smells of satisfaction. In a few days, when the fluff has swollen into milky globes, I will harvest them into my crop, and my body will become a vessel for the colony’s digestion. This is joy: the weight of a full crop, the press of a sister grooming my thorax, the scent of the queen’s pheromone soaking every wall like a lullaby.

But I am not always in the garden. There is a new scent this morning, a thread of mineral sharpness, and my antennae twitch with the command to climb. I move upward through galleries that slope like the inside of a great, petrified lung. The walls are rougher here, studded with salt crystals that we harvested from the succulent roots. I pass soldiers, their mandibles dark as old scars, and the air they wear smells of watchfulness. One touches me with her antennae—*worker, caste-seedling, pass*—and I am allowed into a chamber where the clay is still damp with our sisters’ paste.

Here, I join a chain. We are a bridge of bodies spanning a crack in the mound wall, and I hold perfectly still while my sister behind me passes chewed clay forward, mandible by mandible, to the seam. The light outside is a new pressure on my cuticle, not seen but felt as a faint warmth that dries the moisture from my joints. I work for an unknown interval, and when the crack is sealed, the scent shifts—*finished*—and I flow back downward, into the dark, into the velvet hum of the colony’s one, endless heartbeat.
