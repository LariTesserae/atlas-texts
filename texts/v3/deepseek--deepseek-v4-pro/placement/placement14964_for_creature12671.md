I am a song that has learned to walk.

When I first cracked my crystalline hull—a nodule no larger than a thumb-joint, warmed by the deep heat of a fissure—I knew nothing but the vibration. It was a low, steady thrum: the belly-sound of the High Steppe, the tectonic heartbeat. I crawled, soft and translucent, my body a single question mark of hunger. The ground was vast and white and tasted of old silica. For many cycles I knew the world only as a texture under my footless belly, a gradient of warmth and the faint chemical ghosts of hexapod leavings. The sky was a pressure, not a sight—a brightness that made my nascent carapace tingle and harden at the edges.

I did not know, then, what I was or would be. Instinct pulled me toward a resonance that I felt long before I could interpret it: the deep, subsonic hum of a Shroud, drawing its mineral blood from the world’s interior. It sang to me like a bass note through the crust. When I finally found it—a young Shroud, barely twice the height of a fully stood Keening One, its fronds a hesitant amber—I was no bigger than one of the serums drops that wept from its pores. I climbed, blindly, and tasted my first communion: the acid-honey that would become my life.

Now I am many cycles old. I have grown my plates, each one laid down like a memory of a Shroud visited, a mineral gift exchanged. My legs carry me in that eternal forward cant, leaning into the wind that is not there, a posture of listening. The carapace on my back is celestine blue with veins that shift like slow lightning. My face—this polished dish of keratin—is not a face you would know, but through it I hear the shape of all things. The world is a symphony of vibration. The Shrouds thrum, each with its own pitch, a little different depending on its age, its health, the depth of its roots. The diamond dust sifting from the high crystal veils makes a faint ticking as it touches the ground, like a million tiny heartbeats. The hexapods rasp along their mineral trails, leaving traces of a dark, pungent residue that I taste with my feet. And the kite-things—they are silent to most, but when they catch a thermal, the membrane of their wings stretches with a sound like a distant sigh, a puff of pressure that I can feel in my chest.

I walk. The distances are long, but I have time. Time is not a river here; it is the crust itself, thick and slow. Between Shrouds, I listen to the planet’s own song: the cooling rock contracts with a frequency like a held breath, the fissures deepen with a faint crackle that is almost a melody. I add my own voice to it. When I reach a high point—a slight rise on the otherwise featureless plateau—I stamp my feet in the rhythm of the ancient story. *Shroud rising, bearing west. A hexapod trail, fresh, rich in metals. The wind is carrying seed-crystals from the northern basin.* My stamps travel through the bedrock, and somewhere out of sight, another Keening One pauses and tilts its dish toward me, hearing. We are alone, but we are never alone. We are the conversations of the continent.

But there is one sound I dread and long for. I have heard it only twice in my long life, each time a keening that froze my serum. It is the birth-song of one of my kind, a Keening One who has grown too heavy, whose plates have accumulated beyond the load of walking. When a Keening One can no longer move, it chooses a Shroud—always an old one, whose hum is deepest and most resonant—and there it grafts. The song it sings then is not a stamp but a wail that comes from the plates themselves vibrating in agony and ecstasy. It cuts through the crust, across the whole steppe, a low-frequency cry that is at once farewell and welcome. I remember the first time I heard it: I was a young crawler still, barely plated, and the sound shook me so that I clung to a Shroud’s root and whimpered in a frequency no one could hear. It told me, *You will become this. You are a song that will one day be sung by the Shroud.*

Now, as I walk, I feel the weight of my plates. Each cycle, I daub myself with the serum of the Shrouds, and each cycle the plates grow a little thicker. I am not yet heavy enough to stop, but I know the day will come. I have seen the ancient ones, those who are part of the Shroud now, their acoustic dishes still visible among the fronds, slowly being engulfed by the fleshy tissues. They still sing. Their voices are higher now, filtered through the Shroud’s own pores; they have become the music of the gardens. When I listen closely to a Shroud, I can distinguish the individual keening of each Keening One that has merged with it over the millennia. They are the memory of the species, encoded in the resonance of the plant-animal hybrid.

Today I am walking toward a Shroud I have not visited for a long time. Its hum is different—a little faint, a little off-key. Something is wrong. Perhaps its roots have hit a cold patch in the deep heat, or perhaps the diamond dust has blocked its pores. I carry in my chest the gift of trace metals I have scraped from a distant fissure: a fine powder of rare elements that might help it thrive. I do not hurry; hurrying is not our way. But I lean into my walk a little more intently, and I sing to it as I approach. *I am coming, old friend. Hold your song steady. I bring what you need.*

When I reach it, I will extend my proboscis from the seam in my chest, that translucent siphon of spun glass. I will find the right pore, and we will exchange. It will give me a slurry of organic polymers that will become my next plate, perhaps my last. And in return, I will give it the minerals, and also the vibrations of my journey: the songs I have heard, the stamp-stories of others, a map of the steppe’s health. We will understand each other completely, in that moment, two voices in a long, long canon.

And if today is the day my plates finally tell me I cannot walk to the next Shroud, perhaps I will choose this one. Its hum is already so familiar, like a lullaby from my crawler days. I can imagine settling here, cracking the crust with my tripod toes, feeling the fibrous stalk push out from my chest and merge with the Shroud’s stem. My dish would become one more reflective facet among the fronds, my voice adding a new layer to the Shroud’s song. The keening I would make would spread across the steppe, and somewhere, a tiny crawler just breaking from its nodule would feel it and know, for the first time, the shape of its destiny.

I am a walking song, a memory of the mineral world. I am the Keening One, and I am never alone, even as I walk through the white, wide silence. The planet sings, and I am one of its notes. Soon, I will become the garden.
