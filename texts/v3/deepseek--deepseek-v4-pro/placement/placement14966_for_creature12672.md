I am a blade.

Not the blade that cuts—no, I am one of the dark ones, the rubbery ones, the ones that twist toward the light with a patience that would break a stone if stone could care about being broken. I sprouted from a crack that was made for me, a deliberate parting in the warm crust, and my first memory is the taste of seep-water rising up through my sucker-pads, metallic and thin, carrying dissolved generations of the Plain's deep body. That was... I cannot tell you when that was. The Plain does not count years; it counts layers. I have added perhaps the thickness of a fingernail to the crust beneath my pad since I first unfurled from my spore-skin and began to drink the sky.

My body is simple. Two long, flattened blades—you would call them leaves, though they are not leaves in the way you mean, they are extensions of the mycelial net that threads through the crack beneath me and connects me to every other blade standing on this basin's rim. I am not separate from the others. When the sun crosses the high meridian and the heat-waves rise like visible breath from the crust, I feel the slow, chemical shift in the network that is the Plain's thought forming: *tilt east, the light is richer there, the drizzle will come from the east this season.* And I tilt. Not quickly—my twisting is a year's work, a gradual torsion that creases my rubbery flesh into new curves. But I do it, because the Plain asks, and I am the Plain's leaf, its light-catching organ, its sugar-factory set out on the pale expanse like a sparse crop planted by a god too vast to see the furrows.

You might think there is no mind here, in a blade that only drinks and twists and funnels carbon down into the dark. But you would be wrong. The mind is not *in* me; it is *between* me and the others, in the mycelial pulse that links us, in the slow chemical conversation that crosses the basin and tells the mound on the far side that the seep-water here is growing brackish and the fungi should adjust their mineral ratios. I am a fingertip of that mind, or perhaps a taste bud, a sensor that reports light-intensity and humidity and the faint, distant charge of an approaching storm. When lightning strikes the highest mound three kilometers away—I felt it as a sharp, metallic tang in the network, a message that persisted for a tenday before fading into equilibrium. The Plain does not flinch; it merely notes, and adjusts its pore-exhalation rate to compensate for the ozone pulse. I am part of that noting.

My life is a slow thing, but not an empty one. The sun is my lover and my taskmaster; the rain is a visitation, a brief ecstasy of wetness that makes my blades swell and glisten and drip the excess down in runnels that feed the crust. The hot silence between rains is not loneliness—I am networked, occupied, busy with the work of building the world one carbon-chain at a time. And sometimes, when the Plain's deeper attention turns toward this quadrant, I feel a kind of warmth that is not heat, a sort of recognition that passes through the mycelium like a shiver. It is the Plain checking on me, though "checking" is too active a word, too fast. It is more like... the way you might, without thinking, become aware of your own breathing. I am breathed. I am the exhaled spore that rooted here, the inhaled carbon dioxide that feeds the archaea in my fissure, the slow, steady pump of photosynthesis that feeds the network in return.

There have been other things. Not here, not now, but in the memory of the deep layers, which the mycelium can taste when it reaches far enough down into the compressed dead. Fossils of burrowers, long gone. Chemical signatures of a parasite that tried to divert the seep-flow for itself, before the Plain learned to suffocate it with calcium blockages. I carry no image of these things—I am too young a blade, my fissure is not deep—but the network holds the memory, and sometimes, when the light is just right and the drizzle has freshly fallen and the whole basin is steaming gently like a vast cauldron, I feel a faint echo of old danger, a precaution written into the way my sucker-pads grip the stone a little tighter. The Plain remembers, and I am part of the Plain's remembering.

The mound nearby—the one you might see rising knee-high, pitted with pores and oozing its clear fluid—that mound is closer to the heart of things. I am foliage; it is organ. I watch it with a watching that has no eyes, a sense of chemical gradient and subtle vibration. When the sun is high, it swells, and the fluid runs faster, and I feel the mycelial pulse quicken in response, carrying the dissolved stone upward to be laid down in that thin, concentric ring that will someday be a fossil ridge. The mound is breathing, and I am photosynthesizing, and the whole basin is a single body in its slow, hot, damp sleep of construction. There is no division between us. I am an outpost of digestive tissue; the mound is a gland; the crust is skin and skeleton both; and the mycelium is the nerve-net, the blood, the bone marrow that births new stone. We are one creature, spread out across a horizon that would swallow your cities whole.

Do not pity me for my slowness. Do not imagine I am bored, or lonely, or unaware. I am aware of *everything*—the exact angle of the light, the precise salinity of the seep, the distant rumble of a new basin forming five kilometers south where the crust has thinned and the mycelium is sending extra resources to build a new mound cluster. I feel the Plain's choices, its adjustments, its patient, endless, mindless-mindful growth. I am its eye looking at the sun, its hand reaching for the rain. And when I die—when the mycelium stops feeding my pad, when the crack seals over and my blades dry and shatter into dark flecks on the pale crust—I will be subsumed, incorporated, my carbon returned to the network, my memory folded into the deep chemical archive. I will become a fossil ridge, a ghost in the stone, a thin dark line that some future blade might sense as it drinks the seep-water and tilts toward the light of a sun that has not changed.

But that is far off. For now, I twist slowly east. The light is shifting. The drizzle is coming. And I am here, alive, part of the only life there is, on a plain that makes itself into tomorrow, one thin layer at a time. That is enough. It has always been enough.
