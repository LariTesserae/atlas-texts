My name is a flick against the chin—quick, with the back of the first knuckle, a gesture that says *look up*. It’s an old joke from my grandmother, who thought it was funny to name a blind child after the one thing none of us can do. But I don’t look up. I *listen* up. I feel up. Up is a direction made of air and temperature and the faint, feathery pressure of something alive shifting its weight far above.

For the last eleven seasons, I’ve been a canopy-tender. My station is the high gallery, a woven platform of withies that hugs the uppermost chamber of the root-thicket, where the woody lattice finally begins to thin and the true trees—the giants that cup this whole depression in their roots—break through into the world of air and light. I’ve never felt that light, but I know it’s there because the draft tells me. The draft is a slow, cold breath that seeps down through the ceiling gaps, carrying smells from a place that isn’t here: the dry rustle of dead leaves, the mineral tang of distant stone, the sharp, almost painful freshness of something the elders call *wind*. Wind is like the draft, but stronger, wilder. It doesn’t belong down here. When it comes, it means something has changed up above.

Today, the draft stopped.

I was crouched on my platform, checking the tension of the signal line—a length of braided gut that runs from my station down to the council disc, used to thrum warnings. My fingers were tracing the knots, each one a code: *all clear*, *minor shift*, *pulse incoming*, *evacuate high nests*. I was about to tie the *all clear* knot, because the draft had been steady and sweet all cycle, smelling of wet bark and the faint, spicy bloom of a canopy flower I’ve only ever known as a scent. But then the air changed. It didn’t just fade; it *stopped*, like a hand had pressed a palm over the sky. The temperature evened out to nothing. The smells vanished. And in that sudden absence, I heard something I’ve never heard before: a low, grinding creak, the sound of wood under immense pressure. Not the pulse—that’s a rhythmic shudder, a heartbeat. This was a slow, deliberate groan, the kind a branch makes before it snaps, but magnified a thousandfold.

I didn’t think. I slapped the signal line hard, three rapid thumps—*emergency, unknown*—then I turned and ran.

The vine-bridges are my roads. I know the high ones like I know my own teeth: the seventh span has a repair splice that’s thicker than the rest, so I hop over it instead of stepping on it to avoid the wobble; the ninth span has a slight lean to the left, worn by years of weavers carrying heavy sap-bolts to the spinning hollow. I run them now, bare feet slapping the damp fiber, my toes curling to grip the braids. I’ve never run this fast in my life. The air is a blur of sound: my own breath, harsh in my throat; the hiss of my garment’s edge rubbing my thigh; the wet *thip* of my heels on the bridge. And under that, the groan, still there, still changing—now a series of sharp cracks, like the knucklebones in Keth’s pouch snapping all at once.

I pass the first dwelling, a gourd-hollow drumming with startled movement inside. I feel the heat of a body near the entrance, the shift of air as they turn to listen. I flick my chin in their direction—the motion is my name, but right now it’s also a signal: *look up, pay attention, danger from above*. They’ll understand. They might not know what’s coming, but they’ll know to brace.

My hair starts to glow. It’s the wax, responding to the heat of my terror. I can feel it prickling on my scalp, a cold light that is nothing like warmth. Along the inside of the bridges, the cultivated fungus glimmers faintly, disturbed by my passing. It’s enough to reveal the shape of my own hand as I reach for the next span’s guide rope—pale, long-fingered, the knuckles standing out too sharp because I’m always cold, always a little hungry. My body is a runner’s body: lean calves, a deep ache in my lungs that I’ve learned to ignore, the peculiar loose-hipped gait of someone who spends her days on swaying platforms. My ears, like everyone’s, are large and swivel restlessly, tracking sounds I don’t consciously name: the scrabble of a multi-legged thing in a crevice, the distant slosh of cistern water, the rhythmic beat of someone hammering flint in the low workshop. None of it matters. The only sound that matters is above.

I’m heading for Keth’s capsule. She’s the bone-reader, the story-keeper, and if something is happening that hasn’t happened in living memory, she’ll know what to do. Or if she doesn’t, she’ll know who does—her aunt-by-smell taught her things that aren’t stored in any bone, things that are only passed mouth-to-mouth in the absolute dark. I’ve always been a little afraid of Keth, to be honest. She has a stillness that makes me feel twitchy and childlike. But right now, the thought of her calm hands on my sternum, signing *slow down, tell me clear*, is the only thing steady in my mind.

The groan above shifts pitch. It deepens, becomes a tearing sound—a great, slow rip—and then a blast of air hits me from above. It’s not the draft. It’s *wind*, real wind, thick with a stinging spray of something tiny and hard. Particles. I fling my arm across my face, but they hit my skin anyway, sharp little needles of what my fingers later identify as splinters of rotten wood and something gritty—soil? Sand? Concepts from the elders’ stories, too remote to fully grasp. The wind carries a new smell: hot, dry, full of a kind of energy that makes my hair stand on end, the glow flaring so bright I can sense it through my sealed lids. For the first time in my life, I understand what brightness means. It’s this: an overwhelming surge of sensation that has no object, a pressure behind the eyes that knows only *too much*.

I lose my footing. The bridge lurches, not from a pulse but from the air itself, and I fall sideways, grabbing a root-strut with both hands. The splinters skate over my arms, my neck. I tuck my chin and hold on, my body swinging into the void between bridges. Below me, nothing. I know this gap—it’s a thirty-arm drop to the standing water of the lower pools. If I fall, I’ll hit it wrong and break. I’ve never been afraid of heights before; height is just a direction. But now, with the wind tearing at me, I imagine the cold shock of water, the blind thrash until my soaked garment drags me under. Fear makes my muscles lock. I can’t pull myself up.

Then I feel a hand on my wrist. The grip is dry and hard, callused fingertips pressing into the tendons. The touch is a language: *I have you, pull up, now*. I obey, because that’s Keth’s hand. She doesn’t shake. She doesn’t feel startled. Her body is a rooted thing as she hauls me onto the bridge and steadies me with a palm flat against my spine. Then she signs against my back, quick and precise: *Report. What fell?*

I love that she doesn’t ask *what happened* or *are you okay*. She’s already assessed that I’m intact and moved straight to the threat. I sign back into her shoulder, my fingers trembling: *Sky stopped. Wind came down. Wood tearing. Splinters.* I don’t have a sign for *sky*, so I use the old gestural metaphor: a cupped hand sweeping overhead, the dome of the world. Keth’s hair glimmers with the same frantic light as mine, but her movements are slow, deliberate. She taps my chin—my name, then her own: *Keth, focus, guide me up high.*

I understand. She wants to go to my station. She wants to read the disaster with her own senses. I’m terrified to go back up there, but I’m more terrified of staying here, of not knowing. So I turn around, and now we’re two bodies on the bridge, running in tandem. Keth’s footsteps are silent where mine are sloppy; she’s learned to place her weight perfectly on the center weave. I match her rhythm, and together we climb into the buffeting wind, into the new, sharp smell of a world that until now has only ever been a rumor.

The groan has stopped. Up ahead, where my station should be, there’s a new sound: a soft, steady whisper, like countless tiny legs brushing against a thousand surfaces. And light—real, blistering light—seeps down through a rent in the ceiling, painting my closed eyes with a red ache. For the first time in thirty-one years, I am utterly, blindingly seen.
