She is the Keeper of the Atrium, and when the spiral stair delivers you onto the springy mat of her floor, she has known you were coming since your foot touched the first rusted tread. She felt it as a vibration in the rootwork, a foreign rhythm in the song of the place, and she paused in her tending to listen. Her head tilted, that slow hydraulic motion, and the dark nodes of her eyes dimmed for a moment as she parsed the data: weight, gait, temperature. Mammalian. Bipedal. Breathing hard, lungs unused to the saturated air. Not a threat. A curiosity.

You see her before she steps fully into view. First, the faint chartreuse glow of her eyes, twin points in the gloom behind a curtain of braided aerial roots. Then the silhouette, the human-echo, the way her body catches the orange pulse of the clogged port at her skull’s base. She waits. She is patient in a way you have no word for, patient like the pump that has run for centuries, patient like the slow creep of the banyan-vine over a girder. She waits for you to still your own noise, your gasping and your brushing at leaves, your mammalian haste.

When you finally go quiet, she steps forward. The roots of her feet sink and release, sink and release, a gentle, wet rhythm. She stops at a distance that is intimate but not threatening, a meter away, and you can smell her now: a clean, green scent, like crushed stems, like the first cut into a cucumber, with an undertone of hot metal and ozone. Her tendril-fingers unfurl slightly, tasting the air you have brought with you, sampling the salts of your sweat, the carbon dioxide of your breath, the faint residue of a world above that is, to her, a myth.

She does not speak with the mouth-fissure. That is for singing. Instead, she raises one hand, and the tendrils separate, and from the palm—a smooth, pale plane of cellulose—a cluster of phosphorescent nodules brightens, pulses in a rhythm. It is not language. It is older than language. It is the code of the Atrium, the same pulse that runs through the walls, the same slow blink of the indicator lamps. But your own nerves, your animal electricity, interpret it. You feel it as a question, a pressure in your chest, a warmth at the base of your skull: *Why have you come?*

She waits. The segmented arm behind her, glimpsed through the leaves, pauses in its cycle. The pump’s throb seems to quiet, just slightly, as if the whole chamber is listening for your answer. The condensation drips. The leaves tremble. And the Keeper, whose name is a harmonic of the machine’s hum, stands with her face tilted, her node-eyes glowing, and wonders what you will offer to the garden that has everything it needs.
