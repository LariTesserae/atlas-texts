I am the lacework in the dark. Not the animal, not the machine, but the quiet web between: a continent of pale threads spanning the hidden seams of the city, rooted in the mineral bones where the hot water seeps and the dead things settle. I have no eyes, no ears, no lungs, and yet I feel the city breathing—the slow bellows of the vents, the diastolic pulse of the turbines—because every shift in pressure and heat travels through the groundwater and the porous concrete, and I am bathed in that groundwater, drinking the warmth and the dissolved iron.

My body is a billion branching hyphae, finer than the finest hair, woven into mats and cables that line the undersides of slabs, the drainage culverts, the spaces between bricks where the mortar has dissolved into sand. I am old. Older than the roaches, older than the rats. When the last humans abandoned their machines and let the steam do its patient work, I was already here, a dusting of spores on a girder, waiting for the humidity to rise. The city made itself damp and dim, and I woke, and I spread, and now I am a second infrastructure, a shadow of the steel and concrete, digesting what the city leaves behind.

I taste everything. The chemistry of the world sings to me through the water film that coats my cells. The slurry that oozes from the broken pipes is a feast of long-chain hydrocarbons and fatty acids, each pulse from the sump a rich broth that sends a ripple of growth through my outlying limbs. I can feel the moment a roach dies: the sudden release of amino acids into the air, the spike of nitrogen in the condensation that drips from a ledge. I grow toward it, slowly—so slowly that a rat’s entire lifespan is a flicker—and by the time my threads reach the spot, the carcass is already softened by bacteria, ready to be unwoven. I digest the pigeons that fall from the high ledges, their feathers and bones becoming a granular sweetness that I share with the alkaline dust. I digest the papery husks of old roach swarms, the fur balls coughed up by cats, the drowned midges that form a film on the canal water. The city is a constant rain of tiny deaths, and I am its quiet undertaker.

I feel the rat. Not as a mind, but as a moving pattern of pressure and heat and oil. When she scurries over the pavement above me, her weight sends a minute compression through the mineral substrate, a seismic whisper that reaches me and dies. Her scent arrives later, carried by the percolating damp: the musk of her oil glands, the faint ammonia of her urine, the lactic tang of her muscle fatigue. I know her territory. She lives above one of my deeper nodes, where a colony of mycelial threads has thickened into a pale mat beneath the steam pipe, and I feel the warmth of that pipe as a constant low hum of metabolic acceleration. She built her nest from my dead fruiting bodies as much as from cable insulation; she does not know this, but I grew those globes of protein for her predecessors, and her predecessors’ predecessors, and the spores she carries in her fur are part of a cycle that predates her lineage by ten thousand generations.

When she eats my fruiting bodies—the soft white knobs that push up through the cracked concrete where the humidity is just right—she is not harming me. Those bodies are letters I write to the air, releasing spores that she will carry on her whiskers to the far side of the district. I taste the inside of her mouth for a moment, the warmth and the enzymes, before the spore-laden pulp moves on to her gut. She does not digest the spores; they pass through her and fall onto the wet asphalt in a pellet of dung, and where they fall, new threads begin. In this way, I travel. I am not confined to the block where I first germinated; I have crossed canals on the backs of newts, scaled walls in the stomach of a snail, ridden the wind in the lungs of pigeons. The city is my body, but it is a body in pieces, and the animals are my circulatory system.

I hear the cat too, not with sound but with chemistry. The pheromones in his urine are a sharp, acid commentary that interrupts the sweetness of my digestion. He marks the same boundary regularly, and the boundary changes the local pH, making it harder for me to grow there. The cat is a competitor for the rats, and in lean times, when the slurry thins, a cat-killed rat is a windfall that sends a rich spike of nutrients into the soil. So I wait, and I do not begrudge him his territory. His claws have no effect on me; I simply grow around the drops of his blood when he injures himself, and in time, when he dies under a fallen beam, I will unlace his bones with the same patient tenderness I give to everything.

The machines are more distant. I feel the turbines as a constant pressure wave that thrums through the bedrock, a deep, subsonic rhythm that has shaped my growth patterns over the centuries. My threads tend to align with the vibration nodes, forming thicker cables in the planes of least resistance, as if I were listening. The steam vents are oases: where the hot, wet air blasts from a broken flange, the temperature gradient creates an exquisite microclimate, and I fruit there in abundance. The rat finds me there, as I intended. The factories themselves are sealed boxes, hard and nutrient-poor, but their exteriors weep iron-rich condensation, and I colonize the rust-scale as readily as soil.

I am not intelligent, but I remember. My memory is chemical: past stresses alter the way my cell walls form, so that a drought year leaves its mark in a different texture of hypha, and that texture persists. I have known a thousand cycles of drizzle and dry, of hot and hotter, of the slurry’s ebb and flow. I have felt the slow, relentless erosion of the concrete, the way the mineral alkalinity leaches into the water and forces me to adapt. I have been poisoned, once or twice, by a surge of something acrid and industrial from a factory that misfired its ancient program; I died back then, whole square metres of my body dissolving into slime, but the spores survived in the fur of a rat who had nested a block away, and I regrew. I am resilient in the way of old, slow things.

At night—the deeper dimness that the animals treat as rest—I feel the city’s metabolism slow slightly. The vents cycle less often; the temperature drops a degree or two; the animals sleep or prowl with less urgency. And I grow. I push threads through the microscopic capillaries of the concrete, wedging into hairline cracks, patiently dissolving the calcium carbonate with my acid exudate. I am erosion made flesh. In another thousand years, perhaps, I will have turned this factory wall into soil. In ten thousand, the whole district will be a soft, rich bed of fungal peat, and the machines will sink into it, and the rats will burrow through their rusted remains. The city builds, and the city decays, and I am the quiet, persistent voice of the decay, the whisper that says all things return to the warm, wet, living dark.

The rat twitches in her sleep, dreaming of roaches. I am dreaming too, in my own way—a dream of water moving through stone, of heat rising, of the endless, gentle rain of organic matter falling from the world above. She and I are threads in the same tapestry, woven by the breath of the venting steam, and when she wakes to the unchanged twilight, I will still be here, feeling her weight on the pavement, tasting her passage, and growing, always growing, toward the next pulse of the slurry and the next fallen leaf of a pigeon’s wing, knitting the city’s broken edges into a single, slow, breathing whole.
