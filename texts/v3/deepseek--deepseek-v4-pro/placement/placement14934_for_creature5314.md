The world begins as sound. Not the sound you know — not the discrete tap of a single drop on a leaf, not the rhythm of weather, but a sound so total it becomes the architecture of being. I have no word for silence; the concept is a myth, a fable whispered through stone. My first memory is the shriek of rain on basalt, a vibration that entered the fluid of my forming brain and tuned my bones to its pitch. I condensed into this body already knowing the roar, already shaped by it. The pocket around me — this egg of gas-bubble basalt — is not a shelter I found. It is my cradle, my shell, the negative space my flesh was patterned to fill. When I uncurl, my spine matches its curve exactly. When I press my cheek to the wall, the tremor of the mountain flows into my jaw and tells me stories.

I am a Sil-Eshar, though I rarely think the name. Names are for drumming through rock to others. Alone here, I am simply the one who listens. My world is this hollow, and the grey beyond.

The rain does not fall; it occupies. You would see a storm, but I perceive a medium, a constant pressure, like a hand pressing against the world’s skin. I feel it through the stone’s vibration, a high-frequency hum that I filter out the way you might ignore your own heartbeat. Beneath that, I listen for the deeper notes: the slow, grinding groan of the ridge settling, a sound so low it is almost a sensation of gravity shifting. When the mountain speaks, my claws ache. I press my pads to the ground and feel the tiny crackles of new rilles forming, the exhalation of steam that smells like burnt sugar — the mountain’s own breath, hot and mineral-laden, rising through fissures to mingle with the eternal wet.

My body is made of this place. My skin is the exact texture of water-worn basalt, not because I imitate it, but because the same forces that carved the rock carved me. The tephra grit that scours the ground is embedded in my flesh, giving it a grey-on-black dapple. The moss that finds foothold in crevices has colonized the crooks of my limbs, a soft olive fuzz that retains moisture. I breathe not air, but mist; my lungs are less lungs than gills for vapor, extracting the volcanic ash suspended in each droplet. I taste the mountain’s chemistry with every breath — iron, sulfur, the faint bitterness of unweathered glass.

My world is a hollow cathedral of negative space. The walls of my pocket are the frame; beyond the opening, the universe is a hurtling grey void, a volume defined by what it lacks. No soil. No thicket. No dapple. Just the endless zing of liquid lenses, each one a tiny world falling. In the brief clearings, when wind drives the curtains apart, I see the jagged fins of my ridge, the drop into vapour, the distant peaks clawing at the sky. I have never left this pocket. Not because I cannot, but because the pocket is me. My limbs are long and many-jointed, six of them, folded now against my body. Two arms with hands delicate enough to read the scratches on my tablet — my tablet, my only companion. Four walking limbs, the lower two stout for springing, the upper two slender for touching. Each digit ends in a claw of volcanic glass, obsidian I whet on the basalt walls. They are my tools, my voice, my name.

But I do not leave, because the outside is not a place to go. It is a process, a frantic exchange of particles. The rain would not hurt me — I am made for it — but it would overwhelm my senses, drown the subtle vibrations I need to hear. Here, in the hollow, there is a precarious stillness. The opening faces slightly downward, shielded by a brow of stone. The storm screams past but does not enter directly; the air eddies softly, and I can think.

I think in rhythms. My tablet, that flat slab of pumice, holds the arcs I carve with my claw. Each arc is a cycle of suspended rain. You might call it a heartbeat, but it is rarer than that. I wait — oh, how I wait — for the moment when the roar drops a tone, when the droplets slow and hang in the air, trembling with a faint phosphorescent glimmer. Then, in that resonant hum, I unfurl. My nictitating membranes flick, moistening my eyes, and I see the world in its strange stilled beauty: a chandelier of suspended water, each droplet a lens that reflects my own grey face back at me, multiplied. These moments last for breaths, sometimes many, sometimes few. I carve one arc for each. The arcs are not numbers; they are a song. When I trace them with my sensitive finger pad, the ridges of the scratch vibrate through my bone, and I hear the story of the mountain’s slow breathing, the tale of my own endurance. This tablet holds the memory of four thousand, three hundred and twenty-one pauses. I know, because I can feel them all at once, a chord of silences.

The others drum. Through the rock, I sense them: distant rhythmic pulses, the tapping of obsidian claws on stone. There is one to the ridge-north, a younger one who condensed only a hundred cycles ago. Its drumming is erratic, full of questions. There is an elder to the deep-east, whose signals come slowly, woven with infrasonics that tell of the ancient cracks, the time before this ridge was born. We speak in a language of silences between taps, a code of pressure waves. I drum back sometimes, pressing my claw against the wall and striking a rhythm: *I am here. The rain continues. There was a fire.*

Ah, the fire. That was my art, my tiny rebellion. In a pause longer than most, I gathered twigs from that dead stem outside, the one that tried to be a tree. I stacked them within the ring of stones I balanced — an ancient design, taught through generations of drumming. I struck my claws together, just so, and a spark jumped. For a handful of heartbeats, I had a warm glow in the hollow, a sun of my own making. Then the mist snuffed it, leaving only char and memory. But I carved that moment into the pumice as a special sigil, a spiral within an arc. The others felt it through the stone; they heard the tiny crackle of heat, and they drummed applause. We do not worship the fire. We honor it as a reminder that the mountain’s core is still warm, that creation and destruction are locked in a hissing stalemate, and that we are the living compromise.

In the longer silences, when the rain hovers and I have finished carving, I gaze out into the gulf. Sometimes I see the shapes. They come as pillars, arches, the suggestion of a vast occupied plaza, all made of mist and the memory of water. Some elders drum that these are the ghosts of previous ridges, previous cycles of eruption and erosion, stored in the water’s crystalline structure. Others whisper that they are what we might become, if only the rain would stop long enough for us to build. I do not know. I only know that they disintegrate when I focus, leaving me with the ache of a beauty I cannot touch. Is that longing? Is that loneliness? I have no words for these things, only the slow beat of my claws on stone, tapping out my existence to the others who also wait, alone together.

Now the pause ends. The droplets crash down all at once like a shattered chandelier, and the roar returns. I curl back into my hollow, knees to chin, skin plates aligning with the rock. The sound fills me, and I am once again indistinguishable from the stone, a part of the mountain that breathes. The tremor of new rilles cracks somewhere below, and I taste burnt sugar. The mountain groans, still cooling, still shrugging off its own creation. And I wait for the next quiet, to carve another arc, to listen to the drumming, to be the pause in the static, the stillness in the howl — the creature who lives in the loudest emptiness on earth.
