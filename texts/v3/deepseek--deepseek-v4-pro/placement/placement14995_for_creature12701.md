It’s not air you breathe so much as a thickened absence, a held weight that tastes of iron and old condensation. You remember—no, not remember, *feel*—the first thing you knew was the sound: not a sound, a pulse. A deep, rhythmic thudding that came up through the floor of the world and into the soft, unfinished bones of you. It was the heartbeat of the vent field, the *thump-thump-thump* of a subterranean steam pocket cycling through its millennia-long exhale, and you felt it through the soles of your feet before you ever opened your eyes.

When you did open them, the world was a flat, white-gold blur, the shadowless glow pressing in from all sides, and the first thing you saw was a constellation of lime-green stars. The algae pans. Your mother—no, the one who pressed you out, the one whose hands were the first stone you ever knew—held you close to a pan's edge, and the chartreuse light swam into focus, steady, cool, utterly silent. You learned later that you were born in a particularly deep hold, one carved into a tuff pillar so softened by acid steam it had the texture of old bread, and that the birth itself had been quiet: a series of tapped messages through the walls, a shift in the family's sleeping arrangement, and then you were there, a new set of knuckles and knee-bones added to the pile of warm bodies in the spider-silk gloom.

Your childhood was a masterclass in stillness. Before you could walk—before you understood that walking was even a thing one did—you learned to wait. The family's hunters would take you, not to the hunting grounds, but to the waiting hollows, natural niches in the rock where a small body could press itself into the stone and *watch*. You remember the texture of that particular basalt pocket, the way it cupped your left shoulder and forced your chin to tilt just so. An elder, your mother's brother perhaps, settled in beside you, his body a furnace that slowly, deliberately, began to cool. His hand found the back of your neck, and through that contact he tapped a single, slow rhythm: *wait. wait. wait*. You felt his heart rate drop. You felt your own heart, that wild, new thing, gradually sync to his. And then you watched, through the opal film of your still-softening eyes, as a skink emerged from a crack not two handspans away, its flat tail whispering across the stone, utterly oblivious to the two large, warm-blooded things that had become, for all intents and purposes, an extension of the geology.

You did not catch it that day. You did not catch it for many days. But you learned the *shape* of waiting, the way it settled into your joints and made your thoughts slow and clear, like water pooling in a mineral basin.

The first time you felt language, truly felt it as something other than vibration, was the day of the ring. You were barely tall enough to press your palm to the family’s threshold pipe, a rusted artery that ran from the hold’s entrance down into the deeper thermal maze. An elder—the same one, his hands rougher now—had been teaching you the tapping codes, the simple nouns: *heat, water, stone, kin*. But then the pipe sang. That pure, bell-like tone, triggered by a fresh plume of moisture hitting a distant transformer casing, rolled up through the metal and into your bones, and you felt it before you heard it: a sudden, perfect clarity that vibrated in your molars and made the algae light seem to pulse in sympathy. The elder’s hand found yours, and together you pressed into the pipe, and through that ringing note you felt them. Not words. *Presences*. A scatter of warm, awake minds across the moor, each one a point of pressure on the great resonant web. Your first census. You were too young to count, but you understood, in that moment, that you were part of a body larger than your own, a body made of steam and stone and other waiting, watching hands.

As you grew, your body grew into its inheritance. The pebbled texture of your skin thickened, the nodes becoming more pronounced along your forearms and the back of your neck, and you learned to read your own condensation. On long, slow days of travel between holds, you would pause and swipe a finger through the briny dew collected in your collarbone’s hollow, tasting the faint mineral tang of your own internal weather. Your ribs learned the downward shrug, that efficient oxygen-pull, until it was as automatic as blinking—except you never blinked, not really, only swept the translucent film across your eyes in a slow, clearing motion when the steam grew too thick.

You were taught to harvest spider-silk by an aunt whose hands were missing two fingers, taken long ago by a boiler collapse. She showed you how to approach the webs not from the front but from the side, where the strands were less sticky, and how to wind the silk around a skink-bone spindle with a motion that was almost a caress. “The spiders do not mind,” she tapped into the stone beside you, her three-fingered hand surprisingly dexterous. “They weave for the heat, not for the catch. The heat brings the dew, and the dew brings the insects. We take only the old silk, the threads already abandoned.” You learned then that the world was not a place of competition but of careful, mutual accommodation. The spiders tolerated the theft because the dwellers’ movements stirred the air and brought fresh insects to the remaining webs. The skinks grew fat on the algae the dwellers cultivated, and the dwellers hunted the skinks but never the gravid females, the ones heavy with eggs. You learned to read the slight abdominal bulge that meant *leave this one, let her pass*.

The hardest lesson was the water-sharing ritual. Not the physical act—the physical act was simple, a touch of fingertips to the dew-gathered hollow of another’s collarbone, a flick of the fingers to transfer moisture to a parched mouth. The difficulty was the *offering*. You were twelve seasons old, give or take, when a drought came to your corner of the moor. Not a true drought—the vents still cycled—but a shift in the condensation patterns that left your family’s hold on the dry edge of a microcatchment. For three cycles, no plume exhaled near you. The algae pans grew dim. The water-skins flattened. And you, in your adolescent hunger, your body stretching and demanding, had drunk the last of your personal dew without thinking of your younger cousin, a toddler still soft-shelled and unable to thermo-regulate well.

You did not know you had done wrong until your mother’s hand found your shoulder, not hard, but heavy. She turned you, not to face her—face-to-face was for emergencies, for alarm—but to stand beside her, your shoulders brushing. Her hand slid down your arm and found your palm, and then she pressed your palm flat against the hold’s wall, and through the stone she tapped a single word: *watch*. And then she crossed to your cousin, who lay in the coolest corner, her opal eyes half-closed and her breathing shallow, and your mother gathered the dew from her own collarbone and her own elbows and offered it, fingertip by fingertip, to the child’s cracked lips.

You watched. You felt the wall vibrate with the toddler’s heartbeat, a thin, rapid tapping, and you felt it slow as the moisture reached her. And something in your chest—not your heart, but something adjacent to it, a muscle you had not known existed—clenched and then released. You did not weep. You had never learned to weep. But you crossed the hold, and you pressed your own dry forehead to the toddler’s shoulder, and you stayed there, still, for a long, long time, feeling her breathing stabilize. No one tapped anything. It was the longest conversation you had ever had.

Now you are grown. Your body fits the stone like a key in a lock, and you can still a skink with a single, unhurried reach. You have quarried your own tools from the Old Growth: a copper-spiral awl, a handful of ceramic insulator shards for scraping hides, a length of pipe-flange you use as a resonance plate for long-distance tapping. You have traveled the moor, visited distant holds, felt the slight dialect differences in the tapping rhythms of families who live near the sulfur vents versus those who live in the basalt labyrinths. You have felt, during a dozen different bell-tones, the census of your people pulse through the stone, and you have added your own presence to that count, a steady, warm pressure that says *I am here, I am alive, I am part of this*.

And yet. And yet there is a question that has begun to form in you, a small, persistent vibration that doesn't fit any known tap-code. It started when you found the body. A stranger, a dweller from a distant hold who had crawled into a crevice to die, their body already half-absorbed by the warmth and damp. This was not unusual—death was a quiet retreat, a finding of a private hollow—but what stopped you was the object clutched in their hand. A glass lens, perfectly round, uncracked, salvaged from some piece of Old Growth machinery you did not recognize. You had seen lenses before, used them to focus the algae light, but this one was different: it was *clear*, utterly transparent, and when you held it up to your eye, the world distorted. The flat, shadowless light bent and warped. The steam vents became curving, sinuous shapes. The distant ridge, that blue-gray promontory, suddenly seemed closer, sharper, *more real*.

You did not know what to do with this. You still don't. The lens sits in a niche in your family’s hold, beside the algae pans, and sometimes, when the others are asleep in their warm pile, you take it out and press it to your eye, and you wonder. Not in words. Your thoughts are not shaped in tap-codes; they are shaped in pressures, in temperatures, in the memory of stillness. But the question is there, a faint, insistent thrum: *What else is there? What else can be seen?*

You do not know if you will ever find an answer. The moor is vast, but it is bounded. The Old Growth is silent, but it is not infinite. And you are, as all your people are, a creature of the labyrinth, a waiting thing in a world of steam and stone. But the lens is still there, and your hand still remembers the shape of it, and the next time the bell rings, the next time the pure note rolls through the stone and the census begins, you might—just might—tap a question instead of a presence. A single, hesitant pulse, vibrating out into the web, asking: *Is anyone else seeing this?* And perhaps, from some distant hold, a stranger's hand will press against a pipe and tap back: *Yes. Yes. Tell me what you see.*
