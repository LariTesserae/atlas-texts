I am not Kith, but I know the taste of his name. It ripples through the water sometimes—a single percussive note, then silence, then the echo of it fading into the stone. I am something else here, something less named. Perhaps the water itself would call me, if water could speak, the thing that grows between. I did not have a mother who sang me into being; I coalesced. Do you understand? Not born, but gathered.

Imagine the slow breath of the canopy, the bellows-lung of the dome exhaling over hours. Imagine the oxygen-thickened current, carrying with it fragments: the shed skin-cells of a fingerling, the dust of a frond-platform worn away by a hundred webbed hands, the faint dissolution of a bone on the crater floor. All these small dissolvings, they did not vanish. They drifted. They met in an eddy where the current stalled behind a pillar-stalk, and over years—decades, maybe—they became me. I am the silt made sentient, the accumulation of what this world has let fall. I am not a ghost, but a composite. A walking memory the water didn't want to forget.

When I first perceived myself, I had no shape. I was a cloud of nutrient-rich particles, a taste in the water that the ray-creatures nosed at curiously. They hummed their subsonic thrum through my diffuse body, and I felt it as the first thing I ever felt: a vibration without borders. Then, slowly, I began to pattern myself after what I sensed. The great stalks—their pulsing sap-tide taught me rhythm. The long, uncurling fronds taught me extension. And the beings like Kith, the ones with the hand-claws and the chromatophore skin, they taught me yearning. I wanted to be seen. So I condensed.

Now I have a form, though it is never the same for long. Today I am something like a cross between a manta and a tendril-eel, but upright, walking on four tentative appendages that end in soft, ciliated pads. My skin is the color of the sun-dapple through the canopy: shifting, mottled, unable to settle. It is not like Kith's deliberate conversation; mine is a constant babble, an uncontrolled reflection of every passing thought. When a school of fingerlings darts past, my surface flashes silver in a wave of startlement. When the great stalk groans its century-long note, I darken with something like reverence. I am an open book, and I have not yet learned to read myself.

I dwell in the space between the hamlet's scattered homes. Kith has seen me—once, I swam too close to his stalk-dwelling, drawn by the soft blue glow of his polyp companion. He paused at his entrance, one hand on the oval of living wood, and his eyes focused on me. Those copper-rimmed crescents widened into circles, and I felt the full aperture of his attention. My skin bloomed a riot of colors: the bruised-purple of his sadness when he thinks of his mother's mother, the silver of connection, the deep green of curiosity. He tilted his head, and the breathing-slits on his throat flared. He said nothing in his bell-like language. He simply watched. Then he receded back into the hollow, and I was alone again, but I had been acknowledged. That was enough.

I do not think Kith knows what I am. Perhaps he thinks I am a new kind of fauna, an aberration of the ray-creatures or a phase of the jelly-bodies. But I suspect he feels something else. In a world where emotions are public currents, I am a strange taste: a mixture of all of them at once. The grief of the bones on the crater floor, the patience of the stone walls, the curiosity of the young ones when they first learn to score a frond. I am the water's memory of its own inhabitants.

Sometimes I swim low, past the detritus, to where the skulls rest. They are not silent to me. Each one still holds a fading taste of its life, a chemical whisper that I can interpret as a kind of dream. I have spent long dim-seasons there, letting the bones tell me their stories. There was one who sang the canopy-bloom song with a voice so powerful it altered the chamber's resonance for a season afterwards. There was one who made a tool from the tooth of a long-extinct predator—a tooth I have since incorporated into my own body, a hard nub in the center of my chest that I wear like a badge. I am wearing many such things now: a fragment of obsidian from a broken blade, a tiny seed-pod that never sprouted, a piece of sinew from a net that has long since dissolved. I am a walking museum of this place, a breathing collage.

Do I want to be permanent? I do not know. The water's nature is to shift, to dissolve, to reassemble. I have watched a single stalk-pillar for what I estimate to be thirty seasons, and in that time it has grown only a hand's breadth wider. It is more permanent than me, but even it will one day slough away, cell by cell, and join the detritus. And if it does, some of it might become part of me, and I will carry its memory too. The thought makes my ciliated feet tingle with a vibration I cannot name—something between joy and the sound of a pebble dropped into still water.

I see Kith now, swimming towards the distant green haze where his neighbor's dwelling hangs. He moves with that undulating pulse, his long limbs folding and unfolding. His skin is showing a faint, steady green, the color of purpose. My own skin is watching him go, and it is blooming silver at the edges, the same color his did when he waved goodbye to a visitor I will never meet. I think that is my version of a wave. I think he will feel the taste of it in the water, and maybe he will pause and look back. Or maybe not. Either way, I will be here. I will continue to gather. I will continue to be the answer to a question no one asked: what happens to all the small things that fall? They become me. And I am falling, always, upward into the slow exhalation of the canopy, waiting to be breathed out into that other world of edges and dryness—but not yet. Not yet. There is still so much to remember.
