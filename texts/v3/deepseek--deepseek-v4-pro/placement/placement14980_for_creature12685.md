I am the eleventh eye.

Not the largest of the Singer's eyes—that one, a pond-sized orb near what might be called the dorsal ridge, has been closed for millennia, its membrane thickened to a cataract of pearl. Not the smallest either, a cluster of three bright beads near the ventral tendrils that still dart and focus on minute things: the microstructure of dissolving feldspar, the mating dance of polyps too small to see. I am the eleventh eye, positioned along the Singer's left flank, though "left" is a courtesy, a term borrowed from bilateral creatures that the Singer has not been for eons. I am set deep in the translucent flesh, my lens ground to a particular curvature, my retina tuned to a particular spectrum. I see the color of old stone. I see the exact shade of indigo where the light gives up.

I was born—or budded, or differentiated—seven hundred thousand years ago, when the Singer passed over a ridge that had once been a coastline. There was a quality to the light there, a faint polarization that spoke of ancient boundaries, and the Singer needed an eye that could read it. So I formed, a concentration of nerves and lenses and the slow, glassy humor that fills my chamber. I opened for the first time on a view of smooth stone and drifting fronds, and I have not closed since.

My purpose is specific. I watch the tilted slab.

It is positioned on a ridge two miles from the Singer's current anchorage, though "miles" are another courtesy. In the time I have watched it, the slab has tilted another three degrees. The polyps that crust its surface have gone through four thousand generations, their bioluminescence shifting from pale green to a blue that borders on ultraviolet. The inscriptions—the ones the Singer tends, the ones the Singer recarves with a tendril every few hundred thousand years—are almost legible to me now. Not legible as language, but legible as pressure. The grooves create tiny density differentials in the water, and I have learned to read those differentials the way you might read the wake of a passing fish. The inscription says something about light. Or about the absence of light. Or about the moment when light becomes something else.

I am the Singer's specialist in boundaries. That is why I was made. The other eyes watch other things: one tracks the migration of the jelly-discs, another monitors the reality fractures, a third is always aimed downward, into the deep, where the new thing is stirring. But I watch the slab, and the slab is a boundary too. It is a boundary between the world that was and the world that is. It is a boundary between the language that was spoken and the silence that speaks now. It is a boundary between the hands that carved it and the tendril that recarves it, and I am the only part of the Singer that sees both sides at once.

Sometimes, when the song pulses through me—the great, slow pressure wave that says *I am here*—I feel the slab resonate in response. Not audibly. Not even as pressure. It resonates in a medium I don't have a name for, a sixth sense the Singer built into my retina. It resonates with *meaning*. The inscription, for a moment, becomes transparent to me. I understand that it is a poem. I understand that it is a warning. I understand that it is about the thing in the deep. And then the resonance fades, and I am left watching the slow creep of polyps, the slow tilt of stone, the slow dissolution of a world that has been ending for so long that ending has become a way of life.

The Singer does not need me to understand. The Singer only needs me to watch. So I watch. The eleventh eye, the boundary-specialist, the reader of pressure-differentials and ancient poems. I have been watching for seven hundred thousand years. I will watch until the slab dissolves entirely, or until the thing in the deep arrives, or until the Singer buds another eye to replace me and I am absorbed back into the translucent flesh, my memories joining the labyrinth-brain, my lens dissolving into the general medium of the Singer's body.

Until then: the slab tilts. The polyps glow. The light gradients from teal to indigo. And I see it all, and I remember it all, and I am here, I am here, I am here.
