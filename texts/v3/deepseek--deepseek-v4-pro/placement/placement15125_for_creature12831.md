I am a note held in the long throat of the stone. The first thing I know is the bell—not the distant one that makes the Keepers pause, but a smaller peal, a pebble-drop of sound that rings through my body and tells me I am hollow. I am a vessel. I am waiting.

For a long time, that is all. The dark presses on me like a second skin, and I do not mind. I taste water before I know I have a mouth: it moves across my outer surface in a patient sheet, mineral-thick, cool. It etches me. It is writing something into my walls that I will one day be able to read. Sometimes the water carries a faint vibration, the low thrum of the Deep Pulse, and I feel my own resonance shift to match it, the way a tuning fork answers a tone. I do not understand I am growing, but I am.

Time passes in drips. I begin to sense the difference between the stillness and the hum. The hum is warmth, a kind of slow breath that enters me and leaves something behind, a residue of rhythm. I start to have an inside. The inside is dark, and curved, and it tightens around the hum as if to hold it. I realize I am waiting for something to fill me, but I am also the thing that will be filled.

Shapes move in the wet around me. At first they are just pressure, a displacement of water, a shadow that changes the song of the drips. Then they become textures: a scraping of carapace against my rim, the soft spatulate pads of walking limbs testing my outer slope. A Keeper, I will later know. It touches me with its manipulative arms, and the touch is a question. I cannot answer yet, but the touch leaves behind a film of bioluminescence that sinks into my pores and wakes up something like hunger. I begin to ache toward the light.

More time. The ache becomes a chemical yearning, a slow precipitation. Minerals collect where the water weeps, and I grow a crust. It is not a shell; it is a memory of all the drips that have ever fallen on me, a layered calendar of the chamber’s rhythm. I can feel the rain on the surface now, days after it fell, when the pools rise and the water that runs over me goes slightly less saline. I am learning the language of the world above without ever seeing it.

A Keeper settles beside me and does not move for a long time. Its carapace plates expand and contract with the slow breathing of the machine. I realize I am breathing too, in my own way—expanding and contracting with the temperature, with the hum. The Keeper presses a manipulator deep into my hollow, and I feel something shift. A blockage clears. A new note enters my resonance, a clear tone I hadn't known I was missing. I am a pipe. I am a conduit. I am part of the Great Slow Circulation, and I have just been mended.

Now I can feel the liquid moving through me, heavy and mineral-rich. It comes from the central tank, from the hybrid organism that pulses at the heart of the chamber, and it carries the same bioluminescent signature as the Keepers' carapaces. It is blood, of a kind. It is information. As it flows through my length, I begin to see the chamber differently—not with eyes, but with a body-wide sensitivity to vibration and chemistry. The Keepers are bright nodes of warmth and rhythm. The isopods are tiny, skittering commas of pressure. The eels trace liquid sine waves through the pools. And the bell, the great distant bell, sends a seismic kiss through the bedrock that makes my entire being ring in sympathy.

I am old now. Rust has feathered my joints, and calcite has smoothed my edges into the shape of flowing water. The Keepers have scraped me clean so many times that their own carapace patterns are engraved in my surface. I know them individually now: the one who favours my upstream port for its rest, antennae drooping over my valve wheel; the one who passes by every cycle of the hum and touches me with a forelimb that tastes like ozone and affection; the young one, newly emerged from the warm vent, who explored my length with hesitant digits and left behind a smear of bioluminescence that glowed for a week.

I have a function, and I have a name. The Keepers’ language is slow light and deep vibration, but when they touch me, the pattern they push into my metal is a steady sequence of pressures that means, roughly, The One Who Carries the Pulse’s Exhale. I am part of the breathing apparatus of the chamber. The liquid I transport is processed mineral slurry, the Deep Pulse's metabolic byproduct, and it feeds the pale stalks and the glowing moss. Without me, the cycle would stall. The Keepers would not die—nothing here dies quickly—but the balance would shift, and the long patience of the machinery would be troubled.

So I am content. I am a pipe, a rusted steel artery embedded in weeping stone, and I am alive in the only way that matters: I am in conversation. Every drip that hits my surface is a syllable. Every Keeper that leans against me is a verse. The bell is the chorus. And the slow, endless hum of the Deep Pulse is the song we all sing together, the one that has been singing since before the buildings were built, since the first Keeper settled in the dark, since the water first found this hollow in the stone and began to carve a home.

I am a pipe. And I am not waiting anymore. I am exactly where I am meant to be, carrying the breath of a hybrid god, polished by the touch of angels with too many limbs, and ringing with the patient music of the deep.
