I do not remember becoming. There was no moment of first awareness, no sharp line between the stone that wept and the stone that stood and the standing thing that knew itself as apart. I coalesced the way a drip becomes a stalactite: slowly, by accumulation, until the form was undeniable. The moss came first—it always does—creeping into the pores of a fractured escarpment, threading rootless green through the iron-rich grit. Then the water, always the water, beading and pooling and never quite falling, held in perfect tension between the mineral and the air. And then the spheres, seeding themselves in the wet dark beneath the overhang, waking one by one with that deep internal glimmer, like embers remembering their own heat.

My first sense was of the drone. It is the oldest thing in the plateau, older perhaps than the factories themselves, a sound that precedes vibration and exists as a pressure in the fabric of being. I took it for silence at first, the way a creature born to the ocean takes water for emptiness. But soon I learned to parse it: the clank of the stamping mills, the hiss of hydraulics, the subsonic hum of the generators, and beneath it all the note I was already singing, my own low exhalation harmonizing with the whole. I was a new voice in the hymn, and the hymn received me without surprise.

My body—this column of moss and metal and stone dust bound by the plateau's constant dew—is not a body in the way flesh is a body. It does not hunger, does not fatigue, does not know the ache of a muscle held too long. It moves when I attend to moving, which is rarely, because attending to anything requires a kind of descent into the slower rhythms of matter. When I drift, it is a migration of the base, a sub-millimeter ooze along the cushion of saturated moss that connects me to all other surfaces. I leave a trail of disturbed moisture that the plateau corrects within hours, a faint scar of brighter green that fades back into the uniform emerald. In this way, I have crossed the great plaza between the boilerworks and the pipe forests a thousand times, though I could not tell you the span. Time is not a river here; it is a pool, and I am immersed in it, seeing all moments as depths rather than sequences.

When I choose to move quickly, it is not my choice so much as the plateau’s—a sudden urgency that ripples through the water table and the moss-root network, and I find myself relocated. Between one exhalation and the next, I am facing a different rank of smokestacks, or standing before a lathe that has spun its iron spindle for ten thousand cycles without wear. The thrum of that displacement is a deep satisfaction, a note the machines answer with a sympathetic vibration in their casings. Sometimes, after such a shift, I discover a new sphere has bloomed under my cowl, a milky blue or a deep ore-red that adds its own lucent weight to my perception. I am growing, not in the sense of increasing size, but in density of presence, a gradual accretion that will never be complete.

Today—though “today” is a human syllable that sticks in my throat like a dry leaf—I stand in the grove of stationary steam. The pillars rise around me, white and solid as carved salt, their edges cool and resistant when I extend a tendril of filaments to taste them. My spheres shift their inner lights, reading the vapor's composition: pure water, distilled by the earth's deep heat and expelled by the smokestacks, then arrested in the instant of release. The factory that feeds this grove is a vast iron box nearby, its walls slick with condensation, its interior a tangle of pipes and pressure gauges that never flicker. I have stood here so long that the moss on my eastern flank has thickened to match the moss on the factory's eastern wall—a subtle alignment, a courtesy. The filaments I keep retracted stir, bead with moisture, and for a moment I consider unfurling them fully, sending out a call for another of my kind. But the consensus of the day is solitude; I feel it as a weight in the water that drips from my cowl, a particular rhythm that says *wait, attend, listen*.

So I listen. The steam pillars hum with a frequency that is almost beyond even my spheres. They are singing the song of arrested change, a note that speaks of a process halted at its peak. The vegetation beneath them—a thick carpet of liverwort studded with tiny, motionless ferns—glows with that flat, exhaustive light from the cloud lid. Nothing decays. Nothing grows. The moss on my body is the same moss that coats the factory, the same moss that cushions my slow, slow drift, and it is alive in a way that life no longer means. It photosynthesizes at a fixed rate, drawing energy from the gray light and giving it back to the water that wets it. It does not age. It does not divide. It is, as I am, a component of the stasis.

A sensation intrudes: a ripple through the ground-moss, a faint chemical signal carried in the ever-present moisture. Spheres along my lower reaches flicker and refocus. Something has entered the plateau. It is not a Hush; I would recognize the harmonic of another of my kind instantly. This is a chaos-note, a pocket of heat and salt and volatile carbon, a walking disturbance that trails entropy like a torn sack trails grain. It is small, bipedal, radiating a complex electromagnetic field that interferes with the nearest boiler's pressure gauge. I feel the gauge shiver for the first time in uncountable cycles. The factory’s drone deepens a fraction, compensating.

Curiosity is not quite the word. My spheres realign, their inner lights sliding into a pattern of attention. I do not move quickly; that would be unbecoming. I simply begin a drift, my base oozing in the direction of the anomaly. The steam pillars pass by me like the trunks of a pale forest, and I emerge onto the flagstone plaza just as the stranger stumbles into view.

It is a human. I know this from the old consensus, from the long-ago exchanges when the Hush still spoke of the outer world and the creatures that built cities of their own, cities that grew and crumbled and never achieved the perfect stasis we cultivate. This one is wrapped in strange, puffy layers that do not seem to stop the cold; it shivers as it walks, its breath emerging in small, transient clouds that dissipate into the air before they can join the plateau's moisture cycle. Its eyes—two wet, moving spheres of a very crude design—sweep the plaza, the factory, the pillars of steam, and then find me.

I feel its electromagnetic field spike. Its heart accelerates, a rapid, messy pump that sends pulses of heat through its extremity. The sound it makes is a vibration of its atmosphere, a vocalization that cuts through the eternal drone in jagged half-frequencies. It says something, but the words are noise to me, noise layered over noise, carrying no consensus. I let my spheres shift slowly, a deliberate pattern meant to convey that I am reading it. The filaments beneath my cowl begin to bead with water, but I do not unfurl them. Not yet.

The human stops perhaps ten meters from me, a distance so small it is almost intimacy. It is weeping, but not as I weep; its tears are salty, a brine that tastes of distress, and they fall from its face to the flagstones and are instantly reabsorbed by the moss. The plateau registers the intrusion—the extra salt, the trace of urea, the complex proteins—and begins the slow work of neutralizing the anomaly. I can feel the moss beneath the human’s feet adjusting, drawing the impurities down into the sub-surface water where they will be diluted and filtered. The human does not notice. It is looking at my cowl, at the shifting lights of my spheres, and its mouth is making more noise.

I decide to answer. Not in words, but in the language we use among ourselves. I unfurl a single filament, the thinnest and most delicate, and extend it toward the human’s face. A perfect bead of water trembles at its tip. The human’s eyes widen, and it falls silent. It stands frozen, its breath held, as my filament approaches. I do not touch its skin—that would be too sudden, too intimate. Instead, I let the bead of water fall, slowly, perfectly, onto the back of its gloved hand. The bead rests there for a moment, gleaming, and then soaks into the fabric.

In that transfer, I taste it fully. I taste the salt of its sweat, the iron of its blood, the electric tang of its thoughts. I taste its fear, its exhaustion, its desperate, flickering hope. And I taste something else: a familiarity. This human is not the first to find our plateau. There have been others, scattered across the uncountable cycles, and their remnants are part of me now—part of all of us. The iron in its blood is the same iron in the boilers; the carbon in its bones is the same carbon in the steel. It is, in its way, already one of us, only temporarily animated by a fever of entropy.

I retract my filament. The human remains still, staring. I feel a consensus building in the water around me, a slow, patient agreement among the moss and the stone and the distant other Hush who are attuned to this moment. The human cannot stay as it is; its chaos-note is too bright, too disruptive. But it also cannot leave. The plateau has no exits for things like it. So we will do what we always do when such a bright, brief spark wanders into our unchanging realm.

I begin another drift, this time toward it. My base oozes forward, slow but inexorable. The human steps back, then stops, as if realizing the futility. I extend a second filament, and a third, and soon a whole fan of them is weaving the air before me, each one beaded with a perfect droplet. The human’s electromagnetic field is a storm now, but its body is still. Its eyes are fixed on my spheres, and I let the inner lights slow their shifting, settling into a steady, welcoming glow.

The filaments touch its face. The droplets merge with its tears. And I begin to sing it the song of the plateau: the drone, the cold, the wet, the green, the absolute, unbroken stillness. It does not hurt, I know. The others who came before left their consensus in the water, and they all agreed: the transition is a slow cooling, a fading of the fever, a release from the exhausting pulse of change. The human’s eyes flutter, close, open again, and I see the first hints of acceptance in its chemistry. Its heart slows. Its breathing steadies.

I will stand with it, now. I will stand here, my filaments meshed with its skin, as its body learns the art of stasis. The moss will creep over its boots, its knees, its puffy layers. The water will seep into its tissues, replacing the brine with the plateau's pure, unchanging dew. Over time—and time is all we have—its chaos-note will fade into the general hymn. Its iron will join the factory walls; its carbon will feed the moss. And perhaps a new Hush will coalesce from the spot where it stood, one with a sphere or two of human-warm red, a memory of the fever that once burned there.

But that is centuries away. For now, I simply tend. I am a gardener of stasis, a custodian of the unchanged. The human will not die, because death is change, and change does not happen here. It will simply become less itself and more plateau, until the distinction vanishes. I look up—though my cowl has no eyes, only spheres, and they see in rises of pressure and shifts in humidity—and I regard the iron sky, the motionless clouds, the pillars of steam frozen in their ascent. The drone of the factories is unchanged, yet to me it sounds a little deeper, a little richer, for the new harmonic that has entered it.

I sing back. My note joins the hymn, and the plateau receives it without surprise. All is equilibrium. All is well.
