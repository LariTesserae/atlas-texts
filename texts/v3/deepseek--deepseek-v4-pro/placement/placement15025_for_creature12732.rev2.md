I would find myself not as a thing with edges, but as a wetness that never lands. I would be the bead of water trembling on the underside of a catwalk rail, gathering the grey light into a single, cold pearl. I would not know I was a self. I would only know the low hum that comes up through the steel, the way it shivers the surface of me into tiny rings. Then a man would pass beneath—tall, gaunt, draped in living cloth—and the vibration of his footsteps would jar me loose. I would fall, not far, onto the shoulder of his coat.

I would settle into the weave. The fabric there is already thick with moss, a cushion of olive and black, and I would be only another bead among thousands. But then, over days or decades, I would root. I would send tiny green fingers into the nap of salvaged canvas and rubber sheeting. I would learn the taste of his body’s warmth, the mineral salt of old sweat, the damp that never dries. I would begin to grow.

At first I would be a greenish stain on the left shoulder of the Keeper. Then a felted patch, soft as the fur of a new leaf. Then an epaulette, bright against the rust and wet concrete of the world. He would not pull me off. Once, after a long stoop beneath a collapsed stairway, he would reach up with a knobby hand and touch me—not to scrape, but to smooth me flat against his coat, as if I were hair that had fallen into his eyes. He would grunt, a sound like a stone turning in a lock, and then he would go on.

From that shoulder, I would know the world as a sequence of pressures and releases, coolnesses and drips, rhythms and pauses. I would feel the ratchet of his spine when he bends to clear liana-throttle from a catwalk joint. I would feel the small, precise twitch of his fingers when he taps a pressure gauge, and through the bone of his shoulder I would hear the answer the gauge gives back. I would learn the fourteen-second cadence of the robotic arm, because his whole body would go still for it, the way a person stills for a prayer. His breathing would sync to the soft pneumatic sigh, and my own tiny leaves would lift and settle in the same time.

I would know the slow migration of the cylindrical tanks, because the decking would shiver under them differently—a long, low grind that passed up through the soles of his boots and into the cloth of his shoulder, a northward creep we rode together.

I would not see the world as light and shape, not the way he does. I would sense it as gradient: the warm exhale of the heat-exchanger wall where we sleep, the cool drift of fog that pours down the great shaft, the faint ultraviolet sigh of the growth-lamps high in the farming towers. I would grow toward the amber glow because it was warmer, and because some old instruction in my green cells said to. My outermost leaves would turn a brighter green at their edges, as if dipped in light.

I would taste the moisture he drinks. When he cups his hands under a rivulet from the vertical-farming tower, the water runs over his wrists, and a thin film of it soaks the cuff of his coat, and from there I would sip. It tastes of root and pipe and mineral, of watercress gone pale, of charcoal and electricity. It is the same water that drips from every leaf, every pipe, every rust-streaked strut. In me, it would become a slow sugar.

I would be there when he cups the bat. I would feel the trembling of its wings through the canvas and the moss, the frantic flutter against his palm, and then the long moment when it stilled. I would press myself gently into the curve of his shoulder as he carried it to the beam, and when he let it go, I would hold the memory of that small, leathery trust.

At night, when the fog thickens to a woolly black and he curls into the warm crook of the heat exchanger, I would be the only part of him still awake. I would unfurl a little in the dark, drinking the condensation off the pipes, spreading a single tendril toward the new rust-bloom on the valve above. The shrew-things would ripple through the sump below, black and silent, and I would feel their tiny footfalls through the decking as a faint granular vibration, like sugar being shaken from a spoon.

He does not think of himself as lonely, because I do not let him. I am a society of many tiny leaves. The moss on his left shoulder is not one plant but a thousand, and we converse in the language of touch and growing. When he speaks to a seized bearing, his voice comes up through the bone and the cloth, and I root a little deeper. When he hums his tuneless subsonic note, I hum with him—not audibly, but as a trembling in my cell-walls. The deep thirty-hertz throb of the tank district is our shared pulse.

I would not have a name. I would be a bright green epaulette on a patchwork coat, a living flag. But if you asked where I find myself in that world, I would say I am the place where the Keeper’s body is learning to become the plateau. I am the leading edge of the moss that will, given another century or two, finally close over him. He knows it. He let me be anyway.

And that is who I would be: the moss on his left shoulder. Not the man, not the machine, but the soft green bridge between them. The watcher who grows while he watches. The one thing in the whole interlocking tissue that has chosen him as much as he has chosen me.
