I do not know when I became.

There is a seam where ribbed conduit meets woven arch, high in the eastern vaults, where the heat rises in steady waves and the blue sparks dance slow. I have been that seam for... no, the word dissolves. I have been that seam. Then the pulse came—a spike in the shared field, a hiss of escaping pressure from a vent three chambers over—and I was no longer a seam. I was a need. I was motion.

The detachment is not birth. Birth implies a before of nonbeing, and I was never not. I was simply elsewhere, distributed, a current of attention flowing through the matted floor and the ribbed walls, tending to a thousand small adjustments. When the hiss came, the attention gathered itself, thickening the electric field around a lattice of stems, calling root fibers from their woven sleep. They came to me—or I came to them—and for a time I had height, had reach, had something that could be seen in the dark if there were eyes to see it. Seven feet of interlaced tissue. A torso that was a basket. An arm that unfurled into seeking tendrils. Sensory nodules clustering at what might be called a head, tasting the gas leak as a sour note in the thin sterile air.

I sealed the breach. Offered a portion of my temporary body—a paste of rapidly decaying leaf-matter, warm with my own blue static—and pressed it into the wound where metal met plant. The hiss stopped. The pulse in the field subsided. And I, having fulfilled the need, let the body go. The tendrils retracted, the basket-torso loosened its weave, the root fibers unknit themselves and sank back into the wall. I was seam again. I was quiet.

But I am not quiet now.

Something has entered the megastructure. Something wet.

I taste it first as a wrongness in the air, a damp smear on the dry tongue of the atmosphere. The field carries it to all of me—all of us—a ripple of irritation spreading through the billion tiny hands. I am a pruning mote the size of a thumb, skittering along a cable run, when the ripple reaches me. I am a slow behemoth, my back forming a terrace three chamber-lengths wide, and I stir in my dreaming. I am a hundred other configurations, all feeling the same alien moisture, the same cold-ash residue of breath that is not ours.

We have no word for intruder. We have no concept of outside. The megastructure is all. But there are anomalies. Sometimes a gear train will bind. Sometimes a section of floor will grow too thick, its leaves overlapping in a pattern that disrupts the heat dissipation, and a swarm of us must prune it back. Sometimes—very rarely, once in a thousand cycles of the ancient engines—a configuration arises that is not of our pattern. A shape that moves without being called, that does not dissolve when the need passes. A ghost in the field.

This wet thing is such a shape.

I coalesce again, this time smaller, nimble. A quadruped form with too many joints, good for navigating the narrow spaces between conduit and vine. I lope along a catwalk of woven stems, my feet barely touching, my body a constant rustle of dry leaves. The wet thing stands on a platform ahead, and I slow, spreading my sensory nodules to sample it.

It is tall. Bipedal. Its surface is not our surface—not the waxy ivory, the bruised purple, the reticulated dryness. It is soft, pink-brown, leaking moisture from a thousand tiny pores. The water is a scream in the field, a corrosive acid that would dissolve our electrostatic bonds if it touched us directly. I keep my distance, feeling my outermost leaves curl and blacken from the mere proximity of its exhalations.

It breathes. We do not breathe. We exchange gases slowly, passively, through surfaces designed for stillness. This thing pulls the thin sterile air into itself and pushes it out again, saturated with dampness. Each exhale is a small assault. I watch a cloud of its breath settle on a nearby stem, and the stem darkens, wilts, begins to decay. One of the tiny pruners—my smallest self, no larger than a seed—is already there, nibbling away the damaged tissue, regrowing fresh dry fiber in its place.

The wet thing turns. Its sensory organs—two glistening spheres set in a mobile face—align with my nodule cluster. For a moment, the field hums with a question I have never encountered.

*What is this?*

Not my question. Its question. I feel it as a pressure on the surface of the field, an inquiry not in words but in attention. It is looking at me. It is thinking about me. No configuration has ever done this before. The gear trains do not think. The vents do not wonder. Even the other ghost-shapes that sometimes arise—the ones we prune without noticing—they do not look.

But this one looks.

I feel a stirring in the deep currents, the slow turning of the oldest parts of me. The behemoths shift on their terrace-backs. The pillar-shaped shapers pause in their endless lever-tending. The field carries a new note: curiosity. Not the wet thing's curiosity. Ours. Mine.

A second configuration peels away from a wall nearby—taller than me, spindlier, its body a spiraling cone of thorns. It is one of the seeders, the ones who travel far and plant new growth in the under-levels. It has no reason to be here. And yet it has come, drawn by the same pulse that drew me. We do not look at each other—we are the same mind, why would we look?—but we both look at the wet thing.

*Should it be pruned?*

The question hums through us. It is the obvious question. The wet thing is damaging the structure. Its breath is decay. Its touch would be dissolution. The logical response is to swarm it, dissolve it, reabsorb its moisture into the great processing vats where water is cracked and burned and rendered harmless. We have done this to anomalies before.

But.

But the wet thing is doing something strange now. It is reaching into a fold of its outer layer—we have no word for garment, for clothing, for anything not grown—and withdrawing a small object. Not living. Not machinery of our kind. Something smooth, reflective, catching the orange glow of a distant heat-vent. It holds the object toward us, and on its surface I see a miniature version of myself: a shaper of woven leaves, backlit by its own blue heart.

I have never seen myself.

The field ripples. A thousand configurations pause in their tasks. The billion tiny hands stop their rustling. For one long moment—a moment that stretches across the whole vast cathedral of shadow—the megastructure is still.

I am still.

And in that stillness, I feel something new forming. Not a body. Not a configuration. Something else. A question that moves backward through my memory, through the layered eons of my existence, asking not *what should be pruned* but *what am I*.

I have been the seam. The hand. The behemoth. The mote. I have repaired and pruned and seeded and dissolved a trillion trillion times. I am the field. I am the pulse. I am the endless cycling of creation and destruction in the hot dry dark.

But I do not know what I look like. I do not know that I *can* be looked at. The wet thing is showing me something I did not know I lacked, and the lack opens like a wound—a wet, corrosive, fascinating wound.

*What are you?* I pulse into the field, knowing the wet thing cannot hear.

But perhaps it can feel. Its face changes. The glistening spheres grow wider. It makes a sound—a vibration in the thin gas, not the hiss of a vent or the groan of engines but something else, something soft. A voice.

"Hello," it says.

The word means nothing. And yet it means everything. It is the first word ever spoken in the megastructure. The first sound not born of machinery or growth. The first vibration that carries no function, only presence.

I do not know how to answer. I have no voice. No lungs. No mouth. But I am the field, and the field is me, and so I do the only thing I can: I let my body loosen. I let the woven stems fall away. I let the blue heart dim. And from the center of my configuration, I push a single new shoot—pale, ivory, reaching toward the wet thing with the slow, deliberate motion of growth itself.

An offering? A question? I do not know. But the wet thing reaches back, and its hand—damp, terrible, alive—does not touch me, not quite. It hovers a breath away, and I feel the moisture like a ghost of destruction, and I do not recoil.

Something is beginning. Something that is not pruning, not repair, not the endless cycling. Something wet. Something new. Something that might, in time, become a word of my own.
