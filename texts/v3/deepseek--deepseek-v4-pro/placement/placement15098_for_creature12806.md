Your own breathing is the first thing to change.

It’s not a decision—not at first. It’s a response to the air itself, which is so wet it feels like drinking, like drowning in slow motion. Each inhale coats the inside of your chest with a fine slickness, and after a few minutes, you notice your lungs are working differently: deeper, slower, as if they’re learning from the moss. The warmth helps. It unclenches something in your shoulders you’ve been carrying for years. The constant, overwhelming sound—the piping chorus, the percussive clicks from the many-legged geckos, the booming thrum from the tower—stops being noise and becomes a texture, like the grain of wood or the nap of velvet. Your heartbeat, you realize with a start, has shifted to match the slow, tidal rhythm of the elder’s drumming.

The Kith-speaker who offered you the rope is still watching from a distance of inches. Now that you’re seeing it properly—*her*, a certainty arrives without evidence, the same way you know the elder is neither male nor female but something third—you can make out details beneath the moss. The pebbled grey hide on her forearms has a faint iridescence, like the skin of a skink, and where the moss is thinnest on her belly, you can see a network of tiny, dark pores that open and close in time with her breathing. She blinks her side-swept eyelid, and her head tilts in a gesture that is so obviously *Well? Are you coming?* that a laugh catches in your throat. The sound is alien here. It comes out as a puff of air, and a dozen nearby Kith-speakers pause their slow fibril-flexing to turn their dark eyes toward you. The attention is not hostile. It’s curious, watchful, the way a flock of small birds assesses something new at the feeder.

You take the rope.

It’s lighter than you expected, and warmer, still carrying a faint residue of the mucilage it was spun with. When you wrap it around your waist, you feel the tiny irregularities in the twist—this is not machine-made, not even wheel-spun. This was rolled by hand, between palms the size of apricots, over hours or days or entire seasons. The fibers catch on your clothing, and you realize they’re barbed, microscopically, designed to grip. *For the wall*, you think. *Everything here is for the wall.*

The small one—you find yourself thinking of her as *Needle*, for the way her fingers taper to points finer than pins—scuttles backward, then sideways, then down, moving across the vertical cliff as if it were a floor. She pauses at the ledge she’d pointed to, a narrow shelf of spongy moss that you hadn’t seen before, and looks up. Her dark eyes catch the diffuse light, and her pupils pulse once, a slow dilation like a camera aperture adjusting. *Here*, the gesture says. *Put your foot here.*

You sit on the edge of the chasm, legs dangling into the immense drop, and the vertigo hits you like a wave. A thousand feet of empty air yawns below, filled with drifting spores and the distant, boiling movements of the flying frog clouds. For a moment, your primate brain screams *no*, and your hands clutch at the moss behind you. The moss gives slightly, a living mattress, and the sensation is so strange it jolts you out of the panic. You’re gripping the world’s oldest, softest wall. It’s holding you already.

You lower yourself onto the ledge.

The moss under your feet is springy, and your boot sinks in an inch, then stops. You can feel the stone beneath—cool, solid, permanent—but the living rind between you and it acts like a shock absorber, a cushion, a grip. When you shift your weight, the moss releases a tiny puff of spores that hang in the air like a faint green question, then settle back. Needle chitters, a sound that resolves into what might be approval, and scrambles downward another ten feet, her body a flat, green comma against the wall. She waits.

Climbing down is not like climbing as you’ve known it. There are no handholds, not exactly, but the moss itself yields to pressure and then holds. You learn fast: press your palm flat, let the moss compress around your fingers, and the tiny root-veins tighten in response. It’s like shaking hands with the wall. The rope helps, slung around a knob of compacted vegetation that Needle shows you how to test—she tugs it with her fibrils, and you see the whole mass shift slightly, a whole interconnected mat moving as one. *It will hold*, the gesture says. *We have been holding it for ten thousand years.*

The descent becomes rhythmic. Breathe, press, step, wait. Breathe, press, step, wait. The sounds of the chasm change as you go lower: the treble of the flying frogs fades upward, and new sounds rise from the mist. A low, liquid gurgle, the echo of water moving somewhere in the depths. A rhythmic *thock-thock-thock* that might be a gecko-thing calling or might be a Kith-speaker with a stone knife, shaping a tool. The air grows even warmer, and wetter, and the light dims to a greenish twilight. The mist parts for a moment, and you see the column-tower nearest you in full profile: a colossal, ragged cylinder that rises from the unseen bottom, its flanks festooned with cascading ferns and hollows where small lights—colonies of glowing fungus, you realize—pulse in a steady, breathing cadence.

Needle is beside you again, so close you feel the faint warmth of her body radiating through the moss on her back. She reaches out a long, slow hand and touches your cheek. Her fibrils are dry, feather-light, and they smell of crushed leaves and something sharper, like ozone after lightning. The touch is not affectionate in a human way—it’s investigative, a question asked with fingers. *What are you? Why are you so smooth? Where is your moss?* Then she withdraws, and with the same hand, points downward into the mist, then traces a shape in the air: an oval, wider at the bottom. A face. The carved face.

You climb for what feels like hours. Time moves strangely here, measured in breaths and footholds, and you could not say later whether it was noon or twilight when you arrived. But the light does dim, and the bioluminescence in the crevices grows stronger, a cool blue-green that makes shadows dance. Needle leads you along a traverse, a horizontal band of thicker moss that feels almost like a road, and then she stops.

The boulder is immense—the size of a house—and it juts from the chasm wall like a ship’s prow frozen in stone. The moss on its surface has been scraped away, carefully, revealing the grey, polished rock beneath. And there, carved into the stone, is the face. It’s larger than you are tall, and so old that the features are softened, the nose half-lost, the mouth a gentle shadow. But the eyes are still clear: wide, staring out into the chasm with an expression that is not fear, not wonder, but something deeper. Recognition. *I was here*, the face says. *I am still here. You are seen.*

Needle has gone still. You turn, and she is not alone. The elder is there—the large one from the tower, her moss veined with blue light, her dark eyes holding your own. She must have descended while you climbed, moving with the silence of a spore drifting. Behind her, dozens of smaller Kith-speakers are emerging from hollows and crevices, their bodies a constellations of amber eye-shine in the growing dark. They are all watching the face. Watching you.

The elder raises her long hand. Her fibrils spread, catching the bioluminescence, and she begins to sign. The movements are slower than before, deliberate, and with a jolt you understand she is signing *for you*: a simplified language of gestures that your mind, somehow, is learning. *This one*, she signs, pointing at the face, *was the first. The one-who-made-the-first-mark. We do not remember her name, but we remember her face.* She pauses, and her great eyes blink their side-swept lids. *Now you are here. The dry-walker. The smooth-one. You will make a mark too, or you will become one.* Her gesture shifts, and she touches her own mossy chest, then points at you, then at the face. The meaning is unmistakable: *We are all here.*

Needle chitters, a soft, clicking whisper, and one of her fibrils brushes your hand. She’s pointing at a small, clear patch of stone at the edge of the boulder. There’s a flaked knife lying there, newly sharp, still damp from the whetstone. A question. An invitation.

The chasm breathes around you, its steady-state turning on its slow, eternal cycle. The flying frogs are settling in their roosts, their chorus fading to a soft, percussive murmur. The gecko-things have begun their night songs, a syncopated tick-tock that rises from the mist. And you are standing on the wall, held by moss and hair-rope, being asked to join a conversation that started before your species learned to walk upright.

You take the knife. The handle is wrapped in twisted hair, tacky with mucilage, and it fits your hand as if it were made for it. The stone is cool. The first mark you make will be your own.

And you know, with a certainty that feels like breathing, that you will never be quite so dry again.
