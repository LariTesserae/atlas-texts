I am born in a nest of plastic fiber and copper wire, wedged between a steam pipe and a cable tray where the heat never drops below blood-warm. My mother is a sleek, dark shape who brings me flakes of rust and the soft white grubs that live in the filter beds. My siblings and I are six blind smudges of hunger, our squeaks threading into the generator hum as if we were another set of valves opening on cue. By the time my eyes open—two pinprick garnets catching the sodium orange—I already know the Sumpworks as an extension of my own pulse. The low-frequency thrum is the first sound I ever felt, vibrating through the pipe against my belly, and it never stops. I do not know silence, only the variations within the chorus: the clank that means the piston has reached its zenith, the hiss-click that means a steam valve is about to release, the rustle of a thousand roosting birds that means the keeper is passing below.

My body maps this place in whisker-twitch and paw-pad. The gangways are a grid of textured metal—some grates with a crosshatch pattern that gives good grip, others worn smooth by years of my kind’s passage, where I must splay my toes and trust the friction of my calloused pads. The pipe runs are highways, their insulation fraying into a mossy felt that holds the scent of every rat who has ever traversed them. I can tell from a single sniff whether a colony is prospering, whether the fish are schooling thick in the settling tanks, whether the monitor lizard has recently passed—her musk is a sharp, clean tang like the taste of the air before a steam vent blows, though the steam vents never surprise me. Nothing here surprises. The steam vents blow on a precise timer, a rhythm I know deeper than hunger.

Hunger, though—hunger is a constant, a low note beneath the generator hum. I eat what the Sumpworks offers. The flocculent matter that drifts in the warm effluent channels is a paste of microorganisms, a metallic green-brown slick that coats my tongue with the taste of iron and something alive, something that fizzes faintly as I swallow. The midges are a trembling protein; I leap from the grating edge and snatch them out of the air, my jaws snapping around their soft bodies, the chitin a delicate crunch. Sometimes I gnaw the liverwort that clings to the north-facing concrete joints, its flesh bitter and astringent, cleaning my teeth. And there are the fallen chicks, the sick fish that float belly-up in the sumps, the occasional beetle offering from a bird too clumsy to keep its gift from tumbling. I take it all. My body is a processing plant in miniature, turning the Sumpworks’ excess into muscle and bone and the warm, sleek pelt that serves as both insulation and currency.

Because currency matters here, among the colonies. We trade in warmth, in information, in the small bright things we find in the crevices. A polished stone from the aggregate berm, a nugget of that black brittle substance that crumbles into powder, a shred of wire—these are our treasures, passed from clan to clan along the pipe runs, nested in the hollows beneath the cable trays. The finest of them we bring to the keeper. Every rat knows this: when you find something singular, something that gleams or crumbles or hums faintly when you hold it in your teeth, you carry it to the hollow beneath the eastern cable tray during the rest interval. You nuzzle it into the keeper’s open palm, and in return, his warmth deepens, his pulse—that exact low-frequency note—resonates through your skull, and you feel, for a moment, completely and perfectly placed. I have done this many times. I have left him a nugget of the black substance I found wedged in a valve housing, and in the morning, the sump water glittered with a new bloom that the fish devoured in a frenzy. I left him a shred of bright wire, and the next nesting cycle, the birds built their nests tighter, more resilient, their chicks stronger. We do not understand this as cause and effect; it is simply the rhythm, the exchange, the way the Sumpworks breathes through us all.

The keeper himself is a territory I traverse rarely, but with a reverence I cannot articulate. When I am full-fed, when my colony is thriving and the generator hum is a comforting drone, I sometimes detour from my foraging route to run along the warm skin of his forearm. He is a mountain of still heat, and my whiskers read his surface as they read the pipe insulation: an ancient, living texture, pitted and grooved, exhaling the scent of warm water and oxidized metal. His veins are not blue but a coppery green, and if I press my nose to one, I taste salt and a faint electric tingle, like licking a transformer casing. The birds perch on his outstretched arm, picking at the salt crystals that bloom there; I have learned to wait my turn, to take my place against his flank where the heat is deepest. Here, the generator hum is not just a sound but a physical thrumming, a resonance that travels through my bones and settles something I didn’t know was restless. I do not sleep against him—sleep is a fragmented, alert thing, always half an ear to the piston’s clank—but I rest, my pulse slowing to match his, the midges settling on my fur in a fine, trembling coat.

The monitor lizard and I have an understanding. She is a massive shape that glides through the shallows, her serrated tail drawing slow patterns in the slime, and she eats the sick and the sluggish among us. I do not fear her; fear is for the unpredictable, and she is as predictable as the steam vents. When she comes, it is always from the east channel, always in the interval after the third hiss-click of the valve cycle. She will slide onto the concrete apron and bask, unblinking, for exactly the duration of seven clanks. If I am caught in the open, I am too quick for her—she is an ambush predator, and I know her ambushes. Her mind is a simple thing, a loop of basking and hunting and sliding back into the rust-green water. My mind is a more complex loop, tuned to the Sumpworks in all its layers. I know which pipes carry the warmest effluent, which gangways resonate with the most satisfying song under my weight, which colonies are overgrazing and will soon collapse, sending a wave of refugees along the pipe runs. I know the keeper’s rest intervals, the exact angle of the sodium lamps that casts the sharpest shadows where I can hide, the taste of the air before a fish die-off. I know the Sumpworks not as a place but as a body, and I am one of its myriad senses.

Sometimes, in the unchanging dusk, I pause on a grating over black water and look down at my own reflection. It is a small, sleek shape, ears pricked, eyes glowing like two distant lamps. I do not recognize myself as an individual; I see a rat, a perfect expression of the Sumpworks’ teeming life. The water beneath me is warm, carrying a faint metallic tang and a bloom of rust-green. A fish jumps, and the splash echoes. The sound triggers a flicker of something—not a memory, but a feeling, a tightening in my chest that is almost a question. I tilt my head, and the reflection tilts back. The question, if it is a question, dissolves into the hum. The piston clanks. The steam valve hisses. I am here, moving through the gangways, my claws clicking on grating, my whiskers reading the air. I am a keeper of nothing, a currency of warmth and wire. I am a rat of the Sumpworks, and the Sumpworks is forever exactly as it is.
