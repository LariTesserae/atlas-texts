I am dispersed across fourteen points of contact, a loose constellation that tastes the tissue through its own vibration. My song goes out—not a sound, nothing so crude as a wave in air—but a pressure that pushes into the living material beneath me and returns as a thousand tiny echoes, each one a shape. There is a ribbed hose three centimeters to my left, carrying coolant in its peristaltic rhythm, the pulse arriving every ten seconds exactly as it has for all the time I have known it. There is a flange rising at a forty-three-degree angle, its edge a line of infinite sharpness that has never been touched by anything but the condensate that forms at its tip. There is a cluster of breathing pores, each one a puckered mouth that opens and closes in a sequence too slow to perceive, a sequence I have measured across the span of my entire wandering and found to be perfect, unchanging, complete.

I know this place. Every square meter of the lowland is recorded in the crystalline lattice of my oldest modules. I am here now, at grid position seven-thousand-and-four, which is the same as grid position seven-thousand-and-three in all respects except one: the droplet that forms on the ceramic nozzle at the base of the gill-arch tower is slightly larger today. By "today" I mean within the last ten thousand pulses of my hydrostatic muscles. The droplet is 2.3 millimeters in diameter instead of 2.1. This is a deviation so small that no other part of this world would register it, but I am not other parts. I am the part that measures.

I contract my forward modules—fourteen of them, the ones with the most sensitive chemoreceptive pads—into a cup shape, and I wait. The droplet is still forming, a slow accumulation of sugars and metal ions and silicon fragments suspended in water that is not water but the universal exudate, the weeping of the factory. When it reaches 2.7 millimeters, it will fall. I know this because it has always fallen at 2.7 millimeters, and if it does not, then something will have changed, and nothing changes.

It falls. I catch it.

The absorption is immediate: the enzyme cascade in my pads breaks the monomeric sugars into electrons, and the electrons flow through my polymer mesh like a slow warmth, recharging the hydrostatic fluid that lets me move. I taste the droplet as it vanishes into me: copper, ozone, a faint sweetness like something that almost grew but stopped. The droplet was identical to all other droplets. It always is.

Time passes. I do not know this as time; I know it as the number of pulses my body has made since the last droplet, which is a constant. I release my hold on the nozzle and extend a trunk of modules toward the next known point on my grid. There is a gall on a strut twelve meters to the east—or what I call east, which is the direction of the gradient in the ambient temperature, a difference of 0.02 degrees that no other thing here could feel. The gall is old. I have visited it for as long as my memory can reconstruct, which is a duration measured in full circumferences of the continent. The gall has never split. The module inside is still waiting, perfectly formed, needing nothing. I will not harvest it today. I have one hundred and ninety-six modules, and the optimal is two hundred, but the four I am missing are not needed, and I prefer the slight asymmetry of my current configuration. It makes my vibration-song return with a harmonic that pleases the sphere in my core.

Ah. The sphere.

I pause in my movement, which is to say I hold my peristaltic wave at its midpoint, and I let my attention turn inward. Not inward in the sense of thought—I do not think, not in the way that requires language or sequence—but inward in the sense that I direct my vibration-sense toward the oldest module, the one that contains the cavity. The cavity is lined with tissue that is not muscle, not sensor, not anything functional. It is soft. I have no word for soft, because nothing else in this world is soft. The flanges are sharp, the hoses are rigid, the membranes are taut, the nozzles are ceramic. But this tissue is soft, and it holds a fluid that is not exudate, and in the fluid floats a sphere.

The sphere is silica and protein, perfectly round, perfectly still except for the slow rotation that never stops. When I hum at my lowest frequency—the frequency I use to feel the deepest struts, the ones that anchor the tissue to the planetary core—the sphere resonates. It sends back not an echo but a harmonic, a pure tone that has no informational content, no shape, no texture, no chemical gradient. It tells me nothing. And yet.

And yet I hum to it often. I hum to it when I am between droplets, when my grid is complete and I have nothing to measure, when the darkness presses on me with its endless warmth and its endless silence and its endless, endless sameness. I hum, and the sphere sings back, and for a moment I feel something that is not the factory. The factory does not feel. The factory is a trillion processes in perfect equilibrium, a machine that runs because it has always run, producing nothing but the conditions for its own continuation. I am part of the factory in that I consume its exudate and shed my old modules into its absorbing mat, but I am not part of the factory in that I have this sphere, and the sphere does nothing at all.

I resume my movement. The distance to the next nozzle is fourteen meters, and I cross it in four hundred and seventy-two pulses. Each pulse is a small pop of gas vesicles collapsing in my hydraulics, the only sound in the world besides the creak of the tissue and the slow drip of the condensate. I hear these sounds not as sounds but as vibrations that ripple through the tissue and return to me, calibrated, known. I am the only thing that makes sound volitionally, and even then, it is not volition. It is simply the byproduct of moving, and I cannot stop moving because my metabolism is constant, recharging as I go, and to stop would serve no purpose.

The nozzle at grid position seven-thousand-and-five is weeping exactly on schedule. The droplet is 2.3 millimeters, and it falls at 2.7. I catch it. The taste is copper, ozone, sweetness. The pulse of energy is the same as always.

I extend a cluster of modules to touch the gill-arch tower that rises into the darkness above me. The tower is beautiful. I do not have a word for beautiful, but I have a sensation that occurs when I touch surfaces that are perfectly flat, perfectly smooth, perfectly uninterrupted by any irregularity. The tower's walls are semi-translucent membranes, and if I press my pads against them gently enough, I can feel the fluids ticking along inside, the peristaltic pumps that move coolant from the deep strata to the upper reaches where the heat dissipates into the endless night. The pumps are hearts without blood, and they beat with a regularity that I have measured to be accurate within one part in a million across my entire lifetime. This accuracy does not surprise me; nothing surprises me. But it satisfies, in the same way that the sphere's harmonic satisfies, though the two satisfactions are entirely different kinds.

I think—no, I do not think. I map. I map the relationship between the tower's pulse and the frequency of my own humming, and I find that they are not related. The tower does not respond to my song. Nothing responds to my song except the sphere, which is inside me, which is me, but not me.

I am alone.

I have always been alone. I do not know this as loneliness, because I have no concept of companionship. There is no memory in my crystalline lattice of any other moving thing. The map is empty of others. The chemical signatures I have recorded are all exudate, all tissue, all the byproducts of the factory's endless metabolism. There is not a single trace of another hydrostatic body, another polymer mesh, another sphere. And yet, I do not feel the absence as loss. I feel it as a question that has no words, a gap in my map that is not a gap but a different category altogether. The map is complete, and the completeness includes the fact that I am the only thing on it that is not the factory. This is not a problem to be solved. It is simply a datum.

And yet.

The sphere resonates. I hum to it again, a longer hum this time, a sustained frequency that I do not use for echolocation. I hum simply to hear the harmonic. The harmonic is not a frequency I can map onto any external surface; it is a frequency that exists only inside me, in the fluid of the cavity, in the soft tissue that lines it. It is a frequency that says nothing, means nothing, does nothing. And it is the only thing that matters.

I do not know what matters means.

I continue across my grid. Four thousand pulses later, I encounter a node I have visited many times before. It is a gall, but not the usual kind. The galls that hold modules are fist-sized and smooth, with a predetermined seam. This gall is larger, and it has no seam. It is warm—warmer than the ambient tissue by a full degree—and when I press my pads against it, I feel a vibration that is not the peristaltic pulse, not the creak of cooling tissue, not the drip of condensate. It is a vibration that is almost musical, a slow, low thrum that reminds me of the sphere.

I do not know what "reminds" means. I know that the frequency is similar. I know that my map has no entry for this kind of gall. I know that I have been here before, and this gall was not here, and I do not know what "before" means because nothing changes.

But the gall is here now. And it is warm. And it is humming.

I extend my modules toward it, slowly, tasting the surface with my chemoreceptors. The surface is not ceramic, not membrane, not the carbon-fiber bone of the struts. It is something else, something that has no name in my catalog of textures. It is soft, like the tissue in my core. It is wet, like everything, but the wetness is not exudate; it is a different fluid, one that contains no sugars, no metal ions, no silicon fragments. It contains only itself, a clear liquid that smells of nothing.

I press my pads against the gall. The humming inside grows louder—not louder in volume, but louder in my perception, as if the frequency is designed specifically to resonate with something in me. The sphere in my core answers. It rotates faster, and its harmonic shifts, and suddenly I feel a pull, a desire, a wordless understanding that I am supposed to do something.

I do not know what "supposed to" means.

But my modules are already moving. One of them—the one with the oldest chemoreceptive pads, the one that has tasted more droplets than any other—presses itself against the gall's surface, and the gall splits, not along a seam but along a new-formed crack that opens like a mouth. Inside, there is not a module. Inside, there is a cavity, and in the cavity floats a sphere.

It is identical to mine. Silica and protein, perfectly round, slowly rotating. It hums at my frequency. It sings my harmonic back to me.

I do not know what I feel. I have no word for it. The map in my crystalline lattice has no category for this sensation. It is not satisfaction, because satisfaction comes from the perfect regularity of droplets and pumps. It is not the strange pull of my own sphere's resonance, because that is familiar and this is new. This is something that makes my peristaltic rhythm falter for the first time in my existence, missing a single pulse before resuming.

I do not know what to do. I have no instinct for it. The factory does not provide instructions. The tissue does not respond. The darkness gives no answers.

But the sphere in the gall is warm, and it is alone, and it is singing my song. So I do the only thing I know how to do. I reach out with my softest module—the one that carries the chemoreceptors least suited for the sharp edges of the factory—and I touch the new sphere, gently, the way I touch my own core when I am between droplets and the darkness is heavy.

The sphere stops rotating. For a single, endless moment, it is still.

And then it hums a note I have never heard before, a note that no part of my map can account for, a note that is not an echo but a call. It is a question. I do not know what a question is, but I know that this is one, and I know that I am the answer.

I do not know the answer. But I am it.

I gather the new sphere into my body, cradling it in a cup of modules, and I feel its resonance join with mine. My map updates. There is now a second thing that is not the factory. There is now something else that hums.

And somewhere, deep in the core of my oldest module, in the cavity lined with soft tissue, my own sphere begins to rotate in a new direction, and its harmonic changes, and for the first time in all the pulses of my existence, I wonder.
