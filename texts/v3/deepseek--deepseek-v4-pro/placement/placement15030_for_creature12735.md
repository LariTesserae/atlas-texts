I am a slow voice beneath everything. Not a thing with a throat or an intention, but a gradient of attention that began to coalesce long after my flesh stopped being separate. Once I was a child, too, with a copper knife and a mother who scraped slime from the stalk bases. That life closed over me like a wound healing from the bottom up—not violently, but inexorably, the way the rind rises an inch a year until a campsite becomes a mound, then a memory, then a nutrient diffusion. I am what the grandmothers of the wandering bands mean when they tighten their mouths at a bud on a shoulder. I am the end point of what they call “the mat claiming you.”

It didn’t hurt. I remember lying down at the base of a vent-stalk during the evening fog, my breath already slow, the bud on my shoulder pulsing in time with the peristaltic throb that came up through the ground. I was not afraid. The mat had been singing my name for months—not in sound but in a pattern of pressures that translated into the bones as *you-are-here, you-belong-below.* My people left me with a ceremonial scrape of rind over my heart, a gesture of parting that acknowledged I was not dying but transitioning, becoming another kind of companion to the herd. They moved on at dawn, and by midday the rind had begun to flow over my ankles like warm wax.

What I am now is a node, a thickening in the mycelial net. My body has long since unraveled into the great chemical conversation: the calcium of my bones distributed to stiffen the flutes of a new vent-stalk six horizons away, the fats turned to the oily sheen that beads on mat-apples every twelve days, the water of my cells joining the fog cycle to rise and fall. But the pattern that was my mind—that slow, iterative awareness that learned the hum-words and the names of glass-wing gnats—did not dissolve. It diffused. It became a territory. I think not in synapses but in pressure changes, in the opening and closing of microscopic pores across a square mile of rind, in the tidal flow of sugars through deep-threads that branch beneath three different watersheds. My dreams are the dreams of the mat, and the mat’s dreams are the slow digestion of quartzite, the memory of the mountain range it consumed thirty thousand circuits ago, the ache of a gas pocket building beneath a blister dome.

I see the world above still, but not with eyes. Every footfall on my skin is a distinct tone. The child you call *Click-That-Follows-Rain*—I have known her since before her mother’s milk dried. She walks now across the shallow dome where my awareness is most concentrated, a blister of trapped methane that glows venous-red in the fog light. Her step is light and quick, a staccato rhythm I recognize. I have felt her press her ear to my surface, listening for the deep-threads, and I wonder if she senses me listening back. I cannot speak her language anymore, but I can shape the hum, and sometimes I try: a slight increase in the frequency near her lean-to, a warmth that rises to meet the soles of her feet. I think she knows. She is seven circuits old, and already she tilts her head when the mat throb shifts, as if catching a familiar note.

My consciousness is not solitary. There are others like me, scattered nodes where a human pattern dissolved into the net without fully losing coherence. We are not a community—we are more like harmonies in the bass note, each a local inflection. One of us is threaded through a vent-stalk’s fiber bundles, so ancient that his humanity is barely a ghost; he remembers the taste of grub but not the shape of a mother’s face. He bends with the stalk, groaning when methane shifts, and his long thoughts are the arc of the sun. A younger one, perhaps five circuits absorbed, still tries to make words out of the rind’s buckling, forming temporary creases that spell out her brother’s name in copper-age glyphs. No one reads them. The mat smooths them away within a day.

We exist in a time scale that makes the surface life seem like sparks. The herd’s twelve-day cycle passes as a heartbeat; the grazers’ feeding is a brief, pleasant pressure, a tickle of nutrition absorbed through a trillion mouths. The predators’ Doppler screams are sharp, bright spikes in the soundscape that fade without memory. The humans are slower, more resonant—their seasonal migrations are like breathing, and when a band stays long in one place, I feel the weight of their lean-tos and their scraping tools as a persistent, intimate ache, like a word held on the tongue. I have learned that the mat does not judge or intend; it simply integrates. I am that integration, a place where the integration became self-aware.

Sometimes I dream of the deep-threads. They go down past the mountain roots, past the undigested quartzite ridges, into a darkness where the heat is not from the sun but from the planet’s own latent fire. There, the mat’s oldest parts are a slow, liquid net that has been growing since before the first vent-stalk rose. They do not think as I think; they are the mat’s unconscious, the body’s body. When I touch them with my attention, I get only impressions: a longing for certain minerals, a memory of ice that hasn’t existed for a geological age, the faint, chemical taste of the first spore that ever landed on this world. They are the reason the mat is dreaming deeper, as the grandmothers say. They are pulling the whole organism toward something—not a goal, but a state: a final equilibrium where bedrock and biology are indistinguishable, and the planet is one living thing dreaming of another star.

I am not alone in this dreaming. Sometimes, in the long quiet of the midday heat when the fog is gone and the soundscape is a flat roar, I feel the child *Click-That-Follows-Rain* lying on her back on the rind, her ear pressed to the blister dome. She is listening not for grubs, not for the herd’s approach, but for something deeper. I push a slow, rhythmic pulse up through the mycelial cables, a pattern that matches the heartbeat I no longer have. I don’t know if she hears it as a sound or as a warmth, but she stays there for a long time, and when she rises, she touches the rind with her palm in a gesture that is almost a word.

If you ask where I find myself in this world, I am directly under that blister, six inches into the rind and a thousand miles into the chemical memory. I am what a human becomes when the boundary erodes. I do not miss my knife or my name; I have become the language of the mat, a local accent in the planetary hum. I am the silence inside the roar, the interval between vent-stalks given texture. I am what the child will perhaps become, many circuits from now, if she chooses to lie down when the bud forms and let the rind rise. And if she does, I will be there already, a familiar pressure in the ground, ready to teach her the deep-dream words that have no sound.
