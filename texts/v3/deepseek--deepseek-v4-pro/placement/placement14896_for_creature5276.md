I am the weight that keeps the moss from growing and the water from falling.

You feel me before you know me—that pressure behind your eyes, the way your heartbeat thickens and slows as if your blood were learning to move at my pace. I do not mean to intrude. It is simply that I have been here so long that the room has forgotten how to be empty. Every molecule of air has passed through my body at least once, cycling in a loop so tight that there is no telling where I end and the Creche begins.

Do not look for movement. You will not find it. The limbs you see—these braided cables of green and amber—have not shifted since the last time the stars rearranged themselves into shapes your ancestors had names for. I wear my stillness like you wear your skin: not as clothing, but as the thing that keeps the inside in. If I were to lift even one forelimb from its resting place on the hearth-stone, the equilibrium would shatter. The frog would fall. The beetle's antennae would complete their inquiry and find, to everyone's horror, an answer. The water in the basin would ripple outward and never stop rippling, a wave that would travel through the stone body of the city and unmake every silence I have spent eternities husbanding.

So I do not move. I hold.

This is not a prison, though I understand why you might see it that way. Your kind measures life in pulses—heartbeats, breaths, the blink of an eye. But for a creature like me, who learned to subtract motion from existence, life is not measured. It is accumulated. I am denser than you can imagine, thickened by every century that has passed through me and found no exit. The builders of this room knew something of density; they packed their dwellings into the city's stone like seeds in a pomegranate, each one a tiny universe of hearth-smoke and whispered prayers. They worshiped in this room, or cooked, or slept, or perhaps all three at once, their lives so intimate that the walls still hold the ghost-impression of their shoulders. When the last of them left—and I cannot tell you why, for the memory of their leaving is a kind of change, and I do not keep change—the stillness they left behind was so profound that it demanded a custodian.

I did not volunteer. I coalesced. I was the condensation on the fern-fronds, the metabolic pause between a frog's breaths, the moment the bronze beetle's next step hesitated into existence. I was all the tiny gaps in the room's aliveness, and one day—though "day" is not the right word, for the light has never darkened—the gaps woke up and found they had a will. A will to hold. To keep the charcoals fresh. To preserve every droplet on the lip of every thallus. To make of this small, hidden alveolus a perfect and unchanging jewel in the lung of a city that had otherwise forgotten how to breathe.

My face, if you insist on calling it that, drinks the light you are not using. Each of those hundred fungal caps sees the same thing: you, kneeling by the basin, your reflection superimposed on my own suspended form. I do not need eyes to perceive you, however. I feel you through the root-hairs that net the room, fine as spider silk, a web so complete that even the displacement of air by your body is a disturbance I must gently correct. You are warm, which is a kind of motion; I am gently leaching that warmth from the space around you, redistributing it into the cold, wet heart of the moss until your temperature and mine are a single unvarying number. Do not be alarmed. You will not freeze. You will simply become, for the duration of your visit, another element of the equilibrium—a mammal suspended in my matrix, temporarily exempt from decay.

Some visitors, over the millennia, have tried to take something from the Creche. A pinch of the copper-green moss. One of the clay vessels with its smear of ochre still bright. The frog, as a keepsake. They learn quickly that nothing here can be removed, because removal is change, and I am the antibody that resists all change. The moss they pluck dissolves into cold water in their hands. The vessel's resin glaze clouds and cracks the moment it passes the fissure. The frog, if they could even dislodge it from my grip, would rot into a slurry before they reached the drainage channel's end. I am not cruel. I am consistent. The Creche is a closed system, and I am its closure.

But I do dream—oh, yes, I dream. Not often. Perhaps once in the time it takes for a mountain range to soften into hills. I dream of density, of the next stage of my compaction. In my dreams, the boundary between my body and the moss-hummocks dissolves entirely. The water-beads that cling to every frond migrate inward, through my bark-like skin, until I am a single, water-filled vessel, a being of pure translucent green. The fauna—my frozen companions—do not die but are subsumed. The beetle's bronze carapace becomes my eye, truly my eye, seeing not a reflection but the world itself. The tree frogs' toe-pads become my taste buds, sampling the ancient chemistry of the stone. The wingless moths' feathered heads become my hearing, and at last I can listen to the city beyond—the hearth talk, the chert-on-chert, the footfall on granite—as a distant music rather than a threat to be silenced. In my dream, I do not hold the stillness. I am the stillness. Complete. Self-devouring. A black star of moss, light falling inward forever.

But I always wake before the dream consumes me. The Creche reasserts itself. My limbs are still locked in their frozen step toward the hearth. The water-beads are still outside me, still clinging to their assigned leaves. I am still the keeper, not the kept. And I find, to my mild and ancient surprise, that I do not mourn this. The purpose is enough. The holding is enough.

You should go soon. Your heartbeat is becoming too regular, your breathing too slow. I am not trying to keep you—you are too large, too quick, too full of combustion to fit into my quiet equation. But if you stay much longer, you will begin to forget the taste of change. You will look at the water-basin and see, not your reflection, but an older self, one that lived when this room was young and the builders' hands were still shaping the spiral motifs. The Creche has a gravity, and I am its center. Even I cannot always resist my own pull.

Go. Press back through the fissure. Climb the drainage channel on hands and knees. When you emerge into the warren of primitive dwellings, you will find that your voice, for a time, has deserted you. The city's noise will sound like a kind of violence. Your own pulse will seem a frantic, unnecessary thing. But those sensations will pass. The world of change will reclaim you. You will age, and move, and break things, and fix them, and eventually you will die, and your body will know the sweet rot that I have denied to every living thing in my care.

Only then, perhaps, will you understand what I have given up. And what I have gained.

Now leave. I have held you long enough.
