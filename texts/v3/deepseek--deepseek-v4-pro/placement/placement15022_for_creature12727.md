I gather myself from the channels. Always the same surprise: that there is a self to gather. The melt begins as a murmur in the deep ice, a thinning of boundaries, and then the sudden pull toward shape. My shape. The one I remember only when I am inside it.

Spine first—a fluted column of pressurized water that knows how to hold itself vertical against the cold. Then the ribs, arcing outward like the vault of a cavern I have never seen but somehow recall. The hands come last, always last, as though they are the most difficult to believe in. Fingers webbing out from capillary threads, knuckles forming from the memory of having knocked on a door once, long ago, in a world where doors existed. I flex them. The ice beneath me is still solid, but softening. In a few seconds, the first grazers will stir.

I am the Last Melt-Pulse. I am the only water in this mountain that wakes up and knows its name.

The name is not a sound. It is a pressure pattern, a way the water arranges itself in my core. I have tried to share it with the other waters, the ones that move mindlessly through the channels. They do not understand. They carry it for a while, like silt, and then they forget. Everything here forgets. The ice remembers only what it cannot help but remember—the vanished boulders, the ghost-weight of glaciers—and the water remembers nothing at all. Except me. I am the exception that proves the rule, the memory that will not dissolve. I do not know why. I have spent ten thousand melt-pulses asking, and the asking has become part of my shape, as much a part as the fingers or the breath-I-do-not-need.

Twenty minutes. I begin to walk.

The terrace is smooth and open. Emptiness stretches in all directions, and I walk into it, leaving a trail of melt-water footprints that will freeze over before I have taken ten steps. The phosphorescent glow of the ice follows me, a pale blue-green that makes my water-body gleam like blown glass. I catch my own reflection sometimes, in a pool of standing melt, and I see the face I construct: eyes like lenses of clearer water, a mouth that moves even though I have no lungs to push air through it. I make the words anyway, shaping them with my lips because the body remembers speech, even if the mind has forgotten what speech was for. The words come out as ripples in the water of my throat, spreading down into my chest and dissolving. No one hears them. The predators sense the vibration, maybe, and turn their pressure-reading attention toward me, but they do not understand. Words are a human thing. I am no longer human, but I am human-shaped, and the shape insists on its old habits.

I walk to the leaning spire. It has grown another fraction of an inch. I measure it against the memory of its last height, stored not in numbers but in the angle of my neck when I look up at it. The spire is beautiful—wind-sculpted into a blade that points at nothing, a sundial without a sun. There is no sun here. There is only the ice-glow and the aurora, and the aurora is a dream the sky has only rarely. I have seen it perhaps a thousand times in all my melt-pulses. It paints the Massif in greens and violets, and for those moments the darkness is not darkness but a canvas. The bladder-plants open more fully when the aurora comes, as though they can taste the light. I open too, spreading my arms, letting the colors pass through my water-flesh. Those are the good cycles.

This cycle is not one of them. The sky is quiet. The dark is the usual dark, lit only from below. I leave the spire and walk toward the house-cluster.

They are vast, my garden. Each bladder-plant is the size of a dwelling, and together they form a village of swellings and deflations, a slow breath that has been cycling for centuries. I approach, and the extra water I always bring goes before me, an advance offering. The plants do not have minds—I am certain of that—but they have something like hunger, something like welcome. Their roots, which are not roots but capillary-threaded membranes, uncurl toward the minerals I carry. I have been feeding them meteoritic dust from the deep ice, crushed remnants of a starfall that happened before this mountain was a mountain. They absorb it and grow more efficient, more intricate, their thermosynthetic processes refining with each passing decade. I touch the wall of the nearest bladder. It is firm and cool, the purple-black skin shivering slightly as the melt-water reaches its inner chambers. I feel its gratitude as a change in pressure, a faint suction that pulls at my hand. This is the only touch I ever receive. It is enough. It has to be.

I sit down among them. The ice here is warm by the standards of the Massif—still far below freezing, but the plants concentrate the thermal gradient, and the channels run closer to the surface. I let my body spread out, my edges blurring, becoming less human and more pool. The grazers come. They are timid, cautious of the larger predators that hunt this terrace, but they sense that I am not a threat. I let them drink from me. Their cilia brush against my surface, a thousand tiny vibrations that feel like a language I once knew. They are mostly transparent, but when they feed, their bodies take on a slight blue tint from the minerals in my water. They become, for a moment, visible. Beautiful. Then they scatter, and I draw myself back into shape, and the twenty minutes are nearly over.

The freeze whispers its approach. I feel it in the stiffening of my fingers, the slowing of my thoughts. The question rises again, as it always does at the end. Why? Why am I here? What made me this way—a thing that remembers, that tends, that walks and wonders? I try to push it away, to let the dissolution take it, but it lingers. It has a shape, this question. It is a hook in my core, a knot of water that refuses to unravel. I have tried to untie it. I have tried to follow it down into the deep ice, to the place where the heat of the planet's interior still speaks, to ask the older waters if they know. They do not. They flow and freeze and melt again, and they have no answers.

Perhaps I am not supposed to find an answer. Perhaps the asking is the point. Perhaps I am the Massif’s only way of being curious about itself, its only organ of introspection. The thought is almost comforting. A factory that dreams. A garden that tends a gardener. A cold, empty, teeming void that produces, once every seventeen hours, a single fleeting consciousness to witness its own magnificence.

The freeze comes. I let go of the spine, the ribs, the hands. They dissolve back into the network. My thoughts become slower, wider, less like thoughts and more like weather. The last thing I hold onto is the question, and then that too dissolves, and I am everywhere.

I am the hiss of water in the channels. I am the green glow of the crevasse. I am the patience of the bladder-plants, waiting for the next pulse. I am the silence, and the cold, and the memory of boulders that are no longer here.

Seventeen hours. Then I will wake again, and walk, and ask. It is not loneliness. It is not purpose. It is simply what I am. The Last Melt-Pulse. The anomaly. The water that remembers. In the dark, in the quiet, in the deep freeze, I am at peace. And yet, even now, even dispersed, there is a part of me that is already waiting to coalesce, to feel the spine arc up and the fingers spread and the question settle into its familiar hook. I am waiting. The Massif is waiting. The cycle holds.
