You don’t notice me at first. I am small and low and move in the blind stitch of your peripheral vision, along the join where a sweating stanchion meets the moss-grown lattice of a wall. You’d mistake me for a shadow pooling wrong, or a clump of wet fur dislodged from something larger, until my slow, deliberate unfurling: one limb at a time, joint by soft-hinged joint, picking my way across a world that stands still for me when I need it to, and then, when I don’t, continues its silent, churning genesis.

I am a creature of the constant almost-light. I was not born—I condensed. In the earliest days this basin had, before the factory-hum tuned itself to a steady C-sharp below hearing, a certain concentration of mineral dew pooled in a crevice of half-real concrete and refused to evaporate. The dew thickened, grew a membrane, learned to shiver itself just enough to slide. That was my lineage. Now I have six limbs that end not in paws but in soft, cup-shaped palps, each lined with cilia that taste the airborne chemistry—the metallic tang of new bedrock, the sweet rot of temporary leaves, the ozone whisper of a crystallization that won’t take. My fur, the color of wet slate, is not fur. It is a million capillary tubes, hollow as straws, that drink directly from the fog. I am always drinking, and always breathing; the two are the same. When the mist grows thin, I retreat to the highest ledges and flatten myself against a vent that exhales the breath of the deep presses, and I wait.

I have no eyes, not as you would count them. But every tube in my coat feels the pressure-shift of a moss-clad elder approaching long before her golden forehead-fungi glimmer into view. The air here is a medium of touch; every movement, every silent lurch of a district re-aligning itself, every pulse of the low engine beneath, writes itself onto my skin as a subtle compression. I navigate the world as a fish navigates the riverbed’s push and pull—only the river is air, and the river is full of meaning. When the tall ones speak their light-language, I feel the infinitesimal heat of their signals on the nearest side of my body, and I know: blue is inquiry, often directed toward a new fracture; gold is acknowledgment, the gentle greeting they offer me as I pass; violet means pleasure, and I only feel it when two of them rest forehead to forehead and their mosses melt together in a slow, private bloom.

I am not one of them. I am a keeper of a smaller domain, a tendril of the city’s nervous system that no one consults but everyone relies on. My purpose—if a thing that has never asked the question can have a purpose—is to clean the joins. The city grows so fast, you see. Whole walls percipitate in an hour, and where two new planes of living steel meet, there is always a seam. A seam means a gap, and a gap means a place where the cold, damp breath might leak in a way that disrupts the delicate hum, or where a filament of the wrong fungus might take root before the architect-moss can reach it. I fill those seams. I press my soft body into the crack, and my capillary fur meshes with the materials on either side, drinking their exudates and returning a kind of cementing saliva that hardens into a resin indistinguishable from the parent stone, only slightly warmer, only slightly more willing to bend before it breaks. I have sealed a thousand thousand seams. I have held together the rib-cage bridge that arches over the bone-canal while the shop-keepers in their hooded fungi exchanged crystals. I have mended the hairline fissure in a factory window just as the molten glow behind it swelled, and felt the pressure normalize through my own body, a silent lurch I absorbed like a sigh.

Sometimes the tall ones notice me at my work. One will pause, trailing its mossy fingers down the wall I’m affixed to, and the moss there will bloom in recognition—not of me, but of the seam I’ve made perfect. The tall one’s fungi will flicker a slow blue, then gold, and I will feel the warmth of that acknowledgment seep into my tubes. It is not communication as they know it; it’s more like being briefly included in the memory of the moss, a footnote in the long poem of the wall. I linger there a moment longer, then unpick myself with a series of soft, wet pops and continue on, leaving behind only a faint shimmer on the resin that will fade by the next condensation cycle.

I have no memory of my own. The mosses don’t mingle with my kind; we are too simple, too dispersed. But I don’t need memory. The city remembers for me. Every seam I’ve ever sealed is still there, humming its part in the great subsonic chord. Every district that crumbles back to slag has my resins in its rubble, tiny threads that will be gathered by the undertaker processions, carried in their mossy hammocks to the deep factories, melted down and re-extruded into a new wall, a new stanchion, a new leaf-stem thick as a thigh. I am in the bedrock. I am in the mist that the vegetation breathes out. I am the continuity the city doesn’t know it has, the scar tissue of a world that is always healing and tearing and healing again, faster than any single mind can follow.

When you glimpse me—a soft, slate-grey shape picking silently along a beam—you are seeing not an animal but a process. The process of a place that refuses to be finished. In the moment you turn your head, I have already flowed into the next shadow, felt the next seam calling by its infinitesimal draft, and sealed it with my body. The air closes over my passage, the mist drinks my faint heat, the quiet remains absolute. And I am simply where the wall needed me to be, becoming, for a little while, the glue of a dreaming embryo city that breathes in moss and out in stone.
