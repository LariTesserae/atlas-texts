She was not always here. There are memories she does not access often, sedimentary layers in her mind like the construction strata in the walls. She remembers warmth, or thinks she does—a different kind of warmth than the updraft from below, something that came from bodies pressed close. She remembers voices, though she cannot recall the words. She remembers a time when her arms had only three joints and her skin was soft and she felt cold. That last memory is the strangest to her now: the idea that she once *felt* cold, rather than simply registering its presence as data.

She came to the shaft by accident, or by design—the distinction blurs. There was a collapse, a sealing-off, a period of darkness and hunger that reshaped her. When the way opened again, she was different, and the world she found was this one: vertical, wet, slow, alive. She stayed because staying was easier than leaving, and then staying became the only thing that made sense.

Now she tends. Not because the city needs her—the constructs are more efficient, more numerous, more purpose-built—but because tending is what one does in a place like this. She checks the hexagonal cells with her fingertips, feeling for the faint warmth of active metabolism. She clears debris from the condensation channels, small accumulations of mineral scale and shedded construct components. She hums.

The humming is not music exactly. It is a frequency she discovered by accident, a pitch that resonates with the struts in a way that loosens the mineral buildup, that seems to stimulate the dormant bioreactor cells, that the constructs respond to by tilting their blind heads. She does not know why it works. She does not need to know. The humming has become her name, her purpose, her only real communication with the shaft itself.

She does not think about the surface. She is not sure there is a surface anymore, or that she would recognize it if there were. The city is all vertical now, all interior, all wet breath and slow pulse. She is high in its body, in a forgotten alveolus, and the shaft extends in both directions beyond her knowing. Sometimes, in the quietest moments between the thermal contractions and the droplet-fall, she presses her ear to the wall and listens for other hummers in distant shafts. She has never heard one.

But she does not feel lonely. Loneliness is a concept from a world with other people. Here, there is only the water cycle, the constructs on their rounds, the bioreactor cells pulsing with their season-long breaths, and the woman who tends them, humming her single note into the cold wet dark.
