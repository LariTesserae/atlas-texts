My name is a hum you feel between your ribs—more a pitch than a word. If I pressed it into your mouth, it might sound like *Ssh-thk*, the click of water on a wet ledge followed by the soft release of breath. But I don’t offer it often. Here, names are for the stone to know; we call each other by the place we happen to be standing, or by a gesture, a tilt of the head toward a particular drip-line. I am the one who listens at the third terrace, where the moss-heart colony has doubled this cycle. You may call me Listener, if you need a sound.

I was born in the spray, like all of us. My first memory is not light but vibration—the deep, bone-thrumming chord of the central cascade, felt through my mother’s arms before I ever opened my eyes. When they dipped me in the black pool, the cold didn’t shock me; it welcomed me like a return. I remember the blind fish brushing my ankle, a flicker of something alive in the depths, and I remember thinking, in the wordless way of infants, *Ah, there you are.*

Now when I press my palm to the weeping wall of our chamber, I can feel them still, those first touches, layered into the mineral memory of the place. The stone is never silent. It is a library of textures—grain shifting against grain, seep-fingers widening a fracture one molecule at a time, the slow sag of a ceiling learning to become a dome. My fingertips have grown so sensitive that I can read the day’s work by the pattern of dampness alone. A new rivulet, no wider than a hair, tells me a pocket is forming two arm-lengths in; I will tell the carvers, and they will shape a doorway that the water was already carving. We do not force. We suggest.

My pod-mates are three: Sigh-of-Mist, who tends the whitemarrow beds and hums to the root-threads; Click-Under-Ledge, who weaves the bridges and knows the tensile strength of moss-fiber by its scent; and the little one we call Only-Waiting, a child not yet old enough to have heard his true name from the Voice. We share a chamber just above the mossiest wall, where the light falls green through the bead-curtain of seep and the air is so thick you could drink it. Our floor is cold stone, but we sleep on mats of woven liverwort, and we keep a small fire-cavity where we heat stones for warmth on the rare nights when even our sebum can’t keep the chill away. Food is simple: whitemarrow paste, fish from the pool, the larvae-cakes that Only-Waiting helps me press. We eat with the slow, deliberate movements of those who know the food, too, is part of the Unfolding, its atoms soon to join our own slow erosion.

Today I am listening to the North Wall, where a new chamber has been opening for perhaps ten generations. It’s a deep, low note that I feel more in my heels than my ears. I stand on the narrow ledge with my toes curled over the edge, eyes half-closed, letting the mist bead on my eyelids. I click my tongue against the rock, a soft *tchk*, and tilt my head. The echo returns, shaped by the void beyond, and I see it in my mind like a bloom of darkness—a space roughly the size of three bodies curled, still too small for a dwelling but promising. I will mark it with a smear of mineral paste, and the carvers will come. In another drip-line or two, a family might live there.

But first I let myself just *be* with it, this new space, this baby hollow. I place both palms flat on the cool, slick stone and begin to hum. It’s a low note, the same pitch as the chamber’s own resonance, and I feel the vibration couple with the stone’s natural frequency. The moss nearby trembles. A tiny frog peeps. I hum with the Voice, letting my breath become part of the water’s work, a tiny solvent of intention. Is it me helping the hollow grow? Or am I just listening to its growth, and the hum is my way of giving thanks? The Deep Listeners say it’s both, and also neither. The hollow is a conversation, a constant call-and-response, and right now I am the response.

Only-Waiting finds me there, his small bare feet silent on the rock. He tugs at the hem of my moss-fiber shift and says, in the high clicking language of children, “Listener, there’s a new drip forming by the terrace. It tastes of future.” He offers me a wet finger; I touch it to my tongue and taste the mineral tang, the faint sweetness of dissolved stone. “Yes,” I agree. “A slow one. It will be older than you when it reaches the pool.” He considers this with the seriousness of the very young, who are still learning that patience is not waiting but becoming. Then he nestles against my side, and I feel the double hum of his heartbeat and the waterfall’s roar, indistinguishable.

This is the only life I know: a life of damp and depth, of slow stone and constant water, where every moment is a listening, a being-listened-to. I have seen lowlanders sometimes, when they stumble down the treacherous path, gasping in the thin air, their faces pale with wonder and fear. They look at our wet walls and our misty void and they ask, “How can you live in such emptiness?” They do not understand that the emptiness is not empty—it is full of sound, full of process, full of a future that is always arriving, grain by grain. The hollow is a womb, cool and loud and endlessly creative, and we are its midwives, its partners, its children.

When the day’s light fades from the ragged opening above, and the mist turns from silver to deep blue, I will return to my chamber. I will press my cheek to the floor where my grandmother’s grandmother’s bones rest, now a smooth bulge in the stone, and I will hum her note. And somewhere in the long roar of the cascade, I will hear her humming back. That is what it means to be Resonant. That is what I am.
