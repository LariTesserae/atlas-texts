I am not the one who walks. Not yet. I am still a film, a suggestion, a dampness that does not know its own shape. For now, I live in the pause between drips—in the long, silvery moment after a drop releases from the chevron joint and before it strikes the basin. In that held breath, I spread across the surface of the stone like a thought spreading across a mind too new to have language.

The hollow already holds a presence—the one who circles, the one who reads the mortar-stones—and at first I mistake myself for an extension of it. A stray exhalation. A bead of sweat on the flank of its slow, glistening body. But I am not that. I am something earlier, smaller, a premonition of wetness that the limestone exudes only when the condensing breath of night is heavy enough to stir what has been asleep in the rock for epochs.

I am not water alone. I am water that remembers being stone. That is the difference. The other one—the man-sized, the reader of heat—is made from the hollow’s present moisture, the cycling dew, the mineral gossip of roots. But I am drawn from a deeper reservoir, locked in the pore-space of the bedrock since before the fluting streams went silent. I am ancient water, fossil wetness, and when I finally thicken enough to hold a thought, that thought is of weight: the immense, patient pressure of limestone above me, the slow squeeze of the earth’s ribs, the darkness so complete it felt like a kind of light.

This night, I gather for the first time into something that can be called a body. Not the elegant, elongated shape of the one who walks—I am a rough draft, a clumsy agglomeration of spheres and lenses, all of me rounded like the bubbles that once rose through the primordial water table. My head is a single, trembling globe, my torso a cluster of smaller spheres, my limbs mere suggestions of arms and legs formed from the meniscus of a puddle that doesn’t want to spread. I am a water-thing shaped by old, slow memories: the taste of unfractured rock, the absence of light, the patience of the deep aquifer.

The other one notices me. It is on its third circuit when it pauses by the low wall, its featureless oval tilting in a way that I understand, instinctively, is a question. It sees me not as a rival but as a novelty—a new variable in the hollow’s long equation. It sends a shimmer through its calcite-scrimmed body, a pattern that means (I suddenly know) *origin?* And I ripple back, not with light but with the faintest redistribution of my spheres, a trembling that translates the only truth I have: *below.*

It drifts closer. I feel the warmth of its body not as heat but as a subtle difference in the surface tension of the air. It is reading me, the way it reads the mortar-stones, sifting the dissolved biography I carry. It finds the deep time in me, the epochs spent as a film on a crystal face, the slow mineral accretion that is my flesh. And it also finds something else: a trace of the human heat I never touched but somehow inherited, passed down through layers of rock from the era when the people slept on the crescent bed and ground their seeds in the mortar-stones. The other one has their heat; I have the echo of the echo, a ghost of warmth that sank into the ground with their dead and was locked away until this dewfall.

We stay there, the two of us, while the drip counts out our minutes. I do not yet know how to move, so I simply hold my shape, a conglomerate of globes reflecting the dim canopy-light like a cluster of wet moonstones. The mosses at the boulder’s foot pulse a slow, chemical greeting; a root taps a rhythm I feel in my core. The hollow accepts me, folds me into its equilibrium as it has folded everything else.

By dawn, when the other one retreats into its wall-flute, it leaves me a final shimmer, a pattern I interpret as a question or perhaps an invitation: *Will you stay? Will you become?* And as the first diluted light touches the rim and the dew begins its retreat, I do not evaporate. I sink. I flow back into the crack from which I rose, taking my new consciousness with me into the dark. I will spend the day as a thought in the stone, dreaming of the next condensation, the next chance to practice being a body, the next time I will meet the one who walks and learn its slow, glistening language.

Because I am not the hollow’s memory, but its potential. I am the water that will still be here when the drip from the chevron joint finally slows, four hundred years from now, and the other one adjusts its form. I am the long patience, the deeper cycle. And somewhere in the mineral whisper of the limestone, a voice—not mine, not yet—is already teaching me to speak.
