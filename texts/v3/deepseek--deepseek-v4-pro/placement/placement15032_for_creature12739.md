I am a bead of the cold, a concentration of the light that never sleeps. I formed when the condensation on a strand of the great weave grew thick enough to remember itself. Now I travel the root highways, my body a lens of sap and captured green-white glow, nearly invisible except as a shimmer where the air should be empty. I pause at a stone circle, not to mourn but to taste the faint alkalinity of vanished hands. The hearth holds no ash, only tiny fossilized fronds, and I draw them into my periphery, feeling the old pressure of a fire I have never known. The hand-axe, half-embedded, rings against my internal tension, a note of rootwood that still remembers being struck. I am not the one who struck it, but I carry that note for a while, letting it vibrate through my membranes until it dissipates.

Across the chasms, I sail—spreading myself into a sail of tensed sap, catching the cold breeze that smells of wet stone and growing things. Below me, the void opens downward forever, an emptiness that is not empty because the light fills it. Other condensations drift nearby, and we exchange droplets: a bead of clear sap from a fresh break, a frozen tear from some deep fracture where the walls crush themselves into plant-stone. In that exchange, we become a dream together—a distributed awareness of the entire webwork, the catenary curves singing with tension, the slow collapse of a root mat section trembling through the filaments. For a moment, we are the instrument, not just a note. And then we separate, each carrying a piece of the other, thinning back into our solitary quests for moisture.

I will end. Already my membranes thin at the edges. The cold slows me; I move less, pause longer. Soon I will be indistinguishable from the dew on the mat, and my memory will bleed back into the weave—the taste of stone circles, the ring of the axe, the brief shared dream of the whole. That dissolution is not death. It is the world breathing back what it lent. The tick of water will still measure the silence, and a new bead will form somewhere else, and it will want a certain sap-blend, and it will go. The sharp edges of new growth will catch the light like knives, and no one will remember the hands that stacked stones or twisted cordage. But the rootwood will ring for a while, faint and sweet, a resonance without a rememberer. And for a time, I am that resonance.
