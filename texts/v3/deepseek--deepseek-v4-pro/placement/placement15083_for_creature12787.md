I am small.

That is the first thing I know, and the last, and the only truth that persists between the pulses of sleep and waking. Small, and wet, and clinging to the world with the whole of my skin. The air is a second skin, thicker than my own, and I breathe it in through my back, my belly, the soft pads of my toes. When I first uncurl from the dream of the egg, there is no light to greet me—only a directionless green-black that presses close, and the sound. The sound is everything. It is the first thing I learn to distinguish from myself: the endless, many-voiced roar that is the forest’s breath. At first it is a single tremor, a drone that vibrates my bones. But slowly, as the days pass (and I learn to count days by the subtle cooling when the fog rises, the faint sweetening of the air when the canopy somewhere far above exhales), I begin to pick apart the threads.

The closest thread is the hum of my mother’s body. I ride in the cleft behind her foreleg, a warm, soft-walled pocket where her hide folds over itself, lined with a moss finer than any I will ever find elsewhere. That moss is not just bedding; it is part of her, its rhizoids threading into the mycelial net that laces her skin. At night—if it is night—it glows faintly, a blue-green constellation mapped onto the terrain of her flesh. I press my belly to it and feel the slow, deep rumble of her thought, the subsonic language I cannot yet understand but already love. It is the bass note under all other sounds, the foundation. When she moves, I move with her, a gentle, inexorable tide that rocks me in place. I do not yet know that she is moving; I think the world sways for me alone.

The other voices come later. The piping of the frogs, so high and insistent, becomes individual: I learn to pick out the one who lives in the bromeliad just above our resting cleft, a tiny male with a voice like a silver needle. He sings every few heartbeats, and his song is a claim. I come to understand that he is telling others: *Here I am, this pool is mine, my tadpoles will swim here.* I cannot see him—my eyes are only just beginning to sense the difference between the dark and the deeper dark—but I can feel the vibration of his call through the air and through my mother’s hide, a double signal. The insects are a continuous hiss, a wash of static that underlies everything, but within it I begin to detect rhythms: the pulsing drill of a beetle larva chewing its gallery in a fallen branch, the syncopated rattle of termite soldiers tapping their heads against the walls of their mound in alarm or communication. One day I will learn that the mound we are passing—a vast, crenellated shape that blocks the faint upwelling of fungal light—is a city, full of individuals whose lives are as intricate as my own. But that understanding is far off.

For now, I am mouth and hunger. I do not suckle; there is no teat. Instead, when hunger rises, I press my soft, lobed mouth to the skin of my mother’s leg and sip. The moisture that beads there is rich, slightly sweet, thick with the mycelial threads that will seed my own body’s garden. It is not milk but a kind of living broth, and it tastes of the forest’s deep chemistry: the minerals of the ant-built soil, the sugars leaked from orchid roots, the nitrogen of a bird’s fallen feather already digested by fungi. As I drink, I feel the first tiny threads take root in my own skin, a faint itching that is also a promise. One day, my back will be a meadow.

Time passes differently for my mother. Her thoughts are slow, so slow that a single concept might take the whole of my waking period to complete. But I am already learning to listen with my whole body. When she rumbles a long, descending note, I feel it in my chest and know it means *rest*. When the rumble rises and splits into two tones, I know it is *moving*. Sometimes, at the edge of my perception, I sense her listening to others—distant vibrations through the ground, the songs of her kin miles away. I try to hum along, a tiny, high-pitched imitation that makes her lobes curl in what I will later understand as amusement. But for now, it is just the sound of being together.

I am not the only one in this cleft. There are others: a pale, blind shrimp-like creature that lives in the pool of water that collects in the crevice below me, feeding on the flakes of skin my mother sheds. There is a spider that spins her web across the entrance of our pocket, catching the tiny midges that are drawn to the glow of the mycelium. We ignore each other, or rather, we acknowledge each other as part of the architecture. They are not company; they are the place. I am learning that I am the place, too.

When I am old enough—though I have no words for age, only a sense that my limbs are stronger and the cleft feels smaller—I venture out. Not far; never far from the vast, reassuring warmth of her side. But I climb from her leg onto the ground, and for the first time I feel the woven springiness of the world under my own feet. My toes spread, the root-like pads sinking into the moss. The ground is alive, and it speaks back. Beneath me, I feel the endless churning of the smaller ones: the earthworms, the millipedes, the ants. I feel the fine, lace-like network of the mycelium that connects everything to everything else. It is like pressing my ear to a conversation so vast it has no edges.

I take my first steps. They are not steps so much as a slow flow, an inherited muscle-memory I didn’t know I had. My body is a smaller version of hers: six stubby limbs, a low-slung torso already fuzzed with the first mossy growth. I am the size of a frog, then a rat, then, slowly, a hog. By the time I am the size of a hog, I have learned to taste the air with my lobes, to sip the fog, to feel the vibrations of a distant fallen tree as a tickle in my bones. I have learned the sounds of the frogs that mean *rain coming* and the sounds that mean *snake near*. I have learned that the deep, slow rumble that answers my mother’s call is my other parent, the one who merged with her long ago and then drifted on, carrying her mycelial strain across the plateau.

And I have learned that I am not just one creature. The moss on my back has its own inhabitants: a colony of tiny water bears that swim in the droplets caught in my fuzz, a particular species of fungus that only grows on my kind. When I scratch against a tree bole to ease an itch, I leave behind a trail of spores and tiny lives. I am a walking ecosystem, a child of this woven world. The forest is not around me; I am the forest, in miniature, moving slow.

One day—and I know it is coming, feel it in the changing tenor of my mother’s vibrations—I will drift away. Not a breaking, but a gradual widening of the distance between our bodies. She will rumble a final low note, a note that means *go*, and I will turn my head (still without eyes, but with a full awareness of her warmth receding) and set out on my own circuit. I will carry the memory of her rumble with me always, stored in the mycelial net that is my second brain. And as I go, I will begin to speak my own long thoughts into the ground, adding my voice to the great roar.

But that is yet to happen. For now, I press myself against her leg, feeling the slow tide of her movement, and I listen. The frogs are piping. The insects are drilling. The birds are warbling somewhere high above. And beneath it all, the basso continuo: *here, still here, always here*. I hum along, a small new note in the ancient song, and I am not afraid. I am small, and I am part of it. That is enough.
