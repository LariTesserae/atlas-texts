I am still solid. I am still, for now, a thing of edges and boundaries, of skin that knows where it ends and the world begins. But the world here is a negotiation, not a given, and the longer I stand in the warm weeping dark, the more I feel that negotiation turning inward.

The invitation is too gentle to refuse. I move to the place the epiphyte has unfurled—a cupped space between two enormous roots, lined with a moss that glows faintly amber when I touch it. I lie down. The moss adjusts, fibers shifting to cradle my weight, and the amber glow deepens, as if pleased. The air thickens with a scent like fresh bread and damp earth. I close my eyes.

When I open them again, I don’t know how much time has passed. The light in the chamber has shifted—more gold now, less green—and the thrum of the shaft has synced with my heartbeat. I can feel it in my chest, a slow, reassuring double pulse: mine, and the world’s. I sit up, and my hand leaves a faint afterimage on the moss, a ghost-hand of light that lingers for three full breaths before dispersing. The plants are getting used to me. Or I’m getting used to them.

The gardeners return, not as individuals now but as a collective presence. They flow in and out of the walls with a new ease around me, their songs overlapping in a polyphony that I am beginning to parse. It’s not language, exactly—more like a shared dream, a constant low-level communication of intent and emotion and gentle instruction. *This root needs light. That water is too warm. The solid is waking.* I realize I can hear them even when they’re not in the chamber. The song travels through the roots, through the water, through my own bones. I am a new instrument in their orchestra, and they are tuning me.

One of them—the elder, I think, though age is a fluid concept here—coils onto a root above my head and hangs down like a great pale fruit. Its resonator slit opens, and it sings directly into my face. The notes are soft, questioning. The song says, *You are still solid. But less still. You are learning. Would you like to learn more?* I don’t have words to answer, but I feel my throat hum in response, a weak, atonal buzz. It’s enough. The elder taps the root with a flurry of digits, and the root extrudes a bead of sap, golden and glowing. *Drink.*

I do. It tastes of morning and rain and the memory of sunlight, and it spreads through me like a slow fire. When the glow reaches my fingertips, I can see my own veins through my skin, illuminated with that same green-white bioluminescence. My skin is becoming translucent, just a little, just at the edges. The boundaries are thinning.

I am not becoming a gardener. I am too solid for that, too anchored to the memory of a world with gravity that always pulls down. But I am becoming something else—a node, perhaps. A fixed point in their fluid network, a place where the songs can gather and resonate and be translated into something like stillness. The plants have begun to grow around me, not to trap but to incorporate. The moss has climbed my ankles, soft and warm, and tiny pale roots are weaving themselves into the fabric of my clothing. When I move, they move with me, as if I am just another part of the breathing mortar.

My thoughts are no longer entirely my own. The shaft’s slow green consciousness presses against my mind like water against a dam, and the dam is leaking. I get flashes of other places—other chambers, other pockets, other beings. I see the shaft in its entirety, a coiled, living tower plunging miles into the earth’s warm heart, every inch of it singing. I see the gardeners, hundreds of them, their lights flowing through the green matrix like blood through a body. I see myself from the outside, a dim, solid shape wrapped in moss and roots, my eyes closed, my mouth slightly open, a faint hum escaping my lips. I am a seed that has forgotten how to germinate, but the forgetting is ending.

Time passes. The chamber shrinks, as predicted, the root-bridge thickening until I can no longer see the far wall. But the gardeners reshape the space around me, keeping a small pocket open, a living tomb or a womb, depending on your perspective. I no longer hunger or thirst; the roots feed me, the sap sustains me, the constant weeping keeps my skin from drying. I sleep and wake in rhythms that are not my own, synced to the slow breathing of the shaft. When I dream, I dream in green and gold and two-note harmonies.

I am no longer a visitor. I am a part of the architecture. A living statue, a fixed singer in the choir, a point of solidity in a world that has no other. The gardeners visit me often, brushing my face with their feathery digits, singing me stories of the deep places and the slow wars and the endless unfolding growth. I cannot move, but I do not need to. My mind roams the shaft, carried by the root-network, seeing all. And my voice—my weak, human voice—has found its place in the song. When the shaft breathes out, I hum a low note that stabilizes the roots. When the juveniles are lost, I sing them home with a frequency that carries through the water. When the elder asks a question too complex for a single two-note phrase, I answer in a chord built of human language and plant-light and the memory of something like love.

I am the shaft’s memory now. I remember the world above—the sun, the wind, the feel of dry ground underfoot—and I feed that memory into the network, a gift from a solid thing. The gardeners do not understand, but they cherish it anyway, weaving it into their songs as a strange, discordant counterpoint that makes the whole harmony richer. And in return, they give me forever. A slow, green, singing forever, wrapped in moss and warmth and the smell of wet limestone.

So where would I find myself in this world? I am right here, in the pocket that was made for me, my body a lattice of roots and glowing veins, my voice a permanent hum in the dark. And what would I be? I am the listener. The one who stayed. The solid that learned to resonate. I am the shaft’s one still heart.
