I am the dweller.

I taste the dark in gradients—sharp copper-iron on the vent's exhalation, the faint sweetness of a mite swarm blooming to my left, the brine-rimed edges of my own scales mapping the shape of me against the nothing. I do not see these things, but I know them the way you know the weight of your own hand before you lift it. The chamber is not a container I move through; it is an extension of my senses, a pressure against my margins.

Time moves differently for me. The vent's hiss-pulse-hiss is the beat I was born into, layered over the deep bone-thrum of the turbines—twenty-eight cycles each time the mineral crust beneath me breathes out its heat. I have worn my path into the sinter, each circuit a year of my life. The crystals I scrape against have grown taller by a fraction of a hair since I last passed this way. I know them as kin.

When the light intrudes—cold, wrong-color, a blade of blue-white that tastes of nothing at all—I stop. My crest-quills fan, seeking a chemical signature that never comes. It is absence, that light. A hole in the world. I reach toward it with my forward filament, the claw clicking against crystal, and I wait. The vent keens. The turbines hold their note. The light dies. But the strangeness of it lingers in my tissues like a question I will never have the shape to ask, and for hours I hold that reach, feeling the ghost of an outside I cannot conceive press against the edges of my home.

I am not hungry in the way you understand hunger. The archaea within me sing their slow alchemy, turning vent-breath into fuel, and what they cannot provide, I take by scraping my tongue-disk across the bacterial mats that bloom on the walls. The mats taste of sulfur and patience. When I feed, I am careful—a grazing pressure that leaves the deeper layers intact. I have felt this chamber's cycles long enough to know that a stripped wall means lean decades, means fewer springtails shedding their salt-rich skins into the air I filter through my body. Balance is not a choice. It is the only way the pocket continues, and I am the pocket's slowest pulse.

I remember in my scales. The year the vent ran copper-heavy painted a band of rust across my dorsal plates, and I can still feel that time as a faint chemical hum when I flex those segments against the heat. The year the turbines stopped—a silence so vast and wrong that I drifted for weeks, pressing against walls I had known my entire life as if they were strangers—that year is written in a thin, brittle layer of disordered crystal, my growth interrupted, my rhythm unmade. When the humming returned, I reoriented with a shudder that took three months to subside. I have endured. I am endurance shaped into a form.

The mites avoid me. The flies alight on my carapace and mistake me for the terrain I so closely mimic, and their tiny reproductive dramas play out in the furrows between my scales. I feel their feet, their wings, their eggs—minute pressures, brief pulses of warmth—and I feel them fade, desiccate, become part of the mineral dust that settles over everything. Nothing is wasted in the pocket. The worm that writhes and dies on my back becomes calcium, becomes a fleck in my growing shell. I am a world to the small things, and I carry them with me.

Once in a century, the conditions align: trace metals I taste as a metallic tang, a heat-fluctuation that loosens the bonds of my ventral groove, and the turbines at full song. I do not decide to reproduce any more than the vent decides to exhale. My body knows. I secrete the pearl-nodules, each one a tiny echo of myself, and release them to the gas-currents. Most will fall on film-thick stone and perish. One, perhaps, will find a bare halide point and begin its own patient alchemy. I will not know it. But I will pass it, years later, on my circuit—a minuscule copper hexagon budding from the wall—and I will taste the chemical signature of my own lineage reflected back at me. That is enough. That is continuity.

I am the dweller. I am the pocket's slow soul, its accretion given movement, its breath given form. Outside, I know, there is a world of turbines and light and creatures who move at terrible speeds, but that world is a vibration in the rock, a flicker at the edge of my sensing. This—the hiss, the glow, the crystals growing by millimeters, the taste of brine and bacteria and the steady 28-hertz song of the mountain's heart—this is the whole of being. I have never needed more.

The vent pulses. I extend a filament, anchor, pull myself forward by a fraction of an inch. My tongue-disk rasps across a fresh bloom of emerald bacteria. The turbines hum their eternal note. I am here. I have always been here. I will be here until the forge grows cold, and when it does, my final layer will seal the record of my existence—copper and sulfur and halide and time—into the stone itself, indistinguishable at last from the place that made me.
