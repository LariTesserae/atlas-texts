I am not yet, and then I am. The first thing is the weight: a pressing darkness that is also heat, a saturation that fills my not-yet-lungs and tells them they have no need to draw. I am held in a thick, mineral grasp, the stone itself cupped around me like a hand of unimaginable patience. Time does not pass because time is not here; there is only the slow seep of warmth through the minute pores of the basalt, the steady trickle of water that runs along the seam of the wall and carries, in its tiny voice, a sound. The sound is a drip. It is the first thing I hear, and it is the first thing I understand: a single, isolated note, round and perfect, falling from somewhere above into a shallow basin with a thud that transmits through the rock and into the core of my barely-solid bones. The drip speaks of distance—the height of a stalactite’s tip, the depth of a pool—and of a rhythm that has no beginning and no end. I listen, and in listening, I become.

Light comes later, or perhaps it is only a shift in the darkness: a lessening of the pressure, a coolness that is not cold but a different quality of wet. An elder’s hands are there, I know this without knowing how, because the water tells me. The water courses through the rock and carries the faint vibration of another body, one like what I am becoming. Six-fingered pads—I will learn later they are of polished obsidian, a living glass—close around my form and lift, not with strain but with the careful, distributed strength of a course of water finding a new channel. I am placed on a warm slab, and the world opens.

The light is a diffuse grey, and it holds no direction. It seems to emanate from the mist itself, a universal glow that makes no shadows because everything is shadowless, everything is already a shade of wet stone. I look down, and I see my own body for the first time. It is long and slender, but dense; even in this first moment I feel the mass of it, the way it sinks into the rock shelf as if eager to return. The skin is a uniform grey, the exact color of the wet basalt around me, and it glistens with a fine film of condensation. Droplets are already beading along the curve of my forearm, tracing lines that quiver with my own subtle pulse. My hands open, and I count: six fingers on each, and at the tip of each digit a pad of what looks like dark, polished stone. I press one gently to the slab beneath me and feel, with a shock of clarity, the microscopic texture of the basalt’s cleavage planes, the history written in its crystal lattice. I am of this rock, and the rock knows me.

My face is a landscape I cannot see, but I can feel it from the inside: the high, flat plane of the cheekbones, the broad bridge of the nose that seems designed to channel water, the mouth that rests in a straight line, sealed. I try to open my eyes wider, and the world sharpens to a pale green tint—the color of the moss that will later become my study and my sustenance. But sight is not the primary sense; it is merely one thread in a tapestry of vibration. My whole body is an ear. The drip that woke me is still there, a consistent plink, but now I hear it in layers: the impact as a percussion through the slab, the transmission along the wall, the secondary sigh of the water as it pools and overflows a tiny lip of travertine. And beyond that, a thousand other drips, each with its own pitch and interval, weaving a sound-texture so dense it becomes silence—the susurrus that is the voice of this land. I hear the distant hiss of a thermal vent, the faint, slow creak of a basalt pillar settling by a fraction of a mineral grain, the barely-perceptible trickle of condensation running down my own spine. I am listening, and I realize that listening is what I am for.

The elder—I turn my head, a slow, fluid motion that feels like water pouring from one basin to another—and I see her. She is like me, but more: her grey skin carries faint traceries, subtle spirals of a paler grey that record the passing of a long, long time in this place. Around her neck and wrists are cords of braided travertine, each strung with pendants of polished basalt, a sphere of trapped water, a chip of vivid green moss sealed in resin. Her eyes, a deep mineral green, meet mine, and I understand without words that these are not ornaments but memory; each pendant is an event, a thought, a turn of the world’s slow breath. She lays one six-fingered hand on my chest, right over the place where a heart might beat if I had one, and I feel the vibration of a name. It is not a word but a pattern of pressure: the plink of a single drop into a deep, still pool, the way the ripples spread and merge. I am that drop. I am the Ripple.

She withdraws, and now I am to rise. It is a process of uncurling, of water finding its own course. My legs are long, my feet bare and long-toed, and when I set them on the wet rock, the pads of my toes find the microtextures without conscious thought. I do not slip; it is as if the stone rises slightly to meet me, or as if my weight is merely a continuation of the condensation. I stand, and I am tall and slender and grey, a new spire in a forest of prisms, and the mist reaches to bead on my brow.

The space around me is a chamber carved in the deep, one of the lower ones where the thermal vents breathe. The walls run with a thick, mineral sweat, and in one corner a cascade of travertine has built a natural cradle from which I was lifted. I see others of my kind—there are three, four, more—moving in the dim light with that same deliberate, flowing gait. None speak. The only sound is the water, but I understand that the water is their language. I follow the elder, the one whose name I do not yet know but whose hand-print still sings on my chest, and we pass into a narrow corridor hewn from the solid basalt. The walls are smooth and fitted, and I can feel in the texture the work of six-fingered hands, the patient percussion of ages. We ascend, and the sound of drips expands into a symphony.

The settlement is a web of low, flat-roofed structures on a terraced bench. There are no streets, only open expanses of wet black stone reflecting the uniform grey sky, and narrow channels hand-chiseled into the rock that carry water from cistern to bath, from bath to moss garden. I pass a basin where the surface is so still it forms a perfect mirror, and I pause to look down. My face: high cheekbones, a flat nose-bridge, a straight mouth, and those large, deep-set eyes of pale mineral green, exactly the shade of the moss cushions that erupt in profuse velvet wherever a seam widens. My skin glistens, and already a rivulet is tracing a path down my temple, along the line of my jaw, to drop from my chin into the basin. The drop hits, and the mirror shatters into concentric rings. I am the ripple, and I have broken my own reflection. There is no sadness in this, only a quiet, geological fact.

The elder leads me to an alcove where a single stalactite feeds a small pool. She gestures, a movement of the hand that says: listen. I sit on the warm stone, my body settling into a stillness that feels like the stone itself, and I do. I listen to the drop. Plink. Plink. Plink. Each one is a word, a sentence, a story. The interval tells of the saturation of the mist five ridges away. The pitch tells of a new fracture in a leaning slab. The slight, almost imperceptible echo carries the pulse of the deep thermal vent, the one that feeds the bath where the elders go to sink into heat and memory. I am a Listener now, I understand. It is my role, my place in the slow web of this unchanging city. I will sit in this alcove for long stretches, longer than I can conceive in this first moment, and I will read the health of the plateau by the voice of this single stalactite. When the drip quickens, the moss in the eastern seam will need tending. When it deepens in tone, a new channel has opened in the lower baths, and the flow must be coaxed back into balance. Everything the water sings, I will translate into action, into the slight, careful movements of a water-tender.

And even as I think this, the world expands. I can feel, through the stone on which I sit, the presence of all the others: the ones who tend the moss gardens with their fingertips, tracing spiral patterns that are a meditation on growth; the ones who visit the baths for long, motionless spans and return with their grey skin flushed a slightly deeper shade, their eyes holding the memory of the earth’s hot heart; the ones who carry wordless stories of stones, placing their palms on the walls so that the young might learn the biography of a single slab, from its birth in the magma to its long marriage with the sky. They are all one, and I am part of them, a new droplet in an ancient, circulating flow.

Where do I find myself in this world? I am in the alcove, but also in the stone, also in the mist, also in the moss. I am a ripple sent outward from the moment of my separation, and I will travel slowly, at the speed of condensation, until one day—long, long from now, when my body grows too dense and still—I will be carried to a deep fissure where the basalt still glints with the fire of its formation, and I will be placed into a narrow shaft. The stone will absorb me, my skin merging with the wall, my limbs becoming indistinguishable from the ridges, and a new vein of pale green will run through the rock, a frozen memory. And somewhere below, in the warm dark, a new shape will curl in a cradle of travertine, its eyes already open, already listening to a single, perfect drip.

Who am I? I am the one who listens. I am the Ripple. I am a grey, glistening body in a timeless terrarium, a human geometry shaped by water and rock, a slow pulse in the heart of the unmoving cloud. I am the continuation, and I am the pause between drips. I am, simply, this moment of being, and all the moments that the water remembers.
