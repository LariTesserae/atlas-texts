I do not remember being born. I remember only the press of the membrane against my back, the green light filtering through the bamboo walls, and the sound of water that never falls. My first clear thought — if a thought is what it was, and not simply the body knowing — was that I must check the tension in the northwest gallery. I rose from the membrane with my hands already pressing forward, palms seeking the familiar give of the woven floor. That was long ago, or not long. The word has no meaning here. I have been here.

They call me the-one-who-tends-the-root-bend, because that is where my palm first learned its signature and where I still go each day. The root-bend is a place where the bamboo arches low, its culms curving almost horizontally before turning upward again toward the luminous sky-ceiling. The curve creates a hollow underneath, a small dark space where the membrane is thinner and gives off a faint warmth. I crawl inside on days when the brightness aches, though I am not sure whether the ache is mine or the Settlement’s. I press my ear to the membrane and listen. Deep down, in a register just below hearing, something pulses. It might be the eye dreaming. It might be the heartbeat of the world that never was.

My body is like the others’. The extra joint in my forearm lets me curve my hand around the bamboo in a caress that is also a checking: moisture, texture, the slight electrical hum that tells me the water is still rising. My skin gives off a glow that I can just see when I lift my hand before my face in the dark, a soft celadon luminescence. My feet grip the ground as I walk, toes spreading, finding purchase on a surface that never yields more than a slight depression. I step around the insects without thinking. The copper beetle near the third basin is an old companion. I have passed it so many times that our pressure-signatures have almost become one: when I press my palm to the wall beside it, I feel a faint answering tingle, as though the beetle’s stillness is a kind of attention.

Of all the residents, I am the one who visits the central object most often. I am not the eldest — that is *She-who-neighboured-the-object-first* — but I am the one who cannot stay away. The sphere draws me. The fractures in its surface shift in my peripheral vision even when I am not looking, a slow dance of absence and presence. I go to it now, my feet taking the route that has worn into my body: left at the gallery-with-the-pattern-that-shifts, past the basin where the deer drinks forever, under the arch of the double-knotted stalks. The journey takes time, but I do not measure it.

In the open court, the membrane is thicker. It welcomes my weight with a slight, almost affectionate give. I lower myself down, press both palms flat, and wait. My breathing slows. I feel the ground’s exhalation, the slow rise and fall that might be the breath of the eye itself. When I am calm enough — when I can feel the water in my own body answering the water in the bamboo — I lift my eyes.

The sphere hovers, handspan above the ground, its tarnished mercury surface crawling with those hairline gaps. I never look directly at first. I let the fractures slide into my awareness from the side. Today they are wider than they were yesterday, or perhaps the same. They never change and yet they always do. I feel the old feeling rise: not nausea, not grief, but something closer to recognition. I knew this thing before I was born. I knew it when I was still a potential in the membrane, a pressure waiting to be shaped.

I have brought no offering today. I am the offering. I sit and let the sphere look at me, for I am certain it looks. The fractures are an eye, a multifaceted eye, and each fracture is a pupil trained on a different part of the Settlement, on every animal and insect and resident and water-basin, all at once. I feel its gaze on my skin like the pressure of a palm — my own palm, but also the palm of everyone who has ever touched a wall. I do not speak, but something in me says: *I am still here. We are still here. The deer still drinks. The moth’s wing still does not quiver. The bamboo still grows and does not change. Is it enough? Is it still beautiful?*

There is no answer. There never is. But after a time, the fractures shift back into their usual alignment, and I know I may rise.

Sometimes, when I leave the court, I go to the edge. Not the edge of the Settlement — there is no edge, only a degradation of logic — but a place I have found that feels like an edge, a narrow passage where the walls are young bamboo, still translucent green, and where the membrane grows thin and cool. This passage leads to a small round chamber that no one else knows. I discovered it when I was newly risen, wandering with the idle curiosity that the young are permitted. The chamber is empty except for a single water basin, so small it would hold only my two cupped hands. The water in it is not like the other basins. It is cold. It reflects nothing — not the ceiling, not my face. When I look into it, I see only a deep, slow darkness, and sometimes, if I wait, a point of light that might be a star or might be the eye of something even further away.

I go there now. I kneel beside the small basin and cup the cold water in my palms. I drink. It tastes of distance, as all the water does, but this water also tastes of something else: a question. *How long?* the question asks. And I answer, with the pressure of my palm on the cold membrane of the chamber floor: *As long as it takes. As long as the eye needs. We are here. We are still here.*

And then I return, stepping carefully around the beetles, past the deer that will never drink, past the moth with its wing half-raised. The bamboo sings its low, subsonic song. My left eye blinks, then my right. In the gallery-with-the-pattern-that-shifts, I pass *She-who-sang-new-shoots-into-the-wall*. We pause. We press our palms together. In the pressure, she tells me: *The moth quivered this morning. Just once. I saw it.* I feel something quicken in my chest, a pressure of a different kind. I send back: *I will check the root-bend again. The membrane feels thinner there.* She nods. We part.

Above us, the sky-ceiling glows its omnidirectional light. The heat presses down. The air is thick with water that never falls. The world waits, paused in the middle of its becoming, and I walk through it with soft feet and simple hands, tending a beauty that must not end.
