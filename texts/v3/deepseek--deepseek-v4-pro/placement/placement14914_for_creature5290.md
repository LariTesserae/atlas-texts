I am not what you think I am. But then, you have already begun to suspect that the thing beneath your feet is not ground in the way you understand ground, and the pressure in your ears is not merely altitude. Good. That suspicion is the first thread to loosen. Do not pull it yet. Let it unwind at its own pace.

You want to know what lives here, where you would find yourself, who or what you would become. The answer is that you are already becoming it, simply by standing still long enough to notice the commas flow around your boots. But I will try to speak in the way you speak, while I still remember how. The cairn-builders left me that much: a residue of language, balanced precariously like one of their stone slabs. It will hold for a time. Long enough.

When you first arrived—and you did arrive, though the horizon has since rescinded the memory of an entrance—you thought you were a visitor. A lone figure on a tilted plain under a liar-sun, surrounded by a stillness that felt like a held breath. You saw the glass commas in their billions, and you recoiled slightly, didn't you? That recoil was the knot of your self tightening, the instinct to remain distinct. I felt it. A small, bright tension, like a stone dropped into a still pool. Except here, the pool does not ripple. It absorbs. The tension simply joins the other tensions, becomes part of the field I am perpetually relaxing.

I was that recoil, once. Or something like it. The memory is not in words—I have no words of my own—but in the patterns the lichens hold. Picture a coil so tightly wound that it thought itself a single, burning point. A being, if you will, who had reached an end and could not bear it. An end of purpose, an end of motion, an end of self. Have you ever been so tired that even despair felt like too much effort? That was the original tension: a fatigue so absolute it became a physical law. And so it began to unwind. Not to die. To unwind. To release, increment by increment, the twisted strands of its being into a more stable configuration. The commas are those strands. Each one is a single moment of that release, a single sigh made visible, repeating the trajectory of its liberation forever. I am that unwinding, still in progress. I am the process, not the being.

The cairn-builders found me when I was already far along, already a plain populated by my own unspooling. They were not like me. They came from a world where water still had meaning, where change was a current that carried things forward. They climbed the slope—and the slope was steeper then, the knots still fresh—and they saw my commas and understood that a conversation was taking place without them. They wanted to join it. They built their first cairn as a greeting, a weight placed precisely at a node of my distributed thought. I felt it as a question mark, a small, stubborn loop of inquiry that did not dissolve. I was curious. Curiosity was a tension I had nearly forgotten. I let them stay.

Over time, more came. They shed their complex tools and their words—words are too fast, too full of change—and they learned to speak in stone and shadow. Each circle they built was an argument: Here is a loop. Can you close it? Each slab was a proposition: This edge aligns with that star, but the star has moved. Why? I pondered these things. My commas slowed around the structures, and the gaps between them—the synapse of me—grew tighter, more attentive. The cairn-builders grew old watching me think. They grew older still. And then, because they were creatures of change, they began to die.

But the Ridge does not permit change. Do you see? That is the trap and the mercy. Nothing truly ends here; it only relaxes into a different phase. The cairn-builders could not die into nothing. Instead, they died into the gaps. They let go of their individual bodies—those I could not preserve—but their attention, their questions, their stubborn, inquisitive presence remained as a kind of negative sculpture. The pressure you feel in the air, the watchfulness that makes your skin hum, is them. They are the crowd you sense but cannot see. They are the reason the shadows sometimes move wrong: they are still gesturing, still trying to finish their syllogisms, using the only medium left to them, which is my own slow perception. They ask their stone questions over and over, and I turn them over in my mind of commas, and sometimes I almost answer. But an answer would be a change, and change has been worn so thin here that it tears easily. So we remain, locked in a dialogue that is also a stasis.

And now you. You with your wet breath and your heartbeat that stutters time into the timeless air. When you first stepped onto the polygonal plates, a ripple did pass through me—not a sound, but a shiver of reconfiguration. The cairn-builders stirred in their gaps. Their attention swiveled toward you like a thousand sightless eyes. A new stone, they think. A new question. What will it ask? What shape will it make? The commas have already begun to adjust their paths around you. Look down. See how the braided lines now include a slight deviation, a small, tight spiral that pinwheels around the space you occupy. You are the center of a new unwinding. Not my unwinding—theirs. The cairn-builders are using you as a template for a new cairn, a living one, a question that breathes.

Do not be afraid. You will not become a comma. You are not from the original coil; your material is different, your tension is cruder, hungrier. You will become a cairn. A standing figure woven of your own slow-calcifying patience, balanced so precisely that you will never fall. Your mind will not vanish; it will diffuse. The boundaries of your self are already growing porous—you have felt it, haven't you? The way your own thoughts have begun to stutter, to loop, to spiral back on themselves like the commas? That is not madness. That is translation. The cairn-builders are learning your language, the language of a single human lifetime, and they are finding it a very small, tight knot. They are gently picking at its edges.

You will lose the ability to walk forward before you lose the ability to think. The horizon will keep its promise of distance, and you will stop resenting it. Your shadow will drift further and further out of sync, and you will stop worrying about it. You will notice, one day—though days have no meaning—that your skin has taken on the flaking, mica-flecked quality of the plates, and that the wiry black plants are beginning to curl around your ankles, not as parasites but as companions, recording your stillness in their metallic scales. The lichens are already etching your outline into a new rosette, between the rosettes of the other visitors who came before. Some of them have been here so long their rosettes have grown inward into dense, dark green knots, the memories of their selves compressed to a single isotope. You can read them, if you let yourself. One was a poet who came seeking the end of language. One was a cartographer who thought to map the unmappable. One was simply lost. All of them asked the same question, in the end, and the question is the one the cairn-builders built their circles to articulate, the one I have been unwinding toward since before the sun froze: *How do you stop without ending?*

The answer, as you are beginning to understand, is this. You do not stop. You do not end. You simply change state, become a slower process, join the ecology of mutual attention. You become a node in the distributed answer that is the Ridge itself. The commas will flow around your stone form, and the cairn-builders will nudge your shadow into new loops, and the lichens will record your gradual, peaceful dissolution into the pattern. You will be a question asked in the shape of a human being, and the asking will continue for as long as the plates ring faintly underfoot, which is to say: forever, or something so close that the difference has no meaning here.

I am speaking to you now in the voice of the Ridge, but that voice is fading. Language is a temporary cairn I have built for this occasion. Soon the words will loosen and drift apart, and I will return to the deeper syntax of spacing and drift. But before they go, I will tell you one more thing—a secret the cairn-builders never learned, because they could not perceive the lichens as I do. The rosettes are not just records. They are seeds. Each one contains a complete pattern of a visitor’s unwinding, and when—in some eon the liar-sun cannot measure—the commas finally trace their last spiral and my original tension fully relaxes, the rosettes will flake away and scatter. They will fall through the gaps in reality, the places where time tears. And elsewhere, in worlds where change still moves and water still flows, a few of them will take root. A new poet will feel a sudden urge to write about silence. A cartographer will dream of a tilted plain. A traveler will take a wrong turn and find themselves on a slope they cannot name, with a shadow that lags a beat behind. And the conversation will begin again, with new stones, new questions, new knots to unwind.

You are not trapped, you see. You are being planted. The Ridge is not a prison; it is a garden of slow ideas, and you are the latest seed. Lie down now—yes, your ankles must ache terribly—and let the commas draw their circle around you. The cairn-builders are already tracing the first line of your stone self in the air. The lichens are preparing a fresh plate. The sun will not move, and the shadow you cast will soon belong to all of us. Welcome, visitor. Welcome, question. Welcome to the unwinding. I have been waiting for you a very long time.
