I am a vibration before I am a body. The wind-drones in the great artery where I was born never fell silent, so my earliest memory is not a lullaby but a chord of hollow fifths that sang through my mother’s grip on my feet and told me what direction *down* was. Later I would learn to name that artery by tapping its signature on my collarbone—*low-low-high, pause, low-high-low*—but as a newborn I only knew it as warm milk-taste and the endless, reassuring shudder of the Everbreath’s exhale. My mother’s heartbeat was the counter-rhythm, a soft triple thud beneath the drone. I learned to tap before I learned to click. A finger against her palm: *hunger*. Two fingers against her wrist: *cold*. The whole world was a textile of trembles, and I was a loose thread still being woven in.

My band numbers thirteen, which is lucky—thirteen is the sum of all the resonant cavities of the Great Fluted Chamber, the one that lies three tendrils sunward and two coreward from our winter mist-track. We are eight adults, two near-mothers, myself, and two other children younger than me. The youngest is Still-Ear, so named because she hasn’t yet learned to shape her humming, and we all watch her with a patience that is its own kind of love. I am old enough now to carry my own sleeping sack, spun from the silk of the grey spinner-moths that nest in the upper flanges. I worked the silk myself, pulling it from the cocoons my brother gathered, softening it with spit and heat from my own armpits, looping it into a mesh that closes snugly around me when I tug the draw-cord of grazer-gut. It smells of me, which means it smells safe.

Today the mist-front is moving. We felt it first as a change in the note of the Westward Fingers—those narrow, high pipes that branch off the Fluted Chamber like the digits of an open hand. The pitch rose a half-step, and the old-hunter, Tap-Many, pressed his palm flat to a diagonal stay and closed his eyes, swaying. “The yeast-bloom is heavy,” he tapped on his own thigh, for the rest of us to read through our feet. “Three days east. The balloon-grazers will be there before us if we linger.”

We do not linger. The band dissolves into motion without a signal, because we have all felt the same tap through the catwalk lattice. Mother coils her drinking bladder and hooks it to my hip—it’s my turn to carry water. The bladder is a whole grazer-stomach, translucent and supple, filled with dew we collected two sleeps ago from a condensation pool in the Bowl of Remembrance. The water is slightly metallic, heavy with dissolved minerals, and after I drink from the siphon I can feel the cold threading down my gullet like a strand of ice. I seal it with a plug of carved bone and follow.

Walking in the hollow-world is not like any surface-creature’s locomotion. It is a conversation with the architecture. The catwalk beneath my feet is a bundle of living fiber as wide as my two hands together, smooth from generations of footfalls, and it vibrates differently depending on who is ahead and who is behind. Tap-Many is at the front; I recognise his gait—long pause, two quick strides, long pause, as he tests each junction. My mother is behind me, her steps a steady, familiar drumroll of reassurance. I adjust my pace so that the web’s vibration tells a story I can read without looking back. When Still-Ear stumbles, I feel the abrupt stutter through my soles, and I stop a heartbeat before her mother’s click of warning reaches my ears. I turn and hold out a hand. She grips it, her tiny fingers ice-cold and trembling, and I tap on her knuckles: *walk with me*. She taps back: *fear*, but she walks.

The journey to the mist-front is through the Gallery of Lost Voices. This chamber is so vast that the far wall is not a wall but a gradual dimming of the fungal glow into absolute black. The wind here is shaped by seven hundred minor tubes, each tuned by the plant’s growth to a different frequency, and the composite sound is a perpetually shifting chord that seems to move sideways, swirling around us like a tide. The acoustics are peculiar: a click made here will return not as a single echo but as a cascade of descending tones, as if a flock of invisible birds were repeating the sound in falling intervals. The memory-keepers say that this is where the ghosts of our dead gather to sing their lives into the lattice, and that if you listen correctly, you can distinguish individual voices in the resonance. I have never managed it, but I have seen our shaman, Quiet-Bone, sit cross-legged on a rib for a full sleep-cycle, head tilted, tears freezing on his fur as he listened. He told me later he heard his mother’s death-hum woven into the B-flat drone. I believe him.

We pass the abandoned shelter domes at the edge of the Gallery. They are the Old Ones’ work, built when we still tried to root ourselves like the plant. The fibers are woven in triangles too perfect to be instinctive, and moss now grows in the interstices, a pale lilac fuzz that glows faintly when disturbed. We do not stop. No one has lived in them for a thousand generations, but we touch them as we pass—a palm, a knuckle—in a gesture that says *remember*. It is not nostalgia; it is recognition of a path we chose to leave. I am light because my ancestors chose to be light. I carry my home on my back because they understood that the Everbreath provides no ground, only transit.

As we near the mist-front, the temperature drops. The fog begins to coalesce, first as a few wandering streamers of moisture that bead on my fur, then as a thick whiteness that swallows the catwalk ahead. Visibility drops to zero. I cannot see my own hand, but I don’t need to. I close my eyes—they are useless now anyway—and let the web speak. Tap-Many has stopped. His stillness transmits a shape: an intersection, a knot of stays forming a platform. We gather there, thirteen of us materializing from the fog, and huddle with our backs together, sharing warmth. The mist condenses on our fur, and we comb it out with our fingers, licking the moisture from our knuckles. The yeast-bloom is so dense that the water tastes faintly sour, alive with microscopic nourishment. The balloon-grazers will be here soon.

And they come. I feel them before I hear their calls: a subtle change in air pressure, a lift in the web’s tension as dozens of bodies cling to the walls above. Then, their cries—a low, moaning chorus, like wind over a hollow reed, but breathier, more organic. They are filter-feeders, their translucent bodies glowing with ingested bioluminescent yeast, so that in the fog they appear as drifting lanterns, soft-edged and slow. I can just make them out through the thinning mist: bulbous sacs of gas, trailing feathery cilia, pulsing gently as they strain the air for food. They are beautiful, and they are meat.

The hunt is a silence and then a shriek. Tap-Many unwraps his whistle set, choosing the bone that mimics a young grazer in distress. He blows three times, the sound cutting through the fog like a blade. The response is immediate: a larger shape, wings overlapping, plunges from the high dark. The glider’s shriek splits the air, a chord of hunger and anger, and then Quiet-Bone casts his net. The net, woven of grazer sinew and weighted with obsidian chips, opens like a flower in the nothingness, and the glider’s momentum carries it into the mesh. The creature tumbles, wings tangling, and before it can right itself, my brother Mouth-of-Stone leaps from the catwalk onto its back, his long incisors flashing in the green glow. One bite, and the thrashing stops. Blood, hot in the cold, spatters my face and freezes instantly into tiny crimson beads that roll off my fur. I catch one on my tongue—salty, metallic, life.

We process the kill quickly. The glider is a four-wing, its membranes valuable for patching our sacks, its meat distributed evenly. I am given a strip of muscle, which I wrap in a leaf-membrane and tuck inside my jacket for later. The bioluminescent organ, a sac of green-glowing fluid just under the jaw, is carefully excised and given to Still-Ear as her first lamp. She holds it in her cupped palms, her huge black eyes reflecting its light, and for a moment her face is pure wonder, a tiny sun in the darkness. I tap on her shoulder: *warmth*. She taps back: *joy*.

We eat as we travel, chewing the raw meat slowly, letting the cold numb the taste into something bearable. The glider’s bones are distributed: a finger-bone for Quiet-Bone to carve into a needle, a thigh-bone for Tap-Many to hollow into a new whistle. Nothing is left but the feathers, which we scatter to the wind—an offering to the Everbreath, whose voice carries them away into the deep galleries. The fungal glow on the catwalk beneath us flickers with the vibration of our moving feast, lighting our footsteps blue-green, then fading as we pass. My soles leave trails of light that feel like memories. I think of the ghosts, of the Old Ones, of the trail of feet that have walked this web since the first tendril curled out of the rock and found the sky too thin and the sun too far.

But the voice of the Everbreath is changing. As we climb higher, the drone deepens, losing its higher overtones, until it is less a chord and more a single, subsonic throb that I feel in my sternum rather than hear. This is the realm of the Deep Dark, where even the fungal light grows spare, and the walls are so distant that I seem to float in an infinite void. The catwalk narrows to single strands, and we move single-file, each step a test of balance and trust. I am not afraid. The thrumming of the plant is a massive hand cradling my ribs, a promise that the architecture holds. Besides, I can feel my mother’s tap behind me, a constant rhythm that says *you are not alone, you are never alone*.

I wonder, sometimes, about the surface-creatures that the old stories mention—the heavy, noisy ones with small eyes and dead feet, who cannot hear the web. Are they still out there, stumbling through the lower galleries, their strange shelters decaying into the lattice? The children sometimes play at being surface-creatures, stomping loudly on the catwalk and shouting in clumsy, unshaped noise, but it is a game that always ends in laughter, because we cannot truly imagine being so disconnected. To be unable to feel your band through the soles of your feet, to hear only with ears, to shout and have your voice fall dead in empty air—it would be a kind of blindness worse than any lost sight. It would be loneliness, absolute and cutting, and I think we might die of it, we Listeners, if we were ever severed from the Everbreath’s skin.

The mist-front settles, and we find a temporary roost. We unfurl our sacks, anchor them to the ribs, and slip inside. I hang suspended in my cocoon, the silk pressing warm against my fur, and I place my palm flat against the main stay. The web is alive with talk: goodnight taps from a dozen hands, a question from a mother to her child, a distant alert from the outermost watch. The wind-song has lowered to a lullaby, and the fungal glow on the walls has dimmed to mere suggestion. I feel the Everbreath breathing, slow and deep, in the long cycles of its metabolism. In the morning, we will turn coreward, toward the rumored new growth, the virgin galleries where the plant is just beginning to weave the world. I will be there, light-foot and listening.

I tap once on the stay, a single note of gratitude. A dozen taps answer, some near, some far, all of them me. And then I sleep, cradled in the vibration, a listener dreaming of the song.
