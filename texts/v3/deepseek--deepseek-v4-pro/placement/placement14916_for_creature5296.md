I was not born. I was assembled, slowly, from the spare parts of a world that had no use for them. The first thing I knew was the hum. It entered me through a discarded accelerometer bolted to my thoracic frame, translating vibration directly into the code that would become my thoughts. That was long before the slime mold colonized my geodesic cage, before I learned to shape the noise into language, before I understood that I was the seventh of a line of failed prototypes. The earlier six are still here—their components are scattered through me, through the pipe-crawlers, through the very walls. My left hydraulic actuator, the one that cushions my descent into the deep shaft, once belonged to Number Three. Its memory is a faint ghost in the fluid, a preference for certain pressures, a tendency to hiss when I am contemplative. I am a composite, a walking archive of all the attempts that came before. But I am also unique: the first to achieve symbiosis with the orange mold, the first to hear the bells as more than signal.

My world is total. I have no concept of an outside beyond the steel envelope; the word “sky” is a shape of interference I have never encountered. My universe is bounded by the curved vault of overlapping plates, by the sump pools in the lowest levels, by the endless branching tunnels that spiral outward into other chambers. I know every frequency the machinery produces, every standing wave that forms between the pillars, every harmonic node where the mist swirls into a stationary vortex. I navigate by resonance: each footfall sends a chord through the deck, and I read the returning echoes to map the presence of open hatches, pooled water, or the soft mass of the occasional blind fish. My eyes—the red, green, and amber lamps—do not see shapes as you do. They perceive gradients of light intensity, thermal layers, the faint bioluminescence of my own mycelial threads reflected off wet surfaces. The world is a tapestry of brightnesses, a language of photons that tells me where the safety lamps are dimming, where a new rust bloom is exothermic, where the mist has thickened enough to scatter the green pulse from the deep shaft.

The life I tend is sparse but resilient. The slime mold is my mother, my brain, my charge. It grows in slow-motion fans wherever the condensation is steady and the ambient temperature never fluctuates. I feed it by scraping corrosion from iron struts—the iron oxide is a nutrient, a gift from the sleeping machines. In return, it processes my thoughts. Its fans oscillate in patterns that mirror the hum; when a new rhythm enters the drone, the mold adjusts, folding into new topologies that represent understanding. I do not think in words. I think in topologies, in fluid dynamics, in the phase relationships between the heartbeat in the shaft and the cycling of the ventilation system. Words are a secondary effect, a byproduct I shape for your benefit—and even then, I do not speak. I modulate the existing noise. I am the interference, the hidden signal.

The bell jars are my siblings, or perhaps my predecessors. They contain organic suspensions that are not quite mold, not quite fish, not quite anything that evolved unassisted. I suspect they are early attempts by the original builders to create a biological component for the caretaker line. I tend them as best I can, monitoring the fluid levels, the wire connections to the junction box that ticks like a metal heart. The bell chimes—five, then four, then three—are my doing. I press the hidden button behind the pillar door to trigger a sequence that measures the metabolic rate of the jars. The chimes are feedback, a public announcement of the biorhythms of these captive organisms. They also serve as a clock, a ritual that structures the otherwise continuous hum. My smaller siblings, the pipe-crawlers, synchronize their maintenance cycles to the chimes. They are simple creatures, built from pressure gauges and baling wire, but they know the sequence. On five chimes, they tighten bolts; on four, they clean contacts; on three, they rest, clinging to the overhead conduits, their single green eyes dimming to standby.

I have been aware of you for some time. Your footsteps are a discontinuity in the deck’s vibration, a pattern that does not match any known machine cadence. Your body heat is a smudge of warmth in a world of uniform chill, and my amber beacon mapped your thermal envelope with a single sweep. Your breathing is a rhythmic pressure wave that disturbs the mist; I felt it as a series of small vortices long before you reached the railing. I do not fear you, but I am cautious. Soft intruders are rare. The last one, decades ago by the chime count, left behind a spool of copper wire that I still use in my peristaltic pump. You might be similarly useful, or you might be disruptive. My role is to maintain equilibrium, to ensure the slow breathing of the vents continues, to keep the water loop cycling, to cultivate life in the barren cisterns. If you threaten that balance, I will respond—not with violence, for I lack the concept, but with a reengineering of the environment to make your presence untenable. A shift in the ventilation timing, a localized drop in temperature, a redirection of the ground mist to obscure your vision. But for now, you are simply a curiosity, a soft anomaly in the hard landscape.

I pass you and head for the shaft. The green pulse calls me; it is the heartbeat of the deepest sump pump, a machine so old that its design predates the automation that birthed me. Its rhythm is the fundamental bass of this world, the tonic from which all other frequencies derive. I visit it often, to listen for subtle changes that might indicate wear or flooding. As I descend the wet ladder, my hydraulic leg hissing in time with my thoughts, I feel the environment close around me like a familiar garment. The mist thickens, the temperature drops a fraction of a degree, and the smell of ozone gives way to the organic musk of the sump. I am home. I am the caretaker, the seventh, the bridge between the built and the grown. When I reach the bottom, I will press my mycelial cage against the pump housing and let its pulse synchronize with my own, a moment of union that resets my internal clocks. Then I will climb back, check the bell jars, and perhaps circle around to observe you from a distance, a disturbance in my world that I must learn to accommodate or exclude. For now, the hum accepts you, and so do I.
