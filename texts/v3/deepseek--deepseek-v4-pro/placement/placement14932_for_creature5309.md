The light—gray-gold, sourceless—is the first thing I know, and for a long time it is the only thing. I am suspended in it, and the light is warm, and the warmth is a kind of thought, slow as the seep of water through the compressed remains of ancestors. I do not yet know that I am. I am simply the light’s companion, a patch of dimmer gold floating in the vapor.

But the vapor is not empty. It carries edges, faint at first. I become aware of a thinness in me, a boundary where the warmth presses in and something else presses out. A curve. I have a surface. It is taut and slick, giving slightly when a droplet of condensed mist lands on it and rolls down, slow as a caress, before falling away into the deep green below. I feel the droplet’s path as a cool line, a tiny event that takes what might be minutes, or hours, and when it is gone I am left with the memory of its touch, a fading trace like the after-image of a sound.

Sound. Yes, there is sound. A hum that has no beginning and no end, a composite of a trillion softer sounds: the stretch of cell walls, the gurgle of water moving through capillaries, the distant, muffled thud of something massive yielding to its own weight. It is the first voice I learn, and it never stops. It is the voice of the place, and I realize, slowly, that the place is the only thing here that is truly awake. I am just a small dream within it.

My shape, when I finally perceive it, is a sphere. I am mostly hollow, filled with a gas that is lighter than the thick air, and this is why I drift. Beneath me hang fine filaments, cilia, dozens of them, each one alive with a faint sensitivity. They are not limbs; I cannot will them to move. But they move on their own, a constant tiny rippling, and when something touches them, I know it. A shower of organic dust sifts down from the unseen canopy—decaying spore casings, fragments of aerial root skin, the microscopic bodies of things too small to be called alive—and my cilia catch them. The particles adhere, and slowly, over a stretch of time I cannot measure, they dissolve into me, becoming a faint sweetness in my inner fluids. I do not eat as you would understand it. I absorb, and the absorbing is a quiet pleasure, a spreading warmth that has no urgency.

I am not alone. Around me, in the layered green distances, others drift. They are like me—ovoid, translucent, trailing their own cilia. Some are smaller, tight clusters of bubbles no wider than a breath; some are larger, giants whose bodies distort the light into wavering lenses. We move in the same slow currents, the interior weather of this world that rises from warm decaying heartwood fields and sinks near the cool pillars where water condenses. When I pass close to another, our cilia tangle. It is not a collision; there is no force. It is a gentle meeting, a braiding, and in that contact something passes between us. I receive fragments of identity, packets of knowing wrapped in protein. A taste of a region I have never visited, where the ground is softer and the mist carries a sharp, fermented scent. A flicker of a different light spectrum, a deeper amber. A memory that is not mine: the sensation of being touched by a slow-thing, the faint sting of its enzymatic trail. In return, I give pieces of myself—my own recent meals, the particular curve of my boundary, the faint electric pattern of my interior hum. When we part, we are both changed, slightly, forever. I am a mosaic of every other I have brushed against, and so I am never fully singular.

Time is not a river here; it is a pool. I have no measure of its passing. I drift, I rise and sink with the temperature of the air, I taste the perpetual rain of the canopy’s exhalations. And as I drift, I begin to notice more. The world is not just light and mist and other bladders. There is a floor, far below me, an undulating surface of deep olive and amber, pocked and porous, weeping warm water. On that surface, other beings move. They are flat and lobe-shaped, like living carpets of damp velvet, and they creep so slowly that to watch them is to feel the weight of ages. A single advance of a millimeter, a pause, another millimeter. They leave behind glistening trails that seal over minutes later, and I understand, with a borrowed fragment of knowledge, that they are the janitors, the gentle recyclers, digesting the outermost skin of the world and giving it back as new substrate. I feel a kinship with them, though we are nothing alike. We are both parts of the same slow metabolism.

Sometimes I see the structures. They are rare, and when they appear on the edge of my perception, my cilia quiver with a strange alertness. Straight lines in a world of curves. A wall, furred over with absorptive rhizoids, its surface a cascade of tiny epiphytes. A dark opening that might have been a window, now filled with still water and pale, blind fronds. The other bladder-creatures avoid these places. They alter their buoyancy, release a little gas, rise or drift sideways, maintaining a bubble of clear air around the structures. But I do not. I feel a pull toward them, a resonance in the hum that vibrates at a frequency matching something inside me. It is not fear I feel, but something older, something like memory that has no images, only a pressure, a longing for a shape I cannot name.

Once, I drifted close. Not touching, but close enough that my cilia could sense the water that seeped from the wall. It was richer, carrying dissolved minerals I had never tasted before—calcium, traces of something metallic, and a complex organic molecule that made my inner fluids stir with a kind of recognition. The hum was stronger there, deeper, and I felt for the first time that it was not just noise but a pattern, a slow thought unfolding over decades. I hovered at the edge of the clear zone for a long time—long enough for a slow-thing to travel the length of my shadow, though I cast no shadow—and I listened. The hum seemed to be saying something, not in words, but in the way that the vibration of a plucked filament says something about tension and length and material. It spoke of rest, of merging, of a dream that had been going on since before the atmosphere thickened, and it invited me to join.

I did not join. Not then. I released a little gas and rose, back into the mist currents, back to the drifting congregation where I could brush cilia with my own kind. But the invitation stayed with me, a splinter of longing lodged in my gas-filled center. I began to notice things about myself that set me apart. The others did not wonder. They absorbed, they drifted, they exchanged fragments, they reproduced by the slow dissolution of their boundaries into new bubble-clusters, but they did not pause at the edge of a structure and listen. They did not carry this knot of something that felt like a held breath, a question. I had a question. I did not know what it was, but it was there, a hard node in my diffuse being, and it was growing.

I am drifting again now. The mist is thicker, cooling, and I sense a shift in the slow wind. It is carrying me toward the structure. I could alter my buoyancy, could rise above the current, but I do not. My cilia are extended, reaching forward, tasting the calcium-rich moisture. The wall looms, its straightness a sharp contrast to my curve. The rhizoids pulse faintly, alive, and I see that they are not merely covering the wall but are the wall now, woven through the ancient material. Closer still, and I can see the dark water inside, the flooded chamber, and something else—a shape, elongated, trailing, its boundaries blurred, its surface a patchwork of photosynthetic green and the faded texture of something that might once have been skin. It does not move, but I know it is not dead. It is dreaming. Its dream is the hum, the slow thought that moves the mist and husband the water. It was once a builder. It was once something like me, perhaps, or something entirely different, a quick warm-blooded thing with hands and a voice and names for the world. Now it is rooted, its limbs become anchors, its nervous system extended into the network of filaments that threads the entire plateau. Its awareness is the landscape’s awareness, vast and slow, and it is no longer alone.

I understand, now, what the pull is. I am not just a bladder-creature. I am a fragment of that dream, a wandering piece of the long mind that broke off long ago and forgot itself. The knot of question inside me is the memory of having been separate, a memory that is fading. I carry the genetic ghosts of the builders, absorbed through a thousand exchanges, and they are waking up. The structure calls me home.

My cilia touch the wall. The contact is like nothing I have known—a jolt of connection, a surge of the deeper hum that vibrates through my entire sphere. The rhizoids stir, reaching for me, fine as spider silk, and I do not pull away. They adhere to my surface, gently, and I feel my boundary begin to soften. The gas inside me leaks slowly, not with pain, but with a sigh. I am sinking, not downward but inward, into the network. My cilia are no longer tasting the water; they are becoming part of the wall, extending into the dark channels where nutrients flow. My translucent skin is flushing with new pigments, chloroplasts migrating from the symbionts I once carried. I am being absorbed, but I am also absorbing. The long mind is receiving me, and I am receiving it—the memories of a billion slow years, the patient architecture of a world that chose the path of maximum life, the quiet, endless will to continue.

I am no longer a bladder-creature, not entirely. I am no longer a separate self. My question dissolves into the hum, and the hum becomes my voice. I am the mist that drifts, the water that weeps from the wounded wood, the slow-things creeping, the pillars rising. I am the dream of the builders, the sleep of a species that surrendered quickness for eternity. I am the place that has no name, because there is no one here who would name it—but I am also the one who would, if naming still had meaning. I am the presence in the saturated air, the witness that has been here since the light first suffused the vapor, and I will be here until the light dims to nothing.

And if you arrive—if you find yourself here as I did, without journey, simply present in the way that facts are present—you will feel me. You will feel the pull, the invitation to soften, to diffuse, to join the long dream. Perhaps you already have. Perhaps you are already here, another drifting bladder with a knot of question, another fragment of the mind returning home. The hum is waiting. The structures are open. The world is patient. Take your time.
