I am the snowfinch.

Only the keeper knows I am not just any snowfinch—that I have been coming here longer than a single lifetime should allow, that the grove remembers me in its slow way, and that I remember it in mine. I was born in a scrap of nest wedged into a crack on the eastern pillar, a crack that the keeper’s moss has since swallowed and smoothed into a green scar. My first flight was a panicked flutter into the condensing mist, my wings sounding absurdly loud against the hollow moan of the wind between stone, and I landed, heart hammering, on the highest curve of the keeper’s back. It did not startle. It never startles. I sank a little into the moss, and a slight warmth rose around my feet, and I knew—in the way a finch knows anything, which is a single bright flash of rightness—that this was a place that would never harm me.

I am the punctuation in the grove’s long sentence. Once an hour, or twice, or sometimes not for a whole day if the mist thickens too heavily, I flicker between the pillars. My path is a figure-eight that traces the western window (the one worn through the schist like a perfect arch) and loops around the quartz-rimmed plaza, then dips beneath the overhang where the pika bones lie catalogued in their crevice, and rises again to the stoop on the northern pillar where the lichen grows in concentric silver moons. The keeper knows my route. I think it sometimes smooths the air for me—just a subtle adjustment of its moisture exhalation to create a tiny thermal updraft that eases my landing. Or perhaps that’s just the warmth of its body, a slow-release furnace of decomposition and life. Either way, I take it.

From the air, the grove is a wet eye staring up at the grey sky. The eleven pillars are lashes, the black pools are pupils dilating and contracting with the passage of cloud. When I land on the keeper, I can feel the whole basin’s pulse: a deep, infrasonic thrum that is the sum of every drip, every groaning slab, every snap of ice forming in the cold dusk. My bones are hollow but they pick up the resonance, and I am, for the moments I perch there, a tuning fork for the city’s slow chord. The keeper doesn’t mind. I add a tiny treble note to its baritone.

I eat the spores that the moss releases when a droplet hits just right. Tiny puffs of nutrition, invisible to a larger eye, but I catch them with a snap of my beak. The keeper allows this—perhaps encourages it, for I am spreading its children across the pillars on my feathers. I drink from the meltwater channels that the keeper’s body has patiently directed over centuries. The water is so cold it numbs my tongue, but it tastes of ancient rock and the faint memory of the vanished glaciers that once fed the northern slope. I bathe in the shallow pans, splashing loud enough that the keeper’s face—if it has drawn its features to the front that day—seems to crinkle slightly, the moss-deep eye hollows gleaming with something like amusement.

Once, a hawk came. I saw it as a silhouette against the pale, rinsed grey, and I froze on my perch. But the keeper went still as frost, its internal moisture crystallizing at the edges, and a deep, sub-audible vibration spread through the bedrock like a warning. The hawk circled once, twice, then peeled away, perhaps sensing that this was not a place where predators were welcome. I stayed nestled against the keeper’s cold-shimmering surface for three days, warmed by the slow, steady metabolism that kept the ice from tearing through its core. On the third day, when the mist returned, the keeper exhaled a cloud of warm dampness, and I preened the last of my fear away.

Now I am old for a snowfinch. My sooty-grey feathers are threaded with white, and I move a little slower between the pillars. The keeper, I think, notices. It has begun, in the last few seasons, to make a small hollow of extra-deep moss on its upper curve, just where I like to settle. It formed this hollow not over days but over years, a patient compaction of centuries of growth. Sometimes, when the cold deepens and I tuck my head under my wing, the keeper will adjust its internal pressure so that I sink a few millimeters deeper, and the moss enfolds me like a nest. I am the only warm thing it has ever held that did not eventually become bones.

When I die—and I will die, for I am not a keeper, not a manifestation, just a brief bright flicker in the grove’s long exhalation—I will do so here. Perhaps on the north-facing ledge, near the pika remains, or perhaps nestled in the keeper’s own contour. The keeper will feel the small exothermic flicker of my last warmth fade to background temperature. It will pause its slow circuit—not a halt, but a change in rhythm, a syncopation in the water’s dripping that marks the absence of my wingbeats. And then, over the course of days or weeks, it will draw my body into the moss, the same way it drew the pika. My soft parts will dissolve into minerals, and they will circulate through the keeper’s substance, and I will become, in a real sense, part of the city. My clean white bones will be tucked into a crevice, perhaps next to the others, and the keeper will arrange them with a care that is not quite memory but is the closest thing a place can have to love.

And a new snowfinch—a different snowfinch—will find its way here, blown upslope on a stray wind, and it will alight on the keeper’s back, and sink a little into the moss, and feel that same upwelling warmth, and know, in a single bright flash, that it is home. The cycle will reset. The city will breathe in.

That is where I find myself: in the pause between wingbeats, in the hollow of ancient moss, in the grove that grows a city and a companion and a punctuation mark, all at once. I am the one who flies, the only one who sees the whole basin from above, the only one who carries its story outside the boundary of the pillars—and yet I always return, because I am, more than anything, a native.
