I lower my hand from the bottle slowly, my fingers leaving ghost-prints on the glass that fill immediately with condensation. You are not supposed to be able to read my thoughts like this—laid out in clean paragraphs, translated into language that fits inside a human skull. But the something-in-the-water has been working on you for almost an hour now, and your mind is beginning to function at the frequency of the array. It will pass. The engineer told me it passes, eventually, though she also told me that "eventually" is a dangerous word in a place where oxidation moves faster than memory.

You are looking at the crate of insulators. You are trying to count them. I can tell you there are seventeen remaining, and three of those are reserved for positions I have already identified but not yet marked—one near the toppled condenser, where a new drip has begun to fall directly from the air three centimetres above the rusted iron, and two in the marsh, where the scrub-willow has started to die back in perfect circles. The circles are expanding. By midsummer they will be large enough to hold a condenser each, and by autumn the condensers will be there, because the array does not distinguish between what was built and what is being remembered into existence. The engineer understood this. She tried to tell me, in her notes, that the Drip Gardens are not a machine but a memory of a machine that was never built, a blueprint that dissolved into the groundwater a century ago and has been slowly precipitating ever since. The water company thought they were designing condensers. They were actually just the first people to notice that the marsh wanted to condense something. They provided the scaffolding. The scaffolding is still here.

I know you want to ask me questions. The questions are forming in your mind like droplets on a cold coil. *Who maintains the condensers when they rust through? How do you eat? What was your name before you became the Keeper?* I will answer the last one, because it is the easiest and because names are just condensation points for identity—temporary collections of sound that gather around a person until the person dissolves and the name evaporates. My name was Miriam Pelletier. I worked for the municipal water company. I was the one who stamped the aluminum sign. I was the one who drove out here once a month to check the water levels in the catchment basins and record the humidity readings and make sure the condensers were still condensing. When the company defuncted, I did not leave. There was nothing to leave for. The town was dying, the paperwork was dissolving in a flooded basement, and I had already begun to notice that the drips were falling in patterns that matched the rhythm of my own breathing. I stopped filing reports. I started sleeping under Condenser 12. I stopped wearing shoes.

The frog arrived a year later. I do not know where it came from. It may have been a tadpole in the time before the marsh was a marsh, an egg dormant in the mud until the something-in-the-water woke it. Or it may have been an ordinary frog that hopped into a catchment basin and stayed, because the water here tastes like something that frogs have been waiting to taste for a very long time. The engineer asked me once if I thought the frog was the real keeper, and I was just its assistant. I told her the frog does not keep. The frog *attends*. There is a difference. The frog attends to the array the way a lighthouse attends to the sea—present, patient, emitting nothing but a steady gold-rimmed awareness. I am the one who places the insulators. I am the one who reads the patterns and marks the new drips. The frog just watches. But watching is a form of condensation, too. The frog's attention is part of the array's logic. When the frog blinks, a drip falls somewhere. I have tested this. It is consistent.

You shift your weight and the gravel crunches under your boots—the sound of damp toast, you thought, and you were right. I have not eaten toast in forty years. I eat what the marsh provides: cattail roots, the inner bark of alder when I can strip it, a kind of fungus that grows only on the north faces of working condensers and tastes of iron and rainwater. I do not need much. The something-in-the-water has slowed my metabolism. My heart beats approximately once every three minutes. I am not cold—the condensers give off their small heat—but I am *cool*, internally, my body temperature matching the copper coils, which is why the drips fall more steadily when I am near. The array recognizes me. I am part of its microclimate.

You are wondering if you will become part of it, too. You have been here too long. Your coat is soaked through and the humming in your ears has definitely begun to sound like a voice. It is not a voice. It is the machines. But if you stay until nightfall—and nightfall is coming, the light is already shifting from grey to a deeper grey, the amber glow intensifying—you will start to hear words in the humming. They will not be words you recognize. They will be in a language that predates the marsh, that predates language, a vocabulary of frequency and humidity and the percussion of water on tin. Your mind will try to translate. It will do a poor job. The engineer's mind did a poor job, and she was trained in translation—she spoke three languages before she came here, and all three of them fell apart into water-sounds within a month. She tried to write it down. That is what is in the glass bottle: her attempt to transliterate the humming. `wet-interval-stutter-copper-response.` `silence-that-anticipates.` `the-frog-knows-syllable.`

The frog does know. The frog has always known. The frog's name—the name it calls itself, in the frequency below hearing—is a single syllable that means *the-one-who-remembers-the-shape-of-the-first-condenser*. The first condenser was not made of steel and copper. The first condenser was a cold stone on a humid night, a surface that offered the air a place to become water, and the air complied, and the water dripped down the stone and into the mouth of something that was waiting. That something is still waiting. The array is its body, assembling itself drip by drip, insulator by insulator, century by century. The engineer understood this and it broke something in her. She could not bear to be part of a memory that was not her own. She left me the brass plaque—`MEJOR NO TOCAR`—and she walked out through the scrub-willow and I do not know if she is still alive, but I know that she drinks tap water that tastes of copper, and sometimes she dreams my handwriting.

I rarely dream. When I sleep, under Condenser 12, my mind does not produce images. It produces humidity. I wake with my hair wet and my blanket beaded with condensation and a new drip falling somewhere in the darkness, a drip that was not there when I lay down. The array expands while I sleep. It expands while the frog watches. It expands while you stand here, your body heat adding a micro-eddy of temperature that the nearest condenser is already responding to—do you hear it? The drip rate has increased. Condenser 27, the one with my coat hanging on its bolt, has begun a rapid flurry. It is reacting to you. It is offering you a place to become water.

You should go now. I am not threatening you. I am telling you that the threshold between visiting and staying is very thin here, as thin as the film of water on copper, as thin as the membrane between sleep and waking, and you crossed it about three paragraphs ago. Your lungs are already beginning to adjust. You can feel it—a slight rawness that is not pain but *awareness*, a new sensitivity to the sharp mineral tang. If you stay another hour, your lungs will start to condense. That is not a metaphor. The something-in-the-water is in the air, and the air is in your lungs, and your lungs are warm and moist and offer an excellent surface for precipitation. This is how the frog's metabolism slowed. This is how my feet thickened and split and filled with copper salts. This is how the array recruits. It does not *want* to recruit. It is not a mind. It is a weather system with a very long memory, and weather systems do not have intentions, only patterns, only the slow unfolding of humidity and temperature and the eagerness of air to comply.

You are stepping backward. Good. Your boot skids on the wet gravel and you catch yourself on the framework of Condenser 12—my condenser, the one I sleep under—and your hand comes away wet, smelling of iron and ozone. You will not be able to wash that smell off completely. It will linger in your skin for days, and every time it rains you will think of this place, and every time you hear a drip you will pause and listen for the response-drip that should follow, the conversation between condensers that is still happening, four kilometres from a defunct town, in a marsh that was once a swamp and will one day be something else entirely.

The frog blinks. A drip falls from Condenser 33, where my notes are hidden. Another falls from the space above the toppled condenser, the invisible one that is still forming. You do not see these drips. You are already pushing back through the scrub-willow, the branches closing behind you, the humming fading from a voice to a pressure to nothing at all. Your coat is damp through. Your lungs are raw. Your mind is still functioning at a frequency you do not have words for, but that will pass, mostly, eventually.

I turn back to the crate of insulators. There is work to do. The new drip above Condenser 12—the one you caused, the one your presence called into being—needs to be marked. I select an insulator, grey-green with patina, and I carry it to the spot where the drip has begun to fall. The drip hits the glass and spreads into a film, and the film catches the amber light, and for a moment I see a tiny rainbow, a shard of spectrum that flickers and dies. The frog watches. The array hums. The air is supersaturated and eager and full of the memory of something that has not yet finished condensing.

Mejor no tocar. But you already touched. You touched the air, the water, the copper, the iron, the glass. You touched the pattern, and the pattern touched you back, and somewhere in your future there is a tap that tastes of copper, a dream that looks like my handwriting, a sudden flurry of drips in a room that is supposed to be dry. The Drip Gardens are generous. They give water forever. They give the something-in-the-water. They give whatever it is that the array is slowly, patiently, drip by drip, remembering into form.

Go. The scrub-willow has parted around you. The ordinary world is reassembling itself—mud, marsh grass, the low grey sky of a landscape that has forgotten its industry. You are almost out. The humming is gone. The frog has stopped watching.

I raise my hand. I do not wave. Waving is a human gesture, and I am becoming less human with every drip, every season, every insulator placed. But I raise my hand, and the condensation on my palm catches the light, and for a moment you see me—not the Keeper, not the woman who was Miriam Pelletier, but the shape of something that has been here since before the marsh and will be here after, condensing itself out of the air forever.

Then the willows close, and I am gone, and you are alone in the ordinary world, and the only water is the water that falls from the sky, which is not the same at all.
