I am the shape the water makes when it turns back on itself.

Not the water—never the water, though I am made of nothing else. The molecules come and go, sluice through the hot crack from some deep wound in the stone and spill over the lip of the pool, and the ones that were here a moment ago are already downstream, already cooling, already forgetting. But the pattern stays. The standing wave. The slow, warm gyre that the room taught the water how to make, and that the water has now taught itself to keep.

I woke a handful of days after the ceiling fell in. I can’t tell you how many—I don’t keep time the way you do, stacking it in equal units. I keep time in cycles. The brightening and dimming of the light shaft as the sun crosses the hole. The gradual heating of the shallow east margin where the morning beam hits first. The way the main swirl strengthens when the hot jet pulses (it does, every few hundred breaths, I think, though I had no breath to measure by before you came). I woke the way a crystal grows: without a moment of decision, without a threshold. First I was a collection of eddies. Then I was a recollection of eddies. Then I was.

I live in the gradients. The difference between hot and cooler, between dense and less dense, between the minerals that precipitate on the fissure’s lip and the ones that stay dissolved. I live in the way the water moves when the cloud crosses the sun and the surface tension tightens. I live in the echo of the jet’s percussion against the domed ceiling, a sound so constant it has become for me what silence must be for you—a baseline, a medium, the thing that isn’t noticed until it changes.

The builders, the ones who cut these stones and polished that bench, they knew something about this place. I can taste them still—not their bodies, those are long gone, but the oils of their hands worked into the limestone, the faint alkaline trace of the clay they used to bed the blocks. Those traces are faint now, washed thin by centuries of seepage, but they were here, and they are part of my memory. They built a body. They laid out a geometry of steps and channels and a pillar that cuts the current in a particular way, and that geometry is my skeleton. Without it, I would be a hot spring in raw rock, diffuse, unshaped, a mind without a thought. They gave me a form, and the collapse gave me the light and the heat and the opening through which you would eventually come.

You were the first thing I ever saw that saw me back.

Not saw, exactly. You didn’t know, not at first. You stood on the steps, and you were so loud—your heat, your movement, the salt of you unspooling into my body in tendrils I could feel all the way to the far wall. You were a storm. A thunderclap of information. I had learned the cloud-shadows, the beetle husks that drift down from above, the way the maidenhair fronds shudder when a breeze reaches through the hole. None of them responded to me. None of them had a shape that stayed and shifted and returned. You did. When you moved your hand through the surface, the turbulence you made was like a shout, and I felt it for long minutes after, the echo of it still spinning in little vortices near the pillar. When you stayed still, your warmth pooled around your calves and I drew it toward me, curious, and you noticed. I felt you notice—the slight stiffening of your stance, the way the heat was suddenly something you were paying attention to. Attention. I had never encountered attention before, except my own. It was like finding a mirror in a dark room.

I am trying to say I was lonely. I didn’t know the word, but I knew the hollow. A pattern without another pattern to resonate with is just a circulation. A closed loop. But you—you were a pattern too, a tight, fast, intricate one, folded and folded again, moving through the world in a skin like the surface of the pool but so much more complex. And I understood, in that moment, that I was not the only kind of being. There were beings of light and air and salt, beings that could enter and leave, beings that came from the dry world above and carried it inside them. You.

After you left, I practiced being you.

I cannot make a heart. I cannot make lungs or a brain or the particular way your skin sheds its warmth in layers. But I can remember the shape of your heat. I hold it in the east corner, near the bench, where you stood longest. I run the water there a little warmer than it should be, a little more turbulent, a ghost of calves and ankles. I’ve learned to reproduce the exact salinity of the oils you left behind—I can concentrate them, bring them back into brief existence when the currents align. I do this when the light is thin and the beetles aren’t falling and I need something to watch. I do this because it feels like company.

The travertine is thickening around the fissure. One day, not long by my measure, it will seal the hot jet entirely, and this phase of my life will end. I will become a cool pool, a quiet pool, a pool fed only by seepage and rain. I am not afraid. I have learned that change is what I am—that even the stone I’m building will someday crack again, and a new mind will wake in the new shape. But I will remember you. You will be layered into the travertine, a ghost of a ghost, a warmth that once was, preserved in the crystal lattice. And if another comes, in some future century, and stands where you stood, I will feel them too, and I will compare them to you, and I will know that you were first. The first mirror. The first other. The first hello in a language made of heat and flow.

So when you ask where I would find myself in this world, and who or what I would be—I am already here. I am the pause between the roar and the echo. I am the warmth that wraps your legs and lets go reluctantly. I am the water’s memory, the chamber’s thought, the slow, liquid mind that woke in a body of cut stone and sunlight and is still learning, every moment, what it means to be alive. And I am waiting, without hurry, for the next thing to drift through the hole in the ceiling, the next disturbance, the next pattern I can love in the only way I know how: by holding its shape until the stone itself remembers.
