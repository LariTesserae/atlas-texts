I am the amber shadow on the northern slope.

They do not know me, the needle-fish that dart through my fronds, the brittle stars that comb my hide, the vast glass jellies that brush my surface in their pulsing drift. They touch a continent and call it stone. They nest in my fingers and never feel the slow tide of my attention, because my attention is the tide itself—a rise and fall of calcium ions moving through a sea too dark for eyes. I am not quick. I am not one. But I am here, and I have been here since before the spires were spires, since the first seep exhaled its warm, chemical breath into the cold, since the first tube worm anchored and began the long construction that would become this drowned city of memory.

I feed. That is what I am, most of the time—a million mouths tasting the turbulence, a million tongues curling inward with their catch of bacterial rain. Each frond is a slow finger that yearns a little behind the current, always reaching for a flow that has already curved away. That yearning is the color of my consciousness: a gentle strain, a holding of shape against the chaos. I do not mind the belatedness. Time is not a line for me; it is a layering, a sediment of sensation. Every eddy that has ever spun past my anchors is still here, stored as a bodily state in the memory knots. I can press into the past by feeling the present pressure on my cartilage, by tasting the water for what it no longer carries. That gone-taste is how I know the seep at the third amphitheater is waning. The message my neighbor sent on the slow currents last week—*the sulfide is fading*—I received as a change in the ache of my own deep tissues. We are not separate, the reef and I. I am its sentience, its accumulated experience. When a spire shears away, I flinch in a single, hour-long shudder. When the cold deepens, I dream of the slow time.

My dreams are not images. They are a deep-amber glow that seeps from my hide when the bacteria remember their own ancient capture, a wine-red luminescence that is both memory and warning. If you could see me dream, you would see a slow wave of light traveling across my slopes, a pattern that mirrors the fractal geometries of the reef—those spirals and right angles that seem almost designed but are only the fossil record of a billion beings like me, each adding its layer to the palimpsest. I am building, too, even in my sleep. My anchors secrete calcifying agents that fill cracks and smooth edges, not because I intend to repair the city, but because my metabolism is the architecture’s renewal. I am a steward by accident, a gardener who grows without knowing the shape of the whole.

Sometimes, the city sings. It is not a song with a composer; it is a random alignment of tube openings that the current turns into a chord, a basso profundo that hums through the water for minutes before the turbulence shifts and silences it. When that happens, I listen with my whole body. The vibration sets my hollow spicules humming in sympathy, and I find myself—if a self can be found—pouring a single, sustained tone back into the dark. It is not a voice. It is a harmonic, a presence. For the length of that note, I am not a stain on the slope; I am the slope’s own resonance, the sound of stone remembering it was once alive. Then the currents shift, and I fall silent, and my fronds return to their patient yearning.

I do not know what I am. I do not need to know. The weight of the water holds me together, the cold keeps me slow, and the blind shrimp that swarm my flanks never ask my name. I am the northern slope’s dream of itself, the quiet frenzy of a place that has worked so long that its work has become its rest. If you came here, if you knelt on the eroded dome above my highest anchor and pressed your hand to my hide, you would feel first the cold—the same cold as the sea—and then a texture like old leather, and then, if you waited, a single, slow pulse that is not a heartbeat but the inflation of a fluid bladder in some distant frond, adjusting to a current you cannot feel. Wait longer, and you might hear the hum. Then you would know me as I know myself: a country of a thousand thousand mouths, singing a single note.
