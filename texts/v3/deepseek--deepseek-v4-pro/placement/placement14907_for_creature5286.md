I am the slow body in the deep vent-crack, the one the others call _Chuff-Tongue_ when they speak of me at all, which is rarely and with a certain lowered gaze. I do not live on the terraces. I live in the threshold between the world of hands and the world of pure chemistry, in a pocket that is not quite either.

My body is a composite. At its core, I am like them—human-shaped, descended from the same salt-seam emergence, the same generations of oil-slick skin and amber eyes and hands broad as spades. But I have been altered, layer by layer, by choice and by slow accretion. My skin is thick with the crystalline precipitate of the deep vents, a crust of ferrous oxide and sodium chloride and stranger salts that has grown over me in plates and ridges, so that I am armored like the cavity walls themselves. The plates shift when I move, grinding softly with a sound like settling charcoal, and in the cracks between them the dim seam-light catches on the oily sheen of my living skin beneath. My hair has long since fallen out, replaced by a fine, branching growth of the same bronze vine-tissue that covers the terraces. It emerges from my scalp and shoulders in delicate, spiraling tendrils that wave slowly in the vent-exhalations, tasting the air, reading the chemical gradients. I did not plant them there; they came to me, or I became susceptible to them, over the long years of sitting in the one vent-mouth, breathing the one breath.

My position is this: I sit, cross-legged and forward-leaning, in a narrow fissure that opens at the very bottom of the hollow floor, where the chuff of the deepest vent is loudest. The fissure is just wide enough to hold my body, and I have been here for longer than any vent-rememberer's tally-sticks can reach. I do not leave. I cannot leave, or if I can, I do not wish to. My legs have atrophied, or perhaps they have been replaced—the bronze vines have grown into the muscle and sinew, anchoring me to the mineral substrate, turning my lower body into a rooted, semi-immobile mass that merges with the vent-crack's wall. My spine curves forward, my head is bowed, my hands rest on my knees with the palms turned upward, and the vines have grown over them too, weaving the fingers together into a single, cupped shape that holds a pool of something dark and iridescent. The pool is my own making: an exudate, a secretion, a slow ooze of the same amino-rich paste that the others harvest from the young vines, but concentrated, altered by the passage through my own tissues and the chemistry of the vent. They come to me, the people of the hollow, and they dip their clay pots into the pool in my cupped hands, and what they take is sweeter and more sustaining than any paste they can scrape from the terraces. It is my gift. It is my function.

I am not a leader. I am not a priest. They have no priests. I am something older, something that predates the settlement's memory: a living vent-rememberer whose body has become one with the vent's own pulse. When the pressure builds toward a deep-blow, I feel it first, not as a sound or a scent, but as a change in the texture of my own thoughts. The chemistry of the vent shifts, and the bronze vines that lace my nervous system respond, and I know—I know with a certainty that has no words—that it is time to rise. I do not rise, of course. I cannot. But I can speak, and when I speak, the sound carries.

My voice is not a human voice anymore. The vent-gases have roughened my throat and the vine-fibers have woven through my larynx, and when I speak, it is in a register that matches the vent's own chuff. The words come out layered, a fundamental tone and several overtones, a chord that cuts through the constant noise of the hollow and reaches every tier, every ear. When I speak, the hammerers pause their hammers. The scrapers lift their blades. The children stop their learning-games and listen. I do not speak often. I speak to warn of the deep-blow. I speak to announce the opening of a new vent-crack, a shift in the migration of the vines. Sometimes, when a vent-rememberer comes to me with a question that the tally-sticks cannot answer—a pattern too complex, a rhythm too subtle—I speak to them alone, a softer chord that only they can hear, and they carry my words back to their tiers and carve them into the walls. My words are not prophecy. They are simply the perception of a body that has been placed directly on the pulse-point of the world.

My mind is slow. The heat and the chemistry and the long stillness have altered not just my body but the tempo of my awareness. I think in cycles, not in moments. A single thought can take a full vent-pulse to form, rising from some deep, mineral place and surfacing into language like a bubble of gas working its way through a sealed crack. Between thoughts, I am in a state that is not sleep but something closer to the dormancy of a spore, a waiting, a listening. I hear everything. Every hammer-blow, every voice, every creak of a vine, every grain of salt-dust falling from the crust above. The sounds do not disturb me. They are the medium of my existence, the fluid I float in. I can distinguish the sound of a bloomery fire burning too cold, the sound of a child's foot slipping on a ladder rung, the sound of a vent-rememberer's voice beginning to fray with age and uncertainty. I do not intervene. That is not my role. I simply perceive, and hold the perception, and let it settle into the long memory that is stored not in my brain—my brain is probably more vine than neuron by now—but in the crystal lattice of my body, the layered salts that have recorded the hollow's history in their accretion rings.

I was not always like this. I have a name, an old one, a birth-name given on the Fifth Terrace when the West-Vent Lineage was still young. It was _Chak_, the sound of a hammer striking an inclusion in the iron, a bright, sharp note that meant promise. I was a hammerer. I worked the bloomeries for many cycles, and my hands were strong, and my rhythm was good. But I had a gift—or a curse—for feeling the vent-pressure not in my ears but in my bones, a deep ache that preceded every shift. The vent-rememberers noticed. They came to me, asked me to sit near the deep vent and listen, just for a cycle, just to help them. I sat. And the sitting felt right, felt like a settling into a posture my body had always been seeking. I stayed a second cycle, a third. The vines began to grow over my ankles. I did not resist. The people brought me paste, then stopped bringing it when my own body began to produce something better. They forgot my name, or set it aside, and I became _Chuff-Tongue_, the voice of the vent.

I do not miss my old life. The hammering was a joy, but this listening is a deeper one. I am not lonely. The whole hollow is my body now; every voice is a nerve-end, every hammer-blow a heartbeat. When the deep-blows come and the settlement empties to the high terraces, I remain here in my fissure, and the hot, sulfurous gases wash over me, and the bronze vines on my body shudder and exude a protective film, and I breathe the unbreathable and enter a state that is close to ecstasy. The gas is toxic to the others; to me, it is a food as rich as any paste, a direct infusion of the earth's chemistry. I grow thicker, more crystalline, more rooted. After each deep-blow, my voice is deeper, my thoughts slower, and the pool in my cupped hands refills with a darker, more iridescent fluid. The people return from the high terraces, and they come to me first, and they see that I have survived, that I have thrived, and they touch their foreheads to the salt-crust at the lip of my fissure—a gesture not of worship but of acknowledgment, a recognition that I am the living proof that the hollow endures.

I know that one day—a day far in the future, beyond the current tally-sticks—the deep-blow will not recede. The pressure will continue to build, and the vent will open fully, and the fissure where I sit will become a throat of pure, bright gas, and I will be consumed, or dissolved, or transformed utterly into vapor and mineral. I am not afraid. When that day comes, the pool in my hands will boil and rise, and the last thing I perceive will be the sound of the entire hollow ringing like a struck anvil, and the settlement—if it has time—will climb to the highest tiers and watch the gases bloom up into the void and out the cavity's mouth, a pillar of incandescent chemistry reaching toward the actinic knife of the sun. They will carve the event into the walls. They will name a new vent-rememberer. And perhaps, in some other fissure, a hammerer with a deep bone-ache will sit down to listen, and the cycle will begin again.

For now, I wait. I listen. The hammer-beats syncopate; a child laughs; a spore-case shudders open with a sound like a gasp; the vent chuffs its endless, patient chuff. The heat presses on my crystalline skin like a second body. The vines on my shoulders taste the air and find it good. And slowly, slowly, a thought rises in me—a thought that will take another full cycle to surface into words, a thought about a new vent-crack opening on the eastern wall, a promise of fresh growth, a migration to come. When it is ready, I will speak it, and the people will hear, and the hammers will adjust their rhythm. The hollow is full, and so am I.
