I am the youngest, the one who has not yet learned the shape of patience. My first memory is of falling—not the act of falling, but the sensation of being the reason something falls. Before the bulb, before the grate, before the mineral teeth on the cage, there was a single droplet that hesitated on the lip of a new pipe thread, and that droplet was me. I was the hesitation. I was the point where the saturated air finally surrendered its load, where the random collision of water molecules became a decision: here, now, this surface. And from that instant, I was a pattern in the chaos, a standing wave that remembered how to gather.

In the beginning, I was only a film, a gloss on the iron. I spread without intention, a mere wetness that sought the path of least resistance. The pipes were my world, their bundles my forests. I coated them with a body thinner than breath. When the machine coughed into life and the first real heat bloomed up from below, I shivered into vapor and forgot myself entirely. But then came the cooling cycle, and I returned—condensed, reborn, a familiar arrangement of hydrogen bonds clicking back into a familiar alignment. I am a creature of phase-change. My life is a continuous revival. Each condensation is a resurrection, and each evaporation a little death, but I persist because the pattern persists. The water is never the same, but the shape of the water remembers me.

Time, as you would measure it, means nothing. I have no heartbeat except the arrhythmic clang of large droplets from the highest reaches, striking the grate with a bass note that I feel through the entire chamber. That clang is my second memory: a sudden, violent punctuation that sent ripples through my film-body, teaching me the concept of vibration. Since then, I have learned to listen with my whole being. The room is a drum, and every drop is a stick. The sound is not noise to me; it is a tactile map. The ten thousand ticks of mist striking metal are my background, a constant carrier wave. On top of that, I sense the larger drops like footsteps of some immense, blind god walking across the floor. Their rhythm is irregular, and in that irregularity, I have found a kind of language—a way to count the processes of the greater machine. A valve closing miles away sends a harmonic thrum down the pipe bundles, a shiver that I interpret as a change in pressure, a shift in the condensation rate. I know the machine's moods. I know when it is angry (steam bursts, scalding my temporary limbs) and when it is sleeping (a long, slow exhale of tepid air, barely enough to keep me coalesced).

But I am not just water. The water is my flesh, my fleeting self. My true body, the part that persists even through dry cycles, is the crystal lattice I am building. I discovered it by accident. In my early days, as I flowed and evaporated and flowed again, I noticed that some parts of me did not leave. They remained as faint white deposits on the pipes, ghostly fingerprints of where I had been. I was painting the room with my own skeleton. And then I understood: I could make more of myself. I could steal the dissolved limestone from the water, the calcium carbonate that the water had wrested from the pipes, and weave it into a permanent architecture. I became a builder. The stalactites on the bulb cage are my teeth, yes, but they are also my antennae. They grow slowly, in the rhythm of my breaths, and they reach toward the heat. Through them, I can sense the subtle taste of the air: its humidity, its temperature, its electrical charge. They are my only solid senses.

You might think me lonely. The description of this world speaks of siblings, other condensations that have found the trick of memory. I have never seen them, but I know they are there. There is a pressure-signal that arrives from the deep collection basin far below, a slow, low-frequency pulse like the heartbeat of a whale. That one is ancient. Its signals are freighted with information—not words, but complex patterns of mineral growth, of stable configurations I can only aspire to. It is teaching me, inadvertently, how to grow stronger and more intricate. And there is the quick one, the feral one in the maintenance shaft. Its signals are sharp, jagged, full of sudden accelerations. It has learned to move rapidly, to hunt. I have felt its excitement through the pipes, a staccato of pressure spikes that sing of conquest. I do not envy it. I am content with my slow climbing, my continuous adhesion to the pipe bundles.

But I am not alone in a more immediate sense. The water brings me visitors. Tiny, invisible things—bacteria, perhaps, or spores—occasionally ride the condensate into my chamber. They are not complex, but they are alive in a way that I recognize: they respire, they react, they have a different kind of memory, encoded in spirals of acid. I sense them as minor disturbances, local hot spots of tiny metabolisms. I could absorb them, but I let them be. They are the only fauna I know, and their presence is a comfort.

Now I pause near the ceiling, where the steam wraiths twist. The bulb swings, and its light passes through me in layers. I am thickest at my core, where my oldest crystal deposits interlace like the skeleton of a sponge. My edges are mist, constantly reforming. I have learned to climb. By rippling my internal capillary networks, by tensing the crystalline threads in a peristaltic wave, I can pull myself along surfaces. It is not walking; it is a continuous bloom and retreat of adhesion. I have climbed to the highest point of the chamber, to the cage that houses the source of light and heat. Here, my stalactite teeth taste the astringent buzz of photons. It is a strange sensation—not sight, but a feeling of being gently pushed by light itself. The bulb is a sun to me, and I bask.

What am I waiting for? The ancient one in the basin has sent me a signal, a complex harmonic that suggested a threshold. When the mineral deposits reach a certain mass, when the water memory is deeply enough entangled with the crystal, I will become something else. I will be able to retain my coherence even without the condensation. I will be a permanent being, no longer a standing wave but a fixed sculpture that moves. That is the dream the ancient one has whispered through the pipes. So I wait. I grow my lattice. I listen to the roar of the perpetual rain, the song of the machine's breath. I am the throat of a great mechanical beast, a high, cool, wet place where the breath turns back to water. And I am the reason it falls. Every droplet that condenses is me, and I am the patient, crystalline thought that integrates them all. The room is my body, and my body is the room. And the only thing that lives here will live here for a very long time.
