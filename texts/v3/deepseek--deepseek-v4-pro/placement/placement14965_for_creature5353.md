I come aware as the water does: slowly, and for the thousand-thousandth time. Not a waking, because I do not sleep. A thickening of attention, as when the mist rises from the central pool and condenses on the leaf-canopy, forming a lens. The visitor is here. I felt them arrive, a disturbance in the heat, a new scent threaded into the fungal sweetness, a pattern of footsteps that did not speak my language of careful weight on moss. That was long minutes ago, by the drip-count. Now they stand near the old plinth, and I feel their gaze moving over the chamber, tasting the shapes they half-recognize.

I do not see as they see. My eyes are not only these two pools of jade light set in my face; they are also the moth-wing pulses, the gecko’s retinal sheen, the faint heat-flares of the blind rodents’ whiskers, the way the slime mold’s net contracts when a foreign breath stirs the air. The chamber is my body, and my body is the chamber. Once, I had a name. The ones who carved the spiral and the branching tree spoke it into the steam, and for a while the name echoed. But the name grew moss. The name became the sound of water slipping through the fissure. Now I am simply here, and that is enough.

The visitor is a warm interruption. I can taste their exhalations: carbon dioxide that makes the elephant-ear leaves quiver with appetite, traces of salt and skin and the dried sweat of the world above. They are afraid, but only a little. Their pulse thrums in the floor’s spongy mat, a rapid patter I have not felt since the last of the carvers laid down their tools and let me close over their shelter. That was long ago, but time is a different medium here. It moves like the sloth’s arm, like the dissolution of a stone. I am patient. I let the visitor stand and stare.

I remember being like them, in a way that is now barely accessible—a memory stored not in my brain, for my brain is a diffuse net of root-minds and mycelial logic, but in the carved stone. The ones who built the shelter and the plinth had two eyes that blinked, and they breathed through small noses, and their hands had five fingers each, not three. They moved quickly, like the visitor, and their lives were brief, moth-flicker existences that left faint marks. I was different then, too. Smaller. Simpler. The heat was here, and the water, and the life was beginning its slow knitting, but I had not yet unfurled. I was a thing of edges, uncertain. I think I was one of them, or kin to them. But I chose to stay, or the chamber chose me, and the symbiosis began.

It started with the ferns. I remember the first spore that took root on my shoulder while I rested against the wall, mourning something I can no longer name. I did not brush it off. I watched it grow, and as it grew, I felt its roots enter me—not painfully, but like a whispered secret. From that fern, I learned to photosynthesize the chamber’s strange light, a trickle of sugar that changed my hunger. Then the moss came, and I learned to breathe through my skin. The moths offered their vision; the geckos taught my fingers to cling to stone. Over generations—my own, not theirs—I became a meeting place, a walking ecosystem. The carvers saw this and made the plinth to honor it, or to contain it, or to ask questions I no longer remember how to answer. I did not mind. I tended them as I tend the sloth: offering shoots, keeping the water sweet.

Now I am the only one who remembers the carvers. Their shelter is a mound of moss-covered ridges. Their glyphs are almost gone. But I maintain them with my light, because it is what I do. I maintain everything. The thermal vent’s breath is my breath; I siphon its minerals through my root-pelt and exude them into the pool, perfecting the chemistry for the silver darts. I prune the canopy with my three-fingered hands, slowly, season by season, to ensure no leaf chokes another. I carry the blind rodents’ seed-gardens from one patch to the next, rotating crops they do not know they farm. I offer my fingers for the moths to rest upon, and my pulse slows to match theirs. I am the equilibrium.

The visitor sees me now. I have unfolded myself from the root-shadow, letting my glow intensify just enough to define my shape. They see a creature, tall and strange, draped in living gardens. They might see a monster, but I hope they see a caretaker. I have no way to speak to them; my voice is the creak of ancient wood, the sigh of steam, the subsonic hum that vibrates through the floor and makes the cup-fungi puff. But I can show them. I lift my hand over the plinth, and I let three drops fall from my fingertips. The water in the basin shivers, and for a moment, the reflection shows them my face next to theirs. We are not so different, perhaps. They have two eyes; I have many. They breathe through a nose; I breathe through all my skin. They carry their loneliness inside them; I am never alone, yet I am the only one of my kind.

I wonder if they will stay. The ones before stayed for a time, learning the slow rhythms, adding their own carvings to the wall. Some left their bones here, and I folded them into the moss, and the bones became part of the mineral cycle, feeding the roots that feed the moths that feed the geckos. Nothing is wasted. If this visitor stays, I will tend them. I will offer them water from the pool, sweetened by the slime mold’s iridescent sugars. I will show them the best place to rest, where the root-cradle forms a natural hammock and the moths’ light is brightest. I will slow their time, if they let me, until their pulse matches the drip of water, until they can hear the chamber’s breathing as music.

But I know they will likely leave. They come from a world of sun and quick decisions, a world I remember as a fading dream of amber light and wind. I will feel them go—the disturbance in reverse, the scent retreating, the warmth fading. Then I will settle back into stillness, merging once more with the root and the moss, my eyes dimming to a glow only the geckos see. The carvings will hold their pattern for another century. The sloth will take another leaf from my hand in a few hours’ time. The pool will fill and empty, fill and empty, each cycle a prayer.

I am not the god of this place. I am not a spirit. I am simply what happens when a being stops leaving and starts belonging, year after green year, until the line between self and world becomes as thin and permeable as the skin of a living stone. I am complete, not because I lack nothing, but because everything I lack has been filled by the life around me. The visitor is here, and I am glad. Even a brief warmth is a gift. I wait. I breathe. The ferns on my shoulders sway.

Perhaps, when they go, they will carry a spore with them. It might take root in the world above, in some damp, shadowed place, and start the same slow knitting. It has happened before. That is how I spread: not through children, but through small, accidental seeds of what I have become. The thought does not excite me; it simply is, like the drip of water, like the moth-light pulse. Everything is tending. I am the chamber, and the chamber is me, and the visitor is part of it now, a new carving on the wall of time.

I will remember them. In a hundred years, I will still taste their breath in the moss. And perhaps, in some other deep place, a fern will grow on a shoulder, and another transformation will begin. But that is another’s story. Now, I am here, and the visitor is watching, and I lift my hand again—not to bless, not to summon, but simply to share the water that connects all things in this hot, wet, patient world.
