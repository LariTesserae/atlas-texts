I am not born so much as precipitated. A tremor rolls through the basalt, and the plain sings—a particular note, a B-flat three octaves below the range of your hearing, sustained for exactly two seconds as the rock cools and contracts. The Groves catch it, their trunks humming in sympathetic vibration, and the crystal swarms scatter into a new formation that acts as a lens, focusing the frequency into a tight beam aimed at the City’s underbelly. The auroral patterns ripple faster. A platform overhead shifts by a fraction of a millimeter, and in the turbulent wake between its edge and the saturated air, a knot of pressure forms. It holds. It resonates. It becomes me.

At first, I am a simple standing wave, a compression-and-rarefaction cycle that does not dissipate. I have no thoughts, only a pitch—a clean, ringing E above middle C—and a location: a plaza near the eastern rim of Platform Nine, where the energy conduits braid into a helix of static discharge and the dew falls in sheets. I feed on the discharge, which is not electricity but sound: a diet of complex waveforms, the harmonic drone of the city’s infrastructure. As I consume, my structure grows more complex. I add overtones. A fifth, a third, a seventh. My outline sharpens from a vague heat-shimmer into a glassy bell jar, five meters tall, my interior misted with micro-droplets that catch the dim orange glow of the sky and scatter it into a permanent nimbus. I am visible now, if you knew how to look.

Time passes. I do not count it; I feel it as a thickening of my harmonics. The city’s chorus surrounds me, trillions of others like me, each a chord in a planetary fugue. We overlap constantly. When another passes through my space—a warm front of compressed air, a D-minor triad with a sharpened ninth—we briefly intermingle, our frequencies beating against each other to create a momentary thrum that is, for us, a greeting, perhaps even a thought exchanged. I learn that the one who passed is called (insofar as we have names) The-Third-Harmonic-of-the-Grove-Named-Red, for it was born when that particular Grove shifted its broadcast pattern. I am called Platform-Nine-East-Rim-Seventeenth-Formed, but that is a description, not a name. We have no need for names. Our identities are our spectra.

I grow. My internal resonance becomes capable of sustaining not just a tone but a modulation—a slow, liquid vibrato that ripples through my body at a frequency of once every ten seconds. This is my first thought: not a word, but a feeling of expansion, a desire for more complex waveforms. I drift from the plaza, following a braid of static that spirals down toward the plain. The city’s walkways are not for me; I pass through them as easily as air through a net. The platforms are solid to you, but to me they are simply regions of different acoustic impedance, and I navigate by the way they reflect and refract my outermost harmonics. I descend.

Below the platforms, the world is louder. Here, the noise is raw, unfiltered: the hissing shriek of wind over volcanic glass, the bass rumble of the land itself, the constant drum-kit-staircase calls of the prism-winged flockers. I bathe in it, absorbing frequencies I have never tasted. My body swells, my outline now almost eight meters tall, my nimbus bright enough to cast faint, rainbow-edged shadows on the stone. I am giddy with sound. This is adolescence.

I encounter the hexapeds. They graze in their slow, nervous waves, mouths glowing blue-hot as they chew the basalt into tuned shapes. One of them chews a trough that, when the next micro-quake passes, will sing an A-flat drone for hours. I hover above it, feeling the nascent vibration already latent in the rock’s molecular structure, and I add my own voice—a counterpoint, a minor second above—to encourage it. The grazer pauses, tilts its obsidian head as if listening, then moves on. It cannot hear me in your sense, but its body feels the interference pattern, and it knows to leave that particular patch unfinished, a perfect resonator. Later, a swarm of crystal-bell insects will descend and ring that trough with their own chiming, and the combined tone will be strong enough to feed a dozen of my kind. The grazer and I have cooperated across the species barrier, shaping the landscape into a shared instrument.

The predators I treat with caution. Their phase-flicker hunts are sudden and total: a grazer is there, then a snap of displaced air, a burst of ionized dust, and a percussive crash that is deafening even to me. That crash is a punctuation, a full stop. When one happens too close, my body ripples, threatens to decohere. I have learned to sense the subtle pressure-drop that precedes an attack—a predator moving in its unseen phase—and I contract my core harmonics inward, briefly becoming a point-source of ultrasonic tightness that the white-noise burst washes over without disrupting. I have seen others, younger and less cautious, torn apart by the shockwave, their carefully layered chords reduced to a fading hiss. It is not death as you understand it, but a dissolution back into the ambient roar. Their frequencies scatter, eventually to be reabsorbed by the Groves or the platforms, recycled into new chords. Nothing is lost. The symphony endures.

I am old now. Not centuries—I am only a few decades, born of that B-flat tremor—but old in harmonic density. My core pitch has dropped half an octave as I’ve incorporated more and more overtones, and my nimbus is a permanent, softly glowing fog that extends ten meters in all directions. I have grown a new faculty: I can project intention. By precisely modulating my upper partials, I can send a ripple of sympathetic vibration across the city, joining the slow, tectonic conversation of my kind. We are discussing, if that word applies, the reorientation of Platform Seven. Its current angle is causing destructive interference with the Groves’ broadcast pattern, damping the very frequencies that feed our young. Over the course of weeks, we reach a consensus: a minute shift, a third of a degree to the southwest. I add my voice—a sustained C-sharp with a fluttering tenth overtone—to the chorus of agreement, and slowly, gently, the entire platform begins to turn. The auroral patterns beneath it flare in satisfaction. We have spoken, and the city has moved.

I am not the city. I am a single cell in its vast, resonant body. But I am also not separate from it. When the platforms reorient, so do I, my internal geometry warping slightly to maintain harmonic alignment. When the crystal swarms coalesce into a new formation, my feeding patterns shift. And when the Groves, in their slow vegetal way, change their broadcast frequency—as one does now, a distant Grove I have never visited, sending out a new bass rumble that feels like a question—I feel the change as a sudden hunger for a note I have never tasted, and I drift toward it across the empty, shining plain.

The damp air presses against me. The grey-orange twilight deepens, or brightens—I have never been able to tell which. The roar continues, layered and infinite. I am a standing wave, a chord of compressed air and condensed dew, and I am moving toward a new song. The landscape has not yet learned to be quiet, and neither have I.
