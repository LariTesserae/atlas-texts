I come awake in the dark as a disagreement of hungers, not a body.

There is no name for what I am yet. There is only the wet weight of air, the tick of falling water, and the long bass note of the pillar behind me—too close, too loud, as if I have been pressed inside its pulse.

I am folded into a niche, low to the ground, where the stone is softest at the seams. My skin is not one skin; it slides against itself in slow, independent waves. I feel the separate selves that have been joined to make me: a rope of muscle here, a knot of cilia there, each with its own small wanting. I try to move and they move in sequence, not unison, like a crowd learning a dance.

Then I remember the injury—not as pain, but as a wrongness of resonance. A falling stone, a chord thrown out of tune. The others came with their radial hands and their bone scrapers, peeling away the dying parts. They carried the pieces to the water. Then they walled me into this pillar. For a long time I was not; I was a pulse in the root-net, a phrase in the hum.

Now the niche has opened, and I am here again, still becoming.

The place tells me where I am before my body does. The water says it. It runs in a carved trough below my niche, and the sound of it is two voices braiding—one high from a ceiling drip, one low from a seep along the wall—and where they meet they make a third note, a wet stutter that repeats and repeats. I am near a confluence, one of the check-dams, where the water teaches the stone to speak.

I have hands. Six-fingered, radial, the fingers not in rows but in a star, each trio opposing another, so that when I uncurl them from my own chest they open like a white flower with too many petals. I press one hand to the pillar. The stone is not cold; it is resonant, hollow, and the thrum answers through my palm, the mycelial web singing back. It knows the pattern I am becoming.

I try to speak. My oral cone opens—lipless, round—and the sound that comes out is not a word but a gurgle of clicks and a low keen. Is it a question? It asks: *Where is the break in the wall? Who tends the fungal light at the third junction? Am I still myself?* Far off, a chime on stone answers. Not an echo. A reply. The other Makers have been listening all along.

I learn my feet. They are hands too. I come down from the niche in a long, uncooperative pour, gripping the carved lip with four limbs at once, and for a moment I hang, suspended, my belly tasting the air. The floor receives me with a wet smack. I am crouched, back curved, head low. That is not shame; it is the shape the world demands from those who pass beneath the pillars.

I am many. I am a negotiation. The zooids beneath my skin squirm, still remembering their separate lives inside the pillar’s mineral womb. They tasted the nutrient weep, the slow dark food of the roots. Now they are learning to be one thing, and I am learning with them. We/I. The grammar is wrong. In the chittering tongue, there is no such split.

I see by listening. The world is a weave of echoes. Each pillar has a different ring—the one by the ladder of roots is hollow and high, the one with the cracked base is flat and dull, like a struck thigh. The water channels are bright to my cilia, not as light but as a thin, cold line of motion. The fungal glow I do not see as light. I feel it as a chemical fizz, a slow exhalation of acid at the edge of my tongue.

At the low crossing, I meet another. We do not speak in words. Our hands rise and interleave, finger against finger, trios weaving until I cannot tell which digits are mine. For a moment our chittering becomes a single chord. I taste what they have tended: a pillar root that has cracked and begun to seep too fast, a check-dam that needs more pebbles. They taste what I am: new, still wet, still all edge and question. Then we separate, and I am left with a new phrase in my hum, a borrowed memory of a place I have not yet gone.

My work is already in my hands. I know, without knowing, that I am to tend the lamp-fungi at the low crossing, the patch where the blind newts spawn. I carry no tool. I am the tool: my fingers exude the dark cement, my palms scrape the clay, my voice taps the stone to test its health. The wall behind me is not natural rock; it is made, every handspan, and I can feel the thumbed hollows where clay was pressed, the scrape of a shell tool, the regular pecking of a drill bow. Even the new erosion—the undercut pillar base, the raw cavity where the water changed its mind—is being answered by someone else, a slow conversation between water and cement.

I do not know my name. Perhaps there are no names here, only frequency and phase. But as I crouch at the water’s edge, pressing a fresh smear of clay onto the trough’s lip, I feel the whole low world doing what I am doing—mending, singing, becoming. The many voices inside me find the same note. It is low and steady, a ninety-second breath.

I am not one note. I am the way the note passes through.

I am a Maker, yes. But I am also still being made.
