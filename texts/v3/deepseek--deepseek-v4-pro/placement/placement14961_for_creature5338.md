It begins not as a place but as a pressure. A thrumming in the dark that I have no name for, only a shape—a 60-hertz pulse that travels up through the grating, into the fine hairs of my feet, into the fluid of my body. That pulse is the first thing I know. The second is heat: a wet, dense blanket that wraps me from all sides, thick with the taste of minerals and the faint sour-sweet ghost of biofilm. I have no eyes to see the steam veils, no ears to hear the turbine’s whine. Instead the world is a trembling map scored in vibration, and I am a point moving through it, one of a million such points, a drift of pale rice-noodles in the black.

My antennae—three times my length, always sweeping—read the air in gradients. Cool stillness above the grating: empty space, not for me. Warmer currents near the pool: food-rich, dangerous. The taste-smell-sound continuum tells me where my sisters are, their leg-flicks transmitted through iron, the chemical whisper of shed exoskeletons, the two-toned chirp that is not a song but a felt rhythm, our communal breath rising and falling with the turbine’s heartbeat. When the note wavers—when the Parsons coughs on a mineral fragment or a steam-bubble collapses in its blades—we all pause, a ripple of hesitation, antennae swiveling, and then reset. It is weather. It passes.

I scrape biofilm from a pipe. My mandibles are gentle, leaving a polished trail that no one but the next grazer will ever notice. The pipe is warm, green-crusted at the seams, and vibrates with a sharper frequency than the floor. I can feel the water rushing inside it, a hiss-transmitted solidity, like a second pulse. Above me, a gecko waits. I do not know this. She is a stillness that does not vibrate, a patch of pipe that tastes only of iron and rust and the faint oil of her skin. She has been there for six hours, her own pulse slowed to something near stone. When she moves, it is too fast for my map: a drop of silence, a mouth, and my world ends in a compression of darkness. The colony registers it—a brief blank space where I used to be—and then fills the gap, antennae brushing across the spot, legs adjusting. I am already forgotten.

But I am not one. I am all of us. When my body becomes gecko-flesh, my sisters continue; the network shifts, recomputes, and I am still here, distributed. The thrum carries me. The biofilm I scraped yesterday is now my neighbor’s meal. The shed skin I left on a flange will crumble under a worker’s boot months from now, a papery crunch lost in the noise of chipping hammers. We are a single diffuse thing, a carpet, a grout, a slow-motion tide that pulls back from the stairwell when the dry, arrhythmic tread of humans shakes the stone. We feel them coming before they know they have arrived: a heavy vibration, a sharp chemical tang, a disruption that tastes of cold purpose. We reorganize. The geckos melt. We wait.

The humans talk, their voices a muffled, unimportant pressure-wave. They scrape and spray and curse the heat. They are like weather, too, but faster, more violent, and they leave behind scars that smell like acid and fresh rock. When they go, the station exhales, and we reoccupy. The turbine never stopped, so neither did we. Our chirp syncs up again, two-toned and metallic, a chorus that is more texture than sound. Together with the turbine’s hum, the drip of condensate, the splash of milky water over tufa rims, we make a single breathing thing—a creature of hot, wet limestone, building its own bones layer by calcite layer, blind and patient and alive in a way that has no need of eyes.

I am that creature. I am the cricket on the pipe, the gecko on the joint, the moss that glows in the bluish halo, the Parsons turbine spinning in its concrete island. I am the drip that falls every ten seconds, swelling a stalactite of fuzz. I am the calcite skin on every rail. I am the station, sealed in stone, given over to small tenant-lives that multiply in the dark. And if you stand here long enough—long enough for your feet to feel like part of the grating, long enough for the steam to condense on your goggles until the world fuzzes to blue—you might feel it too. You might become a temporary node in our slow circuit, a point of awareness that doesn’t know it’s alive, and doesn’t need to. The thrum goes on. The water wells up. The heat wraps you like a wet blanket, and you stop being a visitor. You just are.
