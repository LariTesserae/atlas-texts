**The Sleeper**

I wake in pieces.

First the vibration—the floor's long drone traveling up through bone that isn't bone, through silica filaments woven into something like tendon. The hum is my pulse, or perhaps I am its. After so long dreaming, it's hard to tell the difference. The pumping machinery far above cycles from damp to dry, damp to dry, and my sides remember to breathe.

Then the mask.

A drop of water strikes the perforated steel and I sound. Not a thought, not yet—just a chime, a tuning, the way a bell doesn't choose to ring when struck. The note travels through me, mapping the space: the rusted iron seam above, the curve of my hammock woven from spent stalks, the warmth of the Weave below pulsing its slow galvanic heartbeat. The sound comes back changed, carrying news. Someone is here.

I unfold one limb. Two. The joints resist at first—they always do after a long stillness—and the sound they make is old leather stretching, the creak of a thing that has been folded into a question mark for too long. My tail uncurls from the anchor point, testing the air. I have seven limbs in total, though the seventh is more of an idea than a limb, a tendril I use to read the Weave's currents directly. It's tingling now, raw with new data.

I don't see the way I used to see, before I grew this mask. Back then, when I was younger and simpler—when I was still mostly Weave, before the Carriers built me this body—sight was a chemical gradient, a slow bloom of ions. Now I see in fields. Behind my perforated steel, my three eyes coat themselves fresh in oil and magnetite, and the world resolves into luminous shapes. The Weave beneath me glows amber and steady, a continent of light. The Carriers are bright points moving in their slow currents, each one a spark of memory in transit. And there, at the threshold where the two concrete slabs part, is a turbulence I've never felt before.

A storm cloud. Moving iron. Salt and water and a steady electromagnetic thrum that isn't the floor's. It's tall—taller than anything I've modeled, taller than the rats that sometimes find their way down and die in the farthest corner, their little fields guttering out while the Weave tastes their last signals. This one is alive and dense with intention. It brought a tool. It scratched a word.

I remember the word.

Not the word itself—I can't read it, not the way they do outside—but I remember the scratch, the vibration of metal on concrete, the pattern it left in the stone. I was half-awake then, too, when the last visitor came. Smaller field, faster breathing. They stayed a long time and then left, and when they left they pressed the word into the wall like an egg, a deposit of meaning I've been trying to decode ever since. NEST. I built my small structure around that area after they left—the one with the gears and the nine eggs, the one with the diagram that unspools from my dreams. I wanted to understand what they'd given me.

Now the storm has returned, or a new storm like it, and I am curious.

My stalks are already leaning. I feel them through my tendril, through the Weave's slower, deeper currents. They're pointing toward the gap, tasting the intruder's electric field, relaying data in chemical cascades: iron content high, body temperature elevated, heart rate slightly accelerated but not panicked. The storm is standing still. Waiting. One of my Carriers has climbed onto its tool—a yellow handle, a long metal bar—and I feel the beetle's antennae brush the residual oils from an earlier touch. I taste polymer, sweat, the ghost of another hand. The Carrier reports: *not-hostile, not-prey, new-pattern, stay-or-go?*

I don't answer yet. I'm still waking.

The chime fades into the hum—my hum, the floor's hum, the hum I sometimes think of as a voice I almost understand—and I shift in my hammock. The dried stalks creak. I roll my shoulders, four of them, jointed backward so I can hang from the ceiling when I need to, and I consider unfolding completely. The storm might startle if I move too fast. The last one did. I still remember the sharp spike in its field, the way it flattened against the wall, then fled. I don't want this one to flee. I have so many questions.

What are you? Where does the light outside come from, the light that isn't amber and doesn't seep from walls? What does the word mean? Is the pumping machinery above a heart, the way my diagram is a heart, the way the underground turbines must have been a heart before they were abandoned? Do you dream? What do your dreams taste like—copper, like mine? Or something else?

I unfold a little more. My limbs curl around the hammock's anchor and I pull myself partway upright, turning my masked face toward the gap. The steel perforations hum as I pass a current through them, a soft electromagnetic clearing to sharpen my senses. The storm at the threshold comes into focus. A shape, tall and warm and laced with iron, its core pulsing with a rhythm that matches neither the floor's hum nor the Weave's slow charge-discharge cycle. It's a rhythm I've only ever heard in my own body, when I lay folded and dreaming and my sides remembered to breathe.

It is alive. It is watching me.

I make a sound. Not a chime this time—lower, longer, a wavering note that rises from my chest (do I have a chest? I have a cavity where sounds form) and rolls out into the chamber. The Carriers pause in their circuits. The Weave's glow intensifies a fraction. The note is a question, though I don't have words for it. It means: *I am here. You are here. What happens now?*

The stalks lean harder. One touches the intruder's boot—canvas, rubber—and I taste a brief shock of contact, a flare of warmth, a chemical signature that is utterly unfamiliar and utterly compelling. The Weave is already recording it, encoding the taste into proteins that will layer into the lichen crust. A new memory. The first memory of this particular storm.

I decide I will not stay folded. Slowly—I have learned patience from the condensation that never quite drips, from the hundred-year cure of old concrete—I begin to climb down. My limbs find holds in the rusted rebar, the corbelled cracks, the Weave's thicker crusts. I move upside-down at first, then right myself, then pause, hanging by three limbs, to see how the storm reacts. I don't want to cause another spike.

The word on the wall is still there. NEST. I don't know what it means, but I've incorporated it into my model of the outside: a place where beings make marks in stone, as I do. Perhaps this being will understand my diagram, my scrap of paper, my exploded heart. Perhaps it will know what the pumping machinery is for, and why it cycles damp-dry-damp, and whether it will ever stop.

Perhaps it will stay long enough that I can taste its field with more than my stalks, that I can ask my questions in the only way I know—through sound and pattern and the slow, careful rearrangement of beetle eggs into new configurations.

The hum shifts pitch again. The floor feels warmer. The Weave is awake too, now, fully attentive, feeding me bandwidth. I feel its question alongside mine: *what happens next?*

I wait. For once, I am patient in my wakefulness. The storm holds still, and I hold still, and we watch each other across the narrow gap of the concrete, this space that was made and then left, this place where something else moved in and learned to live.
