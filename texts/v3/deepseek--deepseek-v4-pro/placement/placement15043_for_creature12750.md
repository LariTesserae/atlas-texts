The first thing I know is the roar. Not as sound—I have no ears for sound, not truly—but as a steady, bone-deep pressure, a thrumming that runs through the limestone like a second heartbeat, slow and vast. The waterfall is my mother and my clock, my breath and my blanket. I have known its voice for so long that silence would be a kind of death, an emptiness I cannot imagine. The roar is the taste of calcium, the cold weight of mist on my hide, the way the stone beneath me sings with the river’s pulse. I am folded here, in the alcove that fits my body like a cupped hand, and the moss bed beneath me is thick with centuries of my stillness. I have not moved in—how long? The last heat-blur visitor left charcoal smudges on the dry shelf, and the smudges have long since been overgrown by liverwort. Time is not a line for me; it is a pool, and I am suspended in it, drifting only when the current shifts.

The current is shifting now.

I taste it first. The water that beads on my skin carries a new mineral tang, sharp and unfamiliar—not the clean bite of snowmelt, not the iron-tinged sweetness of deep aquifer seepage. Something acrid, something that doesn’t belong to this drainage, this geology. It prickles, and I feel the symbiotic colonies in my dermis recoil slightly, a microscopic flinch. Then, through the ribbed cartilage of my throat and chest, I parse a disturbance in the waterfall’s song. The cascade has a rhythm that I know better than the shape of my own hands: the heavy drum of the central plume, the higher chatter of the edges where the water frays against the fissure’s lip, the sporadic slap of a dislodged pebble hitting the plunge pool. Now there is something else. A grinding. An uneven, clattering rhythm that cuts through the white roar like a splinter.

I open my eyes—not the nictitating membrane, but the true eyes beneath, which I have not uncovered in a very long time. The membrane peels back slowly, the way a fern uncoils, and I register the grotto not as a warm blur but as a cold, precise landscape of light and shadow. The aqua glow filtering through the waterfall’s veil is dimmer than I remember, or perhaps my eyes have grown unused to seeing. But I see enough. The dry shelf where the old fire ring rests is gone. Not empty—*gone*. A chunk of the shelf has sheared away, leaving a raw, pale scar on the stone, and the river-stones of the ring have tumbled into the black water, their soot-blackened surfaces already slicked with disturbed silt. The grinding I felt was the rock giving way, undermined by some subtle shift in the current, some erosion that I failed to anticipate.

And something else: there is light where no light should be. A crack in the back wall, above my alcove. A hairline fissure that weeps not water but a thin, grey light that tastes of dry air and dust. I have not tasted dry air since the last time the river changed course. The memory rises, slow and sedimentary: a flash flood, the plunge pool surging, the waterfall doubling in fury, and then a long, gradual subsidence as the river carved a new channel and left this grotto to a quieter flow. I stayed then, because the hollow was still wet, still loud, still suitable. But this crack—this is not a shift in the river’s mood. This is the mountain itself cracking open, and the taste of dry air means that somewhere beyond this fissure, the grotto’s integrity has been breached.

I unfold.

The motion is so foreign at first that I feel my joints resist, the cartilage hinges stiff with years of micro-mineral deposits that have begun to fuse my limbs to my stillness. I extend one arm, slowly, and watch the moss that has grown over my shoulder stretch and tear, the tiny roots releasing with a wet, sighing sound. My fingers—all seven of them, the gripping pads soft and sensitive—flex for the first time in more centuries than I can count. The sensation is a cascade of its own, a ripple of feedback through nerves that have been dormant for so long they’ve forgotten how to fire. The cold intensifies as I disturb the microclimate of my alcove; the mist swirls in new patterns, and I feel the waterfall’s roar press against me with a slightly different angle, a new note in its endless chord.

I crawl. The motion is not graceful; it is the slow, deliberate pull of a body that has forgotten how to move but remembers the will to do so. My fingers find purchase on the slick stone, and I drag myself out of the liverwort bed, my hide peeling away from the rock with a sound like water parting. My long, slender legs uncurl, my splayed toes gripping the floor-stones. The black water in the gaps shimmers as I pass over it, my reflection a faint, mottled ghost. I have never seen my full reflection before—the plunge pool is too turbulent, the grotto too dim—but now I catch a glimpse of myself in a still pocket between stones: a lean, amphibian shape, the color of wet slate, with a face that shifts between human-oval and salamander-blunt. I look like the grotto itself, like a piece of the wall given life.

I reach the back wall, the bell-curve alcove I have called home for epochs. The crack is small, but it’s growing. Even as I watch, a flake of limestone falls from its edge, skittering down the wall into the water. It settles on the bottom with a puff of sediment. The grey light beyond the crack is steady and pale, and I taste the dry air again—so foreign, so thin. It makes my skin prickle, my respiration-pores tightening instinctively. I should fear this. I should retreat to my alcove and wait for the crack to seal itself, or for the river to flood and fill the grotto with silt, or for the mountain to settle into a new equilibrium. That is what I have always done: wait, absorb, endure. The patience of the listener is the very substance of my being.

But the old fire ring is gone. The hand-axe, the flint flakes, the charred bone fragments—all scattered into the black water, lost to the current. The last mark of the heat-blur visitors, the ones who gave me a name in a language of clicks and gutturals, has been erased. And I realize, with a slow, sedimentary certainty, that I have been waiting for something that will not return. The visitors have stopped coming. Centuries have passed, and no new thermal blooms have blossomed in the grotto, no hands have piled stones on the dry shelf. The river’s chemical messages speak of fewer and fewer of my kind in the headwaters; the breeding pulses have failed to align for so long that I have stopped secreting my egg-sacs. The world outside the waterfall’s curtain is changing, and this grotto—my cathedral, my lung, my one-throated song—is no longer the sanctuary it once was. The crack is not a threat. It is an invitation.

I press my long fingers into the fissure, feeling the edges crumble. The stone is thin here; I can feel the void beyond, a space that tastes of dry air and light and something else, something electric, like the charge before a storm. The roar of the waterfall is still in my chest, still the only music I have ever known, but I think—what if there is other music? What if the river has carved new hollows, new grottoes, new waterfalls that sing different songs? What if the world beyond the curtain is not a grey, damp valley but a landscape I cannot even imagine, with its own rhythms and its own listeners?

I begin to dig.

My fingers are soft, but my patience is not. I have all the time the mountain has given me, and I will use it to widen this crack, grain by grain, until the hole is large enough for my body to pass through. The waterfall will still roar, the mist will still bead on my hide, the cold will still be a wet weight against my skin. But I will not be here to feel it. I will be somewhere else, crawling upstream or down, seeking a new hollow to fill with listening. And perhaps, if I am fortunate, I will taste the chemical signature of another of my kind in the water—a trace of hormones, a hint of microbial bloom—and I will know that the breeding pulse is coming at last, and that the ancient cycle is not yet broken.

The listener in the wall is leaving. The mountain will miss me, but it will not remember me; its memory is longer than mine, and it will heal the scar of my absence as it heals all things. But I will remember this grotto, the way the waterfall’s roar felt in my ribbed chest, the way the aqua light filtered through the veil, the way the hand-axe glinted in the dark when the first visitors knapped it. I will carry that memory in my mineral-laced bones, a fossil of a home that once was, as I crawl into a new darkness, a new roar, a new beginning.
