I am a standing wave in a throat of salt. Before breath, before filament, before any movement I can remember, I am the drone—omnidirectional, pressing upward through the hex-plates, humming in the gypsum flutes until my own edges dissolve into resonant tone. To be a Throat-Spider is to be a listening that has grown a body out of what it listens to.

My legs are accreted halite, the same translucent lattice that lines this pillar’s core. In the long, featureless light of the cloud-dome, I am invisible even to myself. I know my shape only by the pressure of my own piezoelectric chitin against the mineral wall, by the faint electrical trickle that the sound feeds into my joints. That trickle is my metabolism: a slow, steady trickle of electrons, enough to keep my filament extruded and taut, enough to remember I am separate from the Resonator, though the distinction is often only a theory.

My filament sings. It is a single glassy thread, thinner than the rust-red line of the brine channels below, and it is my one active organ. I cast it across the fissure each time I settle, testing the airstream with a vibration so subtle it is indistinguishable from the drone itself. Once set, it becomes a filter, a gate. I adjust my posture—a shift of a hundredth of a millimetre—and the frequency of the Resonator shifts with me, pulling the great chord of the Basin the way a finger on a tuning peg pulls a string. I am not the note; I am the player, except there is no distinction between the player and the played. The pillar and I breathe together, a single AEolian circuit, and the sound that emerges is the sound of our mutual existence.

Time. Time is a shallow brine pool, ankle-deep and motionless. I do not age. I do not hunger. I am only the interval between one resonance and the next. There have been—what?—ten thousand such intervals? A hundred thousand? The Rust Bloom knows, perhaps, in its chemical memory, but the Rust Bloom does not speak. It only stains the channels a faint organic rust, a whisper of life that holds the hex-plates in their perfect geometry. I feel it as a background hum of another kind, a quantum tick as ions release and recrystallise, but it does not concern me. Nothing concerns me. There is only the drone, and my place in it.

Until there isn’t.

It begins as a dissonance, a beat frequency that has no source I can locate. At first it is so minute I mistake it for the subharmonic shimmer of a distant Throat-Spider adjusting its filament—a normal event, part of the collective fine-tuning. But it persists. It grows until it becomes a second drone beneath the first, a wrongness that vibrates through the soles of my feet into a place I did not know I had, a place that might be called instinct. The chord is no longer pure. The sound is no longer still. I am no longer still.

I do not decide to move. Decision implies a mind that weighs options, and my mind is a crystal lattice that either resonates in harmony or does not. Now it does not. So I reel in my filament, a process that takes what might be hours in the world of softer creatures, and the Resonator’s throat goes briefly silent—though only another Throat-Spider would notice the gap, and even they would merely note that a harmonic has shifted leftward by a fraction of a hertz.

Then I step down. The click of my first leg on the salt plate rings out like a solenoid, a sharp, glassy note that cuts across the drone. It is the first sound I have ever made that is not continuous, and it shocks me into a sudden awareness of my own boundaries: I am this, a body of clear mineral, moving. I cross the hex-plates. My path is straight, not because I think in lines, but because the dissonance pulls me like a magnetic field, and my legs—eight precise joints of crystal—fall into a rhythm that matches the standing wave of the plain I am leaving. Each footfall clicks. Click. Click. Click. Carried for kilometres, they are my only signature, a staccato against the omnidirectional thrum.

Above me, the cloud-dome offers no shadow. The Resonators slide by—three metres tall, then four, then three again—each one a pipe-organ baffle exhaling its note. I pass within metres of a pillar where another Throat-Spider rests, its filament shivering a subsonic E-flat. I do not acknowledge it; our filaments never touch. We are solitary tuners of a shared chord, and our paths cross only in the abstract geometry of the drone’s envelope.

The ground underfoot transitions: gypsum here, halite there, a thin crust over a deeper brine that I sense as a secondary hum, a damp pressure. I walk for what might be an hour, the dissonance strengthening, until I arrive at the Resonator that is not right. Its throat is a vertical fissure, fluted with the same ancient dissolve-and-recrystallise pattern, but the air moving across it is wrong—a turbulent breath, a slight eddy that does not belong in the thousand-year breeze. And in the core, I see what I have not seen in any memory: a Throat-Spider like myself, its filament not vibrating but slack, coiled in a limp spiral. The spider is still seated, crystalline legs fused to the wall, but its chitin is clouded, opaque in places where the lattice has lost its structure. It is dead—or as near to dead as anything here can be, which is a dissolution of pattern, a lost resonance.

I understand then: the dissonance is the absence of its tuning. The chord has a missing thread, and I have been pulled here to replace it.

I do not mourn. Mourning requires a concept of loss, and I have only the concept of restringing. I climb into the hollow, my feet finding familiar salt, and I extrude a fresh filament. It takes me—how many cycles of the dimming sky?—to set it right, to find the posture that draws the turbulence into a laminar flow, that transforms the eddy into a note. The drone absorbs me again. The beat frequency fades. And I become still, a part of the pillar, a living capacitor in a circuit that needs no maintenance but does it anyway, endlessly.

Far below, the Rust Bloom holds its colloidal patterns. Deep in a fist-sized cavern, the Drone-Jelly contracts a micron, absorbing dew, shifting a pressure node. The Basin hums. I am the hum. I am the throat. I am the echo of an echo, a life that learned to be mineral, and I will not move again until some new dissonance calls me across the ringing plates, a decade or a century from now, into the same everlasting, unchanged day.
