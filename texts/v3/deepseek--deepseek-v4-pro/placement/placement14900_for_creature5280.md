I am called Lir, though my name is not a sound but a shape—the press of a thumb to the hollow of my throat, a slow exhale. I am a Skein-Tender, and my hands know the stories of Pallad. Today, I sit on the eastern bridge, where the liana weave is oldest and the fibers have softened to the texture of wet wool. The mist is thick, a gray-gold weight that tastes of iron and patience. Below me, the stone-back named Uul is passing, his shell a moving mountain of mineral-crusted ridges, and I feel his progress through the soles of my feet: a deep, three-beat thrum, the pace of a heart that has been beating for centuries. The bridge sways, not with his passage but with the city's breath, and I sway with it, my fingers finding the history-cord looped at my waist. It is time to record.

The cord is a new one, spun from the inner bark of the water-root tree, bleached pale by acid condensations. I have been saving it for a moment that felt whole. This morning, while harvesting loom-caps from the pumice gardens, I noticed a new vent had opened on the western flank of the spire we call the Mother's Shoulder. It exhaled, and the steam curled upward in a shape that recalled the spiral of a fiddlehead fern—a sign, the elders would say, of a fertile shift in the deep rock. I breathed the warm plume, and my skin tingled with the Dhar, that sub-audible pressure-phrase that hums in the teeth. It was a naming moment. Not a grand thing, but a small, necessary note in the world's slow composition.

So I knot. My fingers move with the deliberateness of water shaping stone. A loop for the vent, a double twist for the steam's shape, a sequence of half-hitches to mark the time of day (as we measure it—by the dimming of the fungus lanterns, a faint cycle that mirrors no sun). The cord grows a record, a tactile sentence. My teacher, old Saelin, who is nearly blind now and reads the oldest skeins only by touch, would say that a knot is a breath made solid. I feel the truth of that as I work; each movement requires a precise exhalation, a release of air into the heavy atmosphere, and the cord drinks it in.

The Haeli around me move in their own rhythms. On the neighboring bridge, a child named Vael is learning to walk the moss-felted stones, her toes gripping with that instinctive prehensile intelligence. Her mother, a woman whose face is a moon of placid attention, steadies her with a hand on the nape, and Vael's giggle is a ripple of bubbles in the thick air. Below, in the communal peat-bog, a group is turning the compost, their long arms moving in unison like the sweep of aerial roots in a current. The slime-mold that powers the lift creaks past, its trained tendril pulling a basket of freshly harvested rime-fungus, the crystals catching the blue lantern-light and scattering it into a brief constellation. I smell the sharp, electric scent, and my mouth waters.

My task is simple: to tend the archive, to read the past and record the present, but it is also to listen. The Dhar is stronger today. It has been building for weeks, a thickening of the pressure-phrase that we feel in our bones. The elders say that when the Dhar rises, a new landscape feature is about to be born—a peak might crack, a river of cloud might shift, a stone-back might begin its final molting. We are all midwives. I pause in my knotting, close my eyes, and let the hum vibrate through my soles. It is a note I've never felt before, a low, mournful thrum that starts in the pelvis and rises to the crown. It is the sound of stone deciding to become something else.

I open my eyes and look down. Uul has stopped. His great head, beaked and leathery, is lifted toward the new vent. His shell, a landscape of its own, is crusted with the fast-secreting moss that will one day become an arch for a new dwelling. A thin, clear fluid trickles from his eye, and I realize with a start that he is weeping. Not in pain—in recognition. The Dhar is for him, and for the city. Around me, the Haeli have stilled. Hands pause on ropes; voices quiet. We all feel it: a shift in the world's axis, a slow, geological breath that will change the shape of our home.

I tie a final knot, a complex weave that means "transformation," and I wait. The mists part, just a sliver, and I see the jagged crowns below us, a chain of islands that seem to be leaning toward the new vent. Then the cloud closes again, and the hum subsides. Uul lowers his head and resumes his decade-long passage. The city breathes out. I touch the cord to my forehead, then to the damp wood of the bridge, a gesture of continuity, and I rise.

Today, I am the record of a new beginning. Tomorrow, I might be the one to teach Vael the first knot, the one that means "I am here." In Pallad, we are all notes in the unfinished score, and I am content to be a slow, deliberate phrase in the music of the forming world.
