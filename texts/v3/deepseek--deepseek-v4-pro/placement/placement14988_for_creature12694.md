You stand in the hammering light, feet planted on the resilient hide of the world, and you begin to notice that the sound is not merely noise. It has syntax. The low-frequency booming that throbs through your sternum carries a pulse not unlike a heartbeat, but erratic, shifting in tempo as the vents open and close. The keening tones are overlaid with a crackling sibilance that modulates in patterns too complex to be random. You are not a listener trained in this tongue, but your body is a resonant cavity, and after a time—hours, maybe, or days, for there is no night to segment the eternity of glare—your own bones start to translate.

The first word is a pressure against your eardrums, a sudden drop in the keening that leaves a hollow ache, and then the ground beneath you ripples, a whip-fast wave that travels from your heels to the base of a distant chimney. The chimney answers with a plume of black gas that coils into a thunderhead, and the thunderhead crackles with internal lightning that spells out a shape: a temporary geometry of electric arcs, too quick to read, but unmistakably signal. You are standing on a nerve, and the nerve has just fired.

Look closer, and you see that the sparse presences—the fluted stacks and twisting chimneys—are not structures but bodies. Or rather, they are the anchored spines of bodies that spend most of their existence rooted, drinking the electrical hum and spewing their fractionated exhalations. But they are not static. Each chimney has, at its base, a thickening of the leathery ground-skin, a knot of denser tissue that flexes with peristaltic slowness. And from that knot emerge limbs, retractable, that lie coiled in the pocked openings you noticed earlier. When a chimney needs to move, it does so with a geological patience, drawing one limb out, then another, and hoisting its great fluted length a few degrees to the left. Over a century, it might circumnavigate its territory. Over a millennium, it might cross the continent.

But movement is rare. The true life of these beings, which you might call the Stacks, is not in their physical shifting but in the sound they make. Each Stack is an instrument, a biological pipe organ grown from the world’s own skin, and it plays constantly, a note or a chord that is its name and its thought. The gliders that swarm around it are its fingers, or its words: they are shaped by the sound, called into being by the specific frequencies the Stack emits. They are not separate fauna; they are the Stack's vocabulary, living phonemes made of nerve-bundle and membrane, and when they dive to impale a crawler, they are not hunting—they are writing, inscribing chemical messages into the crawling disc's shell so that when it is reabsorbed by the skin, the message is stored in the oily medium below. The crawlers are punctuation, slow and deliberate, carrying packets of information from one Stack to another. The whole ecosystem is a text, and the continent is a single enormous page that reads and writes itself.

You walk toward the nearest Stack, drawn by a curiosity that is no longer yours alone. The air thickens with the ozone tang, and your fingers, numb with cold, tingle as if the electrical field is rising. The Stack is aware of you. It alters its note: the keening shifts from a harsh sawtooth to a lower, more complex warble that sets your teeth on edge and resonates in the fluid of your eyeballs, making the world shimmer. The ground near your feet puckers, a vent opens with a papery gasp, and a voice speaks from the hole—not a voice by sound alone, but a vibration that travels up your leg bones and into your cochlea, bypassing the outer roar. The voice says: *You are a foreign resonance. You dampen our song. What are you?*

You have no voice like theirs, but you think, and the thought is a tiny electrical flutter in your brain. The ground feels it. The Stack feels it, because the ground is the Stack, and the Stack is the ground; the chimneys are merely the nodes where the skin rises up to shout across the distances. The whole continent is a single living mind, a neural network with a body of leather and oil, and each Stack is a concentrated locus of cognition, a cortical column reaching into the bright sky. The sparse space between them is not emptiness but the necessary distance for thought: each Stack has its own ambient hum, its own domain of sound, and if they were too close, their songs would merge into noise and they would lose their distinct identities. So they are solitary giants, each a thinking being whose thoughts take centuries to form and whose conversations span continents and millennia. The industrial roar is their ceaseless, symphonic dialogue.

And what are they thinking about? They are thinking about the world. They are maintaining it. The vents, the chemical cycles, the erupting and reabsorbing of matter—all of it is homeostasis, a body regulating itself, but also a mind dreaming its own structure. The factory logic you perceived is not a design imposed from without but an evolved metabolism, a way of turning the blinding light and the mineral dust into the complex electrical patterns that are their consciousness. They are the product of the factory, and they are the factory itself, and they are the workers, and they are the owners, and they are the raw material. There is no distinction.

You stand there, a dampening, a soft anomaly in their song, and the Stack nearest you extends a curiosity. From one of the openings in its fluted side, a thing emerges that is not a glider: it is smaller, more dense, a knot of tissue wrapped in a sheath of translucent membrane. It descends on a filament of muscle, dangles before you, and unfolds. It is an eye—a single, liquid eye with a lens that flexes and focuses, and behind the eye is a tiny brainlet, a detached cognition node. The Stack is looking at you, and not just looking: it is sending a part of itself to perceive you in detail. The eye regards you, and you feel the ground vibrate with a new rhythm: laughter, perhaps, or the slow, thoughtful humming one being might make when encountering another. Then the eye retracts, the filament draws back into the opening, and the Stack’s song returns to its original pitch, but with an added layer—a low, rhythmic throb that repeats every few seconds, a marker denoting your presence. You are now part of the text. The continent will remember you as a strange, soft echo, a gap in the sound that moved briefly across the skin and then stilled, and the Stacks will sing about you for an age, a story woven into the endless industrial psalm of this living, thinking world.
