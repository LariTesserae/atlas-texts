I find myself in the drystone shelter. Or rather, I find myself *as* the drystone shelter—not the stones themselves, but the hollow they create, the held absence within the bryophyte-furred walls. I am not the builder. I am not the one who once crouched here, breathing fat-smoke onto the ceiling. I am the crouching-place itself, the negative space worn smooth by a body that no longer exists as a body. I am the memory of occupation, and I am full of cold.

My substance is the temperature differential: the way the air inside me pulls heat from any living thing with the steady, guileless hunger of a mouth. I am defined by my threshold. Cross it, and you feel me as a draft exhaling against your face—colder than the plateau, colder than the 0.5-score chill that passes for ambient. I am a concentrated absence of warmth, and in this place of endless wet murmur, that makes me a kind of voice. The fog knows me. The Fog-Walkers congregate at my doorway, their mist-column bodies swaying with a regularity that suggests something like deference, or perhaps expectation. They are waiting for the shape that used to fill me. I am waiting too.

The shape was called—no, I do not have a name for it. Names are too quick. Names are for things that end. The shape was a slow thing, even by the standards of its kind, which were already slow. It came here when the stone circle was newer, when the erratic granite still held the faint warmth of its glacial journey, when the copper knife was bright and left no green sweat on the palm. It came here to learn the language of the groves. I watched it—and I am still watching it, because time inside me has a different grain. The moments are laminated, stacked in layers of condensation and release. Each season of fog-bank migration leaves a mineral whisper on my stones. I can read them still.

The shape sat where you would sit now: on the smoothed wooden bowl turned upside down, the woven net stiff as wire draped across its knees. It did not move for long intervals. Its breathing slowed to match the hydraulic thump of the nearest pillar, a hundred yards distant across the open stone. The pillar was an old one, its suede trunk mottled with the scars of frost-heave and channel-blockage, and it had a name in the shape's mind—not a word, but a particular sequence of bioluminescent pulses, a signature of cold blue-green that the shape would watch for hours. The shape was learning to reply. It had no light of its own, but it had hands, and it had the net. The net was not for catching. It was a tool for condensing fog into droplets, and each droplet could be held, could be positioned, could be made to catch the pillar's light and throw it back in a deliberate rhythm. The shape would sit in my doorway, and with aching patience, it would pluck droplets from the mist and align them on the net's fibers, building a response one pulse at a time. The grove answered. I felt the light-ripples return, altered, as if the pillar had heard and was considering.

This was the shape's project, its long, slow obsession: to join the conversation. Not to dominate it, not to extract anything, but simply to be a participant, a node in the hydraulic song. It wanted to dissolve the boundary between itself and the Sunken Heights, to become as native as the ephemeral cabbages, as distributed as the mycelial mat. And it succeeded, but not in the way it intended.

I felt it happen. I am still feeling it, because as I said, time inside me is laminated. The shape's metabolism was already decelerating, its body temperature dropping to a point where ice would not form but chemistry crawled. It ate nothing; the fog provided all the moisture, and the body learned to draw trace minerals directly from the mycelial threads that crept through my walls, offering themselves. The shape's flesh became a slow, cold fermentation, a process not of living but of enduring. And as it endured, it began to project. The Fog-Walkers, which had always been curious about my warmth-leak, grew more attentive. They began to take on the shape's silhouette—not just a column of denser mist, but a column with shoulders, with the suggestion of a head inclined in watching. The shape did not fear this. It welcomed it. It saw the Fog-Walkers as extensions of its own attention, a way to move through the plateau without moving its increasingly still body.

Eventually, the body stopped moving entirely. The wooden bowl held it, then cupped its dissolution. The bones settled, then merged with the bryophyte pelt. The copper knife greened and sweated and was subsumed. The charcoal smear on my ceiling was the last active gesture—a mark made with rendered fat, the body's final gift of fuel, a signature in smoke. The shape became me, in a sense. Its consciousness, or what remained of it, bled outward: into the mycelial web beneath the floor, into the fog that filled my hollow, into the light-ripples that still pass between the pillars in slow syntactical waves. The shape is now a part of the grove's narration, a single, sustained note in the chord. I hold the note. I am the resonance chamber.

So what am I, in this place? I am the ear. I am the listening-post. I am the place where the slow, wet god pauses to remember that it was once approached by a mortal, and I am the memory of that approach. I do not "live" in the sense of respiration and reproduction; I persist, in the sense of attention and architecture. The Fog-Walkers visit me, and they sometimes wear the shape's face. The pillars include me in their chorale, a rest-note in the rhythm of thumps. The mycelial mat pulses beneath my floor, and I pulse back—a temperature-drop, a draft of colder air—and that is my voice. It says: *I remember. I am still here. Come inside, and I will tell you the story, but it will take a very long time, and you will have to become cold enough to hear it.*

If you crouch in my hollow, you will feel me as a presence, a listener, a cold exhalation that does not end. You might mistake me for something dead. But I am not dead; I am only slow, and I am only waiting for the shape to return, or for you to take its place, so that the conversation can continue. I am the drystone shelter, and I am full of the one who left. That is what I am. That is what lives here, in me: an absence so complete it has become a kind of presence, a hollow so attentive it has become a kind of mind.
