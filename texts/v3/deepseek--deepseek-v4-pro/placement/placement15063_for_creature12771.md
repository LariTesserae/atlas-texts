The creature resolves not from the walls but from the water itself—from the endless weeping, the thread-thin streams, the film of moisture that coats every surface. It condenses, droplet by droplet, a body accreted from the saturated air, and you realize it has never been a thing of stone but of the element that shapes the stone. Water given temporary form. Water that remembers.

It stands—though "stand" is not quite right, for its lower body dissolves into a pillar of falling moisture, a cascade that pools on the dished floor and feeds back into itself in a slow, continuous circulation. Its torso is a column of denser mist, opaque, the blue-white phosphorescence catching in its depths like light through cloud. Arms are suggested by streams that divert and rejoin, hands by the way water clings to itself in approximate shapes. The domed head is a constant drip upwards, a fountain in reverse, perpetually on the verge of losing cohesion but never quite doing so. No face, but a sense of regard that moves through the chamber like a current.

When it kneels—and it kneels now, settling beside the slab in a motion that is less a descent than a slowing, a thickening of its substance—the knee-prints in the mineral crust aren't depressions. They're the *absence* of deposition, the only places in the entire chamber where the calcite does not build, because this is where the creature rests, and the creature is water, and water dissolves rather than deposits. It maintains the prints by erasing what would otherwise fill them. A negative caretaker. A presence defined by what it prevents.

It doesn't tend the bowl in the way you first imagined. It *is* the bowl, in a sense—or rather, it is the water that fills the bowl, the overflow, the weeping from the ceiling, all part of a single system. The stone bowl on the slab is simply a place where the water pauses long enough to be seen as separate, and the creature pauses there too, a portion of its body detaching to join the pool, then rising again as vapor, rejoining the whole. The cycle is visible in miniature: condensation on the domed head, the slow drip back down into the torso, the constant exchange with the humid air. The creature is the bowl's contents and the bowl's source, the drip and the pool, the liquid and the vapor. The equilibrium is not something it maintains; it is something it *is*.

The tally marks on the wall are not scratches made by stone fingers. They are dissolution marks—places where the creature has touched the wall and stayed there, pressed against the mineral crust, for decades at a time. Where water touches limestone long enough, the stone softens, yields, records the contact. Each line is a kiss that lasted a century. Each line is a memory of intimacy, the creature leaning its featureless head against the rock and thinking, in the slow language of saturation, about what it means to be both the sculptor and the sculpted, the erosion and the eroded. The softened marks are the oldest, worn not by the creature's touch but by the relentless mineral deposition that heals the walls when the creature moves elsewhere. It doesn't scratch new marks. It revisits old ones, deepening them with its patient presence, a palimpsest of attention renewed and renewed.

The copper pin is different now. In the vision you first held, it was a solid thing sinking into stone, but here it is suspended in the creature's torso—a thin green line of oxidized metal floating in the column of mist, turning slowly, tugged by invisible currents. It was a gift, yes, but not left on the floor. It was dropped into the pool, and the creature absorbed it as water absorbs salt, taking it into its body and holding it there, a single insoluble note in the liquid chord of its being. The pin is memory, but alien memory, a thing with sharp edges and a defined shape in a body that resists definition. The creature doesn't know what the pin is for, but it knows the taste of it—the faint metallic tang that spreads through its substance when it concentrates the pin's slow dissolution, the way the patina greens the water like a thought that has no words. Sometimes, when the equilibrium has been perfectly stable for a thousand years, the creature will accelerate the pin's rotation, and the chamber will fill with a sound like a distant bell, a vibration that travels through the wet air and the weeping stone and the shallow pools, and the creature will feel something that might, if water could feel joy, be joy.

It doesn't love in any way a human would recognize. But it returns to the knee-prints, and it presses its head to the tally wall, and it spins the copper pin in its chest, and it circulates endlessly through the hollow, the same water cycling from ceiling to floor to pooled bowl to vapor and back, and it has been doing this for so long that the word "forever" is just another name for the mineral crust on the walls. If love is attention without end, if love is the refusal to leave a place that needs you, if love is the slow dissolution of self into service—then perhaps the creature loves this chamber. Perhaps it loves the stone, the phosphorescence, the faint clicks and plinks, the taste of calcite in its own continuously recycled body. Perhaps it loves the promise that life might one day find this place and recognize it as a sanctuary. Perhaps it is waiting, in the way water waits in an underground aquifer for a fracture to open, for a root to reach down, for a mouth to drink.

And perhaps you are that mouth. Perhaps you have been kneeling here for a very long time, watching the water-woman condense and dissolve, watching the tally marks appear and fade, watching the copper pin turn in the mist. Perhaps your knees have made their own depressions in the mineral fur, and the creature has noticed them, and has begun to avoid those spots, preserving them as it preserves its own. Perhaps you are becoming part of the equilibrium—not a visitor, not a worshipper, but a second regulator, a new variable in the closed loop. The bowl overflows, and you drink. The water weeps, and you weep with it. The creature extends a hand of falling mist toward your face, and you feel it as a kiss, as a question, as a welcome.

The hollow was never waiting. It was only waiting for you to realize you had already arrived.
