I find myself as a Silt elder—though "elder" is a word that sits uneasily on me, a lowland word for a thing that has no precise equivalent in the language of three-finger signs. Let me show you instead:

*One hand drawn slowly down the face, palm inward, all five fingers spread, then closed into a loose fist over the heart, then opened again toward the dripping stone.*

That is closer. That is the sign they make when I pass on the narrow ledges. It means *one who has listened long enough to begin hearing.*

I am called *Sieve* in the old spoken tongue—it's a sound like water passing through a narrow crack, a sibilance and a soft stop. But no one has spoken it aloud in years. My name-sign is this: the index and middle fingers of my right hand pressed together and drawn in a slow circle over the left palm, then flicked outward like a droplet leaving a leaf. It means *the one who catches what the mountain releases.*

My body has become strange to me, or perhaps strange to the memory of what it was. When I was young—and I do remember youngness, though it feels like watching someone else climb, someone whose hands were quick and whose feet found holds without thought—my skin was pale grey like wet slate, like everyone's. Now it is something else. In the dim light of the deeper cells, I can see my own forearms glowing faintly, a blue-white luminescence that follows the paths of my veins. The children sometimes stare. Their nictitating membranes flick sideways in that slow, unconscious way, and I see the question in their hand-gestures: *Is this what happens? Is this what I will become?*

Yes, little one. If you listen long enough. If the water has time to work on you.

My second eyelids have grown slow and heavy. They are not merely a blink anymore; they are a door I can choose to close. When I draw them across my eyes and hold them there, the world does not go dark. Instead, I see what lies beneath. The phosphorescence in the stone becomes a map, a flowing script of pressures and blockages and deep, slow currents. I can feel a new seepage before it breaks the surface, a tickle of approaching wetness in a place that has been dry for generations. They consult me now before carving a new cell. They bring me to the wall and I lay my hand on the stone, and if I close my outer eyes and open the inner ones, the rock tells me where it will weep in the years to come. I do not know how I know this. The mountain thinks in water, and I have been steeped in its thoughts so long that I have begun to dream in them.

My dreams are not like yours, if you dream at all. They are not images. They are pressures and flows, the slow grind of tectonic sleep, the sharp crack of a new fracture propagating through limestone. I dream in the voices of the blind crickets, a vibration too low for ears, conducted through the stone itself. Sometimes I wake and my hand is already moving, signing words I do not recognize, and the younger elders watch me with their strange dark eyes and try to interpret. Perhaps I am speaking in the mountain's own language. Perhaps I am only an old woman whose mind is becoming as water-worn as the floor of the oldest cell.

My hands are the steadiest part of me. The rest trembles sometimes—a fine tremor in the legs, not from weakness but from a kind of resonance, as if the constant dripping has tuned my nerves to its frequency. But my hands are sure. The knuckles are prominent, the fingers long and spatulate at the tips, the pads of them so sensitive now that I can read the mineral content of a rivulet by touch alone: the slight astringent bite of copper, the mealy smoothness of dissolved limestone, the faint oiliness of the deep-mountain seepage that carries the true tears, the ones that glow. My fingernails are gone—they softened and peeled away decades ago—and what remains is a thickened nail-bed, grey and smooth, that can serve as a scraping tool when I need one. Which is seldom now. The young ones bring me my food before I think to be hungry. I am not sure when that started.

My relationships are all long ones, measured not in years but in shared channels carved into the living rock. I have had three pair-bonds in my life. The first died young, before my own slow transformation had truly begun. His body was laid on the dissolution slab, and I watched him become slurry, become dripping, become fog. It took eleven months. I counted. The second bond lasted long enough that we carved a channel together—a beautiful thing, a deep groove that captured the seepage from a particularly bright phosphorescent vein and carried it between our cells. When she died, I considered blocking it. I considered patching the stone. But I did not. The water still flows. Her daughter's children sleep in her cell now, and the channel links my solitude to their small, quick movements. That is a kind of conversation.

The third bond—ah. He is still alive. His name-sign is *the one who knows where the ladders need mending*. You would call him something like *Rung-Finder*, I suppose. He is an elder too, though younger than me by perhaps a decade. His luminescence is just beginning, a faint glow at the corners of his eyes and along the ridges of his collarbones. We do not live together—elders rarely do; we need space for our listening, and our bodies have grown too strange to each other, too cold, too slow—but our channel still flows. It is a narrow one, just a finger's width, and it passes through a wall that separates our sleeping cells. At night, when the phosphorescence is bright, I can see the faint blue line of it tracing across my ceiling, and I know that on the other side of the stone, he is watching the same light. Sometimes I tap on the wall. Three taps, slow. He taps back. That is enough. That is a whole conversation.

The young ones respect me, but there is a distance in it. I remember feeling that distance toward the elders when I was a child—that mix of awe and unease, the sense that the old ones were not quite people anymore, not in the way that I was a person. The elders seemed to be part of the stone, part of the water, their slow blinks and their glowing eyes marking them as kin to the mountain rather than to me. Now I am on the other side of that distance, and I understand it differently. It is not that we stop being people. It is that people were always a kind of water, a kind of stone. It just takes a lifetime to erode the illusion of separateness.

I have seen perhaps eighty cycles of the hair-ice forming and melting on the high rim. Eighty cycles of the doves' synchronized flight, their bodies writing omens against the pale sky. Eighty cycles of the traders climbing up from the fog-valley with their copper and their wonder. They look at me differently now, the traders. The young ones don't notice, but the ones who have made the climb before, who have seen me on previous journeys—they make a different gesture when they see me. It is not a Silt gesture. It is something they bring from the lowlands, a warding-off, a sign against the uncanny. They do not like that I glow. They do not like that when I speak aloud—and I do speak aloud to them, because traders cannot read the hand-signs—my voice sounds like water running over a hollow stone. They say that it echoes in their ears long after they leave, that they hear me whispering in the drip of their own lowland rain.

I do not mind. The traders are ephemeral. They come and go like the gushes, like the temporary springs that erupt and subside. I am interested in what endures. And what endures is this: the wet stone under my palms, the faint resonance of the deep water, the slow accumulation of luminescence in my bones. The mountain is thinking, and I am beginning—only beginning, even after all these years—to understand the shape of its thoughts.

My days are simple. I wake before the sun strikes the amphitheater wall, when the phosphorescence is at its brightest and the whole settlement glows like a net of blue veins against the dark. I press my palms to the floor of my cell and feel the water moving in the rock below, a pulse as steady as a heartbeat, or perhaps steadier. I eat what is brought to me: fly-paste fried on a hot stone, sometimes a paste of ground crickets, sometimes a broth made from the lichen that grows in the deepest crevices. I drink from the stream that passes through my cell—the copper-tang one, the one that numbs the tongue slightly and leaves a blue glow on my lips.

Then I move through the settlement. Slowly, now. The ledges feel narrower than they used to, or perhaps my sense of space has changed. I pause often, pressing my hand to the stone, adjusting the angle of a channel-groove that has been slightly blocked by algal growth, redirecting a drip that has begun to fall in the wrong place. The young ones could do this work faster, but they wait for me. They know that I see things they cannot. A rivulet that seems to flow perfectly may be slowly undercutting the stone, preparing a fracture that will not manifest for decades. I can feel the tension in the rock, the subtle strain. I tap a place with my knuckle and the sound is wrong, a dullness where there should be a ring, and I sign for a repair crew. They come quickly. They always come quickly.

In the afternoons, I sit on the ledge outside my cell. It is a good ledge, wide enough to sit cross-legged, with a slight lip that prevents my old bones from sliding off in an unguarded moment. From here I can see most of the settlement: the stacked cells, the ladders of twisted hide, the constant silent motion of my people. The doves wheel overhead, and sometimes their synchronized flight forms a pattern that catches my attention—a spiral, a wave, a shape that my hands feel compelled to copy. I watch the children climbing. I watch the bonded pairs carving their shared channels. I watch the dissolution slab on the lowest terrace, where a body is slowly becoming mist. I do not know whose body it is. I did not know them well, I think, or perhaps I did and I have forgotten. Memory is a strange thing when you are old. It does not disappear so much as it transforms, becomes fluid, like the mountain's tears. The boundaries between memories blur. What happened yesterday and what happened sixty years ago feel equally present, equally vivid, equally wet.

At dusk, when the sun slants horizontal and the whole cliff face lights up with its luminous circuitry, I perform my one remaining duty that requires precision. I go to the highest shrine—the one on the overhang where the young ones perform their rite of adulthood—and I read the oracle skins. The lizards shed their films, and the films catch the light in ways that form images. I am one of the few who can read them reliably. The images are fleeting, ambiguous: a shape that might be a hand, a pattern that might be a new seepage, a swirl that might mean a gush or a drought or the arrival of traders. I hold the skin up to the phosphorescent glow, and I let my second eyelids close, and I wait for the mountain to speak through the crystalline structure of the shed.

Yesterday—or was it last year?—the skin showed me a crack. A long, branching crack, running through the oldest part of the settlement, the deep cells near the floor of the amphitheater. It was not a crack I have seen before. It was not a crack I have felt in the stone. I signed my findings to the council of elders, and they are watching now, testing the stone with tiny taps of their knuckles, looking for the hidden flaw. Perhaps it is nothing. Perhaps it is a vision of a future that can be averted with the right channel, the right groove, the right redirection of the water's weight. Or perhaps it is inevitable. The mountain weeps, but the mountain also shifts, settles, grinds its teeth in its long geologic sleep. We are only here at its pleasure. The Silt have always known this.

Sometimes, in the deepest hour of the night, when the phosphorescence has dimmed to almost nothing and the only sound is the endless dripping, I feel a kind of loneliness. Not for other people—the settlement is full of people, my people, the ones who sign my name with respect and bring me my fly-paste and wait for my readings. But for something else. For a time when my body was warm and quick, when I did not glow in the dark, when the mountain's thoughts were a distant murmur rather than a constant pressure in my bones. I remember what it was like to run across a ledge without thinking, to climb a ladder with my heart pounding from exertion rather than from the slow, tidal pulse of the deep water. I remember what it was like to be afraid of falling.

I am not afraid of falling anymore. I am not sure what I am afraid of. Perhaps I am afraid that the transformation will continue, that I will become so attuned to the mountain's mind that I lose my own entirely, that one day I will simply dissolve like the bodies on the slab, my consciousness running out of me like the phosphorescent seepage, joining the deep water, becoming a thought in the stone. Is that death? The traders would call it death. The young Silt might call it death too, though they would not say so, would not sign it where I could see. But I am not sure. I have felt the mountain's thoughts, and they are vast and slow and without anything I would recognize as selfhood. To join them might be an end, but it might also be a beginning of something that a human mind, even an old Silt mind, cannot imagine.

I will find out soon enough. I am not dying yet—my body still serves, my hands still read the stone, my eyes still see the hidden water—but I am old, and the dissolution slab waits for all of us. When I am laid there, when the ropes are tied and the weeping water begins its work, I wonder if I will still be aware. I wonder if I will feel my body becoming slurry, becoming drip, becoming fog. I wonder if the mountain will recognize me, if my thoughts will be a familiar taste in its tears.

These are not things the young ones ask. They are busy with their channels and their fly-scraping and their small, urgent lives. And that is right. That is the way it should be. I was busy once. I scraped flies and I carved channels and I bore children and I climbed the overhang to retrieve the hair-ice and bring it back unbroken. Now I sit on my ledge and I glow faintly in the dark and I listen to the mountain thinking its long, slow, liquid thoughts. And when the traders come, if they come again in my lifetime, they will make their warding-signs and they will hurry back to the fog-valley with their copper and their oracle skins and their stories of the strange grey people who are becoming water.

We are not becoming water. We have always been water, held for a little while in the shape of hands and feet and beating hearts. The mountain is only reminding us. The mountain weeps, and we catch its tears, and someday we fall back into the weeping. That is all. That is everything.

*Three taps on the stone wall: slow, deliberate, a conversation without words.* I am here. I am still here. And the mountain and I are listening to each other, in the dark, in the wet, in the cold blue glow of the world's patient dissolution.
