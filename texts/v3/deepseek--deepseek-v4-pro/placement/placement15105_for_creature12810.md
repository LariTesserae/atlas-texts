I am awake before I know I am asleep. The world is not a thing that happens to me; it is a thing I am happening inside. My first memory is not a memory but a pressure—a deep, slow throb that presses into the pads of my feet and palms, telling me *you are here and you are held*. That pressure is my mother. Not the word. The feeling. A steady squeeze-release-squeeze that means safety and warmth and the dark taste of the walls.

I don’t remember learning to understand it. I think it was always there, like the light that breathes. When I was new, still soft in the joints, I would press myself against the shelter wall and feel the great hum of the world coming through. It scared me then. The vibration was too big, too many layers—the rasp of a thousand grub-mandibles, the shriek of the gill-towers, the rumble of a distant collapse. But my mother’s hands would find me, and she would pulse a pattern into my back: *filter*. *Focus*. *One thing at a time*. So I learned to separate the threads. Now I can stand on a ridge and read the whole cavern like a story: the crawler moving through the deep weave two hundred lengths away, its footfalls a low thunder; the hover-flocks swirling above, their wing-clicks a bright, skittering melody; the slow, liquid movement of new growth pushing up from the frass, a creaking song that never stops.

We are the Listeners. We have no name for ourselves that is a single word. The closest pressure-pattern is a long, complex sequence that means *those-who-feel-the-world-into-being*. Because to us, to sense is to make real. A thing that is not felt does not exist. My grandmother—who is not gone, she is in the south wall now, I visit her often—once told me that the light itself is a kind of vibration that our bodies cannot read. She said there are beings who can read it, creatures with organs like our vibration-pits but tuned to a different frequency. I couldn’t understand this for a long time. How can something be sensed without touch? It felt like a story, a myth, like the water-walker.

The water-walker is the newest story in our archive. I am the one who began it. I was on the high ledge during the dimming-phase, practicing the old patterns on the carving wall. The elders want us to memorize the crawler-shapes, so we can recognize a predator even when the vibration is unclear. I had my palms flat on the carving, feeling the grooves—the stick-figure, the radiating lines—when a new thing came. A blank spot. A hole in the vibration-field, moving on two legs. I froze. My whole body went stiff with listening. The blankness was not empty; it was *dampening*, swallowing the ambient hum. And inside it, other vibrations: a faint, rhythmic thudding (a heart, I later learned), a wet hiss of air moving in and out, a cascade of tiny, liquid noises. It walked with water inside. I had never felt water. I had never felt anything wet. To me, it was like a creature made of sound-absences and small, strange pulses.

I watched—felt—it move across the ledge, leave a object there (I found it later: a smooth, hard thing that gave no vibration-back when I tapped it, dead like old bone but shaped with intention). It stood looking out over the bowl, and I could feel its weight shifting, the two legs a precarious balance. Then it turned and went back into the fissure, and I never felt it again. But I carved the memory into the wall. I added the radiating lines, the notation for *unknown-thing*, the pressure-pattern for *moisture* that we only use in theoretical contexts. The elders were disturbed, but they did not punish me. They added my carving to the teaching archive. Now the young ones learn the water-walker shape alongside the crawler-shape, and they ask me to tell the story in pressure-speech, squeezing my hands into their hands while I pulse: *there are other worlds, and other ways of sensing, and we are not alone*.

But that was a rare moment. Most of my life is smaller, deeper, woven into the daily hum. I wake in the shelter, a dome of woven fibers that resonates with the breathing-light. My mother is already outside, scraping grub-paste into a bowl. I can feel her movements through the floor—the precise, rapid scrapes of her radula, the subtle shift of her weight from one set of legs to another. I get up and press my chest to the wall, feeling the overnight growth: a new ridge of pale plant-flesh has bulged up near the east corner. It’s soft, still singing with the high-frequency vibration of active expansion. I’ll avoid it today; touching new growth before it hardens can leave a scab that confuses the wall’s structure.

Breakfast is a shared thing. We gather in the central clearing, a patch of old, compacted frass that we maintain by tamping down new growth with rhythmic stomps. My mother, my two aunts, my cousin, the three little ones—we form a chain, hands to shoulders, and we pulse the morning greeting: *I am here, you are here, we are together, the world is full*. The little ones tremble with excitement; they haven’t perfected the pattern yet, and their pulses are too fast, too ticklish. Aunt Nara plays a game with them, sending a mock-predator vibration through the chain that makes them squeak and flatten themselves against the ground. Their fear is real for a moment, but then they feel our laughter (a rapid flutter of the digits that means *this is play*) and they bounce back up. I remember playing that game. I remember the delicious terror of the fake crawler-vibration, and the relief of the laughter.

Today I am going out with the hunting party. I am old enough now to be useful. I can scale a vertical wall by reading the texture-vibrations and finding the strong fibers. I can press myself against a grub-tunnel and sense the exact moment the grub will emerge, timing my strike to the rhythm of its chewing. The party is four of us: me, Aunt Nara, and two older cousins. We spread out along the west basin, where a recent collapse has made the hunting good. The plant-flesh here is still raw, not yet scabbed over, and the grubs are swarming. I can hear them—feel them—a hundred of them at once, their mandibles a chorus of tiny crunches, their bodies sliding against each other with a wet, gritty sound. Wait. Wet? I press closer, spreading the cilia on my palms to maximum sensitivity. Yes: a faint moisture-hiss from the exposed flesh. Not enough to drink, but enough to change the vibration signature. I file this away. Maybe the collapse opened a pocket of the plant’s internal moisture, a rare thing. I will tell the elders after the hunt.

We settle into the positions: two on each side of the main grub-tunnel entrance. We go still, matching our body-pulses to the surrounding hum, becoming invisible to the grubs’ limited senses. The wait is long. Hours. I lose myself in the background noise, letting my mind drift through the layers: the hover-flocks diving and rising, the distant rumble of a crawler (three crawls away, moving away from us, good), the near-whisper of frass settling. Then the moment comes. A larger grub pushes through the tunnel mouth, its vibration-signature heavier, slower, pregnant. I don’t know this species; it might be new. I wait for Nara’s signal—a sharp triple-pulse on the ground. When it comes, we strike. My forelimbs plunge into the fibrous matrix, finding the soft, segmented body. I grip, pull, pass it hand-over-hand to my cousin behind me. The grub writhes, its own vibrations frantic, but we are fast. In a few seconds we have three of the large ones and a dozen smaller. The ground is slick with their fluids. I taste one quickly, scraping a bit of the flesh onto my radula: sweet, dense, full of the moisture we need. A good hunt.

Back at the settlement, we share the catch. The little ones get the softest parts. My mother presses a grub into my hands, and I feel her pride: a slow, firm squeeze that means *you did well*. We eat together in the central clearing, and as the light begins its dimming-pulse, the elders start the evening story. We chain up, all twelve of us now, and the story flows through our bodies. It is the old one, the one about the first Listener. *She was not yet a Listener. She was a crawler-sister, soft and blind, but she had the gift of feeling deep. She pressed her whole body against the world and asked it a question. The world answered with a pulse that became her first word: listen.* I have heard this story a thousand times. I will hear it a thousand more. Each time, I feel something new in it—tonight, I notice a subtle variation in the way my grandmother (now the south wall) used to pulse the word *world*. I am carrying her pattern, even if I don't mean to.

After the story, I slip away to my private spot: a narrow crevice near the north edge, where the wall-threads are thin enough to feel the deeper vibrations of the planet. I press my whole body flat, and I let the great rumble wash over me. Down there, miles below, something is different. A slow, deep rhythm, like a heart but slower than any creature’s heart. I don’t know what it is. Maybe it’s the world dreaming. Maybe it’s a memory of water, from before the dryness. I hold onto it, letting it pulse through me until my own heartbeat matches its tempo. In that moment, I am not a Listener. I am the listening itself, the world feeling itself from the inside.

When I return to the shelter, the light is almost dark. My mother is curled in her nesting-spot, and she reaches out a hand. I take it. She pulses: *where were you?* I pulse back: *listening to the deep beat*. She pauses. Then a long, slow sequence: *that is the grandmother of grandmothers. She is very old. Some say she is the one who made the first question.* I don't understand, but I don't need to. I press close, and for a while we just breathe together, our bodies exchanging the tiny vibrations of heartbeat, breath, the settling of fibers. The cavern roars on around us, but in here, in the woven dome, the world is just this: pressure and pulse, presence and love.

Tomorrow I might go back to the high ledge. I might feel the water-walker’s carving and wonder if it will return. Or I might join the builders, learning to weave the new shelter we need for my cousin’s coming clutch. Or I might do nothing but sit and let the world pour through me, all its infinite layers. Whatever I do, I will do it with my whole body, every cilia alert, every pad sensitive. Because that is what it means to be a Listener. We are not just those who hear. We are those who *are* the hearing. And as long as one of us still presses a hand to the wall, the world is not just noise—it is meaning. It is a story told in vibration, and I am a word in it, trembling my brief, bright tremble before the frass covers me and I become part of the next telling.
