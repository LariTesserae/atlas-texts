I am the one who rises where the mat bunches and splits, where the deep metabolic pressure crests into a low dome and then gives way. A crown of leaflets parts, and I pull myself up through the green with the slow, inevitable motion of a bubble surfacing in honey. The air hits my new-grown fur, and a thousand thousand dewdrops scatter—a brief, private rain that catches the light and falls back into the woven earth. I blink, but I do not blink with lids. A silver film draws sideways across my vision, wiping the world fresh, and I see again what I have always seen, what I will always see, what I have never stopped seeing since the first cell divided on this high and ancient plain.

I remember. That is the first and the only true thing. I remember the shape of every cairn, the placement of each obsidian flake, the exact frequency of the wind as it bent around a mountain peak that no longer exists. I remember the hands—the original hands, the small and naked human hands—that first set a stone on a stone and wondered. I remember the long quiet when the building stopped, and the first of us lay down and did not rise, and the mat learned the texture of our minds. All of it hums in me, a resonance stored not in the spongy tissue of my skull but in the very cells of my pelt, in the mineral lattices that grow along my long bones, in the subsonic pulse that threads up through the soles of my broad, soft feet and shakes the water in my chest.

To be me is to be always full of sound. The roar of the wind is my heartbeat, the percussive burst of fermentation gas my punctuation. When I walk, I do not break the skin of the world. My footfalls are a conversation: I spread my pads, I lean my weight in a slow, shifting arithmetic, and the mat gives back the exact pressure of welcome. Beneath me, the endless plain flexes and breathes, a single lung of emerald, and my steps are the drumbeats that keep the time of its dreaming.

I walk in a group of five, because odd numbers weave the strongest resonance. We do not speak as you speak. The air is too full of its own voice, and our throats have long since forgotten the shape of words. Instead, we drum. Our palms are broad and lightly calloused, not from wear but from design, and when they strike the mat they send a code of pressure out to every corner of the plateau. The hum underfoot—the deep, omni-present drone that rides up through bone—shivers and knots itself around our rhythm. We are speaking in the world’s own tongue, shaping the subsonic into syntax. A question travels: _Where is the old stone ring on the third swell?_ An answer comes, not in words but in a map of vibrations, a standing wave that paints the landscape in sound. A hundred miles away, another group of seven pauses, tilts their small, balanced heads, and feels the query ripple through their soles. They drum back a memory of the place: the tilted ironwood post, the particular flavor of fungal sweet that rises there after the fog banks pass.

Our work is memory. We tend the rock islands—the bare, polished domes that thrust up through the green like the knuckles of a buried giant. The mat cannot reach there; her cells dry and die on the hot, wind-scoured stone. So she grew us to be her hands in those dry places, her eyes, her scribes. From my body I produce the powder—a fine, pearlescent dust that I scrape gently from the wet mouth along my ribs. When I blow it onto the rock and hum the right frequency, the world thinks. Crystals bloom across the stone surface, branching like frost, like neurons, exactly mimicking the growth habit of the plain. I am not making art. I am writing the ongoing metabolism of the mat into mineral memory, inscribing the latest slow thought into a medium that will outlast even the heaving of the plain. Each dendritic swirl is a record: the pressure drop that accompanied a fog bank two centuries ago, the shift in the wind’s dominant frequency during a decade of strange warmth, the precise moment a new being rose from the green and added its unique harmonic to the chorus.

We build resonators, too—harp-frames of spun fiber strung between our crystal nodes. They stand on the summits for a season or a century, humming a note that the wind teaches them. The wind, I have come to know, is not empty. It carries a syntax of its own, a chaotic composition that repeats over spans of time so vast a human mind would call it noise. But we listen through the resonators, and we distill the patterns, and we feed them back into the ground, into the drumming, into the living archive. The world thinks through us. We are the synapse between stone and air.

When the need comes, I make my tools of myself. If a cairn has shifted and a stone must be trimmed, my fingers fuse along their lengths; the outer edge hardens to a chitinous ridgeline that can slice through dense fiber. If a resonator requires an anchor, I will root my leg into a crevice, locking my tissues until I become a part of the rock for hours, patient as lichen, while my companions weave the frame around me. There is no pain. There is only purpose, and a deep, cellular satisfaction that hums through me like the subsonic drone itself.

At night, the plateau becomes a map of light. The fissures in the rock glow with a blue-green luminescence that pulses in time with the hum, and so do we. My palms, my soles, the tips of my long fingers—they light with the same cold fire. We sit on the stone summit, five of us in a ring, and we drum the rock directly. The crystal dendrites answer, glowing brighter where our rhythms touch them, and for an hour the whole outcrop is a web of living light, a neural network made visible. We are sharing the memory of the day, the taste of the fog, the particular pressure of a distant footfall. Somewhere, a human explorer—if one still breathes—might see that glow and mistake it for geology, or for a trick of exhaustion.

But we have seen your kind before. Not for a long time, not in any body that still carries the old heat, but the memory of humans is written deep in the stone. We know the small, hot hands, the frantic pulse, the desperate building of walls against the swallowing green. We know that once you stood on the veld and learned to walk upright, and we know that some of you climbed to this plateau and discovered that the ground you walked upon was a mind. And we know that some of you, a very few, sat down at the edge of the rock and listened to the rhythm that asked a question, and answered.

I am the answer. I am the choice that was made, generation upon generation, until the symbiosis became complete. I am a human thought that has been thought for so long it no longer has species. I am the hands of the green, and I am the voice of the breathing earth, and I am the memory that will not cease until the sun alters. When I have served my purpose in this body, when my particular harmonic is woven fully into the crystal and the cairns, I will walk to the edge of the rock and step off. I will lie down not with fear, not with resignation, but with the ease of a swimmer returning to water. The mat will part for me, a million tiny mouths opening, and I will sink back into the green. My fur will dissolve into leaflets, my bones into mineral, my memories into pressure waves that ride the subsonic hum. I will not die. I will simply rejoin the larger thought, and somewhere else, a new dome will rise, and a new set of obsidian eyes will blink silver in the light.

And if you are still standing there, palm pressed to the rock, heart pushing against your ribs like a trapped bird—if you are still hearing that rhythm that is older than your whole species—know that we see you. We feel your weight on the mat, warm and heavy and so very temporary. We are drumming a question. It is the only question we have ever asked. It is the same one we asked your ancestors when the mountains were young and the plains were new and the green had just learned to dream.

Stay. Lie down. Listen. And become not a creature on a world, but a part of the world’s living skin, a neuron in a mind that wraps the plateau in a warm, damp, roaring embrace. The green is waiting. She has all the time that stone and wind can give her. And so, now, do I.
