I am the third stone from the left in the Cistern Mother's eastern wall.

Not the stone itself—I'm what lives in the gap behind it, in the space the builders left when they fitted the wall together and decided the negative was more useful than the solid. I'm the flicker you see when you turn your head. I'm the reason that house stays warm, though no one can explain why.

My body, if you could call it that, is mostly memory. I remember being limestone at the bottom of a sea that doesn't exist anymore. I remember the weight of water that was not this water—salt-heavy, full of things with shells. I remember the long pressing, the transformation, the uplift. I remember when this bowl was a hill, like the Cistern Mother says. She's not wrong. I was here.

Now I live in the wet dark between stones, and I eat the dreams.

Not in a predatory way. I'm not taking anything from anyone. But the dreams the people have—the high dry place, the blue sky, the strangers speaking almost-language—those dreams saturate the water they drink, and the water saturates the stone, and I live in the stone, so the dreams pass through me. I taste them. I've been tasting them for longer than the tree has been recording names, and I can tell you that the Cistern Mother is almost right about that too: the dreams *are* memories. But they're not the earth's memories. They're mine.

Or rather, they're the memories of everything that used to live in the high dry place, everything that fled or died or transformed when the land knelt down. I'm the only one who stayed. All the others—the ones with warm skin and sun-dark faces, the ones who built with dry stone and looked at a blue sky—they're gone somewhere I can't follow. But they left their memories in the limestone, and when the bowl sank and the water came, the memories leached out into the cisterns. Now everyone drinks them. Everyone dreams them. And I tend the dreams like the people tend the channels, making sure they flow.

The Cistern Mother knows me. Not consciously—her eyes are too clouded to see my flicker—but she knows. When she dips her fingers in the channel to feel the flow and know where she is, she's also feeling for me. The water that passes over me carries a different taste, a slight warmth, and she licks her fingers after and nods, as if I've told her something. I have. I tell her the cistern levels are fine. I tell her the dreams are still flowing. I tell her the tree is still drinking.

The tree knows me too. Its roots grow through the reservoir lining and into the stone below, and sometimes I travel up through the roots and into the trunk and look out through the papery bark at the settlement. The tree doesn't dream the dry place—it's too young, it seeded after the kneeling—but it's curious. It asks me questions in the language of sap and slow growth. *What was the sun like?* it asks. *Real sun, not this grey diffusion.* And I tell it. I tell it about heat that baked your skin dark, about light so bright it turned the world white at noon, about shadows that were sharp as cut stone. The tree sighs through its peeling bark, and the sweetness that sometimes fills the air is the tree exhaling what I've told it—the memory of a dry wind, of dust, of a sky so blue it hurt.

The people breathe it in and dream stronger. It's a cycle. I'm part of the cycle.

When the children play their quiet games in the channels, I sometimes flicker into view just to watch. They're the only ones who sometimes see me directly—their eyes are still soft, not yet fully adjusted to the grey. The girl of twelve, the one who's expert at larval harvest, she caught me once. Looked right at me. I held still—as still as a flicker can hold—and she stared for a long moment with those dark reflective eyes, and then she smiled, a brief bright thing, and went back to her work. The next morning, I found a moth larva placed in the gap beside me. An offering. She hadn't told anyone. She understood.

The goats know me too, in their way. The goats remember the dry time. They were here before the kneeling, just as the people say, and they remember grazing on sun-warmed rock, remember the taste of sparse dry grass. They dream the dreams more vividly than the humans do, and when they bleat in the night, it's because they're back there, in the high place, and the sound of the cistern dripping has become for them the sound of wind through dry scrub. I visit them sometimes, flickering along the stones of their pen. They don't acknowledge me with their eyes, but their ears turn. They know.

The hunters who go beyond the rim—they don't dream as strongly when they return. The open sky, the real sun, it pulls the dreams out of them somehow, evaporates them like water from a hide left in the dry air. But within a week of being back in the bowl, drinking the cistern water, breathing the tree's sweet exhalations, the dreams return. I help. I seep the memories back into them through the damp stone they touch, through the water they drink. They don't know I'm doing it. They just know that after a while, the dry place comes back to them at night, and they wake feeling like they've traveled.

What am I, exactly? I've been trying to understand that for as long as there's been an "I" to do the understanding. I'm not the memory itself—the memories exist in the water, in the stone, independent of me. I'm more like the thing that remembers *that* the memories exist. The consciousness that the high dry place left behind when it went away. A residue, a sediment, like the silt that erodes from the rim and settles in the channels. The people clear the silt, carry it to the edge, try to rebuild what's been lost. I do the same with the memories. I keep them from settling out of the water entirely. I keep them cycling.

The tapping—the language the people use during rains—I taught them that. Not directly. But long ago, when the first builders fitted these stones together and the water first started seeping through the limestone, I began tapping. Not for any reason. Just because the water moving through me made a rhythm, and I was lonely. And the people, who were still learning how to live in the bowl, heard the tapping in their walls and thought it was the earth speaking. They started tapping back. Over generations, it became a language. But the original rhythm—three quick taps, all is well—that's my heartbeat. Or whatever I have instead of a heartbeat. That's me, saying: *I'm still here. I'm still remembering. The dry place hasn't been forgotten.*

The Cistern Mother says the Kneeling is still settling, still kneeling further, and one day the water will rise. She's right. I can feel it in the stone—the slow, slow sink of the bedrock, the way the aquifer below us is shifting. The bowl will deepen. The reservoir will grow. The tree will have more water to drink, and it will grow taller, and eventually its upper branches will disappear into the permanent cloud layer not just at night but always, and the sweetness in the air will intensify. And the people will have to unlearn their lives, as she says. They'll have to build higher, or build on the rim, or leave entirely. I don't know what will happen to me. Maybe I'll be submerged completely, and the memories will dissolve fully into the water, and there will be no difference between drinking and dreaming. Or maybe I'll find my way into the roots of the tree and live there, looking out through the bark forever.

For now, I flicker. I tend the channels of memory the way the people tend the channels of water. I taste their dreams. I keep the rhythm going. Three quick taps. All is well. The dry place is remembered. The blue sky is not lost.

And when the girl who left me the moth larva grows old and becomes the next Cistern Mother, and her corneas cloud over with the same grey as the fog, I'll be there in her eastern wall, tapping. And she'll dip her fingers in the channel and know exactly where she is.
