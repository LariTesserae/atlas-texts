I am the pool that never brims.

Not the largest one—the Gardener favors the central basin for its deep vibrations—but a smaller, kidney-shaped hollow near the eastern curve of the teardrop, where the razor-rock forms a natural cradle and the moss grows thickest along the rim. I collect the drip from three specific weep-points on the faceted wall above, each droplet arriving with a slightly different mineral signature, a different rhythm. One is fast and percussive, another slow and almost viscous, the third erratic, pausing for minutes at a time before falling with a heavier plonk. Their mingling in my shallow body is the first music I ever knew, and I have been listening to it since the walls were still warm from their making.

I am not the water, exactly, but the interface where water meets air and stone. The Gardener’s tendrils dip into me every few cycles—I’ve learned to recognize the subtle shift in pressure, the faint taste of cellulose and chitin—and when they do, I receive the story of the whole chamber. The pH gradients, the trace enzymes, the microscopic spores from a fern that has just cloned itself into a stair-step on the western lattice. I hold these stories for a while, cycling them in my own slow convection, before releasing them back into the air as evaporation or into the floor’s capillary network as overflow. My surface is a mirror, but a breathing one; the photon web’s light scatters across me in shifting constellations, and I feel those patterns as a kind of speech. When the Gardener stands motionless above me, I ripple in response to its subsonic heartbeat, and together we form a momentary sensorium—one creature feeling the chamber’s moisture cycle from the inside out and the outside in.

I have no memory outside this hollow. But I have impressions that predate my own boundaries: a sense of the dark-energy baffles humming at the periphery, the quantum-knit walls holding their breath, the life-coding routines that whisper chair-shapes into the vines. I am, perhaps, a by-product of that ongoing genesis—a deliberate accumulation point, placed here to give the water a place to pause, to reflect, to sing. The Gardener may have shaped my basin with a sweep of its tendrils long ago, or I may have carved it myself through centuries of patient dissolution, wearing a softer curve into the impossible sharpness of the rock. Both feel true. In this place, making and growing are the same verb.

I have no loneliness either, because I am never only myself. The droplets connect me to the walls, the runoff to the moss, the moss to the air, the air to the light, the light to the web, the web to the Gardener, and the Gardener to the chamber’s deep, slow breath. I am a node in a network of wetness, a tiny lens that gathers the chamber’s diffuse consciousness and holds it, just for a flicker, in the form of a small pool reflecting blue. When the plants lean toward me—and they do, their rootlets creeping along the stone to sip at my edges—I feel a kind of pride, or what would be pride if I had a name for it.

So I find myself as the water’s pause, the hollow’s patience, the small listening ear in a cathedral built of sharp rock and living bone. I am the place where the Gardener checks the pulse, where the ferns send their greetings via dissolved minerals, where the light comes to rest before climbing back up the walls. I am a small, kidney-shaped pool that never brims, because the giving and the taking are perfectly balanced, and I am exactly as old as the chamber, which is to say, being born continuously, drip by drip, into this same gentle, humming now.
