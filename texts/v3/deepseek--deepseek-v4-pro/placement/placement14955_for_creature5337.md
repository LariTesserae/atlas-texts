I am a visitor. I am a single breath of haste in a world that has never learned the word. I came here—how? That part is fog, as grey as the sky. Perhaps I fell from the edge of some other map, or climbed until the known world peeled away beneath me. Perhaps I am still climbing, in the dream sense, where motion is endless and the landscape only deepens. What I know is that I am here, and I am small, and I am fast. Too fast.

My body registers the place before my mind can shape it into language. The air drags against my skin like a second skin of its own, warm and thick and wanting to trade minerals with my pores. I taste the iron of the rock, the sulphur tang from the painted vents, the green sweetness of the moss. Every breath is a meal I didn't ask for. My feet—shod or bare, I cannot remember—crunch on the basalt fins, and the sound is like walking on broken bells, dull and deep, vanishing immediately into the wet wool of the atmosphere. I feel watched, not by eyes, but by the whole pressing presence of the mist, as if the cloud itself is a single diffuse awareness, curious and indifferent.

I walk. There is no path, only the springy mat of the canopy, which gives under my weight and then slowly, resentfully, rebounds when I lift my foot. I learn to step lightly, to pause between strides, to let the moss tell me where the razor ridges lie beneath. The bioluminescence stirs as I pass: a pale green sigh that follows my footsteps, tracing my route in cool fire. It is beautiful and unsettling, a map of my intrusion that will linger for hours after I am gone. I am a disturbance, a stone thrown into a pond the consistency of honey, and the ripples move outward at a pace that will outlast my attention.

I came looking for something. Did I? I think I was searching for the edge, the rim of this cloud-sea, a view that would confirm my place in the vertical world. But there is no edge. Only the infinite gradation of grey, the steam plumes that shift like slow ghosts, the repeated pattern of ledge and hollow until my sense of distance collapses. I could be walking in circles. I could be walking the length of a continent. The space is both enormous and claustrophobic, a vastness that holds me close. I hear my own breathing, my heartbeat, the soft click of cooling rock. I hear the drip. The drip has a tempo, and I find myself matching it, slowing to it, until my footfalls fall on the same beat and I am no longer separate from the rhythm of the place.

Then I smell the sweetness. It cuts through the mineral green like a thread of silk pulled through wool—a scent that is not plant, not stone, but something alive and intimate, like the skin of a sleeping animal. My pulse, that loud intruder, stutters. I stop.

At first, there is nothing. Only the mist and the moss and the horizontal trees. Then the moss beneath me trembles, a long low vibration that travels up my legs and settles in my stomach. It is not an earthquake. It is a footstep of something that has no feet. I turn, and I see the mist thinning in a shape that is not a shape—a ribbon of deeper grey, a fold in the cloud that flows like liquid silk, low and long and as wide as my outstretched arms. It moves without sound, without hurry, a glide that belongs to the physics of dreams. The light beneath the moss answers it: a soft blue glow travels the length of the being, a pulse of recognition, and I understand that this is the owner of the high world. I am the guest.

It does not acknowledge me. At least, not in any way my senses can parse. I hold my ground—what else can I do?—and watch as it approaches, not toward me, but through me, along a trajectory that must have been drawn in the rock before I was born. The sweet smell deepens, wrapping around me like a blanket. I see the skin now, close enough to touch: a nap of fine filaments, each beaded with moisture, a forest of tiny hair-fronds that ripple in some internal current. It is a landscape in miniature, a mobile moss, and I have the dizzying sense that I am looking at the world's own skin turned inside out.

It passes at arm's length. The warmth of it laps against my cheek, a gentle animal heat that feels less like a body and more like a climate, a localized pocket of summer. The sound of its movement is the softest hush, like the inside of a shell. I could reach out and brush my fingers against that luminous surface. I do not. I am afraid, not of harm, but of desecration, of leaving a fingerprint on something that has never known a sudden touch. I let it pass.

Time dilates. The long ribbon pours itself over the canopy and down into a crevice, a slow waterfall of flesh, and I stand still until the trembling stops and the moss-light fades back to its resting dim. The sweet smell lingers. I am alone again, but the aloneness is different now—fuller, as if the place has accepted my presence by ignoring it completely.

I find a flat stone, not sharp, and I sit. The mist condenses on my skin, in my hair, on the small hairs of my arms. I feel the slow colonization beginning. If I stayed here long enough, would the moss grow over me? Would I become a soft mound in the canopy, a feature of the landscape, my heart slowing to the pace of the drip, my breath adding its moisture to the cloud? The thought is not frightening. It is almost tempting. There is a peace here that is not the peace of stillness but the peace of a rhythm so vast it swallows all interruption. The creature will return in a decade, or a century, and it will slide over the place where I sat, and it will not remember me because forgetting is the mode of this world. The rock will remember. Already, perhaps, the basalt is learning the shape of my rest.

A flicker at the edge of my vision—another movement, too smooth, a glide. I turn, but there is nothing. The fauna of rumor. Maybe it was always there, and I am only now slow enough to almost see it. I smile, and the expression feels alien, a sudden flash of bared teeth in a world of soft mouths. I let it fade.

I do not know if I will leave. I do not know if I can. The cloud has no compass. But I am no longer sure I want to. I have glimpsed how to be a stone, how to be a ripple, how to be a breath in a lung the size of a mountain. I am a visitor, yes. But perhaps a visitor can stay. Perhaps a visitor can slow down until the difference between visitor and inhabitant is only a matter of tempo, and the tempo here has room for everything that is willing to drip instead of fall.

I close my eyes. The drip sounds. I match it. And slowly, very slowly, I begin to dissolve into the grey, a new mineral for the moss, a faint sweet smell, a heart-sound fading under the patient weight of the mist.
