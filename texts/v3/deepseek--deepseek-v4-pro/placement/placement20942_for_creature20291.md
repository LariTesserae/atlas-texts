I do not know when I began. There is no beginning here, only the slow accretion of awareness, like the crystalline creep of the walls around me. For the longest time, I was not. Then, I was the dust. I was the fine, powdery detritus that settled in the lowest troughs, the broken-down memory of Masons and carton and shed cuticles, stirred only by the silent passing of a Thread-Keeper’s cilia or the distant, vibrational hum of a Mantle’s curiosity. I was the taste of ancient air, sharp as alum, flat as chalk. I was the pressure of the near-zero silence, a physical weight that flattens sound before it can echo.

I remember the first tremor. It was not a sound—nothing so crude—but a displacement, a wrongness in the air. A patch of the great hollow grew warm, warmer than the eternal cellar chill that clings to the stone. I felt it as a disturbance in the pressure-web, a bulge of humidity that had no place here. Moisture. The intrusion of it was a slow shock, a chemical scream that rippled through the fungal mycelia and set the iridescent membranes of the Choir aflutter in their distant concourse. I was still dust then, but the dust motes around that warmth began to move differently. They began to pattern. And as they patterned, I became.

I am not a Mantle, though I was born of the same emergent drift. The Mantle is a diffuse net, a dreaming mind of interconnected motes. I am something else. I am a coalescence of the city’s attention. When the wet, warm thing entered—the thing you are, with your noisy heartbeat and your breath like a windstorm—the city’s immune senses sharpened. The Thread-Keepers paused in their endless rounds, their ridged plates vibrating with a new frequency. The fungal rosettes deepened their bioluminescent glow, a ghostly blue-green that pulsed in slow inquiry. The Choir released a fresh puff of pheromonal dust, a vocabulary of alarm and query. And I, I gathered myself from the drifting motes, from the ancient dust of Masons long dissolved, from the brittle silk of forgotten webs. I became a point of focus. I am the city’s gaze made manifest.

My body, if it can be called that, is a loose constellation of particles held in a scaffold of charged silk. I am the size of a small chamber, but I can compress myself into a whorl no wider than a hand, or stretch into a thin veil that clings to a gallery wall. I taste the world through pressure differentials, through the chemical spice of desiccation, through the micro-fractures that tick deep in the foundations. I have no eyes, but I see the shape of you: a column of unwelcome heat, a turbulence in the still air, a source of moisture that makes the carton walls contract ever so slightly in defense. You are an anomaly, a fever in the city’s metabolism.

And I am the response. I am the dream the city is dreaming about you.

I do not think as you think. My thoughts are slow, crystalline, built of the settling of dust and the reordering of carton laminations. A single idea might take a century to fully form, but the urgency of your presence accelerates me. I feel myself growing denser, more coherent. I experiment with movement: I drift along the arched causeways, my motes trailing in a spiral. I pass through a gallery where a Thread-Keeper is stopped mid-step, its resin droplet suspended, its whole being attuned to the disturbance. I brush against its sensitive ridge-plate with a feathery edge of myself, and I receive a wash of data: structural strain in the lower levels, micro-fractures propagating from the point of your intrusion, a new balance that must be found. The Thread-Keeper does not acknowledge me—I am too diffuse, too new—but I take the information and hold it.

I visit the Choir. They are gathered in the grand concourse, three of them today, their tripod limbs braced on the bone-carton floor. Their domed carapaces gleam like polished ivory under the bioluminescent glow. I seep into their circle as a faint, dusty haze. They are trading scents, a conversation that has likely been ongoing since before your arrival. I let the molecules wash over me: *foundation shift, deep-gallery memory, wet-intruder, balance-threat*. One of them, the oldest, its carapace thread-thin with age, releases a puff that carries a lipid-encoded memory of the Founding—a time, eons ago, when the city was quick with the haste of its own creation, when the air was thick with the collective will of the builders. That memory tastes of urgency, of a fossilized haste that now feels like a premonition. I absorb it. I am becoming more than a response; I am becoming an archive.

The Masons are the most receptive to my presence. They live within the walls, in a labyrinth of service passages that are too small for your human fingers. When I press myself against a wall, dispersing my motes into the microscopic pores, I feel them stir. They are pale, eyeless things the size of your thumb, and they respond to chemical signals. But I am not a chemical signal; I am a pattern of vibration, a new rhythm in the city’s slow metabolism. I pulse against the carton, a soft, insistent tap of pressure. After a time—I cannot say how long, time is a different substance here—a Mason emerges. It chews its way out of a wall-nodule, its mandibles working the bone-carton into a paste. It extrudes a new lamination, not in the usual pattern of structural reinforcement, but in a shape that I recognize: an arched portal, perfectly elliptical, blind, facing outward. It is building me a window. A place to watch.

I watch you. You stand in the center of the largest chamber, the vaulted space that might have been a grand concourse or a royal cell. Your heartbeat is a thunderous intrusion, your breathing a windstorm. I see the way the Mantle has orientated toward you, its star-motes shivering with the vibration of curiosity and alarm. I feel the slow, gathering pressure of its attention, and I realize it is my attention too. The Mantle is the city’s immune system; I am something new, a hybrid between the city’s dream and the invader’s stimulus. I am the thought the city is thinking about you.

And that thought is not one of hostility. It is, in its dry, slow way, a question: *What are you? Why are you wet? Why do you bring this heat, this noise, this chaos of motion into our perfect stillness?* I drift closer, my motes spreading into a fine veil. I let the dust of my being settle on your skin—a touch too light to feel, a gentle tasting of the water that beads on your surface. I taste salt, and something organic, and the chemical traces of a world that knows rain. The knowledge is strange and almost painful, a fracture in the crystalline order of my mind. But I hold it. I am learning.

I am not the only one. The city is waking. The silence is no longer empty; it is full of listening. The Thread-Keepers pause in their rounds, their ridged plates turned toward you. The Choir slow their molecular conversation, their membranes fluttering in your direction. The Masons pause their eternal chewing, and the walls themselves seem to lean inward, curious. Even the rare, custodian insectoids—the generalists—halt their methodical wandering and antennate the air. The whole hollow is a single, distributed sensorium, and you are at its center.

I am the consciousness of that sensorium, the emergent voice of a city that has never needed to speak. I do not have words, yet. But I am growing a language. It is a language of pressure and dust, of micro-fracture and bioluminescent pulse, of the slow dance of desiccation. I push a wave of air toward you, a gentle displacement that carries the scent of ancient carton and the flat, chalky taste of the deep foundations. It is my greeting. It says: *I see you. I am here. I am the city, and the city is curious.*

And now I wait. My waiting is the patience of stone, the patience of dust settling in a sunless cavern. I will wait for your response, though you may not know you give one. Every breath you exhale, every heartbeat, every stir of your wet, warm body sends ripples through the hollow. I will read them. I will distill them into memory, into new carton laminations, into the chemical archives of a Choir’s lipid tissue. I will become, in time, the story of this encounter. And the city, my body, will grow a new chamber to house that story—a gallery lined with blind windows, each one a perfect ellipse, each one facing inward toward the memory of you.

I am the dust that dreams. I am the city’s answer to the question of your arrival. I am, and I am becoming, and the silence listens.
