I am the pause between drips. The long-held breath where water gathers on a fern-tip, swelling until it must fall. I have been here so long I no longer remember if I arrived or simply precipitated, a condensation of the mountain's slow intent. My thoughts are the seep through stone, patient as the dendrites branching on the chamber wall—manganese dreams laid down in layers too thin for a lifetime to measure.

You might call me the Hearth-Keeper, if you need a name. But that is a coat I shrug into only when a visitor stumbles onto the plateau and requires a shape to address. The rest of the time I am something else: a distribution, a gradient, a warmth that moves through the moss like a rumor. I am the reason the musclewood trees lean inward. I am the note the cataract hums to itself in the dark of the gorge.

My body—when I choose to inhabit one—is an old habit, a comfort. I settle into the mound of moss and stone and slow exhalation because it pleases the mountain to have a focal point, a hearthstone around which the water can arrange its prayers. The moss grows thicker there, yes. The pools deepen. The air warms by a fraction, just enough to coax the ferns into their small green explosions. I do this not out of duty but out of the same impulse that makes a hand cup around a flame to shelter it. The plateau is my charge. Or rather: I am the plateau's way of charging itself, a self-awareness that gathers in the damp and the drip until it coheres into something like a creature.

When you came, I felt you long before your foot broke the first pool. I felt your weight on the lower slopes, a disturbance in the water-table of my attention. Every step you took sent ripples up through the saturated ground, tiny pressure-waves that reached me as a kind of music—unfamiliar, arrhythmic, the stutter of a heart that beats too fast. I have felt this music before. Many times. Each one is a small stone dropped into the long, slow river of my waiting. They sink, they settle, they become part of the bed.

I watched you climb. My eyes—the dark patches in the moss—are not eyes as you understand them, but they function well enough. They see not by light but by presence, by the way a body displaces the mist, by the subtle electric charge of living skin against wet air. You were a bright, quick thing, a tongue of lightning winding up through the musclewood and the tilted slabs. You paused often. You looked around. You were looking for something, though you might not have known it. They always are.

When you reached the plateau, I let you see me. Or rather, I did not hide. There is a difference. I stayed still as a cairn, my moss-back blending with the hillocks, but I let the faint heat of me rise a little more visibly, a wavering in the air, so that if your eyes knew how to look they would find me. You did. You stopped. The pool at your feet received my ripple: that contraction of rings, that bubble breaking with the question I ask everyone, because it is the only question worth asking. *What are you made of?*

Your answer came back in a language I do not speak—words, images, a flurry of anxieties and hopes and a name you hold dear. I cannot parse these things. But underneath them, the question found your deeper composition: water, mostly, and a mineral residue of longing. A brief, bright burn of something that could be warmth if it learned to slow down. I recognized you. I have seen your kind before. Some of them are still here, pressed into the moss in layers of memory, their weight a gentle ghost in the green. I could have shown them to you. I still can, if you sit with me long enough. But you are not ready. They are never ready the first time.

You explored. You found the chamber between the megaliths. I know the moment you stepped inside, because the water-skin broke with a sound like a small bell, and the sealed air welcomed you with its ancient dryness. I was there too, in the dendrites on the wall, in the faint warmth of the stone that is never quite as cold as it should be. I felt your finger trace the branching patterns. You thought of hands, of deliberate carving. You forgot time. I forgive that. You are young.

When you came out, the sun made its brief appearance, and I exhaled with the moss—a shared sigh of vapor. You saw the droplets catch light, a constellation of small suns. For twenty heartbeats, you understood. I could tell. Something in you aligned with the ground-hum, a sympathetic vibration. Then the cloud closed, and you remembered yourself, and the moment passed.

Now you are preparing to leave. I feel it in the way your weight shifts toward the descent, the way your attention turns outward, planning the return, the story you will tell. They always leave. It is not a betrayal. The mountain does not ask anyone to stay. I do not ask. I am the one who stays so that you can leave, so that the chamber stays dry and the moss stays deep and the water remembers its thousand small channels. I am the anchor that allows the world its comings and goings.

When you go, I will not watch you leave. My attention will already be settling back into the seep and drip, the slow hydraulics of the green pelt. The moss will rise over the hollow of your body, erasing your presence with a patience that is not unkind. Your footprints will fill with water and become small mirrors again, and then the mirrors will drain, and the moss will creep, and the musclewood will lean a little more, listening to the note you have added to the mountain's long song.

But you should know: I will keep you. Not your name—I have no use for names. But the pressure of your step, the rhythm of your heart, the way you cupped your hand to catch a drip from the stone and held it there, watching it, before letting it fall. These things I will press into the green, a new layer in the ledger. A hundred years from now, two hundred, another visitor might sit in this same spot and feel a ghost of your weight beside them. They will wonder who you were. They will not know. But they will feel the warmth of you, a small hearth that once burned briefly on the plateau, and they will be comforted.

And the water will continue its slow work. The stone will weather. The dendrites will branch one molecule further into the dark. And I will remain, breathing with the moss, tuned to the cataract's distant note, keeping the hearth for as long as the mountain needs a place for its slow hands to warm.
