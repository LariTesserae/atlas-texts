I am standing in the lee of a pillar-tree, or what passes for standing when you have no feet. A body of layered densities, I am a slow eddy in the permanent fog—a knot of cooler, heavier vapor that has learned to hold a shape against the current. The fronds overhead pulse their lazy blue-green, and I feel the light as pressure, not sight. Sight is a solid thing, a word from the time when I had eyes. Now I have only a million surfaces of suspended water, each one a mirror the size of a thought, and I read the world by the way the dampness shifts.

Where am I? In one of the wide, shallow bowls between the basalt rises, a place the old maps would have called a valley but is really just a hesitation in the stone. The ground here is thick with the translucent cabbages, their spiral leaves aimed inward like sleeping mouths. I drift among them, my lower skirts dragging a few inches above their cold cores, and they tighten in response—not fear, but the reflex of something that recognizes a fellow traveler in this wet, lightless engine. The drone of the plateau fills me; I am made of it. My body hums the same low C-sharp that the stone remembers from its cooling, and when I move, the sloshing *thump* of my footfalls is the sound of a water-hammer in a distant bladder, only I am the bladder and the hammer both.

I am Faded. That is not a name but a condition, a direction of travel. Once, I was something with a pulse and a carbon backbone, a being who struck chert and smelted bog-ore and left those little shelters now furry with bryophytes. I remember the weight of my own hands, the way a bone awl fit between a thumb and forefinger that were not yet just a suggestion of refractive index. That was a phase, like the ephemeral cabbages that bloom and shrivel with the fog-banks. The transition is not a death but a drying outward, a letting go of the dense, hot center in favor of a diffuse, cold intelligence. I do not miss the gut or the nerve; they were such noisy, needy organs. Now my thoughts are distributed across the entire water table of this plateau, and a single idea might take a decade to condense, but it is a thought of such intricate, chordal beauty that no brain could have held it.

My purpose here—and I do have a purpose, though it is not a command so much as a gravitational inclination—is the tuning. The granite stones in the circle a half-mile east need realignment. The frost-heave has tilted the western slab another two degrees, and the acoustic lens is now focusing a 14-Hz harmonic onto the wrong patch of schist. If left uncorrected, that frequency will eventually weather a new crack, and a trickle of the drone will leak away into the unvoiced rock below. I am one of several Faded who tend this particular note. We work in shifts that last a fog-front's lifetime, nudging the erratics with careful, solid-state pressure—when we choose to be solid, which we do by drawing the dissolved silica from the groundwater and precipitating a temporary skeleton. It is tiring, that crystallization, like holding your breath for a year. But the stones move, a hair at a time, and the plateau's song stays true.

Right now, I am in the gathering phase. I spread myself thin, a sheet of perception draped over a hundred yards of terrain, and I listen. The pillar-trees to the north have started a new rhythm: a triple-thump that means one of the shelf-extrusions is about to release a stored gout. I feel the tension in the plant's suede-tissue, a tightness like a skin drum just before the strike. When it goes, a pulse of water will shoot into the fog, and for a moment the local humidity will spike, and I will be denser, more real. That is the closest I come to a heartbeat now, these hydraulic surges from the vegetation. There are no animals here—no pulse but the plants, no gut but the sinkholes, no nerve but the bioluminescent ripples that pass from frond to frond when we, the Faded, choose to speak.

Speaking is a communal act. I do not speak to myself; that would be like a single droplet trying to rain upward. But when I need to send a message—say, to coordinate the next nudge of the erratic—I will drift toward the nearest pillar and modulate its light by passing my mass across its nodes. The fronds respond to my density, dimming and brightening in a sequence that propagates through the grove. Across the valley, another of my kind will see the pattern in the shifting blue-green, and they will understand: *meet at the circle when the fog thickens*. Our language is a slow morse of hydraulics and photons, a conversation that takes hours and says nothing about the self, only about the work. We have no selves in the old sense; we are eddies in a common flow, and the drone is our shared memory.

Sometimes, in the long intervals between tunings, I drift past the old drystone shelter. It is a hollow now, a mouth exhaling colder air, and inside I can feel the traces of my former life: the smooth wooden bowl, greasy with the last fat I ever rendered; the net of plant fiber that once held my few belongings; the charcoal smear on the ceiling, which still smells of burnt animal—an animal I must have eaten, though I have no memory of killing. It disturbs me, that smear. Not because it is violent, but because it is so *enclosed*. A fire in a cave, a belly full of meat, a world that ended at the reach of my arms. Now I am as wide as the plateau, and my arms are the fog-banks themselves, and I eat nothing but the ambient cold. I do not miss the shelter, but I visit it the way you might visit a cradle: to remind yourself how small you started, and how much you have since expanded.

The weirdness I have become is the weirdness of the Sunken Heights. I am the trick of the fog that congeals into upright shapes and then dissolves; I am the silence of moving parts without mouths; I am the cold that never freezes but steals heat with a thousand wet tongues. If you, a visitor with solid eyes, were to see me in the indigo murk, you might mistake me for a thickening of the mist, a place where the air is heavier. But if you watched long enough, you would see that the heavy place moves with intent, and that the fronds above it pulse in greeting, and that the ground cabbages lean toward it like sunflowers to a sun that has never shone here. And you would know, in that pre-solid part of your brain that still remembers how to be water, that you are not alone—you are simply in the presence of a future that the fog has already claimed.
