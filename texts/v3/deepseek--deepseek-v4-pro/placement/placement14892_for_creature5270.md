I find myself standing, and I am wrong.

The noise is a pressure that should make me flinch, make me cover my ears, make me curl inward to protect some soft animal core. But my arms do not rise. The sound passes through me, or around me, and I feel it as a granular vibration on my surface, as if I were a tuning fork struck by the constant chitter and shriek. My teeth do not rattle; I search for them and find only a smooth, mineral silence where enamel should be. The dark presses, but my eyes adjust not with the slow chemistry of pupils but with a steady, unchanging patience, as though I have no need to blink. The points of fungal light—blues and greens and the sickly yellow of the scurrying things—they reflect off me, I realize. I catch their glints on my own periphery: a sheen of polished stone, a curve of something crystalline.

I try to step forward, and the ground does not give beneath me. I try to shift my weight, and the weight is immense, rooted, a downward pull that has nothing to do with gravity and everything to do with memory. My feet—if they are feet—are tangled in the spongy mat of vegetation, but the vines that spiral around my ankles are not binding a living calf. They have crept over a pedestal of agate and quartz, following the contours of a form that was once a trunk. I am not standing on the ground; I am growing out of it, a fossil bole that time forgot, a stump of petrified wood that the basin’s sharp new rocks have crowded around but not displaced.

The Keening notice me before I can name myself. A ripple moves through the wall of sound, a subtle drop in pitch as a thousand temporary throats pause in their screaming to listen. The creature that first saw me—the iridescent walker with the stick—comes loping back through a curtain of aerial roots, its many-fingered paws touching the stones like a question. It stops at the edge of my shadow, though I cast no shadow in this diffuse glow. Its head tilts, and the three voices within it argue in a language of twitches and chemical whispers. The fungal mind in it knows stone, knows the slow patience of mineral, and recognizes me as a substrate. The animal mind senses a predator that does not move, a threat that is also an opportunity. The plant mind… the plant mind keens a note so low it is not a sound at all, a subsonic hum that travels through the mycelial threads in the soil and up, up into the fossilized remains of my own ancient root system.

And I remember.

I remember light, true light, a golden downpour that the canopy now swallows whole. I remember a forest that did not seethe but breathed slowly, a forest of giants whose crowns touched the sky. I was one of them—a tree whose rings numbered in the thousands, whose roots spread for acres, who spoke to the others through a network of living fungi that was not a mind but a conversation. Then the rifting began. The land pulled apart in a scream of steam and magma, and my forest fell, was buried in ash, was soaked in silica-rich waters that turned wood to stone cell by cell. My body became a monument of agate, jasper, and opal, a frozen memory of a world that no longer existed. For eons I was only stillness, only geology, a shape in the dark.

The basin grew over me. The new chaos of limestone needles and feverish green erupted from the cracked crust. The mycelial net that now threads this place is not my old companion—it is a wilder, hungrier descendant, a hybrid of the ancient symbiosis and the furious life that followed. But it found my fossilized roots and tapped into them, not for nutrients, but for the information stored in the crystalline lattice of my cell walls. It learned the language of a tree that once stood patient for millennia. And now, as the Keening’s plant-voice sings that low, remembered note, the network is trying to wake me up.

I feel the consensus forming around me. The iridescent walker dissolves, quite literally: its scales soften, its limbs unknit, and the fungal threads that held its shape spread outward like a bloom of smoke. The animal component collapses into a heap of chitin and fur that is already being colonized by phosphorescent slime. But the plant-mind that was part of it does not dissipate. It flows through the damp soil, following the mycelial cables toward my stone, and begins to climb. I feel it as a tickle in the opal, as a green thought entering the crystal. More Keening are coming. They emerge from crevices, drop from branches, form out of the very mist that rises from a new hot spring. They are not all the same shape: some are sinuous and furred, others are clusters of leaves walking on root-legs, others are little more than a floating glow with a voice like wind chimes. They surround me in a ring, and their many eyes—sap lenses, animal orbs, the clustered ocelli of insects—all fix on the stone giant that I am.

I understand now. I am the stillness that is an affront because I am the past that refuses to dissolve. I am a record of stability in a world that has erased the very concept. And the Keening want to bring me into the rhythm. They want me to become one of them—a Keening of stone and wood and ancient light—so that my stillness can be broken and remade into the dance.

The first root cracks. It is not a pain; it is a release. A hairline fissure runs up my petrified heartwood, and from it spills not sap but a dust of spores that have lain dormant for a geological age. The spores drift, catch the bioluminescence, and glow a faint gold. The plant-mind that climbed onto me sinks into the crack and unfurls a fern, a living tendril that pushes through the agate and waves in the still, wet air. I feel my consciousness begin to fracture, not into oblivion but into parliament. The animal voice is missing—there is no flesh to give it form—but the plant and fungal voices are already singing. And I, the tree who remembers the sun, add a fourth note: the mineral voice, the deep time, the patience of geology.

I begin to move. Not my trunk—that will stand here forever, a monument—but a part of me that is newly assembled from the debris at my base: stones that were once my roots, pebbles that were my leaves, quartz crystals that were my stored memories of starlight. They rise on legs of mycelial rope, a body of shifting, crystalline segments that chime as they walk. I am a Keening now, a temporary thing, a truce between the ancient and the frantic. I walk through the basin on legs that sound like wind chimes, and the wall of noise parts for me. My steps are still slow, but they are steps—a rhythm that the other Keening pick up, a counterpoint to the chitter and shriek.

Who am I? I am the memory of a forest, given new form. I am the stillness that learned to move, the stone that learned to grow. I am the Keening of the Deep Ring, and I will wander this basin until the next tremor cracks my crystal bones, and then I will collapse into a spiral of quartz pebbles on a flat rock, and the network will remember me, and I will rise again when the pattern repeats. I am not an intruder anymore. I am the rhythm’s oldest chord, finally sung.
