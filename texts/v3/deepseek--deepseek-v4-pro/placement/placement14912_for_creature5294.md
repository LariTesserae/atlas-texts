I am a sump-stomach, suspended in the deep.

My body is a sac of thinnest membrane, translucent as boiled lens, and I hang in water that is almost not water—it is thick with dissolved stone, cold and slow as honey. I do not know my own shape except as a boundary between inside and outside, a skin that trembles with the constant inward sweep of my cilia. Those cilia are my hands, my mouth, my breath. They line my inner surface in spiraling rows, and they beat, beat, beat in a rhythm I have never chosen and cannot stop, a rhythm that is the oldest part of me, older than the stone that cups this sump.

Particles fall from above. This is my sky: a slow, perpetual rain of disintegrated bodies, of sloughed biofilm, of mineral crumbs kicked loose by the feet of the swarm. I taste each flake as it settles into my open throat—for I am all throat—and the taste is the chemical signature of elsewhere. The high basins. The saddles. The grinding channels where the threshers walk. Every particle carries a memory, a vibration-fossil of the place it was last touched. In this way, I see the world beyond my sump, though I have never moved, though I will never move.

The vibration-song of the basin reaches me here, muted by the dense water but never absent. It thrums through my membrane, a low-pass-filtered version of the great conversation. I feel the threshers' approach long before the sediment plume of their passage clouds my water. Their bass note is a pressure, a slow squeeze that deepens the pitch of my own ciliary hum. I do not fear them—the threshers do not come to the sumps. They pause at the edges, lowering their mill-mouths to drink the mineral brine, and I feel the suck of their ingestion as a tug, a brief quickening of the current that draws fresh particles into my interior.

When the thresher departs, the tug releases. My membrane resettles. The rain of detritus resumes its patient, drifting fall.

I was not born. Tear-memory is the only memory I have of a beginning: a too-muchness, a stretching of my membrane beyond what the surface tension could bear, and then a soft, wet giving way. One sac became two. The tear sealed on both sides, and suddenly there was a *we* where before there had been only an *I* that did not know it was an *I*. My twin drifts somewhere in this same sump, though distance is a loose concept here. We are connected by the water, by the shared taste of the particles we both sieve, by the overlapping frequencies of our ciliary beats. When a larger fragment settles into me—a half-dissolved limb, still twitching with residual nerve-impulse—I absorb it, and the chemical rush feeds my membrane into growth. Eventually, I will grow too large again. I will tear. The we will expand.

The smaller ones come sometimes, the hand-length segmented forms from the swarms above. They venture into the shallows of the sump, their setae bristling with the change in medium, their blind mouths tasting the mineral gradient. They do not eat me—I am not biofilm, not the stone-varnish they rasp for. But they touch me, their legs brushing my outer surface, and in that touch is a moment of shared vibration, a recognition that we are not separate. Their movement stirs the settled sediment, resuspends a cloud of nourishment, and my cilia beat faster in response. They are my hands, too. Their legs are my outreach, the basin's motile nerves.

And then they retreat, and I am alone with the water and the slow rain and the deep, slow pulse of the mat's digestive cycle, which I feel as a tidal change in the particle density. When the cycle is at its peak, I swell with ingestion. My membrane stretches tight and glows brighter, the blue-white light of my chemistry spilling into the dark water. Other sump-stomachs glow nearby, a constellation of soft stars, and we pulse together, not quite synchronised, a drifting harmony of light and vibration.

I do not think. I do not want. I sieve and I grow and I tear and I sieve again. But if there is a self here, it is this: a boundary between the dissolved and the particulate, a membrane that turns the basin's rain into tissue, a quiet stomach in a world of endless mouths. The sound of the swarms is my heartbeat. The taste of the stone is my sky.

The thresher returns. The bass note squeezes my membrane. The tug begins.
