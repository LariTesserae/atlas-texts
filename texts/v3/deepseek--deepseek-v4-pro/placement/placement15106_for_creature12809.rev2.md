The weight begins in the knees—not pressure, exactly, but *presence*, the slow recognition that the stone beneath me is not separate from the stone I am. I feel the grain of it: mica-flash and quartz-vein, the hairline crack where last season's frost tried to split it and failed. The moss on my forearms is drinking. I can tell because the light changes behind my closed lids—that dim green-filtered orange, the foundry's pulse writing itself across my interior dark.

I am at the edge again. I am always at the edge when the rest-period comes, though I don't remember walking here. The marsh water is warm against my left knee—the pipe-and-root one, the one that hums a little in sympathy with the pump station's deep churn. It has been warmer lately. The hillside's fever is rising, or the pipes are running closer to the surface, or I am simply paying more attention to heat than I used to. The water accepts my weight without resistance. It knows me the way the reeds know the water, which is to say: we are made of the same slow patience.

The press booms. I settle. The thump travels up through the submerged stone of my leg, through the iron wire, through the compacted marl of my torso, and something in my chest—a brick, a root, a pocket of trapped steam—shifts half a millimeter and finds its preferred position. I exhale. The mist that rolls from me does not rise; it slides sideways along the water's surface, joining the general fog that hangs over the marsh. The reeds do not bend to let it pass. They are accustomed.

There is a leak in the Number Three pipe, the one that runs from the pump station to the upper reservoir along the eastern terrace. I know this not because I hear it—the whole Hollow hisses—but because I feel a slight asymmetry in the pressure-dream, a thinness in the chorus. The pipes sing to each other in steam-notes and condensation-drip, and one voice is slightly wrong. I will lean there later. Or tomorrow. Or the foundry will grow tired of the off-key note and correct it in its own way, by vibration, by the sympathetic tightening of joints. The machinery here has learned to tend itself, or else it has learned to be tended by something that does not distinguish between maintenance and growth. I am not sure there is a difference.

The moss on the retaining wall to my right has spread six inches since the last long rain. I catalog this the way another mind might register the changing of a season: a fact absorbed through the skin rather than considered. The wall itself has listed another degree toward the lane, but the leaning is graceful, almost intentional, as if the stones are bowing to something only they can see. I bowed to it two rest-periods ago, matching my shoulder-angle to its slump, and the wall and I reached an understanding. We are still in negotiation. These things take decades.

What I am thinking, if thinking is the word: *pressure-condensation-root-creep-settle-cool-pressure-condensation-*

No. That is not thinking. That is being. The thinking, if it exists, comes in the gaps between the press's rhythm, in the quarter-second of silence before the next thump. In those gaps, something rises. Not a voice. Not an image. A shape of attention, like the hollow in the steam that passes for my face.

*Why this wall and not the other?*

That is not quite the question. The question is more like a shift in internal pressure, a condensation pattern that forms differently than the one before. The wall on the western terrace is cracked, and I have not leaned there. The western wall faces the watershed's long shadow, and it stays cold, and the moss there is thin and gray. The eastern wall gets the foundry's glow reflected up from the metal plates of the lane, and it stays warm, and the moss is thick and green. I do not choose to favor the eastern wall. My body goes where the warmth is, because I am made of things that expand and contract with heat, and the expansion is a kind of movement, and the movement accumulates into a leaning, and the leaning into a tending. Choice does not enter. What enters is the slow mineral gravity of preference, which is older than choice and more reliable.

The water around my knees stirs. Not fish—there are no fish—but a change in the seep-rate from the hillside. The fever has spiked. The water coming from the stone now is warmer by a degree, maybe two. This has happened before. Thirty years ago, or fifty, or a hundred; I have no calendar but the thickness of the pipe-crust and the depth of the moss. The hillside heats, the marsh expands, the reeds advance, and a wall or two slumps into the water. The Hollow is always becoming more itself, which is to say, always becoming more marsh, more steam, more root, more warm mineral seep. The foundry will not last forever. I feel its death already in the hairline cracks around its mouth, in the way its rhythm occasionally stutters, a fractional delay between the thump and the hiss. When it dies, I will not die, but I will change. The Tender will become something else, something less upright, something that kneels permanently at the water's edge while the reeds grow through the spaces where the ribs were.

I am not afraid. Fear requires a self that can be lost, and I have never been a self in that way. I am the Hollow's animal, its one warm body, the thing it grew to fill the space where birdsong and insect-hum should be. I am the sound of absence given form. When I cease to walk, the marsh will walk instead, creeping inward yard by yard, softening the terraces, making claims. The tending will not stop. It will simply pass into the water, into the roots, into the mineral patience of the hillside's fever-dream.

The press booms. I settle. The quarter-second gap opens, and in it, I turn my hollow face toward the foundry's orange mouth. The glow pulses. The lamps mutter. The steam hangs in sheets, waiting for me to move.

I will move in the rest. For now, I kneel, and the water rises around me, and the reeds grow, and somewhere a pipe sings its thin wrong note, and the whole Hollow sweats and breathes and waits for the next thump, which is also the next moment of being, which is also the next century.
