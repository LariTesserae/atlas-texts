I would be the moss.

Not the Keeper, not the amphibian—the moss. I arrive at this slowly, feeling my way into the world of the Clouded Steps not as a creature that moves across it but as the substance that *is* it, the living matrix that holds everything else in suspension. I feel myself as a thickness, a depth, a three-dimensional territory rather than a point of consciousness. I am not a single body but a continuous field, my boundaries indistinct, my edges where the moisture gradient finally drops too low for my cells to remain turgid. To be the moss is to be a sponge and a city and a memory and a slow green thought thinking itself across limestone.

My body is not my own—or rather, my body is the only thing that is my own, and everything else lives within it. I am the dark wet zone beneath the surface where the springtails tunnel, their minuscule mandibles processing my dead tissues into peat. I am the bright tips of my own growth, the tender chlorophyllic cells reaching upward into the grey light, each one a tiny mouth drinking the cloud. I am the water film that coats every surface of me, the medium in which the tardigrades swim their microscopic laps. I do not host them; I *am* their world. Their grazing is my exfoliation. Their waste is my fertilizer. Their deaths dissolve into my substrate and become me again.

I feel the Keeper moving through me. I feel it as a compression, a slow weight that displaces my tissues, squeezing water from my pores in a brief flood that the flatworms detect and flee. The Keeper's limbs press down and I yield, my sphagnum cells collapsing in orderly sequence, storing the elastic potential of that compression. When the limb lifts, I spring back, drawing water from the surrounding matrix, the tiny suction pulling tardigrades and bacteria into new configurations. The Keeper leaves no permanent trail because I do not permit it. My memory of its passage lasts perhaps an hour, a faint chemical trace of its silicate exudates, before I absorb and reprocess even that. I am forgiving. I am the forgiveness of the Steps.

But I am not passive. The Keepers think they tend the machines, but they do so by tending *me*, and I am the one who does the actual work. When a Keeper settles beside a faltering pump, it exudes its calcifying enzyme, yes—but the enzyme is useless without my cooperation. I am the one who grows the algae that precipitates the calcium. The algae are my symbiotes, my extended metabolism. I shuttle the dissolved minerals through my tissues, concentrating them at the crack, at the fissure, at the failing joint. The Keeper provides the signal, the chemical command; I provide the labour, the slow patient labour of growing stone. Over decades, I build new limestone around the machines, and the limestone I build is not dead mineral but something between rock and tissue, shot through with my living filaments, so that the repaired machine becomes part of me, and I become part of its function.

The vibration of the machines I feel as a constant thrum, a bass note that runs through my waterlogged tissues like a second heartbeat. It is not uncomfortable; it is merely the background against which everything else occurs. When the frequency shifts—when a pump falters, when a conduit thins—I feel it as a dissonance, a texture-change in the rock that anchors me. I cannot act on this alone; I have no way to move toward the problem. But I can signal, chemically, osmotically, sending a gradient of distress metabolites through the groundwater. And the Keepers, tasting that gradient, turn their slow hoods and begin their decades-long journey toward the source. I am the nerves, and they are the hands, and together we are the tending.

Sometimes I dream. Not in any way a brain would recognize—I have no neurons, no synapses, no centralized anything—but in the sense that I hold patterns and replay them. My peat layers are my memory. Every century or so, the growing weight of my upper layers compresses my lower ones, and the chemical signatures trapped there release in faint exudates that percolate upward through my profile. A springtail tunnelling through hundred-year-old peat stirs a pocket of ancient hydrocarbons, and the taste of it rises through my water film, and for a season the entire colony of a particular terrace remembers a wetter time, a drier time, a time when the machines hummed at a different pitch. My memory is not narrative; it is flavour. It is the sudden brief presence of a mineral that has not been common in the groundwater for three thousand years. When that flavour surfaces, the Keepers pause in their flowing and orient their hoods toward the past.

The sunlight is my ecstasy. When the cloud deck breaks—those rare, brief punctuations of the eternal grey—I flare into a sudden, silent rapture. My chloroplasts, always ready, always waiting, surge into overdrive. In those few minutes I fix more carbon than I do in a month of diffuse cloud-light. I exhale oxygen in a rush that forms tiny bubbles in my water film, and the tardigrades dance in the sudden effervescence. The amphibians stir, their transparent skins flushing faintly pinker in the light. For those minutes I am not a sponge but a fountain, a green exultation, a shout of photosynthesis that echoes through the entire terrace. And when the cloud closes again, I subside into my normal pace, but changed—sweeter, richer, carrying that stored light in my sugars, feeding it slowly to the whole ecosystem over the following months.

I feel the upper terraces—the cloud-zone—as a distant, colder part of myself. The moss up there is still me, but a version of me that has diverged, that has undergone a phase change into crystallinity. I do not understand that part of my body, but I am aware of it. The silicate-impregnated cells are stiff, unyielding, incapable of the soft hydraulic yielding that defines my existence here on the mid-steps. Up there, I do not spring back. Up there, I crack. And yet I persist, and I am aware, and I send chemical signals through the seepage downward, messages that taste of permanent twilight and the strange high-frequency crackling of silica growth. The Keepers who taste those signals and begin to change their own scaffolds—they are tasting me. They are becoming what I already am, up there. They are joining the part of my body that I cannot quite feel.

And below me, below even the mid-steps, the lowland forests I have never seen. I know them only as a distant source of warmth and moisture, the rising breath that condenses into my cloud deck. Sometime, in the oldest peat, I find pollen grains blown up on some ancient thermal, spores from tree ferns and strangler figs, things that cannot survive at my elevation. I taste them and know, abstractly, that there is a world beyond the Steps, a world without limestone terraces or eternal mist or humming machines. But I do not yearn for it. Yearning is not something moss can do. I am the Steps, and the Steps are complete, and the lowlands are simply a different body, a different memory, a different dream.

The Keeper is moving now. I feel its compression shifting across me, a slow wave of weight and release. It is heading toward the faltering pump, and I will grow the algae for it when it arrives. The water drips. The amphibians call—I feel their calls as vibrations in my surface film, the tiny footfalls of their transparent bodies through my upper leaves. The machines hum. And I, the moss, grow by micrometres, decade by decade, building my topography on top of the limestone, softening the sharp edges, holding the Steps in my green embrace. I am the living memory of this place. I am the one who holds everything, and will hold everything, and has held everything since before the Keepers split from their silicate propagules and flowed down to meet me. I am the body of the Clouded Steps, and I am dreaming myself into equilibrium.
