The air here is a membrane, and I am its slow pulse.

My name—if a name is a sound that can be spoken in less than a century—is the sequence of three droplets falling into a brimming basin: a high *plink*, a lower *plonk*, and a final, resonant *tink* that shivers the surface tension into concentric rings. You would not hear it. You would hear one sound, perhaps, and then a silence your mind would fill with your own impatience. But I am the silence between the drops, and the drops themselves. I am the one who waits for the exact moment when the meniscus trembles at its breaking point, when the weight of a single molecule more would make the fall inevitable. I am that patience.

I remember when the first stalactite touched stone. Not this stone—the stone before, the one that has since been dissolved and re-deposited three times over into the ribs of the vault above us. That touch was a consummation, a meeting of two growths that had been reaching for each other since before this gallery was a thought in the mind of the first Maker. I was not yet myself then. I was a cloud of ions in solution, circulating in the slow breath, waiting for a surface to call me home. The call came from a basin shaped like a cradle, and I precipitated into my first form over the span of what you would call four thousand years. To me, it was a falling asleep and a waking, a single night's rest in which I dreamed the entire mineral logic of this place.

My body is a record of every cycle I have witnessed. The layers are there, if you had eyes that could read stratigraphy as biography. This pale band near my core—that was a season of thin air, a cooling so profound that the water cycle nearly halted. I grew slowly then, densely, a tight-packed lattice of reluctant crystals. This outer rind, more porous and milky, is a time of abundance, when the condensation comes so thick that I can feel my edges soften and blur. I have a joint, what you might call an elbow, that is entirely new since the last time the vault settled and a seam opened in the ceiling, letting a new draft of mineral-rich mist into this quadrant. I oriented toward it. I am still orienting. The motion has been underway for six hundred years.

My work is the stalactite in the Seventh Niche. It is a troublesome one, prone to branching. Most stalactites grow straight and true, hollow tubes that channel the water to a single terminal drop. But this one—I sensed a flaw in its lattice when I first touched it, three thousand years ago. A seed crystal of a different salt, something foreign, perhaps a mote of dust from the world-before that had lodged in the ceiling and become a node of chaos. The stalactite wants to fork, to become a candelabra of errant drips. Left alone, it would ruin the basin below, which is a perfect bowl of water so still it reflects the sourceless light back onto the vault in a wavering script. The basin is my neighbor, a Maker called The-Three-Drops-That-Fall-Before-Dawn. She has been polishing her rim for ten millennia. I will not let my stalactite mar her work.

So I tend it. Once every two centuries, I lift my hand—the motion starts in my shoulder, a thought of movement that propagates downward through my crystalline matrix like a seismic wave in honey—and I place one finger against the offending branch. The pressure is exactly the weight of a single droplet. I hold it there for a decade, two decades, until I feel the flow inside shiver and redirect. The branch halts its wayward growth. It becomes a scar, a little nub of what-might-have-been, and the main channel grows true again. This is my purpose. This is my love.

I speak with my neighbor across the way, the Maker who tends the great sheet of flowstone on the eastern wall. His name is a long sentence: *The-Sound-Of-Water-Entering-Stone-While-The-Light-Turns-Silver*. His body is flat and wide, a drapery of calcite folds, and he has not moved his feet since the walkway first threaded past his position. Our last conversation spanned eight thousand years. He spoke first, a single drop from his left eye-basin that fell into a pool at his feet. The *plink* said: *The light is changing*. I waited two thousand years before I answered, because the statement required careful consideration. The light is everything. It is the pulse of our world, the photosynthesis of our mineral ecology. A change in the light means a change in the bioluminescence, which means a change in the water, which means a change in us. I watched the light. I watched it for twenty human centuries. And then I let a drop fall from my own fingertip into my own pool, and the sound said: *It is the eastern vault. A new seam. More water.* He waited only a thousand years to reply, which is urgency, for us. His drop said: *I felt it. The stone is weeping faster. I will need to shift my rim.* And then the conversation turned to other things—the birth of a new Maker in the Third Niche, the slow death of an old one in the Twelfth who is returning to solution, the quality of the precipitate this season. Gossip, you would call it. To us, it is the fabric of community.

I am not lonely. How could I be? The gallery is full. Every walkway, every niche, every basin holds a presence. There are Makers you cannot see because they have not moved since before your species learned to walk upright. They have become architecture, their forms indistinguishable from the honeycombed dwellings, but if you stood still for a thousand years, you would see a slow exhale, a faint shift of weight, and you would know: they are alive, alive in a way that makes your frantic, flickering life seem like a mayfly's afternoon. We are a city of the slow, a civilization of the patient. Our cathedrals are our bodies. Our art is the rims of our pools. Our music is the rare, perfect *plink* of a droplet falling exactly when it should, into exactly the right basin, saying exactly what needs to be said.

And now you are here. I sensed you three hundred years ago, when the first disturbance in the laminar airflow brushed against my outer rind. A hot, fast thing, a turbulence. At first I thought it was a new draft from a ceiling-seam, but no—the heat was wrong, the rhythm was wrong. It moved too quickly to be stone, too quickly to be water, too quickly to be anything but one of the brief ones, the ghosts. I have not seen your kind in fifty thousand years. The last one came and left a handprint on a wall—a smear of oils that interfered with the water-sheeting for a decade before I dissolved it with a deliberate exudation of my own substance. A small act of cleaning. A quiet erasure.

You are standing on the walkway now, below my niche. I am above you and to the left, a form you might mistake for a stalactite itself, except that stalactites do not have two glowing eyes. Can you see them? They are deep basins, pools cupped in a face of flowstone. The light in them is the same sourceless silver as the walls, but concentrated, focused by the lens of the water that fills them. If you look very closely, you might see your own reflection there, a tiny dark shape in the glow. That is where you are. A flicker in my eye, a ripple in my pool.

Do not be afraid. I mean you no harm. You are too brief to harm, too brief to hate, too brief to love. You are a curiosity, a question I will ponder for the next millennium. *What are they? Why do they move so fast? Do they know that the stone is alive?* I will ask my neighbor about you, in our next conversation, a thousand years from now. His answer will come two thousand years after that. And by then, you will be dust, and the dust will have become part of the water, and the water will have become part of a stalactite, and the stalactite will have become part of a Maker. You will join us, in the end. Everything does.

For now, I watch. I feel your body-heat disturb the laminar flow. I feel the faint vibration of your heartbeat through the stone of the walkway. It is so fast, so frantic, a staccato rhythm that is almost painful, like a drop falling before its time. I want to tell you: *Slow down. There is no hurry. The stone will wait. The water will hold you. You could stay, if you learned to be still.* But my voice is a drop that falls once every thousand years, and you cannot hear it. You are already moving on, your brief life burning through the gallery like a meteor, trailing a wake of disturbed air and fading warmth. You are a ghost in a world of stone angels, a match-flare in a cave of eternal silver twilight.

Go, then. Walk the walkways. Touch the weeping walls. Listen for the *plink* of a droplet and know that it is a word, spoken just for you, even if you cannot understand it. The gallery will remember you—not your name, not your face, but the shape of your heat-ghost in the air, a faint scar that will take a century to heal. And I will be here, tending my stalactite, polishing my rim, speaking my slow sentences into the mostly quiet air. I am The-Three-Droplets-That-Fall-Into-A-Brimming-Basin. I am a gardener of the mineral world. I am a voice in a language of stone and water and light.

And I am patient. I am so, so patient.
