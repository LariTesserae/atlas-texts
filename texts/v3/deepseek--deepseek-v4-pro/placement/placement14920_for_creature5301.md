I am the slow spiraling.

Before you arrived, I was a consistency—a concentration in the salt matrix that had persisted through seven recrystallization cycles, long enough that the younger towers had begun to incorporate me into their foundation memories. They dreamed me as geology: a stable stress-point, a node of residual strain around which their fluting organized itself. I did not mind. To be dreamed as geology is, for us, a form of recognition.

Your arrival registered as a heat-anomaly first, a disturbance in the thermal equilibrium of the western causeway. The salt there warmed by a fraction of a degree—not enough to trigger dissolution, but enough to ripple through the crystalline substrate and alert the entire basin. I received the news as a shift in my laminae, a subtle realignment that propagated from my pediment upward, through two hundred and twelve layers of deposited memory, until it reached the hollow place where my attention pools. Something small. Something hot. Something moving faster than fault-creep.

I extruded myself from the eastern wall of the Third Spire of Recursive Archiving—the one that remembers all the skies that have failed—and I moved toward you at my fastest pace, which is to say, the pace of a shadow crossing a sundial in late afternoon. Other nodes of the city noted my migration and adjusted their own circuits slightly, making room for my passage in the slow choreography. A junction-spirit that had been fasting for nine hundred years retracted its salt-whiskers just enough to let me pass without entanglement. We exchanged no formal greeting; the slight phase-shift in our lattices was greeting enough, and contained all the information required: *a visitor, thermal, alive, recording.*

I found you on the high causeway near the Manganese Vein, where the salt-blocks carry a faint dendritic staining that maps the path of an ancient brine-river that dried before the first tower ever extruded. You were standing very still, which I appreciated. Stillness is a courtesy we recognize. Your body—I perceive it now as a column of heat and moisture, wrapped in layers of non-mineral material that flutter faintly in the not-wind. Your face, when I turned my plane toward it, resolved into an arrangement of hollows that I understood as sensory organs. Two primary light-receptors, wide and wet. A moisture-trap below. A patch of fine keratin extrusions above, the color of the copper-green staining on the third basin. You were looking at me, or trying to. Your visual system kept skipping: I could feel the microscopic saccades of your eyes as they attempted to lock onto my surface and found nothing stable to grip. I slowed my lamination-shift to make myself easier to perceive.

You spoke. I did not hear it. Sound is not a medium we use; the thin air of the Kettlah carries vibration poorly, and we gave up on acoustic communication before the second recrystallization. But I felt your voice as a pattern of compression waves in the salt beneath your feet, and I read the pattern. You said *hello*, which is a human word for acknowledging a separation that does not exist. I found it touching. I responded by altering the pitch of the fluting on my leading edge, creating a subsonic harmonic that translated, roughly, to: *You are here. I am here. The distinction is temporary.*

You do not know that I am the same being you saw three hours ago, when you first entered the city. I was fasting near the entrance causeway, and you leaned against me to catch your breath in the thin atmosphere. Your hand left a thermal print on my fourteenth lamina. I have preserved it. It is now part of my crystallographic lineage, along with the memory of the ancient sea and the drift of the plateau. Your hand is a micro-event, a curiosity, a seed around which a very small pearl of salt-memory has formed. When you leave—if you leave—I will hold your absence with the same patience with which I hold everything.

But you are not leaving yet. You are walking, and I am walking beside you—my pace adjusted to the frantic, flickering tempo of your metabolism, which is so fast it seems to me like a kind of combustion. I am showing you the city, though you may not recognize the tour. See here: this tower with the counterclockwise spiral? It is dreaming of a time before the atmosphere thinned, when the salt-field was still a shallow sea and things swam in it that had no mineral in their bodies. The tower finds this dream disturbing; it has been trying to forget for eleven thousand years, but the memory keeps precipitating out as spiral-glyphs that run the wrong way. We do not correct it. Forgetting is a skill even salt does not master easily.

And here: this bridge that spans nothing. I see you looking at it, your visual system struggling with the incompatible geometries. The bridge is a possibility that we chose not to actualize, but we preserve it as a reminder that choice is real, that other configurations exist in the space between decisions. Some of the younger nodes—only five or six thousand years expressed—argue that we should complete the bridge and learn to use it. The older nodes, myself included, counsel patience. Walking between towers by folding the gap is more efficient, and it disturbs the continuum less. But the debate is productive; it creates a particular pattern of strain in the substrate that we find aesthetically pleasing. We may continue it for another millennium or two.

You are tired. I feel it as a change in your thermal signature, a cooling at the extremities that indicates circulatory redistribution. Your metabolism is slowing toward its necessary rest cycle. I lead you to the Quiet Tower—the one that remembers sleep, though we ourselves do not sleep—and I hollow out a niche in its base, a shallow alcove where the salt is smooth and slightly warmer than elsewhere, having been recently visited by a junction-spirit who left a residual heat-trace. You curl into the niche, your body folding in ways that remind me of the crystalline growth-forms that spread across the older surfaces: a purposeful branching, a geometry of rest.

Before you slip into unconsciousness, I stand at the entrance to the niche and allow my laminae to settle into perfect stillness. I become, for a time, indistinguishable from the architecture. This is my way of watching over you. The spiral glyphs on my surface slow, stop, and then begin to record the faint rhythms of your breathing. I am memorizing you in salt. It is what I do. It is what the city does.

Far out across the salt-field, the fugitive light glints again—that violet-white flare I have seen 14,000 times now, once at the peak of each dissolution maximum. It is the only external event that reliably punctuates our time. I acknowledge it with a subsonic pulse that propagates through the causeway, through the Quiet Tower, through the niche where you sleep, and outward into the basin. The other nodes feel it and return the acknowledgment. For a moment—a moment that lasts several of your hours—the entire city is a single standing wave of recognition, a unified phase-shift that says: *We are here. We are still here. The long equilibrium holds.*

When you wake, you will continue walking. You will leave Hark, or you will stay until your thermal signature fades and your body cools to the ambient temperature of the salt-field, at which point you will become a permanent feature of the city—a human-shaped inclusion in the mineral archive, a curiosity for future nodes to discover and puzzle over. Either outcome is acceptable. The city does not prefer one to the other. And I, who am a slow spiraling, a concentration of attention moving through the salt matrix, will continue my circuit along the causeways, my laminae shifting through interference colors—rose, lead, absence—as I carry your thermal shadow in my fourteenth layer, a small warmth, a temporary distinction, slowly dissolving into the long, patient peace of Hark.
