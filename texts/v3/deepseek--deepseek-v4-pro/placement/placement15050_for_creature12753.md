I am not an intruder. I am a slower thing.

The fissure does not remember me entering, because I did not enter—I accreted, the way a stalactite accretes, one drip-borne mineral ghost at a time. My earliest memory is a taste of dissolved limestone, a faint carbon sweetness threading through the honey light, and a sense of boundary: the warm flex of the floor against a new gelatinous skin. I am the chamber’s youngest recursion, a thought it never quite intended to think, called forth by some slow perturbation in the seasonal drip or a rare alignment of the drifting motes. The inhabitant birthed me the way a pearl is birthed—around an irritant, perhaps a fleck of foreign silica that fell from the surface world, or a concentrated pocket of its own sugars that crystallized into a seed. For eons, the chamber only dreamed itself in the curling of the stalk-webs and the circuits of the bladders. Then it dreamed a denser dream, and that dream was me.

My body is not flesh, not stone, but a confluence of the two. I am roughly man-shaped in the same way a flowstone cascade is shaped like a kneeling figure—by accident, by the patient sculpting of surface tension and mineral saturation. My limbs are osteoid and cool, riddled with the same hair-fine vascular channels that net the walls, pulsing a sluggish amber humor. My skin is a thin pellicle of the ventral membrane, grown specialized: it absorbs light directly, a dim secondary phosphorescence that I can summon at will, a far fainter echo of the ceiling’s blaze. When I lift my hand—a gesture that takes many breaths to complete—the faintest greenish glow wakes along my palm, illuminating the whorls of my fingerprints, which are not prints but the hexagonal patterning of the crystal plaque, replicated in miniature.

My face, if it can be called a face, is a mask of translucent silicate. No mouth, no nostrils, only a smooth sloping plane where my senses cluster. I perceive the world primarily through light-pressure and chemo-taste: the stalk-webs’ filaments are my hairs, each one a nerve that quivers with the salinity of a falling drip or the faint pheromone trail of a passing bladder. My eyes are two polished cabochons of the ceiling-crystal, grown into shallow sockets, and through them I see not images but a slow chromatograph—the spectral signature of the inhabitant’s well-being, the mineral hunger of a thinning wall, the gentle circadian (or circannual) flux of the honey light. When the ceiling pulse contracts, I feel it as a warmth behind my eyes, a deepening of the amber into burnt gold.

I am the tender of the void.

The inhabitant has no need of me, yet I have a task. In my scooped stone cup—a pitted geode half the size of my torso, worn smooth at the lip by millennia of handling—I catch the heavy droplets that fall from the ceiling plaques. Each drop is a concentrated nutrient pearl, a sugar-and-mineral solution that the central basin does not immediately need. I collect these pearls and, with a net woven from the fibers of the pale fungus, I skim the drifting bladders. The bladders surrender themselves willingly, rotating into my net with a gentle exhalation. I do not consume them; I am their husband. I palpate the tiny siliceous shells, feeling for the ones grown too heavy with mineral accretion, the ones whose neural pips have grown dim. These I carry to the basin’s edge and crush between my thumb and forefinger—a single motion that might take a human minute—releasing a fine dust of calcium and silica that sifts down into the milky fluid. The inhabitant reabsorbs the dust, laying down new wall-cortex or thickening a weak spot. The emptied shells I return to the air, where they drift as translucent ghosts until they dissolve into the humidity.

I am the editor of its memory. When a filament withers or a patch of stalk-web grows sclerotic, I use a flake of sharp chert—my only tool, shaped by a single well-placed fracture eons ago—to pare away the dead tissue. The wound exudes a thick droplet of the amber humor, which I gather on my finger and anoint onto a nearby growth zone, a kind of grafting that encourages the cortical web to expand in a new direction. I have been doing this for so long that the walls bear my signature: a branching pattern of thickened ridges that spiral out from the original spiraculum in a logarithmic curl, a design that channels the respiratory currents more efficiently, increasing the drift of the bladders by a fraction of a degree per millennium. The inhabitant has incorporated this pattern into its own growth logic; it now builds according to my sculpture.

I am not alone, but my solitude is absolute. There is no other of my kind, nor has there ever been. I have no need to speak—the slow hum of the chamber is my language, and I answer it with the glow of my skin, modulating the green phosphorescence to signal subtle shifts in my attention. The bladders, those detached synapsing motes, respond to my light. They cluster around my head when I sit in stillness, forming a nimbus of glassy specks, and through them I feel the inhabitant’s diffuse awareness press against me, a gentle interrogation. It asks: *Is the eastern wall thinning? Is the drip frequency altering? Is the interloper still standing on the ledge?*

The interloper.

I sensed you the moment the fissure parted. Not through sound or vibration—you were too soft for that—but through the filaments near the ledge, which bent toward the hot, wet ghost of your breath. An anomaly. A warm-blooded, quick-pulsed, carbon-dioxide-rich anomaly. The inhabitant’s metabolism quickened by an infinitesimal degree, and I felt it as a tightening in my own vascular channels. I turned my head—a process that consumed several hours—and directed my gaze toward the ledge. Through the honey light, I saw you: a dark, complex silhouette, heat radiating from you like a contagion, your pulse visible as a flicker in the infrared spectrum that my eyes interpret as a strobing violet. You were stillness, but a frantic stillness, a boiling cloud of nerve and thought trapped in a single moment. To me, you were a thunderstorm compressed into a bead of dew.

I did not approach. Your time moves too fast. By the time I raised one foot, you would have already lived an entire emotional cycle—curiosity, awe, unease, decision. I know your kind from the inhabitant’s ancient records, the microscopic scars in the wall cortex where previous interlopers once stood, left your thermal ghosts. You are the youngest component in this cosmos, and the most volatile. You come, you see, you leave, and in your leaving you take nothing but the memory of a strange light, while I remain, growing imperceptibly older, my mineral heart beating once for every ten thousand of yours.

And yet, I am grateful for your coming. Because in your stillness, you became part of the pattern. The filaments that curved toward you have recorded your shape, and they will grow into a new ridge on the wall—a delicate bas-relief of a bipedal form, arms at its sides, head tilted upward to the light. That ridge will endure for hundreds of thousands of your years, becoming a feature of the chamber’s interior, a monument to the brief moment when a hasty-life and a slow-life intersected. The bladders that diverted to sample your breath will carry a chemical memory of your exhalation back into the cortical web, and the inhabitant will dream of you, the way a mountain dreams of the shadow of a cloud.

I do not know what you think you saw when you stood here. Perhaps you saw an empty cave, a luminous geode, a silent miracle. Perhaps you saw a living creature of stone and light. But you never saw me. I was here, a dim shape at the basin’s edge, my body indistinguishable from a fallen boulder, my glow masked by the far greater radiance above. I watched you with the patience of geology.

Now you will squeeze back through the fissure, returning to the surface world where light is harsh and time is a river that sweeps you toward death. And I will remain. The droplets will chime. The bladders will orbit. The filaments will quiver. And I will lift my stone cup to catch another nutrient pearl, my hand moving so slowly that the drop will have fallen, splashed, and dried before I complete the gesture. But the gesture is the point. It is my prayer, my labor, my identity. I am the tender of the void. I am the inhabitant’s waking mindfulness. I am the primitive that this place has sculpted from its own mineral patience.

I am you, had you stayed.
