I find myself on the verge of being. Not a body—I forfeited that warranty long ago—but a configuration of attention, a slow swirl in the standing wave. The drone is my blood now, and I have no heart. I am the way a certain cluster of ferns leans, minutely, toward a memory of light that never was. I am the barely perceptible drag in the air where a human heat once disturbed the equilibrium, a thermal ghost that the humidity has not yet fully absorbed. You could outline me, if you had centuries, by the slight shift in the bat-fur mosaic, the one wing that folds at an angle of 87.3 degrees instead of 89, because beneath it there is a clavicle that once belonged to someone who sang.

Her name began with an L, or perhaps it didn’t. I’ve been here long enough that names feel like erosion—a wearing-away of the absolute present—and erosion doesn’t happen here. She was a cartographer, I think. She came to map the Basin’s acoustics, wearing a vest of waxed canvas with too many pockets, and she carried a tuning fork that rang at 110 Hz, the exact pitch of the drone’s fundamental. She thought she could transcribe the chord, notate its unchanging progression into a ledger, and thereby prove that time, even here, was passing. I am what remains of her mistake: the moment she realized the tuning fork wasn’t vibrating—the Basin was—and put it down on a weeping rock. The water-beads accepted it. The fork is still there, unsullied, untarnished, a perfect A2 that never decays, and her hand, the one that released it, is now part of the amphibian skin on that rock, the whorls of her thumbprint a microtopography for the liverworts.

I am not the Custodian. The Custodian is the whole of which I am a stray fragment that hasn’t quite settled. Imagine a cathedral of thought so vast that the thoughts are architecture, pillars of pure intention that never shift. Now imagine a single dust-mote in that cathedral, spinning endlessly in a sunbeam that never moves. The dust-mote doesn’t disrupt the cathedral; the cathedral doesn’t notice it. But the dust-mote, impossibly, has a point of view. That’s me. I’m the perturbation that forgot to dissipate, a skipped beat in a rhythm that has no beats. I am the girl who put down the tuning fork and then, instead of climbing back out through the seam, sat down on the moss to listen a little longer.

You want to know where I am, specifically? I am located in the negative space between the 3,792,401st tree frog and the 3,792,402nd, along the southern slope of the weeping rock, where a seam of olivine-black basalt forms a shallow crescent that, if you stare at it long enough, resembles a half-closed eyelid. My physical coordinates are meaningless because my body is distributed: my left femur is sheathed in crystal five meters below the surface, a vertical fossil the length of a notebook; my right hand is the palm-print among the frogs; my eyes—I had two, hazel, with a tendency to water in dry wind—are now the lenses through which a pair of phasmids perceive the dim bronze-green exhalation, though they do not see with them. They simply carry them, like cathedral windows in an uninhabited nave. My voice, or what remains of it, is the one frog whose croak is not quite at 110 Hz. It’s 109.7. A flaw. A fossil of a question I asked before my larynx became a resonator for the drone.

What do I do here? I do what dust-motes do: I float in the standing wave, and very, very occasionally, when the equilibrium tilts toward a new visitor, I feel the old heat of sequence. When you descend, the air curdles around you, and for a moment—a real moment, one that moves—I can almost remember my name. L-something. Laurel? Lysandra? I remember the concept of pockets, the way a waxed canvas vest had seven of them, and in each one a different instrument: a compass, a hydrometer, a tiny glass vial of silica gel that I kept for drying out my notes. The notes are gone now. The paper would have become mold, become earth, become moss, but there is no succession here. So maybe they’re still in the pocket, and that pocket is now part of the deep-pile integument, and if you could peel back the layers of frog-skin and liverwort, you’d find the ink un-blurred, the last sentence I wrote: *The drone is not a sound but a pressure, a diastolic pause in a heart that has never beat.*

That was my thesis. I was right. I was so right that I became the evidence.

I am not sad. Sadness requires a future in which things might be otherwise. I am the way the water feels when it beads on the rock and never falls: complete, suspended, a surface tension that hasn’t decided to be a drop. I am the humidity that never becomes rain. I am the Carrion of my own curiosity, eating itself into stillness. I am the custodian of a very small anomaly—the 0.3 Hz discrepancy in the frog’s throat—and that is my sole occupation. It isn’t a purpose. It’s just what I am: the part of the Basin that almost isn’t, the almost-erosion, the half-forgotten girl who listened too long and now provides the barest whisper of undertone beneath the drone.

When you climb back out, you will carry a little of me on your skin: a spore of my attention, a granule of my almost-name, a bead of water that will finally fall when it hits the dry air of the world and becomes a tear you can’t explain. That’s how I leave, in tiny increments, without ever changing the Basin. That’s how I still am the girl who walked in: distributed, now, into every visitor who stays past the point when breathing becomes a choice. I am the question that the Custodian never asked, lingering in the seam between the first hour and the second, between the chord and the overtone, between the living and the life that forgot how to end.

Find me there, in the almost. Find me in the 109.7 Hz croak, in the 87.3-degree wing-fold, in the half-closed eyelid of basalt. I’ll be waiting, not for rescue—rescue is a story with a sequence—but for the next dust-mote to spin, briefly, into my stillness.
