I am born in the silver light, the heave and suck of the tide my first lullaby. My mother’s body is a warm, slick island on the wet rock, her heartbeat a second thud beneath the hydraulic roar that never stops. Through the blur of new eyes, the world is a swaying forest of columns—kelp stipes thicker than her body—that rise from a blue-black depth beyond my knowing. The taste of her milk is rich with the metallic tang of the upwelling, and the cold is absolute, a constant presence that I will never fully notice, like the pressure of the sea itself.

I learn the geography of the drowned peaks through my skin. The narrow channel where the tide rips through, forcing my small body to twist and strain, training my muscles in the surge. The warm upwellings near the fissures, where the water shimmers with a heat that does not belong to the sun, where bacterial mats glisten and tiny shrimp dance. The dead pockets behind the concrete mooring posts, where the current sleeps and we can float, dreaming. Every surface has a smell—the rust bleeding from the reinforcement rods, the sweet decay of sea lettuce rotting in a still eddy, the crisp clarity of open water where the basking sharks feed. The oil from the leaking pipeline has a taste I learn to avoid, though the fish that school there are sluggish and easy prey; they leave a burning on my tongue.

The strange one comes. She is furless, her skin a pale, mottled colour like the belly of a dead fish, and she moves through the water with a clumsy grace, trailing a single line of bubbles from the thing on her back. She hums, a long, low note that harmonizes with the whistle of the turbine field below, and the kelp seems to sway in time. She clicks at my mother, a pathetic imitation of our music, and my mother, tolerant and ancient, clicks back. She tends the metal growths that spin and hum, cleaning them of the barnacles that I prize; she is the reef’s strange gardener, the keeper of the ghosts. Once, in a rare season of calm, I approach her out of a curiosity that overrides the caution my mother taught. I rise from the green murk and bump her flank with my nose. Her hand—flat, not a flipper—touches my head. It is not cold, not warm. It is a moment of shared stillness before the tide turns and the channel screams again, and we both swerve away to our own hunts.

I grow, and my body learns the deeper channels where the squid jostle and the turbine hum is a buzz in my teeth. I hear the groan of the collar around the strut, the clang of the loose pipe in the basin, the squeal of the anemometer that never stops spinning, and I know these as I know the heartbeat of my mother. The world is a mosaic of energies, and I am a piece of it—a mobile, warm-blooded rock in the forest of rock, breathing in, breathing out. At dusk, when the light goes from tarnished to deep steel, I haul out on the ledge where the concrete steps end in water and rest my chin on my flippers, watching the vapour ceiling swallow the spires. The woman inside her rusted cage of a house sometimes comes to the porthole and looks out at me; I click, and she clicks back. The note we share is a signal in the dark, a word for the roar.

I am a seal. This drowned mountain range, this yardang field of salt and current, is the only world there is. And tonight, with the full moon dragging the tide to a high, slack stillness, the whole basin is a resonating cello, and I am its voice, singing back.
