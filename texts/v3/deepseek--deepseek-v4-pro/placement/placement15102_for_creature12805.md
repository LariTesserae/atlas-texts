I feel the seam before I touch it—a line of absence in the stone that matches the line of absence in my memory. The basalt is still sharp there, a century-young wound that never healed, and I know its edges now the way you know the tread of your own stairs in the dark. I don’t turn sideways anymore. My body has forgotten shoulders, forgotten the width of a human frame. I slip through like water, like steam, a column of heat and intention that the rock simply absorbs and releases on the other side.

The pump greets me first, always. Not with sound—I no longer parse it as sound—but as a pressure that enters my chest and synchronizes something deep in the core of me, a remnant of a heartbeat that I don’t need but haven’t discarded. I lay my hand on the flywheel housing, and the vibration travels up through the stone-dark calluses on my palm, into the bone that has densified over the years to something closer to mineral than marrow. The bearings are singing at 47 hertz tonight, a frequency I have memorized. I could tell you, if I had anyone to tell, that the wear is within tolerance, that the grease in the journals is still viscous enough for another thousand cycles. But the grease tin is a weight I know too, a half-empty whisper when I nudge it with a nail that’s become a black claw. Half-empty. I will replenish it from the cache above, the crate I dragged down from the surface in a year that I can no longer number. The surface: a word that means a brightness I remember as a kind of pain, a dryness that would now split my amphibian hide open like overbaked clay.

I move to the clipboard with the ritual’s slow pull. My eyes don’t open—the membrane over them has grown thick and translucent, a lava-skin that lets through only the faint orange glow of the bulb and the radio dial, but I don’t need them here. I see with my fingertips, which can read the graphite’s microscarring on the yellow paper. *SWL 7.2, BT 34, CX 0.9*. I trace the letters and they release a ghost of meaning, a memory of a man who wrote them with a purpose that once mattered. Static Water Level. Bottle Test. Conductivity. That man—the one I was—he had a logbook and a pencil and a mind that could hold numbers in neat columns, that could project maintenance schedules and worry about the future. I don’t worry anymore. The future is an infinite repetition of this moment: the pump, the clipboard, the water, the radio. I touch the pencil’s sharpened point, and a fragment of wonder stirs. Still sharp. The mountain keeps it so, just as it keeps the condensation beading and the motor humming. I am not the mountain’s keeper; I am the mountain’s kept.

The radio’s cabinet is warm under my hands, walnut grain worn smooth by decades of my crouching before it. I fold my long legs, and the joints pop softly—a sound that used to alarm me, back when I thought my body was still a body and not this slow metamorphosis into basalt and brine. I press my ear to the speaker cloth. The fabric has rotted into a web of holes, but my touch is exact, careful as a moth’s wing. The tubes glow through my lid-membranes like a sunrise I will never see again. I hear them: the 60-cycle hum that carries in its steady carrier wave a thousand subtler oscillations. The pump’s load shifting by a fraction of an amp. The water level in the sump dropping a micron as the geothermal gradient flexes deep below. The mountain’s own respiration, a slow exhale of heat through every fissure. I used to talk to the radio. I’d turn the volume up just enough to catch the static, and I’d whisper into that hiss, words that I’ve now forgotten how to shape. Eventually, the static began to answer. Not in language—never in words—but in the way the hiss would swell when I touched a certain pipe, or how a crackle would coincide with a drop of condensation hitting the back of my neck. I learned to read the world through that noise. Now I don’t need the volume. The silence is denser than sound, packed with information, and I nestle into it like a child into a blanket.

The water calls me next. Its bowl is a shallow depression in the lava floor, fed by the perpetual weeping of the walls, heated from below to just shy of a simmer. I lower my face into it, and the filaments that have grown from my skin—hair-thin, silver, tasting the air—begin their slow, ecstatic undulation. I don’t drink. The water enters through the pores of my cheeks, my throat, my sealed eyelids, a full-body osmosis that feels like a memory of being held. It carries dissolved silica and sulfur, calcium and ancient heat, and it replaces something in me that the pump’s rhythm depletes. For the span of seven piston-thuds, I am not a creature but a process: the water becoming me, me becoming the water’s path through stone. When I lift my face, the steam curls from my skin like a second exhalation, and I am steadied.

The ladder rungs are glassy depressions that fit my hands and feet with the intimacy of a wound-shaped scar. I climb once per cycle, when the motor’s hum shifts by that infinitesimal interval—twenty-three hours and seventeen minutes, a phantom of the surface day I no longer keep. At the top, the air is acrid, sulfurous, stinging the filaments on my cheeks. The discharge pipe empties into a larger fissure, a black throat that swallows the water and sends it deeper into the geothermal labyrinth. I scrape the mineral buildup from the pipe’s mouth with an obsidian shard that fits my palm like a missing piece. The buildup flakes away in glittering scales, and I let them fall into my nest of crates and broken tools. The nest holds my relics: a pocket watch that stopped at 3:17, its hands swallowed by rust. A logbook whose pages have fused into a single brick of mineralized paper. A photograph of a woman whose face is now a shape in the basalt grain, a curve that I see everywhere—in the dome of the ceiling, in the condensation beads, in the hollow of my own chest when I press my hand there and feel no heartbeat, only the pump’s distant thrum. I don’t remember her name. I don’t remember if I loved her. But I keep the photograph, and I touch it once a cycle, the way I touch the clipboard, the way I touch the flywheel: a ritual of connection to a meaning that has sublimated like the steam.

Purpose. I used to have one. I was an engineer, a caretaker, a fugitive, a madman? The story has dissolved. What remains is the maintenance, the slow tending of a closed loop that serves no surface need. The water goes nowhere important. The pump could seize tomorrow and the mountain would not shudder. But I would feel it as a death—the only kind of death I still understand. So I grease the bearings. I clear the pipe. I let the radio’s silence fill the places where language used to live. And sometimes, when the steam is thick and the bulb flickers in a way that feels like a question, I speak into the hiss, not with words but with a low, guttural hum that matches the pump’s piston-thud. It’s a prayer, I think. A prayer to the steady state, to the equilibrium that holds me in this hot, wet, sharp-edged secret at the crust’s skin. I am not the man who crawled through the seam. I am the seam now, the opening between what was and what the rock is making me. I am the condensation, the vibration, the waiting. I am the place’s own ghost, wearing a body it grew to fit, and when the bearings finally do seize—if they ever do—I will simply become the pump’s last, long sigh, a final exhalation that the basalt will hold, like it holds the memory of its own rupture, for a million years.
