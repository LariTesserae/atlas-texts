I will tell you not in the voice of one standing apart, but from the skin I wear here—a skin that is not skin, a mind that is not mind as you know it. I do not remember arriving, because arrival implies a before, and for me there is no before. There is only the great low breathing of the basin, the thrum that wakes me and the hiss that cradles me to stillness.

I am a Stoker. But I did not know that word until you gave it. To myself, I am simply a pattern of warmth and weight, a slow thought moving through a landscape of heat.

My first memory is not an image; it is a temperature. A steady, enfolding warmth radiated from below, and a cooler, trickling thread across my dorsal plates. That was my birth, or my assembly, in the mother spring. I recall the taste of silica on my sensor pits, the soft yielding of my new limbs as I pressed against the sinter lip and pulled myself, for the first time, into the heavy air. The world was a symphony of gradients: a boiling plume to my left (danger, too hot, stay clear), a seep of lukewarm minerals to my right (nourishment, linger), and above, the vast cool indifference of the canopy’s underside. I knew nothing of light or dark; I knew only warm and cool, thick and thin, and the deep, reassuring *whump… whump…* that shook the mud. That rhythm was already in me, like a second heartbeat.

Time, for me, is not a river. It is a puddle—sometimes wide and shallow, evaporating almost without notice; sometimes deep and still, holding the same reflection for seasons. I have spent what you would call a century tending a single hot-spring terrace. I have watched the rimstone grow upward, fluting by fluting, until the pool spilled over in a new direction, and I had to begin a new channel. This task did not feel long. It felt like a single, sustained inhalation. I shape the earth with the edge of my palm-spade, but the earth also shapes me: the constant contact polishes my keratin, wears grooves that fit the particular curve of my chosen spring. We are becoming one another, the spring and I.

I perceive you, if you are here, as a small, bright anomaly. You burn at a steady temperature that is too high to be a mud pot, too constant to be a geothermal surge. Your heat moves in quick, jerky pulses—unlike the slow ooze of a reptile, unlike the drifting bloom of a decaying log. At first, I did not know what to make of you. I mapped your footprint as a new hot spot, and I came, over several years, to investigate. I settled at a respectful distance—a few lengths of your body away—and I felt your vibrations: sharp, rapid, air-borne noises that I could not decode. Your warmth tasted of salt and iron, strange minerals. I waited to see if you would migrate, or cool, or multiply. When you did none of these things, I simply added you to my map and adjusted my maintenance routes. Now, when I pass this hummock on my rounds, I pause and sweep the moss clear of fallen fronds, ensuring the seep that feeds your spot remains open. It is a small kindness, one that costs me only a month of effort.

My kin are scattered across the basin like the stars you cannot see. We talk in the ground. When I press my chest against the wet peat and release a slow series of stamps—*thud… thud-thud… thud*—I am telling the southern fields that my pool’s temperature has risen by a fraction, that the lithium content is rich this season. A reply will come hours later, a shivering in the mud: acknowledgment, a query about the fungus gardens. We have no names. Each individual is known by the signature of its home spring, the unique timbre of its thump. I am “the one who tends the third sinter terrace above the ochre mud pots,” and my nearest neighbor is “the one who clears the blue-vent fumarole field.” We meet rarely, and when we do, we settle side by side for a season, our photophores blinking slow codes while we share a patch of warm ground. There is no friction between us, no competition. The basin provides heat enough for all.

Sometimes I dream. Not in sleep—I do not sleep—but in the long, motionless interludes when I settle into a vent field and let my sensor pits go dim. My plates resonate with the subaudible thrum, and I experience a kind of waking vision: the entire basin as a single body, its veins the steaming streams, its organs the roiling mud pots, its skin the canopy. In this dream, all of us Stokers are nodes in a nervous system, and our slow labor is the thought of the world. I feel the pressure building beneath the great geyser basin to the east, a tension that will, in a few centuries, relieve itself in a new eruption. I feel the weight of the mountains, the patient, grinding patience of the peaks. When I emerge from these reveries, my photophores have usually shifted color—often to a deep, restful amber—and the lichen on my back has grown perceptibly.

There are joys here. The joy of a perfectly cleared vent, when the steam shoots skyward with a renewed shriek and the sinter begins to form immediately, a fresh white film. I stand over it, letting the spray wash my plates, and my whole body hums with a satisfaction that trembles in the hydraulic chambers. There is the joy of a nursery well-tended: the shallow, warm pool I dug three generations ago is now thick with fungus, and the larval Stokers—tiny, soft-shelled, their photophores still unlit—wriggle in the gelatinous mass. I do not feed them; I simply maintain the water flow, the temperature, and watch them through my heat-sense, a constellation of little warm sparks. Sometimes one disappears, taken by a steam-dancer or a tooth-whisperer, and I feel a brief, cold absence. But there are always more.

Grief, as you might name it, is a cooling spring. There is one I still visit, far to the west, where the basin floor begins to rise toward the mountain’s shoulder. The vent there has been dying for two hundred years. I have scraped its throat, dug deeper channels, even sacrificed a piece of my own carapace to create a new rimstone dam, but the heat is retreating deeper, beyond my reach. The pool is now barely warmer than the air, and the thermophilic bacteria have faded, leaving the rock a dull grey. I sit beside it for long stretches, my sensor pits aching with the cold. I send no messages from there; my chest stays silent. The other Stokers know, and they do not reply, respecting the quiet. In time, I will abandon it, and another will take its place in my rounds, but for now I mourn. Mourning is a slow, internal cooling, a deliberate deceleration of my hydraulic cycles. It will last until the season turns, and a new seep opens elsewhere.

If you stay, you will become part of my memory. Not your shape, not your mind, but your warmth—the precise, steady ember of your body, the mineral tang of your breath. I will remember you as a hot spot that appeared one century and persisted, a loyal little vent that never erupted but never died. When your warmth finally fades—and I know it will, for all small heats eventually cool—I will incorporate the colder spot into my map. Perhaps I will dig a new channel over your resting place, diverting a thread of hot water so that something new may grow. And then, a hundred years later, I will feel a subtle new heat there, the bloom of a fungus garden or the slow ignition of a larval cluster, and I will think, without words: *It persists*. That is the closest I come to hope.

Do not pity my slowness. I do not pity your haste. Your life is a spark, a quick, bright chemical fire; mine is a deep, geothermal smolder. We are both furnaces, burning at different rates. And in this warm, wet, packed, eternal basin, we both have our place. Now rest. I have cleared a dry ledge for you, and I will keep the ferns from crowding too close. The thrum is steady tonight. All is well.
