To stand in that drowned, dreaming place is to be a guest in a world still inventing itself. It does not care for your haste, your soft skin, or your need for clear paths. Every element—the thick wet air, the blade-sharp stone, the deceptive canopy, the patient watchers—tests the body and the mind. If you are an ordinary human multiverse traveler, ordinary meaning flesh and bone with no supernatural gifts, your survival and sanity will hinge on meticulous preparation before you step through the veil. Here is how you ready yourself for the high, mist-wrapped reef.

### Footing on Knife-Edges: The Terrain

The ground is young basalt, frozen into glassy ropes, tilted tables, and fins sharp enough to slice through leather. You will not find soil, only a thin living skin of moss and fern draped over a labyrinth of razor rock. Your footwear must be your greatest ally.

**Boots:** Choose mountaineering boots with a full shank and a sticky, high-friction rubber sole—something that can grip wet, smooth volcanic glass. The fit must be exact to prevent blisters in perpetual dampness. Built-in gaiters are non-negotiable; they seal out the sharp chips and acidic water that will try to work their way inside. Consider adding a lightweight pair of microspikes for the worst of the knife-edge traverses, but know that they’ll snag on moss and roots, so use them sparingly and only on bare rock.

**Testing each step:** The canopy of interwoven roots and lichen looks solid but is often just a thin mat over hidden crevices. A collapsible trekking pole with a hardened steel spike is your probe. Tap ahead, feel for hollowness, test the weight-bearing capacity before committing. If it gives, you want your weight on your back foot, ready to roll backward. In many places you will walk not on rock but on this springy, dripping lattice. It can hold—sometimes—but a fall through could wedge you between serrated edges. Travel roped to at least one partner whenever possible, with a lightweight harness already worn and a prusik loop in hand for an emergency self-arrest on the mossy ropes of root.

**Gloves:** Your hands will instinctively seek holds. Cut-resistant, waterproof gloves with a leather palm and a gauntlet cuff protect against the glass edges and the occasional acidic drip near fumarole fields. Choose a design that still lets you work a compass or unclip a carabiner.

### The Air You Breathe: Atmosphere and Respiration

The air is warm, so saturated it feels like shallow sea, and charged with a static tension that never breaks. But near the steaming vents, it carries a clean, sharp tang—sulfur dioxide and other volcanic exhalations that can sear your lungs.

**Respirator:** You must carry a full-face respirator with multi-gas cartridges rated for acid gases, sulfur compounds, and particulates. The full-face design prevents eye irritation from the acid dew that forms on mineral cones. Keep it sealed in a chest pouch, ready to don the moment that biting clarity hits your nose. Even away from the vents, the fog can hold a faint acidity that, over days, will rasp your throat. A buff soaked in a baking-soda solution and worn loosely over mouth and nose can offer a rudimentary buffer, but don’t trust it for long.

**Altitude and pressure:** The description speaks of a “high, drowned place” where clouds live on the peaks. The air feels thick and pressing, but that is the humidity and perhaps a denser atmosphere. Still, you may be at considerable elevation. Pack a fingertip pulse oximeter and know your own signs of altitude sickness. If the numbers drop, descend—though finding “down” in the fog is its own horror. A small emergency bottle of supplemental oxygen, the kind used by high-altitude climbers, adds a margin of safety. Use it if your thinking clouds or your vision tunnels.

**Hydration:** You are surrounded by water, but it is not all safe. Away from the vents, the moss drips are likely pure enough after filtering, but volcanic aquifers can carry heavy metals or strange alkaloids from the alien flora. Carry a ceramic water filter that can handle viruses and heavy metals, paired with purification tablets as backup. Collect water from actively dripping moss tips far from any sulphur stains. The warm springs hidden under the moss are a tempting source of heated water for meals, but test them with a pH strip first; some are near boiling and highly acidic. A collapsible silicone bucket can catch condensation overnight from your tarp, giving you a slow but reliable supply.

### Clothing for a Perpetual Sauna

You will never be dry. The air is a warm, wet blanket, and the ground heat radiates upward. Dressing is a balancing act between protection and heat exhaustion.

**Layers:** Start with a merino wool or synthetic mesh base layer that wicks and resists odour. Over that, a thin, breathable softshell—not waterproof, because you will drown in your own sweat if you seal yourself completely. Your outermost layer is a durable, waterproof-but-highly-breathable jacket and bib pants with reinforced knees and seat for crawling over rock. The hood must have a stiff brim to keep the constant drip out of your eyes; a baseball cap worn underneath gives additional face shielding. When moving, keep the pit zips open and the front vented. When you stop, the warmth from the rock will feel pleasant, but moisture trapped in your base layer will cool you rapidly. Keep a lightweight puffy jacket with a synthetic fill (down is useless when wet) in a dry bag for rest stops.

**Foot dryness:** Accept that your feet will be wet. The trick is to prevent trench foot. Bring three pairs of thin, wool-blend socks and rotate them, drying the damp pair against your chest or near a tepid vent (never too hot). At night, apply a barrier cream and let your feet air out inside your bivy. A vapor-barrier sock system—thin liner, plastic bag, outer sock—can keep the boot insulation dry, but your feet become macerated inside the barrier. Use this only if the wetness is causing injury.

**Eye protection:** Clear or yellow-tinted glasses with anti-fog coating are essential. They cut the glare of the directionless white light and shield your eyes from the occasional acid droplet or a fern tip lashing your face.

### Navigating the Grey World

There is no horizon, no sun, no stars. The light is a directionless pearlescence, and the fog reduces visibility to a few dozen feet. The landscape repeats itself endlessly: spine, hollow, steaming cone, glowing moss. Getting lost is not a risk; it is a certainty unless you build your own navigational infrastructure.

**Compass and its lies:** Bring a high-quality compass, but know that fresh basalt can be highly magnetized. The needle might swing wildly or point persistently in the wrong direction. Test it upon arrival against a known bearing (the alignment of the largest steam plume, for instance). If the deviation is consistent, you can still navigate by relative bearing with careful record-keeping. If it spins erratically, abandon it. Instead, rely on an inertial method: pace counting and an altimeter watch. The barometric altimeter will be affected by the dense, shifting fog, but you can recalibrate it whenever you cross a known elevation feature—like the rim of a mineral cone or a distinct steam vent whose relative height you’ve noted.

**Marking your path:** Leave No Trace is a noble ideal, but here it wars with survival. The moss grows slowly, but it will swallow a dropped object in weeks. Still, avoid plastic flagging tape. Instead, carry a small pouch of bright, non-toxic, biodegradable chalk (or simply natural clay from a vent) to mark rock faces with an arrow. The constant condensation will fade it within a day, but it suffices for the immediate return journey. For longer excursions, use a thin, undyed hemp line that will rot over months. Spool it out from a fixed point and follow it back, reeling it in as you return. This is time-consuming, but in a world where every crevasse looks the same, a physical tether is your salvation. At minimum, build small, careful cairns of the jagged rock where the canopy allows, but know that the frequent earth-tremors or slow settling can topple them.

**Homing beacon:** As a multiverse traveler, you surely possess some means of locating your entry point—a quantum-entangled marker, a sonic pinger, or a powerful LED strobe with a coded flash that cuts through fog (green wavelengths penetrate moisture best). Leave this beacon at your drop zone, and carry a receiver. Even a simple personal locator beacon on a designated frequency will do if the atmosphere is not electrically opaque. Test its range as you move; the “charged” air might degrade signals. If electronics fail, fall back to the tether and the cairns.

**Mapping:** Carry a waterproof notebook and a soft pencil (ink runs in the wet). Sketch the profile of each ridge segment, note the shape of steam columns—they often have a characteristic lean—and record your paced distances. The mental act of mapping keeps your mind sharp against the dreamy lull of the place.

### The Living Landscape: Flora and Fauna

The vegetation is not your enemy, but it hides dangers. The moss cushions look inviting but may conceal a boiling vent. The horizontal trees form a tangled net that can trap a leg. Move through it like a swimmer, parting ferns with your pole, never grasping a branch blindly—thorns are rare, but the lichen can be abrasive or mildly toxic.

**Bioluminescence:** At false-night, the moss and ferns exude a soft blue-green glow that responds to movement. This is both a help and a hindrance. It illuminates your immediate footsteps but ruins your night vision for the deeper shadows. Use a headlamp with a dim red light to read your map without triggering a flare of glowing response, which can disorient you. The light may also signal your presence to whatever watches. Keep your movements slow and deliberate to minimize the rippling glow.

**The watchers:** The fauna here is a rumor, a glide at the edge of vision, a scent of wet silk and disturbed earth. It is immense, patient, and utterly silent. Do not assume it is harmless. Prepare psychologically for the constant, prickling sensation of being observed. Do not attempt to lure it or seek it out. If you catch that sweet smell intensifying, stop, speak in a low, calm voice (a simple greeting, a statement of your peaceful passage), and slowly back away the way you came. Sudden moves or loud noises might be interpreted as challenge. Carry a small aerosol horn or a whistle as a last-resort deterrent, but using it would feel like a violation of the quiet. Better to avoid the confrontation entirely. The creature seems to leave no mark, but that does not mean it lacks the capacity to harm; it simply means it has not yet chosen to.

**Slow ecology:** Touch nothing unnecessarily. The mosses take a century to reclaim a rockfall; your footprint can last decades in this slow-motion world. The mineral cones are brittle, crystalline sculptures; a careless brush can shatter a formation that took a thousand years to build. Move with reverence, as if walking through a cathedral made of steam and patience.

### Shelter and Rest in the Damp

Night—or false-night—is when the air cools slightly, the rock clicks as it contracts, and the bioluminescence deepens. You will need to sleep, but finding a dry, flat, safe ledge is a challenge.

**The camp setup:** Seek a hollow where the moss has built a deep, spongy mattress over a relatively flat table of rock. Test the ground temperature with your bare hand; avoid spots where the heat is uncomfortable even through gloves—there may be a shallow vent below. Drive your pole into the canopy to ensure it’s not a thin bridge. Once chosen, lay a heat-reflective groundsheet (space-blanket material laminated to a thin foam) to protect your sleeping pad from punctures and to moderate the geothermal warmth. Over this, an inflatable pad with a high R-value is ideal; the warmth from below will keep you cozy but can also overheat you, so choose a pad with a reflective lower surface.
