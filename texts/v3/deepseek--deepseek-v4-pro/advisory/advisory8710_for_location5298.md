You are an ordinary human multiverse traveler—no augments, no native magic, just the soft machine of your body and the ingenuity in your pack. The place you intend to visit is not hostile in the way a vacuum or a frozen moon is hostile. It will not burn you with radiation or crush you with pressure. But it is a newborn wound in the Earth, a pocket of steaming water locked inside fractured stone, and it will test you with edges, heat, noise, and the quiet treachery of things newly broken. Prepare with the care you would bring to a cave dive, a technical canyoneering descent, or a long session in a volcanic sauna—because it is all three, layered onto an archaeological site still trembling with recent violence.

Below is everything you need to do, bring, expect, and plan for, drawn from the raw evidence of the description and from the hum of intuition that any seasoned traveler learns to trust.

### I. Read the Room Before You Enter: What the Description Tells You

The place is a hybrid: a deliberately constructed stone cistern, now invaded by a hot spring and partially collapsed. The only access is a vertical or near-vertical crevice so tight your shoulders will scrape, its walls raw and sharp as shattered glass. Inside, a sunlit chamber roaring with steam and falling water, its ancient masonry drowning under crystalline-clear flood. The water is warm, mineral-rich, and actively depositing travertine; the air is thick, alkaline, loud enough to blank out thought. The geology is unstable—loose ceiling blocks, fresh fracture surfaces, soft carbonate terraces that can crumble under a finger’s pressure. Life is almost entirely absent, which means no biting insects, but also no easy food, no biological warning systems, and a sterile pool that may still hold unrecognized causticity. You are walking into a place that is still inventing itself, and it does not care about your comfort.

### II. Gear List: What to Pack

Carry everything for a wet, hot, sharp, confined environment. Assume you will be utterly alone, out of communication range, and potentially injured. Redundancy in light, water, and first aid is non-negotiable.

**Core Clothing & Skin Protection:**
- **Full-body wetsuit (2-3mm):** The water is warm, but you need protection from abrasion, not insulation from cold. A wetsuit will armor your elbows, knees, and torso against the blade-like crevice walls and submerged sharp debris. Neoprene also dries quickly and handles humidity without becoming a sodden weight.
- **Over the wetsuit: durable canyoneering or caving suit**—lightweight nylon or Cordura with reinforced padded knees and seat. This sacrificial layer will take the rips instead of your skin.
- **Neoprene boots with thick, cleated soles:** High-tops to cover your ankles, stiff enough to edge on rock but with soft rubber for grip on slick algae and wet limestone. The floor is littered with fresh chert splinters that can slice standard watershoes open; choose a model with a hard toe-cap if possible.
- **Cut-resistant gloves:** Full-fingered, high-dexterity (level 5 cut resistance ideal), with textured grip. You’ll be dragging your palms over razors; regular fingerless climbing gloves will fail.
- **Kneepads and elbow pads:** Soft or hard, integrated into your suit or strapped on, because the crevice squeeze will grind your joints against fresh fracture surfaces.
- **Helmet:** Mountaineering or caving helmet with a chinstrap, strong enough to handle a falling cobble. This is non-negotiable—the ceiling is compromised, and the sound alone can dislodge fragments.
- **Eye protection:** Wraparound safety glasses or low-profile swim goggles rated for both impact and UV. The sun shaft is blinding, steam swirls unpredictably, and alkaline droplets will sting. Carry an anti-fog treatment or two.
- **Hearing protection:** The roar inside is continuous and percussive. You will not be able to hear your own thoughts. Use filtered earplugs (musician’s plugs) that reduce decibels evenly while still allowing awareness of shifting rock sounds. Ordinary foam plugs muffle too much; you need to hear a potential collapse or a change in the water’s rhythm but at a safe volume.

**Descent and Movement Equipment:**
- **Static climbing rope (9-10mm, 60m):** Enough to double as a rappel line from a surface anchor and for potential hauling. The crevice’s depth is unknown, but the walls are raw—chances are you’ll need a full-length rappel.
- **Harness:** A padded sit harness with gear loops, plus a chest harness if the squeeze is too tight for a pack on your back (you’ll drag a haul bag instead).
- **Descender/rappel device** with an auto-block function (e.g., Petzl GriGri or similar) to allow hands-free pauses while you navigate razor edges.
- **Prusik cords, slings, and locking carabiners:** Extras for self-rescue, ascending, or bypassing a stuck point.
- **Hardware for anchors:** Cams and nuts are nearly useless in freshly shattered rock; the fissure is “dry-laid stone” and sun-baked earth, not solid granite. Instead, rely on a deadman anchor or a large rock-thread anchor at the surface. Carry a picket or a snow/fluke stake if the surface soil allows—or plan to use a vehicle/tree well back from the fissure edge. Investigate the surface thoroughly before committing. If no natural anchor exists, you may need to bolt (if permitted by your ethics and the local multiversal laws), but that requires a hammer drill and bolts. Given the description, surface stone is likely fractured; test with extreme skepticism.
- **Two 15-meter lengths of 1-inch tubular webbing:** For slinging large boulders or creating equalized anchors away from the friable lip.
- **Ascenders (handled) and a foot loop:** Essential if you must climb back out unassisted. A fall in the crevice is a fall into a blade-box; self-rescue up the rope is your only viable exit.
- **A small, compact haul bag (40-60 liters, waterproof):** Your backpack will not fit through a shoulder-width crack on your back. Plan to remove it, clip it to your harness loop, and tow it behind you, or lower it ahead. The haul bag must be durable and drag-friendly; expect to beat it against rock.

**Water and Sustenance:**
- **Drinking water: 4-6 liters minimum in a hydration bladder plus reserve bottles.** You are entering a sauna; you will sweat heavily and lose salt through your skin in the steaming air. The pool water is alkaline and mineral-laden—do not drink it under any circumstances. Even filtration won’t remove dissolved solids; desalination tablets might, but the metallic tang suggests heavy metals. Carry all your own potable water.
- **Electrolyte supplements:** Tablets or powder to add to your water. Heat exhaustion looms.
- **High-energy, non-perishable food:** Bars, gels, dried fruit. You will likely spend hours inside; the chamber invites lengthy observation. Eat before entering and on the surface afterward; eating inside means removing earplugs/gloves, which is awkward and exposes you to the alkaline fog.

**Lighting:**
- **Primary headlamp, fully waterproof (IPX8), with spare batteries.** The sun shaft provides light, but clouds shift, and the descent crevice is dark. If dusk falls, the chamber becomes pitch black instantly.
- **Secondary handheld dive light** with a narrow beam to penetrate the milky water and illuminate submerged steps.
- **Chemlights or small waterproof marker lights** to deploy as breadcrumbs—secure them to rock protrusions in the crevice, not on the floor where water could carry them away.

**Navigation & Communication:**
- **Wrist compass** (analog, fluid-filled)—simple and non-electronic.
- **Whistle:** With integrated high-decibel pealess design; the roar of the spring will drown out shouts, but a sharp whistle may cut through short distances.
- **No electronic communication will work underground.** Leave a detailed trip plan with a reliable contact, including your expected return time, and carry a personal locator beacon on the surface in a waterproof pouch. Activate it only if you’re in serious trouble above ground; underground it’s useless.
- **Waterproof notebook and pencil:** For recording observations, timing, or leaving a note in case of mishap.

**First Aid & Emergency:**
- **Comprehensive first-aid kit in a roll-top dry bag:** Focus on wound care—large adhesive dressings, butterfly closures, irrigation syringe (to flush out rock dust and mineral water), antiseptic wipes, medical tape, pressure bandage. The sharp stone will produce lacerations with rock flour embedded; infection risk is high even in sterile water, because the water may harbor thermophilic bacteria not recognized by your immune system.
- **Burn gel and non-adherent dressings:** If you slip near the hot fissure, you could suffer a scald from turbulent water above 50°C.
- **Blister care:** The moist, warm environment and abrasive footwear will soften skin; bring moleskin and liquid bandage.
- **Heat illness management:** Instant cold packs, oral rehydration salts.
- **Emergency shelter:** A lightweight bivvy sack or space blanket, in case you become trapped or need to wait out instability. You won’t sleep, but you can retain body heat if the chamber cools at night or if you’re injured and waiting.
- **Spare glasses/contact lenses and supplies:** Steam and splashes will foul your vision constantly.
- **Multitool with pliers and a locking knife:** For gear repair, cutting webbing, or (in desperation) prying a trapped limb. The knife must be corrosion-resistant.

**Monitoring Instruments:**
- **Thermometer (waterproof, digital):** Check water and air temperatures. If water exceeds 42°C (108°F) in most of the pool, wading immersion time should be limited to avoid heat stress. The inlet may be far hotter.
- **pH test strips or a small electronic meter:** Test the water before immersion; a pH above 9 or below 5 could cause skin burns over time.
- **Air quality monitor (portable, O2/CO2/H2S):** Volcanic areas can accumulate carbon dioxide or hydrogen sulfide. The description notes no sulfurous smell, but silent CO2 pooling in depressions could be lethal. The sun shaft likely creates a chimney, but a monitor is cheap insurance. If you feel dizzy, short of breath, or your lips tingle, exit immediately.

**Archaeological & Documentation Tools (Optional):**
- **Waterproof camera or action cam with a suction mount or GorillaPod:** The visuals are extraordinary, but lenses will fog constantly. Use anti-fog inserts and a lens wipe tethered to your wrist.
- **Flexible ruler or scale card for context.**
- **No sampling tools:** The travertine is soft and pristine; do not carve, chisel, or collect. As a multiverse visitor, your ethical baseline is “observe, do not alter.” Leave the chamber exactly as you found it—wet, roaring, and innocent of human touch since the builders.

### III. Physical & Mental Preparation

**Training:**
- **Canyoneering or caving experience:** You need to be proficient at rappelling, ascending a rope, and negotiating tight, sharp squeezes without panic. Practice in dry slots canyons with similar shoulder-width constraints.
- **Heat acclimatization:** Spend time in dry saunas and steam rooms wearing your base gear to understand your body’s limits. Learn to recognize the onset of heat exhaustion—clammy skin, nausea, confusion—while in a stimulant-free state.
- **Psychological readiness for tight spaces and noise:** The fissure will press on your chest and back; the roar will block all external sound except the thump of your own heart. If you are claustrophobic or noise-sensitive, train with earplugs in a sensory-deprivation context, or consider bringing a companion (risking a larger footprint and double the rockfall probability, but increasing rescue options).

**Medical Check:**
- Ensure all cuts and abrasions are completely sealed before you go. The sterile pool may still harbor extremophile microorganisms; any open wound is an invitation.
- If you are prone to ear infections, wear silicone ear plugs designed for divers to keep mineralised water out of your ears.

### IV. Surface Arrival & Anchor Setup

You will likely emerge onto sun-baked earth, a landscape of parched ground and scattered rock. Find the crevice: it may be a barely visible crack from above, exhaling steam or offering only the faintest tremor of its internal roar. Approach cautiously—the lip is unstable, undermined perhaps. Probe the ground with a trekking pole before every step.

**Anchor Construction:**
- Locate solid, load-bearing points at least 5 meters back from the edge—large embedded boulders, a mature tree, or a deadman anchor system dug into consolidated soil. Back these up with redundancy; a single anchor point is a death sentence when the rock is freshly fractured.
- Pad the lip of the crevice with webbing and a rope protector to prevent chafing on the sharp edge. Even a 10-second rappel sawing across that raw stone will sever a tensioned rope.
- Test the anchor with body weight and sledgehammer blows before committing.

**Pre-Descent Check:**
- Don all gear, test lights, seal electronics in waterproof pouches. Drink half a liter of electrolyte water. Remove all dangling items (watch bands, lanyards) that might snag.
- Confirm exit plan: ascending device ready on rope, prusik loops accessible.
- Inform your surface contact (via radio, if they are nearby) that you are entering and expected to be out in X hours. If out of contact range, leave a timed note in your vehicle or at a visible landmark.

### V. The Descent Through the Fissure

This is your highest-risk segment. The rock is unweathered, meaning no rounded edges—every hold is a guillotine. The crack narrows as you go; you may have to remove your helmet temporarily, but only if the overhead threat is absent, which it never is. Keep the helmet on.

- **Rappel using a brake-assisted device**, feeding slowly. Face the wall, or face outward if the width forces a sideways orientation; use your feet to push against one wall and your upper back against the other, chimney-style, but never grind bare skin on the stone. The wetsuit and pads will save you.
- **Maintain constant body tension** to avoid pendulum swings if a foothold breaks.
- **Keep the haul bag clipped to your harness on a short tether** so it hangs below you, not above where it could jam. Alternatively, lower it separately first once you assess the landing zone is clear.
- **Listen:** even with earplugs, you’ll feel the vibration of the roaring spring increasing. The noise will mount as you descend, until it’s a physical pressure on your diaphragm. That’s normal; breathe deeply and steadily. The tunnel is not collapsing; it’s just the acoustics.

Once you feel the fissure widen and see the light change from rock-black to golden mist, you’re approaching the opening. Expect a final overhang or lip; the description says “a shaft of sun falls through a ragged hole high above” and you “slide down the last rock” to meet the topmost submerged step. So the exit from the crevice is likely above the water, perhaps requiring a short downclimb or a careful drop onto the steps. Avoid jumping; the steps are slick and the water’s clarity will deceive your depth perception. Lower yourself gently, testing each footing.

### VI. Inside the Chamber: Movement and Etiquette

Once standing on the submerged stone treads, you are inside a constructed basin. The water is warm, the air a collage of steam and light. Your immediate impressions will overwhelm, but discipline is essential.

**Movement:**
- **Walk slowly and deliberately.** The paved floor is uneven; slabs may tilt under your weight. Use a trekking pole or handheld probe to test the substrate ahead before shifting your weight.
- **The algae bench is a skating rink.** Do not step on the green-slicked areas unless absolutely necessary; if you must, tread with flat feet and micro-steps.
- **Avoid the near-inlet area entirely.** The hot fissure opposite the light shaft is where roiling, milky water enters with force. The temperature gradient there could be sharp, and the travertine terraces around it are “soft, spongy… unconsolidated.” You can watch them thicken from a distance, but do not touch or stand on them; they will crumble, destroying a forming geological record and potentially scalding you.
- **The water magnifies everything**, so a step that appears knee-deep may be up to your chest. Use a pole or paddle to gauge depth before wading further.
- **Currents:** The pool has a slow, muscular swirl. In open water, you’ll feel a gentle tug toward the inlet and a return flow along the edges. This is manageable but can cause disorientation if you close your eyes or focus on the ceiling. Keep a fixed point on the architecture as a reference.

**Environmental Awareness:**
- **Monitor the ceiling continuously.** Look up every few minutes for fresh cracks, falling dust, or shifting shadows. The hole’s edge is “fringed with tiny droplets that catch the sun”—beautiful, but it also indicates active condensation and potential weakening. If you hear a sharp crack above the roar, flatten against the downstream wall, away from the direct fall line of the dome.
- **Steam and oxygen:** The air thick with steam can lower perceived oxygen. If your head throbs or you feel drowsy, move into the direct sunbeam where a chimneylike airflow is strongest. If symptoms persist, ascend immediately.
- **Thermal load:** The water wraps you in constant warmth. Even without exertion, your core temperature can rise. Soak for 10-15 minutes at a time, then sit on the dry lip of a step (the beetle-husk bench, perhaps) with your torso in the cooler air. The chamber’s atmosphere away from the water is still hot, but the lower humidity may help.
- **Noise management:** Keep your earplugs in at all times. If you need to communicate (e.g., with a travel companion), use hand signals or a waterproof writing slate.

### VII. Health Hazards & Mitigation

1. **Lacerations from sharp rock** — The most common injury. Irrigate immediately with sterile saline from your kit, not the pool water. Dress and seal the wound. Rock flakes may carry tetanus; ensure vaccination is current.
2. **Scalding** — If you inadvertently step into a hot plume, move to cooler water immediately, then cool the burn with the air (not more hot water). Apply burn gel.
3. **Chemical irritation** — The alkaline water may cause skin “slipperiness” after prolonged immersion; that’s saponification of your skin oils. Rinse with fresh water from your drinking supply, if you can spare a little, on an extremity. Do not use the pool water to wash your face; use a clean cloth with your own water.
4. **Ear/Mucous membrane infection** — After exiting, flush ears with a diluted vinegar/alcohol solution (carry it) to prevent “hot-tub folliculitis” or rarer geothermal infections.
5. **Panic response** — The combination of squeezing darkness, roaring sound, steam, and isolation can trigger an adrenaline spike that leads to hyperventilation or reckless movement. Have a grounding routine: pause, hold onto a solid architectural feature (a carved bench edge, not a fracture), look at the sunbeam, and breathe in a 4-4-4 count.
6. **Rockfall** — If you hear a sound like a gunshot through the bass roar, or see sparks of falling pebbles, get under the heaviest intact section of masonry near the curved wall. The north wall with the cyanobacteria tideline appears to be the most stable, as it has not been breached by new fissures. The area directly below the dome hole is the most exposed.

### VIII. Emergency Procedures

**Injury or Immobilization:**
- If you suffer a debilitating injury but can still use your arms, initiate self-rescue up the rope using ascenders. If unable to climb, fire the whistle pattern (three blasts) and wait; but assume no one will hear it over the roar. Your best chance is to make your way to the topmost dry step, bivvy down, and attempt a lucid, self-splinting approach. Leave your PLB on the surface? That’s an oversight: bring a second PLB in a sealed container that you carry inside and could activate near the sun shaft by lifting it toward the open sky. Program it for a signal that penetrates the hole.

**Ceiling Collapse:**
- The most critical sign is a rapid increase in falling debris. Move into the water and hug the downstream edge, floating low. The water may absorb some impact, and the overpressure from a ceiling slab hitting the pool could create a surge that pushes you into a corner. Protect your head and neck with your arms; the helmet helps.

**Flash Flood?**
- The description mentions a “river above” and a channel that once controlled flow. Heavy rain on the surface could flood the chamber in minutes via the crevice or the chimney hole. Monitor the sky through the aperture; if clouds darken or rain begins, exit immediately—the crevice will become a waterfall and the pool could rise fast. The roar will change pitch as new water joins the spring. React at the first sign of weather change.

### IX. Leaving the Chamber & Returning to the Surface

Ascending the crevice is physically harder than descending. Your muscles will be fatigued from heat, and your gear may be heavier with moisture.

- **Before starting, sit on the highest dry step** and drink at least 500ml of electrolyte fluid. Eat a bar. Dry your hands thoroughly and switch to fresh, dry gloves if needed; grip is critical.
- **Attach your ascender system** and begin the long prusik-jumar up the rope. Maintain tension; avoid resting with your weight on the abrading lip. Ascend steadily, using the walls for friction but never as sole support.
- **Haul your bag behind you** on a shorter tether to prevent it swinging and jamming.
- **Once on the surface,** move well away from the edge before collapsing. De-kit slowly, check your body for wounds, and report in to your contact.

### X. Post-Visit Aftercare

- **Rinse all gear** with plenty of clean fresh water as soon as possible. The mineral-rich water will dry into a crust that degrades metal and fabrics.
- **Shower yourself using mild soap**, paying attention to any scrapes. Irrigate ears.
- **Monitor your health** for 48 hours: watch for fever, unusual rashes, or respiratory symptoms. Seek medical attention if any wound shows redness or you develop a cough; geothermal waters can host amoebas or bacteria like *Legionella*.
- **Reflect.** The place you visited is a fleeting miracle of violence and craft. It will change; the travertine will harden, the fissure may widen, the roof may collapse entirely. You saw it in its raw, loud infancy, a room being re-educated by the earth. Write down everything—your notes are likely the only record this newborn pocket will ever have before it seals or destroys itself.

Pack your bags, traveler. The roar is waiting.
