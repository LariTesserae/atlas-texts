To visit this place, the traveler must prepare not for a destination but for a continuous state of negotiation. This is not a location that accommodates visitors; it is a metabolism. Your presence will be tolerated only if you learn to move within its rhythms, and that requires a fundamental shift in how you think about gear, body, and time.

---

### I. The Body as First Equipment

Begin with your own skin. The constant damp here — not rain, but the ambient exhalation of a living basin — will macerate ordinary flesh within days. Feet especially. You must condition your feet for permanent wetness long before arrival. Spend at least three weeks walking barefoot on wet moss, in shallow streams, on rain-soaked logs. Let the soles thicken but not crack. Learn to read the subtle signals of incipient fungal infection — the sweet-sick smell between toes, the itching that is not quite itching — and treat them with crushed antifungal barks you will need to identify yourself. The settlement has no apothecary; each person tends their own skin.

Bring nothing made of cotton. Cotton rots here. It will dissolve off your body in a week, leaving you wrapped in tatters. Instead, source or make garments of beaten barkcloth, ideally mulberry or fig-family fibers, treated with a light wax from the same palm whose pith you will learn to eat. The cloth should breathe, insulate when wet, and resist the steady microbial nibble of the air. A single tunic and loose trousers will suffice, but bring a spare set sealed in a container that does not exist yet — more on this later. Your garment should have no buttons or metal fasteners; use toggles of polished horn or simple knotted loops. Metal will tarnish green and eventually corrode through the fibers.

Your hair will become a habitat. Long hair will mat with moss spores and the fine, sticky webs of a particular spider that builds between sleep platforms. Keep it cropped close to the skull or bound in tight braids coated with a thin paste of clay and ash — the settlement’s people will show you how if you ask, but their patience for questions is measured in work-cycles. Better to arrive already shorn and clay-capped. Beard and body hair are nests for chiggers and the tiny, biting midges that breed in the black pools. Remove them or seal them.

### II. Footwear and the Question of Ground

You will not walk on ground. The root-lattice is not ground; it is a woven, uneven, waterlogged surface that flexes underfoot. Every step compresses a spongy mat that rebounds behind you. Ordinary shoes would slide, trap water, and rot within half a day. Instead, fashion sandals from a single piece of rawhide, minimally stitched with sinew. They should have a thin, flexible sole — no thicker than three millimeters — that allows your foot to feel the give of the roots and grip their wet contours. The strap should wrap only once across the instep and around the ankle. Leave the toes free to probe and brace. Bring two pairs and expect both to need daily oiling with a rendered animal fat the settlement uses for tool-handles. The fat smell will attract certain beetles; ignore them.

Many travelers prefer to go barefoot entirely after the first week, once their soles have adapted and the initial bruising phase has passed. If you choose this, pack the sandals away in your sealed container. They will be needed if you must leave in haste over the razor-edge ridges beyond the bowl.

### III. Tools of the Hand

Carry nothing that depends on dryness or precision mechanics. The moisture here is a slow, patient infiltrator. It will unglue laminates, rust high-carbon steel, swell wood grain until joints seize, and feed fungi that will digest leather, paper, and natural-fiber cordage in weeks. You must build a small, sealed container — the best material is a hollowed, thick-walled gourd lined with beeswax and stoppered with a tight plug of the same wood, sealed further with a gasket of raw rubber tapped from a tree species you will need to locate in the surrounding ridges. Inside this gourd, and only when it is stoppered, you may keep:

- A small flint and firesteel, though you will rarely use it — fire here is peat-smolder, kept perpetually alive in clay-lined hearths. The people will give you an ember-nest if you ask with proper courtesy.
- A tiny vial of concentrated essential oil — clove, tea tree, or a local equivalent — for emergency wound treatment.
- A single steel needle and a length of sinew thread wrapped around it. The needle will be invaluable for repairing barkcloth, but keep it greased and dry.
- A written guide to the local palms, translated into a script you can read. Do not bring a book; books become swollen, unreadable bricks. Instead, write on thin sheets of hammered bark-paper, each sheet oiled and kept separate. Key knowledge to record: which palm pith is edible, which fig sap can seal a wound, which bromeliad water is potable without boiling, which frog’s skin secretion causes temporary numbness and should be avoided, which ant species’ bite carries a venom that will swell a joint for two days.

Everything else you carry must be openly exposed to the air and made of materials that welcome moisture. A small adze of beaten copper or bronze, its handle a freshly cut branch that will swell tight around the tang. A knife of sharpened bone, preferably the rib of some large ungulate, which will never rust and can be re-sharpened on any gritty stone. A digging stick of fire-hardened palm-wood for prying up edible tubers from the root-mat. A net bag, knotted from the inner bark fibers of a particular liana, which you will learn to re-weave as it frays — the settlement’s children can teach you the basic pattern in exchange for a simple story.

### IV. Sleeping and Shelter

You will not bring a tent. Pitching a tent on the root-lattice is impracticable; the ground is too uneven and wet, and the constant drip from above would pool on any taut fabric, stretching and mildewing it within hours. Instead, you must negotiate for a sleeping space within the settlement’s vertical weave. This requires payment in kind: not money — the concept is meaningless — but labor, skill, or a unique material. Before arrival, learn one skill that a small, hyper-dense vertical village would value. Possibilities:

- The ability to sharpen copper tools using a particular water-stone technique. Copper dulls quickly in wet wood; a skilled sharpener is welcomed for a season.
- Knowledge of a new cordage pattern that is stronger under tension. Bring a sample of a braiding technique they haven’t seen, practiced until your fingers remember it, and offer to teach it.
- Carrying a small quantity of a salt-tolerant seed that might grow in the upper canopy where salt spray from distant ocean storms occasionally drifts. The settlement is always seeking novelty that can be woven into the living walls.
- A talent for mimicking animal calls to a degree that can distract the monkeys, or lure the fat, ground-dwelling birds that forage in the root hollows. If you can reliably summon food, you will be fed.

In exchange, you will be given a sleeping platform: a shelf of closely laid palm-trunk slats, perhaps four feet by six, suspended from a branch-hook structure. You will have no walls. Privacy is not a concept here; the density is such that your neighbors’ sleep-murmurs and the rustle of their barkcloth blankets will be as familiar as your own heartbeat. Accept this. A lightweight barkcloth blanket will be provided, or you may make your own from the inner bark fibers you can strip and beat yourself under instruction.

### V. Food and the Gut

The settlement’s diet is monotonous and deeply local. It centers on a starchy palm-pith porridge, supplemented by grubs pried from rotting logs, small fish scooped from the black pools in woven basket-traps, the occasional bird or monkey when necessary, and a fermented drink made from chewed tuber mash. Introduce your gut to new starches slowly. Two weeks before departure, eat only foods that are bland, fibrous, and unspiced. On arrival, accept any food offered, but in tiny portions at first. The settlement’s microflora — the ambient bacteria and yeasts that live on every leaf and root-surface — will colonize your gut within days. You will experience a brief, intense period of digestive upheaval. The people will recognize this and offer you a bitter bark tea that eases the cramping. Drink it without hesitation; it contains a mild antiparasitic that also calms the smooth muscle of the bowel.

Bring no food of your own except a small, sealed packet of pure salt. Salt here is precious, obtained only from the occasional salt-lick stone found high on the ridges. A gift of a palm-sized bag of clean, white salt will open more doors than any tool. Use it sparingly, only for trade or for the deepest fatigue when your sweat has leached every mineral from your body.

### VI. The Mind and the Din

The sound will be the hardest adjustment. There is no silence here. The baseline noise — frogs, birds, insects, monkeys, the drip of water, the creak of living wood, the murmur of voices — is roughly the decibel level of a busy city street near a park. It continues through the night with only a slight shift in register: the frogs’ chorus intensifies, a different bird species calls, the monkeys settle into grumbling sleep-chatter, the wind in the canopy never fully ceases. You will not find quiet. Instead, you must learn to inhabit the noise until your mind treats it as a steady-state carrier wave rather than a series of interruptions. Some travelers find that a simple ear-covering of soft moss packed lightly into the outer ear can filter the highest frequencies while allowing speech to pass — but this is a temporary crutch. The goal is not blocking but absorption.

The constant motion is the second assault. The walkways sway. The platforms tremble when anyone passes. Storms arrive like a thrown switch: one moment the air is still, the next the entire canopy is roaring and lashing. The people do not flinch; they brace, they grip, they sometimes laugh or shout into the wind. If you panic, you will be seen as a child, and you will be treated gently but with a slight condescension until you learn the rhythm. Before arrival, practice spending hours on a small boat or a suspended hammock-bridge in a wind. Learn to feel the motion in your legs not as danger but as a conversation. The walkways speak in creaks and hums. Listen.

### VII. Social Protocol

The settlement is small — a few dozen people — and your arrival will be immediately known to all. There are no outsiders’ quarters; you will be folded into the closest available sleeping platform, which will be adjacent to at least two other platforms within arm’s reach. You must be clean in a way that respects the shared air: wash daily in one of the black pools, using a handful of sandy grit to scrub your skin. But do not use any soap, even the mildest plant-based soap you might think to bring. Soap disrupts the microbial balance that keeps the pools’ water filtered and the root-lattice free of a particular slick algae. The people will teach you how to scrub with a wad of a fibrous, mildly abrasive leaf instead.

Do not touch anyone’s tools without explicit permission. A bone needle is not just a needle; it was sharpened by a grandmother, shaped to a specific hand, and likely has a name. Similarly, do not sit on a root-buttress that bears the marks of a particular family’s hearth-sitting unless invited. The vertical density means that every surface is claimed, not by boundary markers, but by subtle wear-patterns that you will not yet see. Ask where to sit. Ask where to place your feet. The people will find this polite and unexpectedly endearing.

Gift-giving is continuous and small. Always have something in your hand — a particularly straight palm-slat, a handful of edible grubs you dug yourself, a beautifully veined leaf that can be folded into a cup. Offer these things without ceremony when you come upon someone working. Receive what is given to you with the same casual grace, and find a use for it within the day. A gift left unused is a mild insult, as if you rejected the giver’s presence in the weave.

### VIII. What to Carry — The Final Packing List

All of the above distilled into a physical inventory you can assemble in your home dimension before traveling:

1. **A net-bag** of knotted liana bark, large enough to hold all below, with a long strap to wear cross-body.
2. **One suit of barkcloth** (tunic and loose trousers) oiled lightly with palm wax.
3. **Two pairs rawhide sandals** (thin-soled, single strap).
4. **A small, hollow gourd** lined with beeswax, containing: flint and firesteel; a vial of clove oil; one steel needle with sinew thread; folded bark-paper guides to palms, figs, bromeliads, ants, frogs; a palm-sized bag of clean salt (your trade-gift, already sealed inside).
5. **A bone knife** (rib of cattle or buffalo, sharpened, about six inches long).
6. **A copper adze head** (blade only, no handle — you will fit a handle on site) wrapped in a scrap of oiled barkcloth.
7. **A digging stick** of fire-hardened wood, about two feet long, tapered at one end.
8. **A small bundle of sample cordage** in a braiding pattern unknown to the settlement, practiced in your hands.
9. **A second, empty net-bag** for collecting and carrying found materials (grubs, leaves, palm-slat, gifts).
10. **Your own body**, physically prepared: calloused feet, close-cropped hair, a gut accustomed to bland fiber, ears ready for the din, mind braced for the sway.

### IX. Arrival

You will approach through the cloud forest from below, following a stream course that the root-lattice has channelized. You will smell the settlement before you see it — the peat-smoke, the sweet rot of composting leaf-litter, the musky scent of the monkeys, the faint sour note of fermenting mash. Above you, the canopy will thicken. You will hear the first human voice, a child’s high yip, and then a monkey’s answering screech, indistinguishable in tone.

When the walkway first comes into view, a single liana-rope swaying between two buttress roots, stop. Call out. The people know you are there — you have been tracked by sound and scent for the last hour — but protocol requires you to announce yourself. Speak your name, your dimension of origin, and your trade-offering in a clear voice. The matriarch, from somewhere high and invisible, will answer with a single word that means both “enter” and “prove yourself.”

Step onto the walkway. Feel it bounce. Feel the water drip onto your bare shoulder. Listen to the frogs peal and the parrots shriek. The weave begins now. You are no longer a traveler. You are a strand that has entered the loom, and you will be tested until you either hold tension or snap.
