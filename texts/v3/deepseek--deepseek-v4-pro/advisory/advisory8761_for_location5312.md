For a place like Hark, preparation is less about packing and more about shedding. This is not a hostile world—it will not actively try to kill you—but it will unmake you if you arrive with too many assumptions, too much noise, too firm a grip on the reality you left behind. The city does not punish intrusion; it simply fails to accommodate it. To walk the salt causeways and return with your mind intact, you must learn to move at the city’s pace, breathe its thin clarity, and let the deep mineral silence work through you rather than against you.

### Physical Conditioning
The air on the Kettlah salt-rises is thin—not dangerously so for short stays, but enough that an unacclimated traveler will feel a constant, gauzy detachment. Spend at least two weeks at high altitude before attempting the crossing, or carry a discreet supplemental oxygen system woven into a scarf or collar; you will want your hands free and your senses uncluttered. The cool is pervasive but not bitter, a steady mineral chill that leaches heat slowly over hours. Dress in layers that breathe: wool-silk blends, insulating gel-fabrics, nothing that rustles or crunches. Your outermost layer should be the color of the salt itself—muted gray-violet, bruise-toned—both as camouflage and as a gesture of minimal visual disturbance. The inhabitants, if they register you at all, seem to notice color the way we notice a wrong note in a silent room; muted tones are a courtesy.

### Equipment: What to Bring, and What to Leave
Bring almost nothing. Every object you carry is a statement from another world, a dense knot of your own reality that will chafe against Hark’s negotiable physics. A bulky pack, a glowing device, the clink of metal—these are not merely distractions; they can become perceptual anchors that the city’s instabilities will tug at, causing disorientation or temporal static. Instead, carry only what you can wear or conceal in a slim belt pouch:

- **Salt-hourglass chronometer.** Not a digital watch. Not a mechanical pocket watch. A simple twin-bulb hourglass filled with local salt crystals, calibrated to the city’s own tempo. You’ll craft it yourself before entering, using grains gathered from the outskirts and a prayer of patience. It won’t measure hours in any terrestrial sense, but it will keep you synchronized with the slow pulse of the towers’ growth, preventing the worst of the time slips. Glance at it only when you feel your internal clock slipping; it will tell you when to rest, when to move, when to let a moment stretch.
- **A lens of stilled attention.** This is not mystical. It’s a small, circular pane of neutral-density glass, palm-sized, with a thin lead rim. When the bridges start to warp, when your own shadow points two ways, raise the lens and look through it with one eye closed. It won’t make the bridges safe, but it will dampen the visual paradox enough to let your mind accept the input without skipping. It also reveals the true edges of things—where a tower’s fluting ends and a memory begins. Use it sparingly; overuse can teach you to see only through the lens, and then you’ll miss the living strangeness.
- **A mouth-veil of mineral silk.** The air carries suspended salt-crystal dust, fine as powdered thought. It will coat your lungs with a microscopic layer that is not harmful but is perceptibly foreign; after a few hours, you’ll begin to taste the city’s chemistry on your breath. The veil filters nothing—it merely reminds you that you are breathing a shared atmosphere, that each exhalation adds a human watermark to a place whose own breath is measured in centuries. Wear it, and breathe with intention.
- **A palm-stone from your own world.** Unremarkable. A river pebble, a piece of brick, a seed. It’s your anchor when the silence becomes too dense. Hold it and remember weight, warmth, the sound of water. The stone will feel heavier here, almost geological, and that is a comfort.
- **Water and sustenance.** A small flask, enough for a day’s slow walking. No food that rots, no food that smells. Compressed protein wafers, flavorless, the kind that dissolve without texture. You will not feel hunger; the city’s tempo suppresses visceral needs, but your body still requires fuel. Eat mindfully, privately, and never where a salt-being might observe—the sight of organic consumption is profoundly alien to a mineral ecology, a category error made flesh.

Do not bring: recording devices, weapons, communication gear, or anything that emits artificial light or sound. The city is dim and silent; a sudden photon or vibration would register as a violation, and while nothing will retaliate, the atmosphere itself will recoil—you’ll feel it as a confused pressure, a sense of having been registered as noise. If you must take notes, use a graphite stick on a sheet of smooth salt-bark, and let your markings be minimal.

### Mental and Perceptual Preparation
You will be walking into a world where reality holds by a different set of agreements. The bridges are the most obvious symptom, but the deeper challenge is the city’s relationship with time and identity. Meditate on these specific practices before arrival:

- **Perspective-stacking.** Train in environments with ambiguous figures (illusory contours, Necker cubes) until you can hold two mutually exclusive perceptions simultaneously without choosing one. This is not comfortable—your mind will want to flip—but in Hark, you will need to accept that a tower can be behind itself, that a spiral can run both ways. The lens helps, but your own cognitive flexibility is the true survival tool.
- **Stillness as response.** If you feel a time-skip approaching (a sudden sensation of déjà vu reversed, a sense that you are arriving before you left), do not run. Do not shout. Freeze. The city’s temporal folds are like bubbles; they pass. Movement while caught in one can strand you in a pocket of slow-time, and extracting yourself later is a discipline even the inhabitants find tedious. When in doubt, sit. Let the salt beneath you accept your stillness.
- **The long gaze.** Practice looking at something—a wall, a sky, a stone—for an hour without labeling it. Hark’s sky is not a color; it is an absence of color. If you try to name it, you will fail and feel a dull psychic ache. If you simply look, without linguistic framing, you will begin to see the deeper shapes that move in the dark. This is not recommended for prolonged periods, but a measured dose of it will attune you to the city’s true dimensions. The inhabitants perceive without naming; that is why they are so silent.

### Navigational Cautions
- **Do not use the bridges.** The description is clear: the inhabitants do not cross them. The bridges are perhaps not for crossing; they may be artifacts of a different spatial logic, a relic of some earlier phase of the city’s growth, or a perceptual trap for creatures who insist on surfaces. If you need to move between towers, descend to the ground level and follow the causeways; they are the original, constructed streets, and they hold stable. If a bridge tempts you with its impossible grace, remember: the space between towers is not empty, it is occupied by a different kind of here. Cross it on foot only, and only on the salt-blocks that show the intent of order.
- **Shadow-walk.** Your shadow will betray you. In the perpetual sourceless twilight, it may split, vanish, or lead you in a false direction. Use the hourglass to check your orientation: the salt in the upper bulb will always drift toward the oldest tower in sight. The oldest tower is not necessarily the tallest, but the one whose fluting is most eroded, whose base shows the deepest dendrite mats. Home in on that, and you’ll find the city’s functional heart, where the silence is deepest and the reality is most… settled.
- **If you see an inhabitant, do not approach.** They are not hostile, but they are not separate from the architecture in the way you are separate from your own body. Moving too close to one may cause a kind of ontological crowding, a sense of occupying the same semantic space. You’ll feel it as a pressure in your chest, a conviction that your name has been forgotten. If one moves toward you—which is rare, but recorded—stand aside without flinching and let it pass. Its movement is geological; it will not stop. You might feel a chill, a faint crackling like static on old ice, and then it will be gone, and you will remember its face as a shifting topography that you cannot quite describe.

### Ecological and Cultural Sensitivity
Hark is not empty. It is inhabited by a mineral ecology you cannot touch without altering. The dendritic mats on the walls are slow lives; a single footprint in the wrong place could interrupt a chemical conversation that has been ongoing for three hundred years. Walk only on the constructed causeways. If you must rest, choose a flat salt-block that shows no dendritic growth. If you must touch a wall, do so with the back of your hand, briefly, and then leave a tiny grain of your palm-stone as a gesture of exchange. This is not a rule anyone taught you; it is simply what the silence expects.

The city’s name—Hark, or the Hark—is not a name. It is a placeholder for something that predates sound. Do not try to pronounce it perfectly. Do not try to name the city in your own language. If you must refer to it internally, call it “the place of patience,” and let that be enough.

### What You Will Experience, and How to Carry It Back
You will walk for what feels like hours, then days, then a single suspended moment. The city will not resist you, but it will not confirm you. By the time you leave, you will have absorbed some of its mineral stillness: your thoughts will move slower, your heartbeat will feel like a distant rumor. This is not damage; it is a loan from deep time. When you return to your own world, the noise will feel like an assault. You will miss the silence the way one misses a limb.

Keep the salt-hourglass. It will have stopped flowing at some point during your withdrawal from the Kettlah, the crystals frozen in a delicate equilibrium. If you ever doubt that you visited a place where reality is negotiable, look at the fixed grains, and remember that once, in a city that forgot its name, you moved at a pace the stars would envy, and the bridges sang without sound, and the sky held no color but was full of patient shapes. That memory is now part of your own mineral layer, a thin stratum of elsewhere deposited in the sediment of your life. Carry it without naming it, and you will be welcome should you ever return.
