Before you even think about stepping through the portal, you need to understand this place not as a destination but as a living body you’re about to enter. The Hollow is a sealed, breathing lung buried in the corpse of a dead planet. It is warm, wet, blindingly bright, and filled with a continuous shriek so profound it becomes a physical presence. Visiting it is less like exploring a cave and more like walking into the throat of a sleeping god—one that might not stay asleep if you make a wrong move. As an ordinary human multiverse traveler, you are not adapted to anything here. Your body is fragile, your senses easily overloaded. But with the right preparation, you can observe this impossible pocket world and return with your sanity and skeleton intact.

### Understand What You’re Walking Into

**Geography and Climate**  
The Hollow is a single vast chamber, its curved walls made of warm, vitrified material—smooth as polished bone and faintly oily to the touch. The floor slopes gently toward a central “green mountain,” a dense, interwoven mass of luminescent vegetation that rises like an island in an empty sea. Temperatures hold steady at 30°C with suffocating humidity; the air is thick, still, and smells of wet earth and an undercurrent of ozone. There is no day or night, only the constant white-gold radiance shed by the plants themselves. The light is bright enough to read by but not harsh enough to damage eyes, though its ceaselessness will play tricks on your circadian rhythm.

**The Voice**  
The defining feature of this world is sound. From the heart of the green mountain comes a continuous, roaring, metallic shriek—a hybrid of a high-pressure steam whistle and a subterranean mechanical churn. It is not background noise; it is a pressure wave that fills the entire chamber to the point where you can feel it in your chest like the beat of a colossal drum. The sound bounces off the hard, curved walls, layering and amplifying into a near-solid wall of noise. Without protection, staying here for more than a few minutes will cause permanent hearing damage. Staying longer invites physical trauma to organ systems from relentless low-frequency vibration. This is not a place where you can simply “shout over the noise”; communication will be entirely non-verbal or electronic.

**Life Forms**  
- **The Green Mountain**: This is not a plant but a single, ancient organism—a symbiotic fusion of vegetation and something else that breathes, screams, and periodically releases clouds of golden, choking spores. It is absolutely not to be touched or entered. The absent people who once lived here considered such an act unthinkable, and their abandoned tools reinforce that taboo. The mountain is the source of light, heat, steam, and the deafening voice. You are here to observe, not to interact.  
- **Sky Jellyfish**: High in the inaccessible darkness of the ceiling, translucent jellyfish-like creatures drift on thermal currents. Their long, threadlike tentacles dangle into the open air, silently sampling it for… something. They are almost invisible against the constant shriek. Whether their sting is venomous, adhesive, or something stranger is unknown, but their hunting ground is the empty space between the walls and the mountain. Assume they are dangerous.  
- **Spores**: Periodic eruptions from the mountain release dense, golden clouds of spores. They are described as “choking,” and the former inhabitants took care to keep their dwellings sealed with coarse woven fabric, suggesting the spores are at minimum an extreme respiratory irritant, possibly more.

**The Settlement**  
Carved directly into the vitrified walls are hemispherical hollows—nests lined with dried vine fiber. These are safe havens: elevated slightly from the floor, acoustically somewhat shadowed (though far from silent), and furnished with remnants of a vanished people. They are ready-made for a traveler to use as a base camp. Leave them as you find them.

### Essential Gear: Your Survival Kit

Pack everything as if you are entering a hostile-environment cleanroom the size of a cathedral, with a noise problem worse than a rocket launch.

**Hearing Protection (Critical—Do Not Skip)**  
You need layered, redundant systems. The shriek likely exceeds 140 decibels in the open chamber; the sound pressure alone can damage your inner ear, sinuses, and even internal organs. Begin with high-fidelity earplugs capable of a Noise Reduction Rating (NRR) of at least 33 dB. Over these, wear industrial-grade earmuffs rated NRR 34+. For true safety, add an active noise-cancellation collar that inverts the dominant frequencies—this will cut the psycho-acoustic weight of the noise, making it possible to think. A full-head communication rig with bone-conduction microphones will let you speak with companions via text-to-voice relay, because shouting is futile. Bring spares. The moment your seal fails, the sound becomes a weapon.

**Respiratory Protection**  
A full-face respirator with organic vapor/particulate filters (P100 or equivalent) is mandatory. The spores are unknown, and the air may contain fine droplets of steam condensate laced with irritants. The full-face design also protects your eyes from spore clouds and offers a secondary seal against the jellyfish tentacles if one drifts too close. Carry extra filters; humidity will clog them faster than usual.

**Body Covering**  
Wear a lightweight, breathable but full-coverage suit. A Tychem or similar hazmat coverall (rated for particulates and light chemical splash) will guard against oily wall residue, accidental spore accumulation, and any incidental contact with jellyfish tentacles. The suit should have elastic cuffs at wrists and ankles, and a hood that integrates snugly with your respirator. Choose a light color so you can spot hitchhiking spores. Layer moisture-wicking base garments underneath; you will sweat in 30°C humidity.

**Footwear**  
The vitrified floor is smooth but described as “faintly oily.” That means a high slip risk. Wear non-slip, closed-toe boots with aggressive tread and oil-resistant soles. A walking stick or trekking pole with a rubber tip is an excellent stabilizer. The floor is otherwise clear of debris, but a fall into a low spot could be dangerous.

**Tactile Protection**  
Gloves are essential. Thin, cut-resistant work gloves give you dexterity to handle gear while protecting your skin from the warm, oily walls (which may harbor microscopic bioactive residues). Bring an extra pair of heavier heat-resistant gloves if you ever intend to get close to steam vents—though you shouldn’t.

**Illumination**  
Ambient light from the green mountain is bright enough to see by, but it is diffuse and may cast confusing shadows. A headlamp with red and white modes (water-resistant, easily operated with gloves) is a backup. Also bring a high-intensity handheld flashlight for checking the ceiling for jellyfish tentacles—more on that later.

**Shelter and Base Camp**  
You will be spending most of your resting hours inside one of the wall cells. These are ready-made alcoves, but you can improve them: bring a lightweight camping pad or inflatable mattress that can conform to the curved floor, a compact hammock if the cell’s dimensions allow, and a mylar emergency blanket to reflect your own body heat if you need warmth (though the walls already radiate warmth). A small, battery-powered fan will combat the dead air. A microfiber towel will manage sweat and condensation.

**Observation Tools**  
Binoculars or a spotting scope with image stabilization are your best friends. Use them to study the mountain’s breathing cycle, observe the sky jellyfish, and watch for spore eruptions from a safe distance at the chamber periphery. A robust, waterproof digital camera with a good zoom lens will document everything. A notebook and all-weather pen are quieter than electronics, which can glitch under extreme acoustic vibration.

**Hydration and Nutrition**  
In 30°C heat and high humidity, dehydration sets in fast. Carry at least two liters of water in a hydration bladder with an insulated tube; drink often even if you don’t feel thirsty, as the constant low-frequency vibration can mask normal body signals. Salty snacks or electrolyte tablets replace lost sodium. Simple, high-calorie energy bars are your food—nothing that requires cooking or strong scent.

**First Aid and Medical**  
- **For spore exposure**: Fast-acting antihistamine, a rescue inhaler (albuterol), and epinephrine autoinjector in case of anaphylaxis.  
- **For jellyfish stings**: Earthly jellyfish treatments (vinegar) may be useless or dangerous. Carry a generic sting relief spray containing lidocaine and an ammonia-based after-bite. At the first sign of a sting—burning, numbness, rapid swelling—retreat to your cell immediately, remove the suit, and irrigate the area with clean water.  
- **For sound-related trauma**: While ear protection prevents hearing loss, you may still experience nausea, disorientation, or chest tightness from infrasound. Anti-nausea medication and mild sedatives (used cautiously) can help.  
- **General**: Blister care, antiseptic wipes, wide-spectrum antibiotic cream, and a tourniquet for the worst-case scenario.

**Multi-Versal Navigation and Emergency Signaling**  
Carry your interdimensional recall beacon or portal activator on your person at all times, in a pocket you can reach even with impaired vision or motor control. A personal locator beacon that operates on unjammable frequencies is wise. Pre-set an extraction protocol: if you lose consciousness or are immobilized, the beacon should automatically activate.

**Mental Prep Tools**  
The constant shriek, even muffled, is a psychological assault. Bring noise-cancelling earbuds that can play calming music or pink noise through your earplugs to give your brain a pattern to follow. A tiny photo of home, a familiar scent (peppermint oil in a sealed pouch), or a small tactile comfort object goes a long way.

### Safety Protocols: How to Survive

**1. Acclimatize at the Portal Threshold**  
Before stepping fully into the chamber, pause in the transition zone. Put on your full hearing protection and respirator. Let your body adjust to the heat and humidity. Practice moving, looking around, and using your communication device. If you feel the vibration too intensely in your chest, reconsider—this is your body’s warning.

**2. Never Enter the Central Vegetation**  
Treat the green mountain as a lethal hazard. The boundary between the bare floor and the vegetation is a hard line. Do not touch the leaves, do not pull vines, do not attempt to cut into it out of curiosity. The “throat of the voice” is not a metaphor—entering it will subject you to extreme steam, crushing plant mass, and potentially the organism’s direct attention. The settlement’s entire culture was built around never doing this. Respect that.

**3. Watch the Ceiling Constantly**  
Jellyfish tentacles are silent, almost invisible, and may drift down from the darkness at any time. Before you leave the shelter of a wall cell, shine your flashlight beam upward and scan for dangling threads. Look for subtle shimmers against the bioluminescent glow. If you see even one tentacle, wait. Observe its movement. If it’s drifting toward the floor, retreat. A good rule: never walk in the open when you can see a tentacle lower than the height of your head.

**4. Spore Eruption Procedure**  
If you notice the central mountain’s exhalation rhythm change, a deep rumble building, or a golden haze starting to billow, *move calmly but urgently toward your wall cell*. Do not run; the floor is slippery. Once inside the cell, activate your full-face respirator (if not already wearing it), seal the cuffs of your suit, and use a spare mylar blanket to partially cover the cell opening if you have time. The spores are dense enough to be “choking clouds,” so they will billow across the floor like a dust storm. Wait them out; eruptions likely last minutes to an hour.

**5. Movement on the Floor**  
The empty floor is a vast, sterile expanse with no cover. When crossing it, travel in small groups if accompanied, maintaining a 360-degree watch. Keep a slow, deliberate pace to avoid slipping. Your trekking pole will help you test the surface ahead. If you detect a new smell—sweet, metallic, or acrid—through your respirator, stop and reassess.

**6. Base Camp in the Cells**  
The wall cells are your sanctuary. Choose one that is at least halfway up the wall, accessible without difficult climbing. The coarse woven fabric inside may be fragile; handle it only with gloved hands to preserve it. Lay your sleeping pad and arrange gear so nothing rolls out. At “night” (your own scheduled rest period, since there is no night), cover your eyes with a mask and use the fan to keep air circulating. Even with hearing protection, the muffled shriek will pervade your dreams. Be ready for disturbed sleep.

**7. Leave No Trace**  
The Hollow is a closed-loop ecology and a cultural site. Do not take tools, fabrics, or even a cutting of the vegetation. Do not draw on the walls or leave behind any waste. Pack out everything you bring in, including human waste (carry a compact dry toilet kit). The spores and jellyfish are the only things that should be disturbed.

### What It Will Really Feel Like

Imagine standing on the edge of that immense, smooth floor, the green mountain blazing before you like a living sun. Even through your protective layers, you will feel the heat of the walls on your skin and the thrum of the shriek in your teeth. Every few seconds, the bright light will flicker minutely as a spire of steam vents from the unseen mouth, the pitch climbing momentarily. The air is like a warm, wet blanket. You’ll scan the ceiling and see nothing, then catch a faint, glassy strand—a tentacle—sliding downward twenty meters away, and your heart will leap. The abandoned nest behind you, with its ancient woven bed and black glass axe, will remind you that others lived in this roar, and listened, and are now gone. It’s terrifying. It’s magnificent. And if you are prepared, you will be able to sit and watch the breathing of a world that has no name, understanding that you are a guest in a place that was old before your own dimension cooled.

- [ ] Footwear: Oil-resistant, non-slip boots; trekking pole  
- [ ] Gloves: Two pairs (cut-resistant, thermal backup)  
- [ ] Shelter: Sleeping pad, mylar blanket, small fan, eye mask  
- [ ] Observation: Binoculars, camera, notebook, pen  
- [ ] Hydration: 2L water bladder, electrolyte tabs  
- [ ] Food: Energy bars, jerky, high-calorie snacks  
- [ ] First aid: Inhaler, antihistamine, epipen, sting spray, basic kit  
- [ ] Navigation: Portal recall beacon, personal locator, compass (if magnetic field exists)  
- [ ] Sanitation: Dry toilet kit, wet wipes, disinfectant gel  
- [ ] Mental: Music player with earbuds, comfort token, mild sedative (consult physician)

The Hollow will test every sense you have and a few you didn’t know you possessed. But with methodical preparation, you won’t just survive—you’ll witness one of the multiverse’s most extraordinary secret places, and come back with a story no one else can tell.
