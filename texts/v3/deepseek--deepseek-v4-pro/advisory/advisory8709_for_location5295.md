Here is a practical guide for an ordinary human multiverse traveler — one shaped by water, green plants, and a narrow thermal tolerance — preparing to visit the salt-crust pocket.

---

### A Traveller’s Notes on the Pocket in the Salt

You are not going to another continent. You are not going to another planet. You are going to a place where the fundamental solvent of biology is oil, not water, and where the air itself is a dry, abrasive presence that will treat your body as a leaking sack of inconvenient chemistry. Prepare as if for a deep-ocean dive, a desert crossing, and a foundry visit combined. The following is not definitive — the pocket changes — but it will keep you alive and may make you welcome.

---

#### 1. Understanding What You’re Walking Into

- **Atmosphere and heat:** The temperature down in the cavity holds at a steady 2.8 — likely a local measure keyed to the metabolic sweet spot of the bronze-vine chemistry. For your Earth-calibrated senses, imagine the still, hot air in the upper room of a glassblower’s workshop, or the moment you open a bread oven and the dry surge hits your face. That’s the baseline. It never lets up. It is hot enough that standard electronics without ruggedisation will shut down; hotter than a sauna but with zero moisture. Your sweat will evaporate almost before it forms, which is a deceptive danger: you won’t feel wet, and you won’t notice how much water you’re losing into that greedy, parched atmosphere.
- **Light:** Outside, the sun is a slicing white actinic glare. Inside the hollow, it’s perpetually twilit: the reddish-orange glow of hot mineral seams, the faint blue of bioluminescent growths, the ember-pulse of charcoal beds. Your eyes will strain. Depth perception becomes difficult on the rope bridges. Colours are pushed toward amber, umber, and near-black. Bring your own light, but use it sparingly or filter it warm; actinic white will blind the locals.
- **No water, anywhere:** This is the critical fact. The chemistry here runs on metallic oxides and sulfurous vapours. Your body chemistry runs on water. The pocket will take your water via exhalation, perspiration, and the simple osmotic pull of an environment that has no moisture to give back. There is no stream, no dew, no rain. Even the local “food” — the oily paste — will further desiccate you if you attempt to eat it without preparation.
- **Sound:** The hollow acts as a soundbox. Every hammer blow, every shout, every footfall on salt crust echoes and mixes into a perpetual low roar. The deep chuff of the vents is a physical pressure on the eardrums. If you are noise-sensitive, you must address this beforehand, or you will break within hours.
- **The locals:** They are human. That is astonishing and important. They breathe the same gas mix (likely, though it’s laced with sulphur and metal particulates) and their bodies have adapted over generations to metabolise the oily paste of the vines. To them, you are a pale, cool, dripping ghost, probably reeking of water and strange bacterial byproducts. They are not hostile, but they are busy, their cycles exacting, and their toleration of fragile off-worlders has limits.

---

#### 2. Pre-Departure Physiological Preparation

You cannot truly acclimate in advance, but you can reduce the shock.

- **Heat training:** Spend at least two weeks doing sustained physical labour in hot, dry conditions, ideally while wearing a respiratory mask to accustom yourself to the suffocating feeling. Saunas are wet heat; you need dry. Use a kiln-warmed room if one can be found, or a hot attic in summer. Learn to recognise the pre-symptoms of heat exhaustion when your skin is dry.
- **Hydration loading:** In the final 48 hours before departure, hydrate aggressively with electrolyte-heavy fluids. Not just water — you need to saturate your tissues with salts that will help you retain what you drink. Avoid diuretics completely.
- **Respiratory priming:** The atmosphere carries fine dust — salt crystals, metallic oxide particles, the sharp dried spores of the bronze vines. Your lungs will not like this. Use a daily saline nasal rinse and deep breathing exercises in dusty environments (hay lofts, old cellars) while wearing a light filter for a few hours each day. It won’t stop the irritation, but it will dampen the panic of feeling your airways tighten.
- **Taste and smell preparation:** The “living reek” — hot metal, fermentation, oily sharpness — can be nauseating to a newcomer. You can’t simulate it perfectly, but visiting a working blacksmith’s forge while someone ferments kimchi or sauerkraut nearby will give you the sensory clash. Go, and stand there for an hour. If you gag, you have work to do.

#### 3. Core Gear and Supplies

Your kit must create a mobile micro-environment around your body, and be field-repairable with the primitive tools available in the cavity. Nothing that relies on a power grid. Nothing that cracks under heat-load.

**Water System (Critical)**
- A pressurised, insulated drinking bladder with a bite-valve tube routed *inside* your suit, so the water doesn’t flash-evaporate as you drink. Capacity: no less than 6 litres. Your consumption will be brutal — assume 1.5 litres per hour of moderate exertion. The pocket will not refill you.
- A secondary sealed canteen, metal, to act as a thermal buffer. If your primary bladder fails, this is your emergency supply.
- A hand-pumped micro-filtration mask that recaptures exhaled moisture. These are available from several multiverse outfitters; they reclaim about 40% of your respiratory water loss. Not perfect, but it stretches your supply by hours.
- Water purification tablets and a sterile collapsible container. There is *no* local water, but if you must reuse any fluid from your own condensation collection, you need to make it safe.

**Respiratory Protection**
- A well-fitted half-mask respirator with activated charcoal and fine particulate filters. The charcoal will help with the sulphurous vapours; the particulate layer stops the dust and spores. Bring three sets of filters. They will clog fast in the oily air.
- A silk or fine-mesh pre-filter worn over the mask to catch the larger oil droplets. You can clean this without burning through your main filters.
- Nasal ointment (lanolin-based, lightly mentholated) to line your nostrils and prevent cracking. The dry air will cause nosebleeds otherwise.

**Thermal Management**
- A cooling vest of the phase-change or circulating type. Power it with a manually rechargeable pump if possible, not batteries. The settlement can provide muscle power; they cannot recharge lithium ion. Alternatively, a simple chiller pack vest with replaceable cold packs — you can cool the packs by lowering them on a rope into the upper empty void of the cavity at night cycle, where the air is marginally cooler, though this is slow.
- A reflective emergency blanket, not to capture heat but to rig as a radiant-heat shield if you must rest near a vent mouth.

**Lighting**
- A headlamp with a warm-red and a low-blue setting. White light is brutal to the locals’ eyes and yours in the dimness; red preserves night vision and blends better with the native glow.
- A backup hand-crank torch. No batteries.
- A small lantern that burns the local oil (once you arrive). This might be a crucial trade item — obtain a suitable wick-and-reservoir lamp with an adjustable flame.

**Clothing and Footwear**
This is a layering exercise against dry heat and abrasive, crumbling terrain.

- **Footwear:** High-top leather boots (no synthetic mesh — it will melt or catch on salt crust). The soles must be non-slip on iron, salt, and oily wood. Pack a tube of heat-resistant adhesive and a repair awl. The rope bridges and ladders will chew through soft soles.
- **Inner layer:** A moisture-wicking, odour-resistant long-sleeve shirt and leggings of a merino-synthetic blend. This will hold your sweat long enough for evaporative cooling before the air steals it.
- **Middle layer:** A lightweight, tightly-woven cotton or linen oversuit, full-length, that you can dampen with a tiny amount of your precious water. In the dry heat, this becomes a evaporative chiller.
- **Outer layer:** A hooded, loose robe or coat of heat-reflecting fabric (silvered or white), with deep cuffs you can cinch. This shields you from the radiant heat of the mineral seams and the occasional gas-gout.
- **Gloves:** Thin leather work gloves, durable but tactile, to handle hot tools, rope, and the slightly caustic oil of the vines.
- **Head and neck:** A broad-brimmed, wide hat of stiff felt, plus a neck flap. Under it, wear a tightly-wrapped cotton scarf that you can moisten. The scarf will also help filter the air when your mask is off briefly.

**Navigation and Information**
- A simple mechanical watch, luminous dial. The day-night cycle here is foreign; you’ll need to track your own rest intervals.
- A small slate or wax tablet and stylus for notes. Paper will disintegrate in the dry, oily dust.
- A hand-cranked audio recorder of some primitive type if you wish to capture the sonic environment. Digital electronics may fail.
- A strong, compact monocular for inspecting distant terraces and bridge conditions.

**Survival and Medical Kit**
- Electrolyte powder sachets (enough to double your water’s efficacy).
- Burn cream and sterile dressings — heat and hot metal are the primary injury risks.
- Eye drops (preservative-free, artificial tears) to combat the dryness. Use constantly.
- Lip balm, heavy-duty, unflavoured.
- A full set of replacement filters and a small multi-tool with knife, pliers, and a file for sharpening hooks or tools.

#### 4. Traveling to the Pocket

You will arrive at the crust — the white salt plain — possibly hours’ walk from the hidden lip.

- The surface is blinding, actinic, and featureless. Wear glacier goggles or equivalent welding-grade eye protection. Heat shimmer disorients; you can lose your way within minutes.
- The sun-cut at noon reveals the lip by throwing a sudden, sharp shadow line. Do not attempt to walk in the open during the high light hours without a fixed compass bearing. The cavity’s location is easily missed until you are nearly upon it.
- The overhang of the lip is crumbly salt-crust. Do not walk out onto it casually. Test with a probing stick; the ground can give way. The locals likely have a known safe route down — ladders or notched logs. Wait to be seen, or signal with a mirror, rather than attempting a solo descent. If you must descend unaided, follow the most traveled-looking path, however precarious it seems, because it has held their weight.
- As you approach the lip, listen. You will hear the cavity before you see it: that constant chuff and ring, rising like a distant industry. It is the sound of the town, and it is your beacon.

---

#### 5. Inside the Cavity: First Hours

The descent is a transition through a heat gradient. Stop on a high terrace for ten minutes to let your senses adjust and to observe the movement of the settlement below.

- Visually, the world will seem painfully dim after the surface glare. Do not turn on your headlamp immediately. Let your eyes dilate. The glow of the mineral seams will gradually reveal the tangle of bridges and roofs.
- The noise will hit you as a physical wave. If you are prone to sensory overwhelm, place wax or oil-soaked cotton in your ears until you find your balance. You will still hear, but the edge is taken off.
- The air’s heat wraps you like a blanket. Immediately assess your cooling vest and start sipping water from the tube in your suit at regular intervals — small sips every five minutes, not gulps at hourly breaks.
- Expect to be greeted. The people will notice a stranger. They see few. Your demeanour should be calm, unarmoured (no visible weapons), and patient. Do not rush down into the main settlement without invitation.

---

#### 6. Living in the Pocket

**Interacting with the Inhabitants**
- The locals’ work rhythm is relentless. They move with the vines. Do not interrupt a team shifting a bloomery or relaying a terrace unless invited. Stand aside, observe, and offer no opinions.
- Your primary value is your manual labour if you wish to earn your keep. You are likely weaker and less heat-adapted, but you can carry, haul, or scrape. Offer your hands, not your advice.
- Trade goods that are valuable: a well-made iron knife of a design they don’t produce (thin, flexible blades), bronze or copper wire, a lens (monocular), and any sparker or fire-lighting tool more efficient than their current method. The lamp that burns their vine oil is also a fine gift — show them that you respect their resource.
- Do not speak of water. It is an abstract poison to them, not a blessing. Pouring out water to wash your hands or clean an object would be incomprehensibly wasteful and possibly offensive in a place where moisture invites decay of their structural salts. Keep your water consumption discreet. They may not understand why you must drink so often; do it unostentatiously inside your suit.
- There is no fauna to offer as food, and you cannot eat their oil-paste safely. You must be self-sufficient with your rations. If they offer you the paste, a small symbolic taste (a dab on the tongue) might be polite, but do not swallow more; it will accelerate dehydration and your gut biome cannot handle the inorganic nutrients. A diplomatic refusal is acceptable once you indicate you have your own “special” provisions for off-world metabolism.

**Movement and Shelter**
- Walk only on the ladders and bridges. The salt-crust ledges not reinforced with fibre-brick are untrustworthy.
- The locals may assign you a guest niche — a small carved room in the ferrous stone wall. It will be hot, but the stone is a stable temperature. Set up your cooling vest and a reflective lean-to inside to create a micro-cooler.
- The void above is yours to use. If you need a psychological break, climb (with permission) to the very highest bridge, a rope crossing the emptiness. There, the heat is slightly less intense, the noise a little muted, and you can look down into the glowing, living tangle. It is a place to breathe, though the drop is absolute.

**Managing the “Weather”**
- The vent-gas gouts: you will feel the pressure change in your ears, and the locals will start tally-stick checks. When they move upward, you move upward. Do not delay. The gas is sulfurous and hot; your filters will be overwhelmed, and your lungs will burn. Stay on the upper terraces until they descend again, which may take a full day. Conserve your water and patience.
- Spore releases: the bronze vines occasionally puff out clouds of yellow or purple spores. Keep your mask on. Do not touch your eyes.

---

#### 7. Emergency Protocols

- **Heat exhaustion/stroke:** If you stop sweating entirely and feel a cold, dry skin despite the heat, you are in crisis. Immediately retreat to the coolest niche you can find (near the void rim, or ask locals for the “cold room” — they may have storage carved deep in the ferrous stone). Unseal your suit, apply wet cloths from your reserved water to your neck and wrists, and sip water very slowly. You must cool your core. Do not resume activity for at least one full cycle.
- **Dehydration:** If your urine becomes dark and scant, you are behind. Increase electrolyte intake, reduce exertion, and consider using your condensation mask for a prolonged rest.
- **Respiratory distress:** If your lungs begin to burn and you cough ceaselessly, move to the highest terrace immediately, double the pre-filtering, and consider a steam tent: use a tiny amount of your water on a cloth held over your nose to moisten the air you breathe. This is a last resort because it wastes water, but it can save your mucous membranes.
- **Injury:** A fall from a bridge or ladder is the highest danger. The locals likely have bone-setters and improvised dressings made from vine fibre. Accept their help. Your sterility standards may be higher, but their knowledge of working with the local materials is superior. Trust them with the setting; use your own bandages if possible.

---

#### 8. Departure: Leaving the Pocket

The climb to the surface will feel like stepping into a freezer and a blinding, silent void. The sudden absence of noise can cause temporary disorientation. Sit at the lip for a long while. Breathe the dry but cleaner air. Shed your heavy outer layers slowly; your body will need to re-equilibrate.

Your water system may be empty. Your skin will be cracked, your ears ringing, your hair brittle, your clothes steeped in that hot-metal-and-oil reek for weeks. And yet you will have walked the bridges over the glowing void, heard the chuff of the breathing earth, and seen a thriving, waterless, animal-less human city that has made a garden of the rock’s exhalation. The preparation is brutal, but the gift is singular. Travel well.
