The first thing to understand about the Wet Dark Plateau—and I name it only so you have a word for it—is that you will never be dry, never be warm, and never see a shadow cast by a distinct point of light. Your body, a creature of sunlight and sharp images, will rebel. But the rebellion is the point. Every multiverse traveler eventually seeks out worlds that do not conform to primate expectations, and this one is a cathedral of sustained, watery gloom.

I have been there. This is what you need to know, written by someone who stood on that fractured basalt and felt the sound below hearing change his heartbeat.

### Pre-Visit Conditioning
A week before you depart, start taking cold showers. Not just brief, invigorating ones—I mean standing under a trickle of 2°C water until you can stop shivering. The air on the Plateau is a suspicion above freezing, and the damp is so pervasive that your skin will feel permanently beaded with ice water. If your body has not learned to metronome its core temperature in such conditions, you will start to shiver and never stop. This is not a place for hypothermia novices.

Alongside the cold, reduce light in your living space. Sleep in a room so dark you cannot see your hand. Eat simple, bland foods for a few days—your gut will protest the mineral tang of the water you will inevitably ingest with every breath. The air is heavy with microscopic droplets that taste faintly of iron and ozone, and they will settle on your tongue and coat your throat. If you are unused to such, your body may mistake it for thirst and overhydrate storage pods like the creatures you will see. Adjust your palate to accept mineralised water, for you will be inhaling it.

Mentally, learn to sit still with your own pulse. With no stars, no sun, no moon, your circadian rhythm will crumble. You will depend on your watch. This seems trivial until you realize your mind has lost any anchor to time's passing: the fauna's drone is a constant, the city's growth invisible, and your flashlight battery's dimming the only marker of forward motion. I recommend practicing sensory deprivation—dark room, white noise generator—for an hour each day, while repeating a mental inventory of your location to maintain orientation. You will otherwise drift, and drifting here means hallucinations and dead reckoning failures.

### Clothing and Armor
Dress as if you were crawling inside a cold, weeping limestone cave crossed with an industrial freezer. My bare-minimum starting layer is merino wool close to the skin, a mid-layer of thick polar fleece, and an outer shell that is both windproof and genuinely submersible. Do not rely on water-resistant coatings; the humidity here is so absolute that any exposed skin will bead with condensation within three minutes, and any permeable fabric will draw moisture into your thermal layers. Gore-Tex breathes, and that is the problem: the outside air is more saturated than your skin vapor. I use a lightweight dry suit without the hood, layered over your clothes, taped at the cuffs. Pack extra seals.

Your feet will suffer most. The basaltic ground is freshly extruded, an endless floor of shattered obsidian-like flakes that will slice through soft rubber. Wear full-shank mountaineering boots with Kevlar-fiber soles—rock plate is not optional, steel-reinforced is better. The terrain is not just sharp; it’s slippery. A wet, algae-less, glass-sharp slope is a hazard you cannot overprepare for. Two sets of microspikes for your boots, plus spares, because the constant moisture and freezing edges will work-harden the metal and snap them. Also, a pair of stout kneepads: you will fall, and the basalt will open your skin like a scalpel.

For your hands, high-dexterity cut-resistant gloves (polyethylene weave) inside waterproof overmitts. You’ll need to manipulate gear, maybe write or adjust your light. The ambient temperature isn’t Arctic-frightful, but if you let your fingers numb below 7°C, you’ll drop a flashlight down a fissure. For your head, a balaclava is enough; but be aware that if you walk through pockets of freezing air near the outer ledges, the condensation on your face will ice into a varnish. I recommend a thin smear of petroleum jelly on your skin that will be exposed to the air—eyebrows, upper lip, ears. It sounds vain, but ice crystals will be drawn to your tears and cut your eyelids if you’re not careful.

### Visibility and Lighting
You will arrive with every instinct screaming for light. Suppress those instincts. The Plateau's darkness rating of 0.1 is calculated on a scale where 0 is an absence of electromagnetic flux—a photon vacuum it is not. There is a persistent, sourceless bioluminescence; once your eyes dark-adapt for thirty minutes, you will see the canyons as deeper absences in the murk, the great fauna as shifting constellations. That dim sight is your best navigation tool, because any artificial light will erase it for a further half-hour.

Carry two independent light sources: a headlamp with a deep red LED (preserves night vision) and a handheld backup. Keep them sealed in a waterproof bag until absolutely needed, because the condensing air will kill electrical contacts. When you must use the red beam, shield it to your immediate feet and keep it low—not out of poetry, but because the bright reflection off wet basalt will throw a bloom that backscatters, obscuring everything beyond three meters. Only in an emergency should you click on a white light. If you do, be prepared for a surreal moment: the fauna, those living zeppelins, will reflect back a momentary network of drifting neon which will then tighten into invisibility. It is not known whether this disturbs them, but you are in their territory.

A note on bioluminescent mirages: the constant droplets and acoustics cause light to propagate oddly, forming phantom glows and afterimages that move with your gaze. Trust tactile feedback over visuals. A simple carbon-fiber rod can serve as a cane to feel ahead without gouging the city infrastructure.

### Navigational Tactics
There is no magnetic north, no celestial sphere, no GPS. The city is actively growing, at a rate of a hair’s breadth per day, meaning the pebbled sensor arrays on the walls can change position subtly over weeks. You will not be there weeks, but the upshot is the space is fractal and self-similar. Every ribbed spire looks like every other. Without sun and shadow, you have no sense of direction.

Your first action upon arrival: set a point zero. I use a tiny LED beacon that flashes a dim, encrypted pattern only I recognize, placed at my entry or at a landmark that I physically cannot confuse. A lithium-battery-powered blinker can last for years; a chemical glowstick lasts 12 hours and fails in the cold. You can also leave a small reusable chalk-mark on the ground at decision points, but do so sparingly—the city’s nanoscale coatings will digest organic marks over weeks, but we visitors have no business leaving permanent tags. Small, matte black gear markers that reflect only infrared can work if you carry IR optics.

Use a mechanical pedometer and a compass, but do not trust the latter. The basalt contains magnetite and the city’s power conduits generate weak, shifting fields. Use dead reckoning: count paces, record turns on a waterproof wrist slate, and stop every 50 meters to reorient using the great canyon walls as references. Draw a map as you go. The constant sound—a drone at roughly 20-30 Hz and harmonic overtones—can cause vertigo-like disorientation, so set a timer on your watch to ring every ten minutes to enforce a "sanity check": stop, squat down, check your map, confirm with your eyes, and drink.

If you get lost, sit down immediately. Do not wander. The city’s geometry, with its odd angles and holographic fauna-sigils, can make you lose track of which way is up. In your exhausted state, you may become convinced the avenue you took is "just behind you" when you’ve actually turned 180°. The sound is a blanket; sometimes it conveys aural mirages. Be mentally prepared to ground yourself.

### Health and Safety
Hypothermia Risk is Extreme. Even with proper clothing, the perpetual damp condensation saps body heat faster than expected. Pack chemical heat packs for your hands and core, use them before you start shivering. Also, the water is not safe to drink without treatment: it is runoff from nanoscale fabrication and fauna exhalate, containing elemental stock, perhaps benign but also likely loaded with minerals that will give you kidney stones. Carry your own water, or bring a life-straw style filter, but note that prolonged purring of the moisture yields a metallic flavor. Hydrate constantly, because the dry air moving through your lungs will dehydrate you without you feeling hot.

Hearing Protection is Non-negotiable. The sound is not loud enough to cause immediate damage, but subsonic vibrations can fatigue the tensor tympani muscle and trigger migraines, nausea, and panic attacks in susceptible individuals. Acquire custom-fitted musicians’ earplugs with high-fidelity filters that dampen all frequencies equally, and supplement with active noise-cancelling headphones that generate a counter-phase subsonic hum if you are sensitive. A more low-tech method is to press soft wax plugs deep into the ear canal, but then you lose the ability to hear the higher harmonics that warn of nearby fauna shedding condensation drips that could hit you like a slap. Settle for filtered plugs, and retreat to a shielded nook if you begin to feel chest constriction.

First aid for lacerations is critical. The rock will slice you. Your kit should include instant clotting agents (the wet environment makes bandage adhesion poor), butterfly strips, and iodine wipes. The air biofilm is likely harmless to an unsliced human, but mineral-laden water and open wounds make for a dose of alien sepsis. You will also need an eye wash: cry- ing from condensing irritants is common, and rubbing your eyes with wet gloves is a fast way to infection.

Calorie intake: bring high-fat, high-carbohydrate foods that do not require cooking. The freeze-dried camping meals will rehydrate with chemical heaters, but consume them in dedicated rest periods where you have time to generate waste. Do not discard organic waste; pack it out. The city does not know rot; any microbial stowaway you introduce could cause a slow plague in the biofilm communities that sustain the fauna the city depends on. Use sealable biohazard bags and carry them out, as you would on Everest.

### Coexisting with Fauna and City
The fauna might mistake a stationary human for a dock, so do not camp on exposed ledges. They brush against balconies and, given their sheer tonnage, could inadvertently crush you. Their skin is translucent and veined with living circuitry—and, again, I must emphasize: do not try to activate the sigils that flicker across their bodies by projecting light onto them. I saw a foolish traveler once aim a UV laser at a nearby creature to "see the response." The creature did nothing directly, but its hull oscillated violently and its sonar howled a novel harmonic; within minutes, dozens of others altered their routes to avoid our position. My careless comrade had disrupted a local temperature microgradient, and the creatures corrected for a thermal eddy. The city uses them for atmospheric processing; they are the soul of its metabolism. Your role is reverent pedestrian.

Do not urinate on the rock. If you must eliminate bodily waste, use a portable commode inside your sealed tent, with poly pack-out bags. The ammonia in human waste, in such an alkaline, mineral-rich environment, will react with the biofilm to create volatile compounds that the fauna might interpret as damage markers. More practically, your scent elsewhere will attract nothing because there is no terrestrial scavenger analogue—all ecological roles are filled by the humming drone of macro-fauna and micro-biofilm—but protocol demands minimal intervention.

### Suggested Minimum Gear List
- **Clothing:** Full drysuit with overmitts, base-layer merino wool long johns, polar fleece mid-layer, mountaineering boots with Kevlar insole, neoprene socks, microspikes, knee and elbow pads, balaclava, goggles, vapour barrier gloves inside cut-resistant work gloves.
- **Light:** Red-LED headlamp (waterproof to 10 m), waterproof white-LED backup, chemical glowsticks for emergency marker only. No filter-based night vision unless carefully sealed against moisture, which causes condensation inside the optic prism.
- **Navigation:** Waterproof mechanical compass (for relative bearing only), pedometer, waterproof writing slate, chalk pencil, bright non-adhesive markers (suction-cup with fluorescent post), small programmable beacon.
- **Shelter and Sleeping:** Bivvy tent rated for -5°C, closed-cell foam pad, synthetic sleeping bag (-5°C comfort), moisture-proof vapor barrier liner. This is purely for contingencies; your aim is not to sleep, but if injured you must survive.
- **Food and Water:** Personal water for 48 hours (minimum 4 liters per day for the cold and exertion), high-calorie gel and bar foods, a small stove with liquid fuel (no fire bans to consider, but exhaust moisture adds to your own condensation, so use sparingly).
- **Safety:** Multi-attitude altitude sickness prevention pills? Unlikely, but plateau is "high up" so thin air. No mention of altitude sickness, but "high up ... plateau ... broad plateau" — prepare for altitude, take acetazolamide if needed. Bring painkillers, anti-nausea meds, earplug filters, clotting agent, antibiotics.
- **Waste Kit:** Restop bags, small commode system, containment bags.

### Duration and Extraction
Inaugural trips should be measured in hours. Two hours maximum on the surface, then retreat to your anchoring portal or sealed environment to dry, warm, and rehydrate. The place is not hostile, but its passive resistance—the cold, the wet, the sound—will deplete your strength swiftly. If you stay overnight, do so only with a full camp setup and partner, and be prepared for the strange psychological onset called "drift psychosis" as your sleep cycles fragment without solar cues.

Leave when the city tells you to. I do not mean that in a mystic sense: the fauna’s luminous patterns intensify over a two-hour cycle corresponding to a diel pulse of heat from the planet’s interior. When the cyan shifts to amber, a faint change hardly visible to dark-adapted eyes, the city’s vocalizations become slightly more resonant. It is a clue that your own biomechanical clock is at risk of entraining. Set a timer and obey it. Multiverse travel wisdom dictates you never outstay your ease.

When you are back in your bright, dry, silent apartment with its humming refrigerator and sunlight through windows, you may find yourself restless, fumbling for the drone. Do not mistake this for affection for the darkness. It is merely the departure of total sensory immersion. Read your notes, sip tea, and plan no fewer than two weeks before a return. The Wet Dark Plateau deserves precisely measured respect.
