Let’s call this place the Green Cylinder, for lack of an official name. It’s a relatively recent build, still gleaming and unbreathed by anything that counts as fauna. The baseline atmospheric conditions outside are hostile—high-altitude, low-O2, wind-scoured—while the interior is a completely submerged, engineered aquarium without a single visible free surface. An ordinary human multiverse traveler absolutely can visit, but only if they think of themselves less as a tourist and more as a temporary organ being transplanted into a biotechnological body. I’ve made that kind of trip before, and I’ll walk you through the prep as if I were kitting up for it tomorrow.

---

### 1. Understand exactly what you’re entering

Before packing a single piece of gear, internalise the sensory and metabolic reality of the Green Cylinder:

- **It is a liquid environment, completely filled.** There’s no air pocket. No surface to break. The working fluid is a synthetic seawater analogue, oxygenated and buffered to pH 7.9, held at a steady 27°C. To you, that’s warm silk—but it will leach heat from your core over hours, so exposure protection isn’t optional.
- **The pressure profile is modest but real.** The usable volume extends roughly 10 metres from top to the mesh floor, meaning ambient pressure is around 2 ATA. No decompression obligation for short visits, but your lung-gas dynamics matter enormously—especially if you plan to breathe conventional air.
- **There is no day-night cycle, no weather, no sound above a low industrial hum.** Light intensity is a flat 2.3 (normal daylight equivalent) radiating from a slowly rotating central column. Shadows do not flicker or shift over hours; they creep millimetres per revolution. Your inner clock will go completely untethered.
- **Vegetation density averages 2.8/10—that is, near-maximal.** Every rung, flange, and stanchion is colonised by purpose-grown plant-machine hybrids. They are soft but ubiquitous, and they will touch you constantly. They are biosamplers; they’ll taste your epidermal chemistry and pass data back to the chamber’s governing logic. They are not hostile, merely present and patient.
- **The weirdness index is moderate (2.4).** Expect the uncanny silence of a tended garden that hosts no birdsong, no insect hum, only the sigh of nutrient pumps. The absence of decay—leaves programmed to brown precisely on schedule and dissolve—adds a low-level cognitive dissonance.

Knowing these factors, you can proceed to the core decision that shapes everything else: **how will you breathe?**

### 2. Life-support: the critical fork

Your options break down into three tiers. I’ve listed them from most faithful to the site’s intended experience to most conservative.

#### Option A: Temporary liquid ventilation (the “diffuser graft”)

This is what the place expects. The chamber’s fluid is, explicitly, a liquid-breathing medium. With the right prep, your lungs can be flooded with it safely, and a small implant—an extracorporeal or subclavicular diffuser—exchanges O₂ and CO₂ directly across a membrane, allowing you to “breathe” the water. No bubbles, no mask, no noise. Your chest rises and falls with the same synthetic seawater that touches the leaves.

*How an ordinary multiverse traveler gets this done:*

- Visit a Level-3 biotechnological clinic (any universe with a biomod rating ≥2.0 will have one). The procedure is outpatient: a temporary gill-like cartridge is surgically anchored to a major vessel, usually via a port in the upper chest. The lungs are pre-filled with oxygenated perfluorocarbon, then gradually substituted with the target fluid over a few hours of acclimation.
- The diffuser cartridge can be programmed to reverse the process for egress: it’ll purge the fluid from your lungs and restore air-breathing in a controlled sequence. This is critical—you don’t want to surface into thin mountain air with fluid-filled lungs.
- **Acclimation time:** 6–12 hours. **Cost:** moderate, depending on your insurance across dimensional boundaries.
- **Sensation:** Initially, the feeling of liquid breathing triggers a profound panic response—your diaphragm will want to cough. The diffuser suppresses that reflex. After half an hour, it becomes strangely natural, like being a fetus again. You’ll hear only your own heartbeat and the chamber’s ambient rhythm.
- **Advantages:** No bubbles, no bulky equipment, full integration with the environment. You become the zero-impact visitor the garden expects.
- **Disadvantages:** Invasive, requires medical support, and leaves you utterly dependent on the diffuser’s reliability. Bring a backup emergency air tab just in case.

#### Option B: Artificial gill rebreather (external, bubble-free)

If you’d rather not have surgery, you can wear a device that accomplishes the same effect externally. A closed-circuit “aqua-lung” that extracts dissolved oxygen from the surrounding fluid and vents CO₂ as soluble microbubbles or dissolved gas. The best models (I recommend the Kiyama AquaCycle 7) are silent, emit nothing visible, and fit like a slim backpack with a full-face mask.

- **Mask:** Must be soft-seal, panoramic, and integrated with a bone-conduction comms unit so you can still hear the chamber’s hush without the “dead ear” of a hood.
- **Breathing loop:** The unit passes ambient water through a semipermeable O₂ concentrator; you breathe warmed, humidified gas. Exhalation is re-injected into the fluid through a diffuser stone that creates a cascade of minuscule bubbles—similar to what the chamber’s pipes do—so you won’t disrupt the silence.
- **Power:** The pump needs a compact fuel cell, good for 12+ hours. Bring a spare.
- **Training:** You must practise slow, yogic breathing. Quick, shallow gasps will make the loop struggle to keep up. Learn to exhale in long, continuous streams.
- **Buoyancy:** The unit adds slight negative buoyancy; you’ll tune your weight belt to neutral.

#### Option C: Conventional open-circuit scuba

This is the easiest to acquire but the least immersive. A standard tank and regulator will keep you alive. However:

- **Bubbles:** Every exhale will produce a noisy, silver curtain of bubbles that’ll strike leaves, bounce off gangways, and degrade the acoustic tapestry. You’ll feel instantly like an intruder.
- **Thermal:** The compressed air will be cold and dry; your core will chill faster than the water alone would cause.
- **The “outsider” effect:** You’ll be watching the garden through a mask, breathing a foreign gas, never truly part of its fluid lung. The plants will likely react to your exhaust chemistry with a subtle, system-wide curling. Noticeable. Aesthetic crime.

I strongly recommend **Option A or B** for anyone who wants to understand the place rather than merely survive it.

### 3. Thermal and physical protection

The water is 27°C—tropical, but your body still sheds heat roughly 25 times faster in water than in air. After an hour without insulation, you’ll start shivering; after two, you’ll lose dexterity. Yet the space is intimate and the vegetation densely packed, so bulky neoprene is a liability.

**The ideal suit:** A 2mm full-body skinsuit made of hydrophobic, low-friction elastomer (something like Yamamoto 38) with titanium-lined fabric panels over the spine and chest to reflect body heat. No hood—let your scalp feel the fluid and the occasional leaf-touch. The suit should be pale gray or moss-green to blend visually; avoid stark black or neon, which will look garish in that cathedral light. Gloves are mandatory because you’ll grip diamond-tread rungs and might brush metal edges. Go for thin (1mm) Kevlar-palmed gardening gloves that preserve fine motor control.

**Boots or fins?** You won’t be kicking powerfully—the goal is a languid drift. Small, split-toe fins that slip on like socks are perfect. They allow a gentle sculling kick that won’t snag on vines. The central spiral staircase provides handholds; you can pull yourself downward rung by rung if the foliage permits. Avoid dangling straps, loose D-rings, or anything that can hook a tendril. Every piece of gear should be streamlined, clean, and taped down.

### 4. Access logistics: getting in from the roof of the world

The Green Cylinder sits atop a hollowed mountain peak. That means you’ll likely need to:

- **Reach the summit conventionally.** Depending on the world, this could involve a day’s alpine climb. Pack mountaineering layers, crampons, an ice axe if seasonal, and a portable altimeter. The air outside is thin; if the peak exceeds 4,000 metres, plan for an acclimation day at a mountain hut or use a supplemental O2 system for the final push.
- **Find the entry airlock.** Buried in the rock or nestled under a small dome at the crown, there should be a maintenance vestibule—a small, pressurised room that bleeds into the liquid column. Expect a tight cylindrical passage, perhaps 1.2 metres wide, with a hatch at both ends. You’ll stage here.
- **Stage your gear.** In the vestibule, you can ditch warm-weather gear and don your biomechanical suit. If using Option B, assemble and test the rebreather; if Option A, your diffuser port should already be active. The airlock will flood gradually with the same synthetic fluid, equalising pressure. Once it’s full, the inner hatch opens directly into the chamber. There is no splash.
- **Time your entry.** There’s no rush, but the chamber’s logic might register your entry and start a biosampling protocol. That’s fine; just don’t panic when the leaves lean closer.

### 5. Maintaining orientation and sanity

Once inside, you are a mote in a vertical green throat. The monotony is deliberate, and it will eat your sense of time.

**Mandatory tools:**

- **Dive computer with a “sanity timer.”** Set it to show elapsed time, depth, and a configurable soft alarm every 15 minutes. Program a gentle vibrotactile reminder to check your air/loop status and your own mental state. Without it, you could drift for what feels like minutes but is hours.
- **A personal locator beacon with multiple modes.** Ultrasonic pinger for finding your way back to the airlock; surface relay buoy (deployable from the airlock) to call for outside assistance if the summit weather turns or your gear fails.
- **A small, dimmable dive light** mounted on your wrist. Not for illumination—the ambient light is sufficient—but to “paint” details: the phosphorescent latticework on the central column, the three-fingered vine clamps, the extrusion printers at the base. The light should be a warm spectrum, never cool white, to avoid stress-flaring the biofilters.
- **A spine-mounted camera module** with ambient microphone and accelerometer. You’ll want to capture the slow choreography of the fronds without holding anything. Let the camera run passively; the footage later will be hypnotic.

**Psychological prep:** Practice a simple floating meditation beforehand—lie in a warm, dark floatation tank if possible. Familiarise yourself with the sensation of no visual reference, no sound, and the strange myoclonic jerks your body will do when you start to drift off. The Green Cylinder is kinder than a sensory-deprivation tank because of the constant leaf-touch and the rotating light, but the transition can still trigger mild disorientation. I recommend bringing a small, waterproof music player loaded with a single loop of soft ambient tones at 50 Hz to match the pump frequency. You won’t need to play it—the chamber is its own music—but just in case the silence becomes oppressive, having that option is a mental safety net.

### 6. Chemical and biological decency

This ecosystem is young, freshly installed, and exquisitely balanced. You are a walking contamination vector. Prepare accordingly:

- **Pre-dive cleansing:** In the 24 hours before entry, shower with a perfume-free, biodegradable soap. Avoid scented deodorants, lotions, or insect repellent. No caffeine or alcohol in your system—metabolites will ooze through your skin and perturb the leaves’ chemoreceptors.
- **Gear cleansing:** Soak your suit, fins, and all accessories in a mild vinegar solution (5%) for 30 minutes, then rinse with distilled water. Don’t use bleach or strong disinfectants; residues will kill the microbial mats on the moss.
- **Ingress checklist:** Check for loose hairs, skin flakes, or lint. Wear a thin neoprene skull cap to contain shedding. Your diffuser port or mask should be sealed with medical-grade adhesive if there’s any chance of leakage.
- **Excreta management:** If you’re staying more than a couple of hours, you’ll need a catheter and a faecal containment bag. The chamber’s nutrient loop is not designed to process mammalian waste, and introducing it would cause an algae bloom that could take months to reset. Plan for this with clinical precision.
