To walk the Rimeplate is to walk a threshold—not merely of geography, but of reality itself. If you stand now at the portal’s edge, reading this in some waystation between worlds, understand that this is not a travelogue. This is a survival narrative folded into a packing list, a ritual taking the shape of advice. The Plateau does not tolerate bravado or haste; it unmakes both. I have walked the green, and I am still walking it in some measure, because a piece of that quiet has colonized my bones. Listen.

## I. Before You Go: The Calibration of Self

The Rimeplate will press itself against your mind long before it presses against your soles. Its silence has a weight, its cold a kind of patience. Preparation begins weeks before departure.

**Slow your metabolism.** The Plateau’s rhythm is not yours. Ease yourself toward it: wake before dawn and sit in stillness for an hour. Walk slowly, even indoors, placing each foot with intention. Reduce your spoken words by half. Let meals become deliberate acts. This is not meditation for its own sake; it is a survival technique. On the Rimeplate, haste is a foreign concept not because it’s impractical, but because it invites the erosion. The land frays where rushed minds tread. The locals move as if each step is a negotiation with space. You will, too.

**Acclimate to thin air.** The atmosphere at that altitude is a ghost of itself. If your homeworld has mountains, spend a fortnight at high elevation and sleep in hypoxic tents. If not, use a gradual pressure-chamber regimen. The body must learn to extract meaning from less. Upon arrival, expect a perpetual faint breathlessness, a lightness in the head that can become existential if ignored. I took measurements: oxygen partial pressure hovers near 12% effective sea-level equivalent. Humans can adapt—the locals are proof—but they’ve had generations. Bring diamox if you trust your pharmaceuticals across dimensional variance; otherwise, let acclimatization be your first deliberate act.

**Study the hymnal of cord-knots.** The people of the scattered settlement communicate with yodeled calls, but their memory is kept in intricate knots. You cannot learn their language in full, but you can memorize three critical cords: the traveler’s greeting (a figure-eight with two nested loops), the ward against dead blooms (a spiral with a hanging tail), and the call for shelter (a square knot with one deliberately frayed end). Practice them until your fingers can form them in the dark, in the cold, with numb hands. I will describe them in detail later.

**Calibrate your sense of time.** The diurnal cycle on the Rimeplate is an unreliable clock. Mid-morning cracks and chromatic oozings aside, the duration of day and night can stretch or compress subtly. Do not trust mechanical watches—they may run true, but your perception will warp. Instead, carry a small hourglass of fine, heavy sand, and the first thing you do on arrival is calibrate it against the locals’ own markers (they use water-drip cycles from specific moss patches). You will learn to feel time in your tissues; until then, the hourglass is your anchor.

## II. What to Wear: The Armor of Moisture

The cold is not the enemy; the damp is. The Rimeplate hovers just above freezing, and its moisture remains liquid through some quirk of mineral salts and reality-stress. Water here is lazier than in most worlds; it beads, drips, soaks, but does not crystallize. This means it will find every seam. Your clothing must be a fortress against wet penetration while breathing enough to prevent sweat—because wet underlayers will kill you slowly.

**The layering system, in order of proximity to skin:**

1. **Base layer: silkweight wool with ceramic-infused fibers.** You want a material that pulls moisture away but retains the faintest thermal bloom. I recommend a hooded union suit from the ateliers of the Loomwrights’ Bazaar (they trade across seven dimensions, and their merino-silk blend is unmatched). Avoid pure synthetics; they grow clammy and can irritate skin already stressed by the thin air.

2. **Mid-layer: woven algae-wool with accordion stretch panels.** The Plateau’s chill seeps into marrow. Your mid-layer must trap heat even when compressed. The locals use moss-cloth, which is astonishingly insulating but bulky. A tailored jacket of thick, worsted yarn, with knitted panels at elbows and knees for mobility, strikes the balance. Have it lined with a slick taffeta so it slides under the outer layer without catching.

3. **Outer layer: oiled moss-fiber parka and trousers, or a dimensional equivalent.** This is where you meet the fog. The outer shell must shed the freezing mist like a duck’s back. The traditional Rimeplate material is moss-cloth rubbed with a rendered plant-wax that smells faintly of petrichor and old stone. You can trade for a set upon arrival—the locals will part with it for small gifts—but if not, bring a garment of heavyweight waxed cotton or a breathable waterproof laminate (look for a hydrostatic head above 25,000 mm). Ensure the hood is voluminous enough to accommodate a mask, with a wired brim to shape it against the windless drift.

4. **Additional: a hip-length insulated over-tunic of succulent pith-fiber.** The locals wear these as their outermost garment when stationary. The pith traps thousands of tiny air pockets and is strangely warm for its weight. It is not waterproof, so it goes over the parka only when settled. I will teach you how to make a crude one if you cannot trade.

**For extremities:**

- **Hands:** Two-part system. Thin merino liners, then over-mitts of densely felted moss-cloth with a leather palm for handling cold stone. The mitts should have a slit at the base of the fingers so you can momentarily free your digits for delicate knot-work.
- **Feet:** Vapor-barrier socks. Yes, that old arctic trick. Wear a thin wool sock, then a waterproof-breathable liner (a turkey bag works in a pinch, but better a shaped silicone membrane), then a heavy wool outer sock. This prevents sweat from saturating your insulation and refreezing. Boots: unlined kiss leather, oiled to black, with a sap-based rubber sole for grip on frost-glazed moss. The moss is springy but treacherously slick after fog.
- **Head and face:** The locals are never seen without masks. Theirs are carved of succulent wood, lined with moss-fiber, with narrow slits for eyes and a small breathing port that filters air through a maze of felt. You will make your own from softer materials: a thick balaclava of wool with a separate nose-and-mouth piece that can be pulled aside for drinking. Carry a spare mask for the fog season. The air itself is not toxic, but breathing the full force of the chill directly will cough you raw in hours, and the whispered voices from dead blooms are said to cling to exposed lips. I don’t know if that’s superstition. I wore the mask.
- **Eyes:** The light is pale and high-clouded, but the ice-encrusted towers glint with a thousand tiny prisms. On the rare sunny intervals, snow blindness is a risk. Bring spectacles with side shields, tinted a gentle rose-smoke. In fog, switch to clear lenses.

**Sleeping system:** A bag rated for -10°C will not suffice because the humidity robs insulation of its power. Use a synthetic fill (moisture-resistant) in a waterproof-breathable shell. Sleep on a closed-cell foam pad, thick, with a reflective facing. The moss itself is a mattress, but its own moisture will soak through your groundsheet overnight. Add a vapor barrier liner inside the bag to prevent your body’s vapor from condensing in the fill.

## III. Sustenance: Eating in a World Without Animals

The Rimeplate is lush but offers no flesh. No birds, no insects, no fish in its shallow pools—only vegetation that has learned to thrive on fog and mineral dust. You cannot hunt. You must bring your own food or learn to eat as the locals do.

**Packable rations:** High-fat, freeze-dried meals designed for cold, oxygen-poor environments. Pemmican is ideal; it provides dense energy and does not freeze solid. In your multiverse kit, include vacuum-sealed blocks of bison-berry pemmican, or its synthetic equivalent. Supplement with oatcakes, nut butters in squeezable tubes, and protein powders that can be mixed into tea. Bring a small stove—a liquid-feed pressurized model that burns white gas or a multifuel, because canister stoves sputter in the thin air. Boiling water is not just for meals; it is your primary weapon against the creeping chill.

**Local foraging, with extreme caution:** The starchy pith of succulents is edible and sustaining, but only once properly processed. The locals use stone grinders to crush the pith, then leach it in cold water (repeatedly) to remove a bitterness that numbs the tongue and, I suspect, mildly hallucinogenic alkaloids. I will teach you to identify the safe towers: those with seven vertical facets and a bluish tinge. Water-buds are translucent globes that hang in clusters under low-lying vine canopies; they pop into a refreshing, slightly viscous liquid, a natural isotonic. Harvest them by cupping, not plucking, to avoid damaging the vine.

**The moss-tea rite:** This is more than refreshment; it is a stabilizer. The locals brew a bitter, warming tea from a specific moss—look for the emerald tufts with rust-colored spore stalks. Boil water, infuse a handful of torn moss for the span of a fifty-count, and drink slowly. It warms the core and, critically, seems to anchor perception. Shamans use a stronger decoction during their rituals. I don’t pretend to understand the mechanism; perhaps the biochemistry interacts with the frayed reality. What matters: drink it regularly, as the locals do. I will leave you a small pouch of dried starter moss.

**Water:** The puddles are safe to drink after breaking the ice skin, but they taste of ancient stone and faintly of the other skies. Filter or boil if you’re uncertain. I always did. The moisture in the air can be harvested passively; spread a mesh and let the fog condense on it, funneling into your bottle. This water tastes purer.

## IV. The Ground Itself: Navigating the Erosion

This is where most travelers fail. The Rimeplate is not a static geography; it is a slow, breathing consensus that occasionally forgets itself. Maps are useless, landmarks treacherous, and compasses… my compass needle spun lazily in all directions until I learned the trick: you must first place a small piece of iron-rich schist on a flat moss-pad and let it orient itself to the plateau’s subtle magnetic grain. It takes hours. Better to navigate like the locals.

**The cardinal rule: slow down.** Distance warps when you rush. A journey that took an hour may take two on the return, or the terrain may shuffle. The locals’ pace is a kind of ongoing negotiation. Step, settle, breathe, step. Do not run, even if you see a dead bloom opening nearby. Walk with a rolling footfall, feeling the moss’s give. The moss itself has a grain—the direction its tiny leaf-shoots lean—and that grain points to the west and the high glaciers. It is the most reliable compass you will find.

**Waymarking in a shifting land:** Bring a hundred small stakes of inert material (adamantine is best, but carved basalt works). As you walk, place one every hundred paces. Look back: if they appear to have moved, stop. Retrace your steps slowly until the arrangement stabilizes. The locals use stacks of three stones, each oriented to a specific moss-growth axis. I’ll teach you to build a traveler’s cairn: a flat base stone aligned to the moss-grain, a middle stone tilted at the angle of the noonday sun, and a top stone pointing to the settlement you’re aiming for. The cairns hold their meaning even as the world shifts, because they reference local constants.

**Fog navigation:** The freezing fog arrives silently and robs you of sight beyond arm’s length. In fog, do not walk. Sit down, face the direction you were traveling, and wait. If you hear the moss-hum growing louder, hum back. The vibration seems to echo off the succulent towers; you can learn to sense their proximity like a bat. The locals yodel during fog to keep in contact. Learn the short-range travel call: a rising tone of two notes, the second held. If you hear an answering call, move toward it slowly.

**Pools and dead blooms—absolute avoidance:** Shallow pools whose surfaces reflect an elsewhere sky are marked by rings of dark pebbles. Do not look into them; if you inadvertently see a reflection, look away immediately and place your hand on your face, covering your eyes, for a count of twenty. A reflection can reach back. Dead blooms are worse: holes in space that appear on succulents, about the size of a fist, exuding a darkness that whispers. The locals mark them with stacked stones (three in a column, with a notch). Do not investigate. Do not listen closely. If you hear a voice calling your name, do not answer. Walk away at a right angle to the bloom’s orientation—the locals say the whispers project along a line, so breaking that line is safety.

**Spatial anomalies and “frays”:** The sky sometimes cracks with chromatic light, and a soft shimmer oozes before sealing. When this happens, stop and crouch low to the moss. The land is momentarily less solid under you, and standing upright can cause a sense of falling. Perform the small stability gesture the shamans taught me: touch your forehead, then the moss, then your lips, in a slow triad. It is not magic; it is a psychophysical cue to re-sync your proprioception with the plateau’s. It helps.

## V. The People: A Filigree of Breath

The human presence on the Rimeplate is sparse beyond imagination—thousands scattered across an area the size of an ocean. Encountering anyone is a matter of patience and luck. When you do, your survival may depend on a few key protocols.

**First contact:** If you see a bundled figure in the distance, do not approach directly. Find a slightly elevated hummock, stand still, and make the traveler’s greeting cord-knot: a figure-eight with both ends passing through the central intersection to create two nested loops. Hold it at chest height. Wait. If they respond, they will raise a similar cord or give the yodeling call. Approach slowly, mask on, hands visible.

**Trade and gifts:** The locals have no metal, no written language, yet their minds are not primitive. They value precision and patience. Good gifts: fine stone blades (obsidian or flint from elsewhere), small mirrors (they cherish these but use them only indoors, never near pools), glass beads, and most of all, new cord material—vibrantly dyed sinew or thread. Do not offer electronics, weapons, or anything that disrupts their rhythm. They might not refuse, but the shaman will see you as a fray-agent. In exchange, they will offer moss-cloth, pith-bread, and guidance.

**Shamans and rituals:** The shamans perform daily rites to reinforce the consensus of space and time. You may witness them tracing patterns in the air with carved schist, humming in keys that vibrate the moss. Do not interrupt. If invited, you may sit at the edge of the circle and hum the tonic note if you can find it. Participating in their rituals, even as an observer, seems to confer a protective alignment. I slept better after.

**Taboos:** Never look into a pool. Never touch a dead bloom. Never step on a ritual cairn. Never rush. Never speak loudly or make abrupt gestures; the reality up here is thin enough that shock waves may linger. They believe that the plateau is a dream memory of the world’s youth, and that insolence will wake it badly. They might be right.

**Communication:** Their yodeling calls follow the terrain. The three essential calls:

1. **“I am here” (presence call):** A long, low note that rises slowly in pitch, then cuts off. Used when stationary in fog.
2. **“Approach in peace” (greeting):** Two short notes, a descending minor third, with a vibrato at the end. Repeat twice.
3. **“Danger: dead bloom” (warning):** A sharp, high staccato of three notes, like an alarm. The stone-stacking will begin.

Practice these in the thin air before arriving. Your voice will crack; let it. The effort is what matters.

## VI. Psychological Armor: The Long Green Silence

I can’t overstate this: the quiet is not empty. It is filled with vegetation breathing, water dripping, the subsonic moss-hum, and the slow heave of the land. At first, it is a relief. By day three, the absence of animal noise becomes a presence in itself. By day ten, you may start hearing things that are not there, or perhaps that are there but should not be listened to. The mind, deprived of familiar noise, begins to feed on itself.

**Bring a sound anchor:** A small music box, a harmonica, or a recorded book in a shielded player. Do not use headphones constantly—you need to hear the environment—but during rest periods, let a familiar voice or melody tether you. I carried a tiny wooden flute and played simple sequences, not to entertain but to remind myself that I existed as a distinct being.

**Journaling in cord-knots:** The locals record history in knotted cords. Try this. Take a length of cord and, each evening, tie your day’s events using simple motifs: a square knot for a camp made, a series of half-hitches for distance traveled, a figure-eight for conversation. The act of translating experience into tactile knots grounds you in the present and creates a durable record that doesn’t rely on fragile ink or fading memory. I still have my cord-journals; I can read them in the dark by feel alone.

**Beware the lure of the pools and blooms:** The whispers from dead blooms and the reflections in pools are not just auditory or visual; they seem to tug at some deeper sense of belonging. You may feel an urge to look, to answer, to step through. Acknowledge it, but do not indulge. I used a mental mantra: *“This world is thin, but I am thick. I am a full vessel. I do not leak.”* Sounds foolish. It worked.

**Loneliness protocol:** If you find yourself spiraling into existential dread under the endless green, perform the shamans’ body sweep. Sit cross-legged on the moss, close your eyes, and slowly touch each part of your body with deliberate pressure: feet, knees, hips, chest, shoulders, head. Then touch the moss and tell yourself, “This is real. I am real. The fog will pass.” The philosophical question of whether you are real is less important than the act of asserting it.

## VII. Example Kit Manifest

Here is precisely what I carried, and what I recommend:

**Pack:** Frameless backpack, 60 liters, made of waxed canvas with a waterproof liner. Multiple external lashes for wet tarps.

**Clothing (spare set in dry bag):**  
- 2 silkweight wool union suits (one worn, one spare)  
- 2 algae-wool mid-layer tops and bottoms  
- 1 oiled moss-fiber parka with hood  
- 1 waxed cotton overtrousers  
- 1 insulated pith-fiber tunic (acquired locally)  
- 3 pairs vapor-barrier socks and 4 pairs wool socks  
- 2 pairs insulated mitts, 3 liner gloves  
- 2 balaclavas, 2 nose-mouth masks  
- 1 pair smoke-tinted glacier glasses, 1 clear set

**Footwear:** Tall leather boots, double-tongue, with removable felt liners and sap-rubber soles. Bring spare liners.

**Shelter/sleep:**  
- 4-season tent with strong pole architecture (free-standing, because moss stakes poorly)  
- -18°C synthetic sleeping bag with vapor-barrier liner  
- Closed-cell foam pad, full-length, plus a thin inflatable for comfort  
- Reflective emergency blanket (the moss-hum can be dampened by metallic surfaces)

**Cooking:**  
- Liquid-fuel stove, 1 liter fuel bottle, maintenance kit  
- 1.5-liter titanium pot with lid, nesting mug, spork  
- Water filtration pump and backup chemical tablets (iodine neutralized with ascorbic acid)  
- Thermos (pre-fill with moss tea for the day)

**Navigation & safety:**  
- Small hourglass, calibrated to local cycles  
- 100 basalt or ceramic stakes, with reflective tape  
- Cord for knot-recording (50 meters paracord, plus a spool of colored sinew for gifting)  
- Compass (with piece of local schist packed separately)  
- Whistle with a tone that mimics the yodel call near enough  
- Small mirror (for signaling, never for checking reflection)  
- First-aid kit: emphasis on wound closure strips (the green moss has antiseptic properties, but you need to keep cuts clean), blister care, altitude sickness meds, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and a splint material.

**Psychological/trade:**  
- Music box or flute  
- Sketchbook and charcoal (paper is fine; the damp will not destroy it if kept in a sealed tube)  
- Gifts: obsidian arrowheads, glass beads, dyed cords, small carvings  
- A single adamantine stake (for extreme reality-anchoring; drive it into the moss and hold the loop if the world begins to seriously unravel—I never had to use it, but I felt better having it)

## VIII. Departure: How to Leave Without Taking the Rimeplate With You

When you are ready to return to the portal or move on, do not simply vanish. The locals perceive abrupt disappearances as fray-events, and they may cause reality to tear further. I advise a slow leave-taking:

1. Inform the nearest hermit or shaman that you will “walk into the western fog and not return.” They understand this as a traveler’s dissolution.
2. Build a departure cairn: four stones in a diamond shape, with your traveler’s cord tied around the center stone, and break a small water-bud over it to wet the moss.
3. Walk away at the shamans’ pace, maintaining the moss-grain alignment, until the settlement is out of sight.
4. Activate your travel mechanism only when alone and when no anomalies are visible.

Once home, you may find that your pace remains slow for weeks. The quiet may have seeded itself in you. That is not a wound; it is a souvenir.

Prepare like someone stitching a wound in the sky. The Rimeplate is fragile and indifferent and profoundly alive. It will test you not with challenges but with a slow, steady press, like the hand of an ancient sculptor smoothing away your sharp edges. Travel well.
