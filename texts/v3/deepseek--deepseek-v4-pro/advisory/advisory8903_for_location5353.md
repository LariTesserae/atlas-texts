Standing at the edge of the Throat of the Mender, leaning on whatever walking stick you’ve brought, you will feel something very specific: the quiet erosion of your own sense of scale. Preparing for that feeling is as important as packing for the physical dangers, because the Throat doesn’t kill you with a single blow. It kills you by making you forget you are a visitor, and not part of its slow, soaking metabolism.

Here is how to prepare.

---

### I. On the Metabolism of Light

The sky here is a single, unchanging bruise. There is no sun to navigate by, no stars at night, just the long gray dimming into the bioluminescent twilight of the lichen. Your circadian rhythm will unravel within days if you let it.

**What to bring:**
- A mechanical watch with a luminescent dial, not digital. The humidity will eat through waterproofing seals on anything electronic within a week, but a self-winding movement, sealed with a sacrificial layer of wax, has a better chance. Wind it twice a day, at "morning" and "evening," whether you feel the difference or not. You will need to impose a day on a place that doesn't have one.
- Heavy, amber-tinted goggles. The perpetual gray light will strain your eyes and the bioluminescent shimmer at night plays tricks on depth perception. The amber tint cuts the blue-green haze and gives edges back to the world. Keep them on more than you think you need to.

**Mental practice:**
In the weeks before traveling, learn to sleep in shifts. Polyphasic sleeping isn't just for productivity; it's a defense against a world where you can't trust the sky to tell you when to rest. Practice waking after four hours, being alert for two, sleeping again for three. The Throat's "night" is nine hours of deepening dimness. You will need to be awake for some of it, or the dreaming machine will start dreaming through you.

---

### II. The Vertical Architecture of Walking

The ground is never flat. You will be walking on the hexagonal caps of ancient pillars, each tilted at a fraction of a degree, each coated in a living patina that has the friction of soap. A fall here is not necessarily into a void — the vegetation is so dense you might simply be caught in a net of branches, dangling a thousand meters above the rumored cloud-filled gorges, slowly suffocated by the spore-laden air while the silk-furred browsers watch with slow, low-frequency curiosity.

**What to bring:**
- **Footwear more important than any other item.** Forget waterproof boots. You need boots that grip wet basalt as if it were dry sandstone. The soles should be soft, high-surface-area rubber, the kind used for canyoneering. They will wear down in a month. Bring three pairs, and a repair kit of liquid rubber that cures in the presence of moisture — it's one of the few modern materials that will set in this humidity.
- **Gaiters and a full-body approach suit.** Not for warmth, but for the insects. The mosquito-like things here don't just bite; they deposit a biofilm under your skin that, within days, starts to fluoresce with the same faint script as the lichen. It's not immediately harmful, but it marks you as part of the system. The local fauna *notices*.
- **A hummingbird harness.** A minimal climbing harness, slim enough to wear all day, with a small carabiner and fifteen meters of a line woven from the same carbon filament you'll see in the ancient platforms. You will use it not for climbing cliffs, but for making yourself fast while you sleep. The vegetation moves. Slowly, but it moves.

**What not to bring:**
Bungee cords, climbing rope with any elasticity. The constant wet will rot the rubber core, and a snap-back in this terrain could tumble you into a pool of those prism-skinned eels. Their bite transfers something that makes your own skin refract light for years, a beacon of wrongness in the lower worlds.

---

### III. The Air as Sustenance and Threat

You will taste metal with every breath. That's the spores, and something else — an atomized residue from the dormant tech, a silvery particulate that glitter if you exhale hard through a beam of torchlight. It's not acutely toxic, but your lungs don't know how to clear it.

**What to bring:**
- **A stacked filtration mask.** Not a single filter, which will clog in half a day. You need a modular system: a pre-filter of oiled wool (from an animal that lives in humid climates, if you can find one — muskox qiviut is ideal, but felted alpaca works) that you can change out several times a day. Behind that, a charcoal layer, replaced weekly. Behind that, a final electrostatic layer, scavenged from clean-room supplies, for the fine metal particulate. The mask should cover your nose and mouth entirely. Leave your eyes to the goggles.
- **A tongue scraper of pure silver.** Use it every "morning." The biofilm that grows in your mouth here, fed by the ambient bacteria, will coat your teeth in a resilient gray film if you don't. Silver doesn't tarnish in this atmosphere; it's one of the few things that stays clean.

**Mental practice:**
Learn to breathe slowly through your nose, even while exerting yourself. Panting floods your lungs with unfiltered air. Practice a breathing pace that matches your steps: four steps in, six steps out. It will feel like meditation. It is. The Throat rewards slow rhythms of the body; it might even quiet the insect drone to match you.

### IV. Water and the Principle of Non-Absorption

Water is everywhere, and none of it is safe as it is. The skin-warm pools, the dripping seams, the fog itself — all are alive with the iridescent bacteria. Boiling doesn't always kill the shimmer. Some of it is a spore that survives heat and colonizes your gut, producing a permanent, faint bioluminescence visible through your abdominal wall. It's uncomfortable more than deadly, a constant low ache of light.

**What to bring:**
- **A water processing system that uses ultraviolet light and physical sieving, not just boiling or chemical pills.** The tech here dismantles complex molecules more thoroughly than any fire. You are looking for a ceramic filter with pores smaller than 0.1 microns, followed by a UV-C chamber powered by a hand crank or a very well-sealed battery charged by a micro-hydro turbine you can dangle into a rivulet. Carry a backup chemical treatment — iodine resin — but know it will only work on the water you've already filtered. The visible shimmer will be gone; the invisible spores might not be.
- **A rigid, light-colored water bottle.** You need to *see* the water before you drink it, each time. If it has even a faintly oily sheen, re-process it.

**A note on the streams:**
Never drink from a rivulet that braids through the "cushions of moss" without checking what kind of moss. If it's the liverwort that feels like tongues, the water picks up an alkaloid that causes vivid, shared hallucinations. Your group will start speaking in the fragments of the dead language, and you will understand each other, but you will wake with your tongues blackened and the lichen's script written into your skin, and you will have told the landscape something you didn't mean to share. The other moss, the one that looks like tiny green feathers, is safe after filtration, its water slightly sweet, almost nutritious. Learn the difference by touch before you travel. Practice on liverworts and feather mosses from any humid world; the textures are consistent across dimensions.

### V. The Relics: What to Touch, What to Avoid

The scattered tech is patient, yes. But it is not inert. It recognizes the biology of the world it's embedded in — the slow-turning machine of ecology and leftover design — and you are not part of that design until you've been submerged in it for a very long time.

**Rules of engagement:**
- **Never press your bare palm against a smooth slab that thrums.** That thrum is an induction charge, and it will map your nervous system in seconds. After that, a doorway-shimmer might open to somewhere beneath your conscious understanding, and you might step through it without meaning to, returning hours later with no memory and a new preference for dampness and dim light.
- **The carbon-filament cables are safe to touch if they are not humming.** They are unbelievably strong and light; you can use a broken piece as a sling. But if a cable is vibrating with a subsonic note, even slightly, it's bearing a load in some physics you don't operate on. Don't clip into it. Don't lean on the platform it holds.
- **The staircase in the waterfall repels moss for a reason.** Walk up it if you need to reach the shimmering doorway. The stairs feel normal underfoot, almost dry. The doorway itself will show you a slightly more orderly version of the landscape — the pillars will be perfectly hexagonal, the trees will grow in grids. Don't step through unless you are prepared to never return to your own physics. The orderliness is a trap; it's easier to live in, and you will not want to come back to the chaotic, rotting lushness of the Throat.
- **The crystalline spheres from the weeping machines are valuable.** They resonate with certain frequencies, and can be used to power certain devices, or traded in the outposts between worlds. Collect them if they are fully hardened and detached. If they are still soft and attached by a viscous thread, pulling them off will make the structure from which they weep send out a pulse of coherent light, and for days afterward, the fauna will watch you more intently, cataloging your movements.
- **The mossy knobs that rotate for the birds.** Do not sit on them, even if they look like stools. The birds land there because they are part of the signal network. Your weight will not harm the knob, but the signal it sends will be one of alarm, and the entire lichen-script across the local colony will dim. In the dimming, things that normally stay in the cloud-filled gorges might rise to investigate.

### VI. Campsites and the Long Twilight

You will need to sleep, eventually. The perpetual drizzle means you need a shelter, but the terrain resists tent stakes. The vegetation will grow over your shelter's guy lines within a single sleep cycle, tethering you.

**The best campsites:**
- **Under the overhangs of the basalt columns, where the "cages of living wood" have collapsed.** The fallen tree-cages create a natural lattice, dry at the core, smelling of slow-decaying heartwood and the faint algorithm-glow of lichen. It's as safe as anywhere.
- **Never camp in the shallow basins that pocket the plateau.** The water there is skin-warm and filmed over, and the eels with their prism-skin emerge soundlessly at night to wrap around anything warm. They don't feed on you. They feed on your dreams, literally — they siphon the electrochemical activity of your sleeping brain, leaving you with a profound, hollow emptiness that takes days to refill. You'll know if they've been there by the fractured rainbows on your eyelids in the morning.
- **Hammocks are better than ground beds, but they must be hung from the epiphyte curtains only if you've checked the branches above.** Those orchids that exhale visible vapor? They breathe in carbon dioxide and breathe out a narcotic mist. Sleeping beneath a dense cluster will drop you into a sleep so deep you might not wake for days, and when you do, your own breath will be visible for hours, and you'll crave nectar.

**Your sleep system:**
- A hammock made of a material that doesn't wick moisture — silicone-impregnated nylon is adequate, but the local fibers might be better. The silk-furred quadrupeds shed fibers that can be woven into a rope that refuses to rot. If you can trade for some before you arrive, do so.
- A very thin, very warm breathable insulation layer. The heat is oppressive during the "day," but in the long twilight, it drops to something that feels cold because of the wetness. A reflective emergency blanket is loud and crinkly, which is useful; it alerts you to things moving across it.
- Earplugs. Not just for the insect drone and the tonal wind through the pillars, but for the subsonic hum of the tech. That frequency, over days, will subtly shift your emotional state, making you feel that everything is perfectly fine, that you've always been here, that there's no hurry to leave. You will stop policing your water. You will take off your mask. The earplugs don't block the vibration through your skeleton, but they slow the persuasion.

### VII. Food and Foraging

Bring all your calories. Do not think of this place as a lush garden you can eat from. The red waxy-leaved shrubs produce a berry that is edible and tastes like overripe figs, but it's so rich in the metal particulate that it will coat your teeth in a permanent gray sheen and make your blood detectable to the shimmering eels from a kilometer away. The thread-thin eels themselves are edible if you catch them, but their meat is faintly bioluminescent and will make your own tissues glow for days, turning your iris into a faint ring of light. If you need to be invisible in any other world after this, don't eat the fauna.

**Exceptions:**
- The epiphyte bromeliads hold small ponds in their leaf bases. If you filter this water, it's the cleanest source, because it hasn't touched the basalt yet. A single large bromeliad can provide a day's drinking water.
- There is a fungus that grows only on the aerial roots of the tree-cages. It's a pale, dense shelf fungus, with a texture like dense bread. It is safe to eat, and it tastes like the forest floor smells. It might even help clear the metal from your system. In a true emergency, you can survive on it.

**What to bring:**
Calorie-dense, non-perishable, minimal waste. Dehydrated meals in a hard-sided container, not foil packets that will wick moisture into their seams and spoil. A stove that burns solid fuel or a sealed alcohol gel; the damp wood here will never light. Plan for twice the fuel you think you need; boiling water to safe levels takes longer in the altitude, and you will crave hot food as a bulwark against the grey.

### VIII. The Signal Within the Noise

The landscape doesn't change, but it communicates. The insect chorus shifts register not just with time, but with events. A new structure weeping fluid will change the pitch of the cicada-like chorus for an hour. A rockfall, rare as it is, silences everything for precisely twelve minutes and twelve seconds, a pause so consistent it feels deliberate.

**What to learn:**
Listen for the silences. They are warnings. If the sound stops suddenly, in a wave rippling across the plateau, something is moving that doesn't belong to the usual rhythm. It might be you. It might be something else. In the silence, make yourself small, still, and breathe slowly through your nose. Wait for the drone to return. When it does, you can move again.

**The lichen script:**
Don't stare at it for too long. The repeating dots and dashes are not a language in the human sense, but a kind of positional encoding, describing the shape of the rocks it grows on. If you stare at a patch for more than a few minutes, you'll start to see the pattern everywhere, overlaid on the world, suggesting lines and angles that aren't there. It's a cognitive hazard. Use a camera, if you have one, but don't try to memorize it.

---

### IX. Departure and the Problem of Belonging

The hardest part of the Throat of the Mender is leaving it. The place doesn't want you to go. Not out of malice, but because its whole metabolism is one of gradual reclamation, of making the strange familiar, of weaving everything into the slow, hot, wet equilibrium. You will find yourself thinking, on the third day, that the taste of metal is natural, that the thrum in your teeth is comforting, that you wouldn't mind just sitting on a mossy knob and letting the drizzle run down your back.

**What to bring:**
- A tether to the outside. A small, sealed container with something that does not belong here. A photograph of a desert. A recording of a dry wind. A piece of technology that is utterly mundane and from your origin point — a plastic keychain, a coin, a wrapper for a candy that doesn't exist in this multiverse. Hold it twice a day and remember that the world is large and contains places without constant water.
- A partner, or a voice on a radio. Solitude here is not solitude; the ecosystem is too present, too communicative, and it will fill the space in your mind. If you travel alone, have a check-in schedule with someone on the outside, someone who will notice if your responses become slow, if your voice starts carrying the same pitch as the tone the wind makes through the pillars.

**When you leave:**
Walk away from the plateau edge the way you came, or through a shimmering doorway if you've found one you can calibrate. But be prepared for the withdrawal. The dry air of another world will feel like a wound. Your lungs will ache for the spore-laden moisture. Your skin, used to the constant damp, will flake and crack. You will dream, for weeks, of the script-like glow. This is normal. This is the gift the Throat gives to all travelers who walk through it without being absorbed: a permanent, low-grade yearning for the sound of water on basalt, forever reminding you that somewhere, a dreaming machine is still humming, and a part of you is still there, standing at the edge, listening.
