**Traveler’s Preparatory Guide  
for the Cloistered Plateau**  
*Compiled by the Wayfarers’ Collegium, Department of Non-Urban Environments*

---

### I. Understanding the Destination
Before you pack a single bag, you must internalise the nature of the Cloistered Plateau. It is not merely a wet city; it is a lithic lung, a geology that has become architecture, or perhaps an architecture that has returned to geology. The air is saturated nearly to the point of precipitation, the temperature hovers constantly in a cool band (8–12°C, or 46–54°F), and sound itself behaves strangely—damped by mist, amplified by stone, stretched into rhythmic drips that can, after days, become either a solace or an unravelling. Every surface is slick, every crevice exhales mist, and nothing you carry will stay dry unless it is sealed with an obsessiveness that edges on paranoia.

The Plateau hosts no human-friendly flora or fauna; there is nothing to forage, no wood for fire, no dry tinder anywhere. It is functionally sterile from a survivalist’s perspective, yet teems with a mineral biology—liverworts, crustose lichens, sightless shrimp, swallows that gulp midges from the fog. This is a world that lives on dissolved stone and patience. Your presence is a thermodynamic insult, and the Plateau will try, slowly, to metabolise you—your heat, your water, your breath—into its equilibrium. Preparedness here is not about conquering but about inserting yourself with minimal disruption and maximal resilience.

---

### II. Physical Conditioning
Travelers often underestimate the toll of static cold. On the Plateau you are rarely exposed to wind, but the combination of 90–100% humidity and temperatures just above freezing mandates that your body will leak heat steadily into the stone. Hypothermia can set in at 10°C if you are damp and stationary. Therefore, a preparatory regimen is recommended in the 6–8 weeks before departure:

- **Cold-water acclimation**: Regularly swim or bathe in cool (15–20°C) water, progressing to brief immersions in unheated water (10°C). This will help your body manage peripheral vasoconstriction more efficiently.
- **Isometric holds and balance training**: The ground is a labyrinth of narrow fissures and damp, sloped runnels. Strengthen ankles, knees, and core to navigate slick, uneven stone. Practice standing on one leg with eyes closed on an unstable surface.
- **Breath control**: The air’s weight and the city’s quiet can induce a sensation of respiratory resistance. Diaphragmatic breathing exercises, square breathing, and meditation will help quell the subtle panic the atmosphere can evoke.

### III. Clothing: The Cocoon System
Forget waterproof breathable membranes—they will fail from saturated ambient moisture alone (the humidity outside will equal the humidity inside your jacket). Instead, adopt a system that remains insulating when wet and dries quickly, but that also protects you from the constant leaching of body heat into the dripping stone.

**Base layer**: Merino wool is non-negotiable for its thermal retention when wet, but also consider insulating synthetics that block the hygric transfer. Avoid cotton entirely.

**Mid-layer**: A vapour barrier (VB) suit worn directly against the skin (urine-proof nylon, silicone-coated silnylon, or VB shirts) will keep your microclimate separate from the Plateau’s. This is counter-intuitive for terrestrial hiking, but in a 99% humidity world, your sweat will not evaporate. The VB layer prevents the endless diffusion of moisture into your insulating layers. Over it, a high-loft fleece or synthetic down puffy can be worn. Crucially, your mid-layer must be easily removable for the rare moments when you climb and generate excess internal heat.

**Outer layer**: A durable, fast-drying softshell that offers zero moisture retention but some abrasion resistance for the frequent brushing against wet, gritty stone. Waterproof hardshells become sweat boxes here; they are useless except if you intend to stand under a saturated drip-point for hours. However, consider bringing a lightweight, non-breathable poncho for sudden “fog-falls,” when hidden openings in the upper levels suddenly release sheets of accumulated water. The poncho can also serve as an emergency shelter.

**Extremities**: Neoprene boots (thigh-high) with excellent grip are ideal for the constant ankle-deep cold streams. Pair them with vapour-barrier socks over thin liner socks. For hands, thin waterproof gloves over liner gloves; the stone is cold enough to numb exposed fingers in minutes. A hood system is critical—long peaked hood to keep drips off your face, but also a large brim or an umbrella hat that can deflect the vertical droplets. Your head will be wet regardless; the goal is to keep the trickle off your skin.

### IV. Shelter and Sleeping System
There are no dry spots. You will not sleep on stone without insulation; the heat sink of a billion tonnes of saturated schist will drain you in an hour. Your sleeping system must be a self-contained island:

- **Sleeping mat**: At least R-value 8 (closed-cell + inflatable). Bring two foam mats if possible.
- **Sleeping bag**: Water-resistant treated down or synthetic, with a vapour-barrier liner bag (VBL) *inside* the sleeping bag. Do not let your body moisture into the insulation. Sleep in a very light base layer, no skin exposed. Bring a balaclava and breathable sleep hood.
- **Bivvy bag**: A fully waterproof, breathable bivvy bag for your bag, or a hammock system if you can find anchor points—but the stone offers no reliable hooks. A small, very light tent with a vapour-tight bathtub floor and a fly that can be pitched without stakes (use rock anchors) is ideal. Remember that condensation will still form on the interior of the fly.

### V. Sustenance: Fuel for the Dripping Silence
The Plateau is a nutritional zero. Pack *everything* you will need, and pack it in waterproof, multi-layered containers. The damp will infiltrate zip-loc bags over days; use heat-sealed mylar bags for long stays.

- **Water**: Paradoxically, though the Plateau is wet, potable water is abundant—the cold springs and weeping walls produce mineral-rich, clear water that is likely safe from organic pathogens (there are no large animals), but it will contain dissolved schist dust and calcium carbonate. Filtering through a 0.1-micron membrane will remove sediment and any trace shrimp larvae. Boiling is impossible without fuel. Pack a reliable mechanical filter (e.g., Katadyn Pocket or MSR Guardian) that is easy to field-clean, as the calcium will clog filters. Also carry a backup chemical treatment for viral contamination, though the risk is near-zero.
- **Food**: Calorie-dense, no-cook rations that need no rehydration (to save fuel) are wisest. The air makes attempted hot meals a steamy, frustrating affair. If you require warmth, a small liquid-fuel stove that can burn at altitude in damp air is the only possibility, but you must carry in all fuel. Do not cook near the stone structures; the soot and flame scar the lichen communities that take centuries to regrow. Instead, rely on cold soaking in a leaking-waterproof container, and only heat with extreme caution.
- **Supplies**: Daily calorie count: 3,500–4,500 kcal due to the cold and the constant low-grade exertion of clambering.

### VI. Navigation and Communication
The Cloistered Plateau is a three-dimensional maze with no distant vistas. Traditional compasses may be unreliable if the schist contains magnetite; my readings from a pre-survey indicated local deviations of up to 15 degrees. Map and memory are primary.

- **Dead-reckoning thread**: Carry a kilo-reel of very thin braided line. Anchor it at the entry point or at designated base points and unspool as you explore. The wetness will coat the line in slime, but it will not damage synthetic core. Use a coloured line and vary ties for branches.
- **Altitude and direction**: Though the Plateau is flat overall, the water flow directions provide a constant gradient—downstream always leads to the great sumps (known, terminal points). Knowing where you entered relative to that gradient is vital.
- **Signal mirrors and sound**: The quiet is so profound that a whistle can carry, but the stone baffles and echoes. Carry a high-pitched whistle and agree on signals.
- **Light**: The perpetual grey twilight means you need constant artificial light in the fissures. A headlamp with spare batteries (or dynamo) and a waterproof casing is essential. Red-light mode to preserve night vision. But be warned, the mist backscatters white light heavily; a focused beam is better. Lasers as directional pointers can be useful for signalling across crevices.
- **Personal locator beacon?** Electronically, there's no tech in the city, but the thick, wet stone may attenuate signals. If your multiverse tech can generate subspace pings, fine, but don’t rely on standard GPS.

### VII. Medical and Psychological
**Physical Hazards**:
- **Trench foot**: Change socks twice daily, dry feet thoroughly, and apply anti-fungal powder daily. Carry a small micro-fibre towel dedicated to feet.
- **Stone abrasions**: The rocks are cauliflower-textured; even a light fall shreds skin. Bring a comprehensive wound-care kit with tegaderm, irrigation syringe, and antibiotics.
- **Respiratory**: The mist carries inorganic dust and organic spores. Wear a light filter mask if you are sensitive. Nocturnal exhalations may bring heavier spore loads.
- **Hypothermia and dehydration**: In 100% humidity, your body still loses water through breathing. The coolness masks thirst. Drink pro-actively.

**Psychological Challenges**:
The Plateau’s silence is not empty—it is absolute and pressing. Many travelers report auditory hallucinations (a phantom hum, a distant voice). This is normal. To mitigate:
- Carry a small audio device with pre-loaded music, or a tiny white-noise maker. The sound of human voices helps anchor you.
- Partner travel is strongly advised. Solo travel demands robust mental health.
- If claustrophobia hits, focus on breathing, and remember the fissures always lead upward and out to the higher plateaus where mist sometimes thins.
- Keeping a journal can ground you. Write in waterproof notebooks with soft-lead pencil (no pen will function consistently, and ink will bleed).

### VIII. Clothing and Gear Resupply Philosophy
“More is less” on the Plateau. The weight of your pack will absorb water from the air; even inside sealed bags, condensation cycles occur. Do not bring leather or cotton. Favor synthetics and treated down. Multi-use items are mandatory. A trekking umbrella, a hiking stick with a carbide tip, a sit-pad (closed-cell foam) — all are invaluable. The UIAA multi-purpose spork works only on soft foods.

Lighten internal seams: the quiet magnifies every rustle, zipper pull, and squeak of fabric. The first few days, every small noise you make will seem a sacrilege. You’ll adapt, but pack soft-shell stuff sacks over crinkly stuff.

---

### IX. Etiquette and Environment
The Cloistered Plateau is not ruins. It is a living system; the crustose lichens and the liverworts, the translucent shrimp, and the unseen chemoautotrophic bacteria are part of the city's metabolism. Do not break off flowstone formations for souvenirs. Do not scratch glyphs into walls, not only because it’s unethical but because the walls are continually growing a skin that will immortalise your mark for centuries until it is overgrown. Your presence leaves mineral traces—footprints compress the biocrust. Limit your group size, travel light, and avoid the deepest sinkhole chambers, which may be ecologically sensitive or unstable.

Human waste must be packed out (the stone does not absorb or process organic matter quickly; it will linger as an alien monument). Portable urine containers, double-bagging, and carry-out practices are mandatory.

---

### X. A Final Exercise
Stand once you arrive. Before exploring, read the water. The dripping is not random; it carries a rhythm, a metabolic signature. Sit for an hour and listen until the silence separates into its component parts. Let your own heartbeat join the syncopation. Then move. You are not a tourist; you are a passing meteorological event. Move with the moisture, not against it. Respect the slow lung. And when the silence becomes too much, speak a word aloud—the stone will echo it, wrap it in moisture, and give it back. That returned sound may be the closest you’ll come to a dialogue with a place that will never notice you existed.

*The Collegium accepts no liability for travelers who ignore these cautions. The Plateau is patient. Be equally so.*
