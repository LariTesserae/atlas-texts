To visit this location, a traveler must prepare on multiple levels: physical, sensory, psychological, and ethical. This is not a ruin to be explored, nor a wilderness to be survived. It is an inhabited, aware, industrialized organism—and you are a foreign body entering its interior. Prepare as you would for a diplomatic mission into a living cathedral-factory, where the architecture itself is your host, your environment, and potentially your surgeon.

### I. Physical Preparation and Gear

**The Principle of Non-Contamination**
The city-organism has no immune system designed for animals; its defenses are chemical and slow. You, however, are a walking biome of bacteria, oils, and shed skin cells that could introduce catastrophic rot. You must enter as close to sterile as possible without damaging your own necessary microbiome.

- **Full-Body Sealed Undergarment:** A thin, breathable but impermeable membrane suit, tailored to your skin, that seals at wrists, ankles, and neck. It should be made of a material that does not off-gas volatile organic compounds—no PVC, no plastisol inks. Medical-grade silicone-coated silk is ideal. This traps your biological effluent and prevents direct skin contact with the city's surfaces.
- **Footwear:** The ground is a springy root-mat, often slick with condensation. You need traction that does not tear the living substrate. Footwear should have soles of soft, untreated natural rubber (crepe rubber) with a tread pattern of fine, rounded nubs, not sharp lugs. These must be donned over your suit feet without compromising the seal. Absolutely no leather, which would be perceived as dead flesh.
- **Gloves:** Thin, conductive-fabric gloves that allow you to feel texture and temperature without depositing oils. The city communicates partly through vascular pressure ripples you can feel with your palms.

**Thermal Management**
The environment is damp and cool, but your exertion will generate heat. The suit must breathe moisture vapor outward while retaining body heat—a directional wicking membrane. A light, hooded outer robe of unbleached, undyed linen or hemp over your suit provides an additional layer that can absorb ambient moisture and be wrung out. The hood protects your head from the incessant, fine rainfall of condensation.

**Respiration**
The air is oxygen-rich from the vast photosynthetic mass, but it carries microparticles of resin, living spores, and the metabolic byproducts of bacterial cultures. Your lungs are not adapted to this.

- **Passive Filtration Mask:** A half-face respirator with organic vapor cartridges and a particulate pre-filter. The cartridges' absorbent material should be activated charcoal derived from coconut shells, not petroleum coke—you want to align your chemical signature, however minutely, with the carbon base of this world. The exhalation valve must be fitted with a diffuser to prevent a focused, hot, dry jet of your breath from hitting the delicate membranes of the city. Your breath should exit as a soft, ambient cloud.

**Hydration and Sustenance**
You bring nothing. You do not eat in the city. You do not open your suit. You fast for a period before arrival to quiet your digestive system. You will be provided for, subtly, or you will be rejected. The city understands symbiosis. To enter with your own packaged food is to declare yourself a parasite or a tumor. Bring a single, empty vessel of blown glass, small enough to fit in a belt pouch. The city knows glass: it is sand transformed by heat, akin to its own resin-to-brick process. You may be permitted to collect a small amount of the syrupy amber fluid as a token, a sacrament, never as a meal.

**Lighting**
The central bioluminescent pod and the diffuse grey canopy-light are constant. You should not bring artificial white or flame-spectrum light—it would be an act of visual aggression. If you need a task light for reading a map or inspecting a detail, it must be a diode of deep red (660nm), the color least disruptive to the chloroplast-driven circadian rhythms and the bacterial glow-cultures. It should be shaded and directed only upon the object of your attention.

**Tools**
No blades, no picks, no sampling corers. You are not a miner or a surgeon. The only permissible metal item is a small, un-lubricated brass set of calipers and a lens, for measurement and observation without touch. Brass is favored for its oligodynamic anti-microbial property; it politely cleans itself.

### II. Sensory and Psychological Preparation

**Re-calibrating Silence and Sound**
You must train yourself to hear drips and creaks not as background noise, but as language. Spend a week in a humidity-controlled greenhouse, listening to irrigation timers and the ticks of expanding wood. Learn to identify the sound of a drop falling on a stretched leaf versus a metal flywheel. In the city, the sizzle of a droplet hitting a warm boiler is a sentence. The subsonic thrum of the engines is the city's resting heartbeat. You will feel it more than hear it. Meditate on low-frequency vibrations daily. Lie on a wooden floor while a muted subwoofer plays sustained tones at 40-60 Hz. Let your bones learn to interpret them as reassurance, not threat.

**Proprioception and Movement**
Walk with deliberate, slow-motion care. Practice "fox-walking"—placing the outer edge of the foot down first, then rolling gently inward until your sole is fully in contact, testing the ground's give. You move through a colonnade, not a corridor; your path is a series of arcs between pillars. Never move in straight lines unless invited. Mimic the spiral staircases. Your posture should be upward, taking cues from the columns: elongated, rooted, and fluid. Do not fidget, do not tap surfaces, do not drum your fingers. Sudden rhythmic impacts are a distress call in a world that communicates through fluid pressure and slow flexing.

**The Absence of Animal Life**
This is the deepest psychological challenge. You are the only creature with a nervous system that fires in rapid spikes. For days before arrival, reduce your exposure to other animals, including humans in motion. Starve your brain's expectation of birdsong, insect buzz, or a scurrying rodent. The drifting spore-things are the only "fauna." When one approaches, remain still. If it contacts you, it will detect your non-plant origin. Its dissolution upon touch is likely an apoptosis programmed by the city's immune chemistry; you have just been gently screened. Do not flinch. Flinching is a shriek in the muscle-language the columns perceive through the ground-vibration.

### III. Knowledge and Language Acquisition

**Fundamental Biopolitics**
Study the visible relationships. You must be able to tell a cultivated epiphyte paving stone from a parasitic mold-patch. The former has a deliberate, geometrical edge; the latter has a fractal, invasive edge. You must recognize the secretion glands that fight the mold—they appear as weeping, waxy lenticels—and never mistake them for damage. Know that the liana-cables are tension members; you do not swing on them or pull them. They are under a pre-calculated stress.

**The Industrial-Symbiotic Grammar**
The machines are organs. The copper boiler is a stomach; the pistons are muscles; the bladder-retort is a liver distillate. The cast-iron troughs are arteries. When you see a tendril emerge from the wall to adjust a valve, recognize this as the city's equivalent of a hand scratching an itch or a tongue licking a wound. The tendril has no eyes, no brain in the animal sense, but it is intelligent with the slow-reaction wisdom of a plant. It will be curious about you, but its timescale is hours, not seconds. Do not attempt to communicate with gestures; instead, if you wish to "speak," press your gloved palm gently against a smooth area of bark near a gauge or valve for a count of one hundred slow breaths. The warmth and the steady pulse of your hand may be logged as a signature. You are introducing yourself.

### IV. The Entry Protocol

Arrival must be gradual. The multiverse portal must not tear open; it must be an osmotic shift. Ideally, the traveler should spend the final hours before the transition in a moist, cool, dimly-lit vestibule chamber that mimics the city's atmosphere. The portal's threshold should be bi-directional, allowing a pre-mingling of the two airs so your lungs begin to adapt before your body crosses. You step through not into the center, but at the edge of the chamber, near a column-base, as if you have coalesced from the condensation itself.

Upon entry, immediately find a broad, flat epiphyte "paver" and stand still. Do not walk forward yet. Let the city sense you. The subsonic hum will shift subtly. Drips may fall with a different rhythm near you. This is the city orienting its localized perception. Your first act should be to cup your gloved hands and hold them out, palms up, to catch the next fine rain of droplets. When the water pools in your palms, let it bead and fall over the sides. You are showing that you accept the city's moisture but do not consume it greedily. This is a gesture of non-threat.

Only when a liana-cable nearest you adjusts its tension with a soft, unusual creak—an acknowledgment—do you begin to explore, moving along the springy floor toward the central hanging lamp, the heart of this echoing nave of green and quiet industry. You are an observer, a foreign symbiote candidate, and your entire preparation is a petition to be allowed to witness, for a short time, from the inside of a being that solved the paradox by never knowing it existed.
