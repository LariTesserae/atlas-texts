To stand inside that hidden forge, you must first become a thing apart from the ordinary world—a sealed, insulated observer, a creature of careful protocols. The pocket does not welcome soft biology; it treats unshielded flesh as raw material to be cured, coated, and eventually incorporated into its slow mineral archive. As an ordinary human who merely steps between realities, your preparation must be absolute.

### 1. The Thermal Exoskeleton
Begin with a full-body, actively cooled suit. Passive insulation alone will murder you within minutes. The ambient temperature near the vent mouth soars past 60°C, and the radiant heat from the fissure can spike well above that in silent pulses. Your suit must be layered: an inner wicking skin, a middle phase-change layer (paraffin microcapsules or a circulating gel network), and an outermost reflective metallic weave—aluminized PBI or a multiverse-equivalent that sheds infrared like a mirror. The cooling system should cycle a low-toxicity refrigerant through capillary tubes sewn against your chest, spine, and femoral arteries. Carry a compact heat-exchange backpack that vents to a surface colder than the rock (a deployable cold-sink plate you place outside the pocket, perhaps, or a short-lived chemical cooling pack). Power it with a dense solid-state battery; bring at least one spare, swapped before saturation.

Your gloves must sacrifice dexterity for survival. Layered aluminized Kevlar over silk liners, with a cooling circuit reaching the fingertips. Boots: thick-soled, with a puncture-proof insole of spring steel or diamond-fiber composite. The floor is a shatter of fresh spalls and needle-crystals—halide salts and sulfur spines that will pierce rubber and leather like a fakir’s bed. The soles should also have a high-friction tread, because every surface carries a microscopic rime of saline dust that feels almost greasy. Gaiters or a full shroud over the boots will prevent spall fragments from slipping inside.

### 2. Breathing in the Impossible
The pocket’s atmosphere is a noxious exhalation: carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide, wisps of hydrogen sulfide, and sharp metal chlorides. There is no oxygen; it’s a smothering cocktail. You need a closed-circuit rebreather, not a simple canister mask. A compressed-air open system would frost your exhalation valve into an instant plug of ice or, worse, react with the sulfur gases to form corrosive acids on the metal surfaces. Instead, wear a full-face mask fed by a micro-scrubber that recirculates oxygen, with a CO₂ absorbent and a chemical filter rated for acid gases. The mask must form a positive-pressure seal to keep out the ever-present drift of mineral dust, shed insect frass, and desiccated bacterial flakes. Test it with a fit machine before you cross the boundary.

Carry a small emergency bail-out bottle of pure oxygen, just enough for a five-minute retreat. The pocket is small—you can physically exit in two steps—but panic in that tight, dark, roaring space can extend seconds into eternities.

### 3. Light and Shadow
The only native illuminations are the vent’s deep merlot glow and the infrequent orange flare of burning hydrogen sulfide. That is not enough for human eyes. Yet ordinary flashlights would be an insult: their sharp beam would bleach the delicate color of the bacterial mats and startle the fragile flies into a panic cloud. Instead, mount a dimmable, red-spectrum headlamp with a diffuser. Run it at the lowest usable setting, or use infrared illuminators paired with night-vision optics built into your mask. This will turn the pocket into a ghostly monochrome landscape without disrupting the life rhythms. Bring a small, shielded UV wand for brief inspection of mineral fluorescence—the halides will sparkle a cold blue—but use it sparingly.

A backup light source, a simple chemical glow-stick wrapped in red cellophane, can reside in a sealed chest pocket.

### 4. Physical Protection Against the Living Carpet
The pocket is crawling with life, but it’s not sterile. The mites, springtails, and sulfur-fly larvae are harmless to you, but their shed skins and dried frass form an airborne organic dust that your suit’s seals and filters must repel. The suit outer layer should be electrostatic-dissipative to reduce dust attraction, but still smooth and non-porous—a silicone-impregnated fabric rather than a fuzzy weave. Every seam, zipper, and closure must be coated with a viscous sealing wax (a low-volatility silicone grease) to prevent fine particulate from creeping into your cooling loop or irritating your skin.

You will also want to protect the pocket from you. Before entering, shower with a mild, fragrance-free microbial cleanser to reduce your shed bacteria, and wear a thin antimicrobial liner that traps skin flakes. The chemoautotrophic mats are the fragile base of this tiny ecosystem; a single exhalation of organic-rich breath could seed a fungal bloom that takes decades to correct. Once inside, resist the urge to touch the walls even with your protected gloves—the crystalline lips are brittle, and a single brush can shatter decades of patient accretion.

### 5. Acoustic Damping and Sonic Discipline
The sound is a layered roar: the vent’s hissing howl, percussive chuffing from collapsing gas pockets, and the low-frequency thrum of distant turbines conducted through the rock. Without protection, the sheer volume will cause physical pain and disorientation within seconds. Inside your sealed helmet, the noise can still resonate through the suit structure and your bones. Use custom-molded earplugs with a flat attenuation of 25–30 dB, and over them, a set of active-noise-cancelling earmuffs tuned to cancel the continuous low-frequency hum. You should still hear the higher chuffs and crackles—they are your only warning of sudden gas releases—so don’t go deaf. Tune the system to pass those sounds through at a safe level.

Your own movements should be slow and deliberate. The ground crunches underfoot; each step compresses a century of mineral dust into a sudden, sharp snap. Move with the patience of a glacier, and you’ll minimize your sonic footprint. If you must communicate, use a throat microphone and a tight-beam short-range radio, not your own voice, which would be swallowed instantly.

### 6. Duration and Hydration
No matter how good your cooling suit, your body is an exothermic machine. The pocket continuously pumps heat into you; you can only reject so much through a portable system. Plan for a maximum stay of thirty minutes core, with a ten-minute safety buffer. Bring a small, insulated drinking tube integrated into your mask—a hypertonic, electrolyte-laden drink, slightly warmed so it doesn’t shock your throat. The air you breathe from the rebreather will be brutally dry, so pre-hydrate with an extra liter an hour before entry. Dehydration accelerates heat stress and mental fog, and you need absolute clarity to navigate the tight space and monitor your gear.

### 7. Psychological Preparation
This is a closet-sized pocket of absolute darkness, screaming sound, and oppressive dry heat—a sensory assault that can trigger primal panic. Meditate on confinement before you go. Practice in a sensory-deprivation tank with high heat and piped-in industrial noise. Program your suit’s heads-up display to show calm, minimal vitals (heart rate, core temp, oxygen partial pressure) and a simple countdown timer. No blinking alarms. Wear a thin, elastic pressure garment under the cooling layer; the constant hug can soothe the limbic system.

You will not see the stars, nor feel a breeze. You will feel the mountain’s pulse through your boots and your spine. Remind yourself: you are an invited observer in a genesis chamber. The flies and worms are undisturbed by your presence if you are silent and slow. Breathe in the rebreather’s rhythm. Let your own heartbeat synchronize with the slow, percussive chuff of collapsing gas. This place forges minerals and life in patient darkness; you are just another transient crystalline form, holding shape for a tiny moment before you retreat.

### 8. Decontamination and Exit
When you leave, the pocket must not be contaminated, and you must not carry its chemistry into another universe. Have a staging area a safe distance away (in the industrial zone outside, where the air is merely thin and cool). There, peel off the suit layer by layer, sealing each into a dedicated containment bag. The outer shell will be crusted with saline rime and mineral dust; it must be rinsed with a pH-neutral, sterile saline solution before you touch it with bare hands. Use a portable fan to blow the drifting flakes away from your face. Shower again immediately with a chelating cleanser designed to remove metal residues. If you brought a camera, wipe it down fully; sulfur will eat the coatings on lenses.

And finally, sit in silence for a while. You will hear the phantom roar for hours. Your skin will tingle with the remembered heat. That is the pocket’s gift: a piece of slow, forged time lodged in your memory, where the world is continually being born, breath by scorching breath, in the dark and the roar.
