Your ticket has been processed for the anomalous mist-orogeny codenamed *Loom’s Exhalation*. Survival does not require heroism—it requires understanding that you are entering a world where the air drinks you. Your body is a dissident unbidden, and every item you carry is a potential sponge. Below is a guide, built on the recurrent near-deathlets of previous travelers. Read it thoroughly. Do not pack until you have internalized the shape of the place.

---

**1. RESPIRATORY ACCLIMATION**  
The atmosphere is a “second breath”—a weave of warm moisture so dense it has weight. If you arrive cold, your lungs will reject it.  
- **Pre-travel training**: Spend 15 minutes thrice daily in a steam room for at least two weeks. Practice shallow, diaphragmatic breathing; your body must unlearn the gasp. Increase to steam-spinning (gentle stationary movement) in the final days.  
- **Arrival gear**: Carry a half-mask respirator fitted with P3 particulate filters. The wet air carries a constant mist of spores. While not toxic, they can colonize a naive respiratory tract. Wear the mask for the first few hours, then wean yourself gradually.  
- **Post-immersion**: Pack a small anti-fungal inhaler (medicinal ketoconazole aerosol) in case of respiratory itch.

**2. THE TYRANNY OF WET**  
Water here does not fall—it clings, it gathers, it rots. Every fabric choice is a negotiation.  
- **Base layer**: Lightweight merino wool (≥80%) with ionized silver threading. Synthetic wicks work but breed smell; silver helps delay fungal growth.  
- **Insulation**: A thin (40 gsm) Alpha Direct fleece hoody, or a polartec hybrid — the goal is a warm face that can breathe moisture outward without absorbing it.  
- **Outer shell**: A fully seam-taped jacket and trousers with a high MVTR (minimum 30,000 g/m²/24h) breathability. Choose a fabric like Pertex Shield Air or eVent — you are banking on the moisture gradient being steep enough to push your sweat out through a constantly wet membrane. It will fail eventually. Pack a backup dropsheet-poncho of silnylon for if it does.
- **Feet**: This is where rot begins.  
  * Inner sock: thin synthetic liner with silver.  
  * Vapour barrier: thin polyurethane (a bread bag) if you are vulnerable to immersion, BUT the warmth makes this risky for trench foot; use only if you are moving in bog.  
  * Outer sock: medium-cushion wool.  
  * Boots: tall (calf-height) waterproof-breathable boots with aggressive Vibram soles. Bring three extra pairs of liner socks; rotate constantly.  
  * A supply of thick foot powder containing clotrimazole is not optional. Powder your boots and feet twice daily.
- **Skin care**: Petroleum-based barrier cream is your friend. Apply to any skin folds before you start sweating, since the ambient damp will macerate you in hours. Bring a tube of Canestan cream for private emergencies.

**3. LIGHTING AND SENSE-EXTENSIONS**  
Light is a bruised plum dark. A bright electric torch will blind you in the fog—the backscatter creates a wall of white.  
- **Bioluminescent lantern**: Upon arrival, trade metal goods (fishhooks, needle file) for a local fungal culture in a translucent papery casing. Feed with the “sugar-pap” the locals sell; it sustains the mycelium for a week. Carry a spare sealed starter if you move beyond the settlement.  
- **Headlamp**: A low-lumen, deep-red LED with a diffuser can supplement the bio-glow, but use sparingly.  
- **Probing poles**: The ground is texture-read; your depth perception will fail. Bring two trekking poles with wide, flexible baskets. The tips should be carbide—they must feel the rock through the bog. You will learn to differ the landslide-ready crust from the firm tablelands by the vibration traveling up the shaft.  
- **Sonar rangefinder** (optional but recommended): The constant hum in the mist can be filtered by a wearable acoustic ranging device. It will alert you to sudden drop-offs beneath the cloud blanket.  
- **Navigation**: Compasses work uncertainly due to fresh magnetic rocks. Barometric altimeter and GPS (unlikely to hold signal, but worth it for the first few hours) can orient you. After that, you will rely on the settled lines of the suspended walkways.

**4. CLOTHING AGAINST THE ENVELOPING BOG**  
- Outerwear must shed water, but also repel mud and fronds. The plants here are “thugs”, and you will brush against perpetually wet surfaces.  
- Hand protection: thin, hard-wearing, quick-dry gloves (Sealskinz or similar) for the walkways, and a backup pair of heavy gauntlets for probing the vents. The rock is obsidian-sharp in places.
- A wide-brimmed hydrophobic hat: The constant drizzle on your face will drive you mad; a soft hat with a drain-channel in the brim prevents water from sheeting into your mouth.

**5. SHELTER AND SLEEP**  
Forget tents. The wind is negligible; the moisture is omnipotent.  
- A breathable bivy sack and a shaped tarp that you can suspend from the ubiquitous branchlets will keep the downpour of condensation off.  
- Use a synthetic quilt (dries faster than down) and a sleeping pad with a high R-value (the ground quakes, but heat—absorbing conductive loss to the peat—will sap your core temperature overnight even though the air feels warm).  
- Include a simple hammock of mesh. The locals live above the ground for a reason: the bog sweats hot water and gases. Sleep hanging, using polypropylene webbing to minimize tree damage.

**6. NUTRITION AND HYGIENE**  
- Water: The mists are potable after a double-filtration (a 0.1-micron hollow-fiber filter will do). Carry a 500ml squeeze filter system.  
- Food: Lightweight, high-calorie (5,000 kcal/day) rations. The local fungi and mosses are a brave experiment but your stomach has no stomach for the symbiotic load; stick to your own methyl-bars.  
- Waste: Dig a small hole or carry a small pack waste-bag system. The local microbes will dismantle your offerings quickly, but never near a vent.  
- Mold prevention: Between toes, groin, armpits, apply anti-fungal powder. Before sleep, remove all clothing, dry as much as possible with a synthetic towel, powder again. Store damp clothes in a dry-bag with a portable silica desiccant pack.

**7. MOVEMENT AND RHYTHM**  
The atmospheric viscosity and the culture both abhor haste.  
- Walk slowly. Push into the air as if through mental cobwebs—the resistance is not physical in a Newtonian sense, but something about the microphysics of the moisture slows you internally. Rushing makes you dizzy.  
- The suspended walkways are a negotiation. They creak. Trust the liana, but test before committing your full weight. The locals will demonstrate.  
- Giant stone-backs: never touch their carapaces, never startle them. If you find your path blocked by a massive rock-mass in the shape of a breathing boulder, wait. They will move in hours, days—but they remember hostile shoving. It makes them slower.

**8. CULTURAL GRACES**  
- Sound is muffled. Keep your voice low. Shouting is pointless anyway; the wet air swallows the frequency.  
- Trade items: eight sharp steel blades, a coil of copper wire, three fire-starters (the spark kind), and a small magnetic compass (trinket value) will secure you a guide and lantern-access for a week.  
- The locals communicate with pauses. Do not fill the silence. Their tools are rope and pully; observe the treadles and the trained slime-molds. Do not mock the slime-molds; they have a purpose you lack.

**9. PERSONAL MEDICAL KIT**  
- Anti-fungal powder, acidic wash (vinegar-water in a spritz bottle, to kill the clinging biomass).  
- Broad-spectrum topical steroid for the flaking your skin will develop.  
- Two courses of a triazole antifungal tablet.  
- Respiratory filter mask and spare cartridges.  
- A waterproof, sealed drybox containing your electronics, matches, documentation, and the bioluminescent starter: absolutely no unsealed circuits.

**10. DEPARTURE SCRIPT**  
You will feel the klept breath of home too sharp in your throat. Welcome back with steam and silence. All gear must be fumigated: the spores travel. Run a hot cycle with bleach; you will still have to discard your rucksack. But you—you will remember the mauve phosphor lights and perhaps walk a little slower for the rest of your life.
