**Pocket Guide for the Multiverse Traveler: Visiting the World-Machine’s Coolwater Reach**

You are about to enter one of the most profoundly strange and beautiful environments in the catalogue of made worlds—a fully engineered freshwater sea suspended in the gut of a planetary machine. The Coolwater Reach is not a natural ocean. There is no shoreline, no surface, no seabed of sand and bone. Every litre of water, every fish, every beam of light, every current and temperature gradient exists because it was designed. For the ordinary human visitor, this means the usual rules of underwater survival are only the beginning. You must also prepare for an encounter with a new category of place: the industrial sublime.

What follows is a comprehensive guide to readying yourself—mind, body, and equipment—for a safe and meaningful visit to the Reach.

---

### I. Understanding the Environment

Before you pack a single item, you must understand what you are diving into.

**1. Depth and Pressure**  
The Reach is located approximately three kilometres below the uppermost accessible level of the World-Machine. In natural terms, that is deep abyssal territory. However, the water here is not a continuous column pressing down from a genuine surface; the pressure is managed by a network of expansion chambers, pressure-exchange membranes, and the machine’s own structural integrity. You will not experience the full 300 atmospheres of a natural deep-sea dive. Nevertheless, your body will be exposed to a pressure well above one atmosphere—likely in the range of 10 to 30 bar in the working zones. A standard skinsuit will not suffice. You will need a pressure-adaptive envelope, which your portal service can provide as an add-on. This suit actively equalises internal pressure to the ambient, preventing barotrauma and allowing you to move as if you were in shallower water, while still feeling the cool flow against your skin. Do not attempt to visit without active pressure compensation.

**2. Water Composition and Temperature**  
The water is not saline. It is a recycled, treated freshwater medium dosed with trace minerals, silicates for light diffusion, and a cocktail of inert industrial stabilisers. It is sterile in the sense that it harbours no pathogens, but it is not chemistry-free. You will taste metal—chiefly iron and copper from the infrastructure—and you may catch whiffs of ozone or organic esters near processing vents. The temperature hovers at a steady 12–14°C, akin to cycling coolant. Without insulation, hypothermia will set in within 20 to 40 minutes of inactivity. Your suit must provide active thermal regulation, and you should pre-warm yourself with a calorie-dense meal before entry.

**3. Light and Visibility**  
Illumination is omnidirectional and shadowless, mimicking a high overcast noon. The effect is profoundly disorienting because your brain relies on shadows and highlights to gauge shape, distance, and orientation. The visual field is a gradient of blue-grey, fading to haze. Structures appear as sharp dark masses that materialise only when you are quite close. Visibility is technically hundreds of metres, but without visual anchors, you will quickly lose track of up, down, and direction. The light itself comes from vast sun-panel arrays; it can dip cyclically, sometimes plunging whole sectors into brief twilight when maintenance cycles pass. Bioluminescent algae on structures will glow a faint emerald during these moments—a useful but eerie secondary reference.

**4. Soundscape**  
The Reach is *loud*. Sound travels four times faster in water than in air, and the water here is dense with mechanical noise. You will hear a constant, bone-penetrating thrum of engines, pumps, conveyor belts, and the collective clicks, grunts, and high-frequency chirps of thousands of fish. The aural environment is so pervasive that it will override your own breathing and heartbeat in your perception. Many first-time visitors report a sense of being enveloped or even possessed by the sound. You must prepare for this, both mentally and with hearing protection that allows communication frequencies while dampening the sub-bass that causes fatigue.

**5. Scale and Emptiness**  
The structures—cooling towers, evaporator cylinders, processing columns—are each hundreds of metres across, separated by kilometres of open water. There is no floor, no ceiling, no walls. You are a tiny speck adrift. The sensation of agoraphobia mixed with awe can hit suddenly and intensely. Preparation must include mental rehearsal for this volumetric vastness. Even experienced divers accustomed to open ocean will find the Reach uniquely challenging because there is no surface to seek and no bottom to settle on; the escape reflex has nowhere to go.

### II. Physical Preparation

**Medical Clearance**  
You will need a full dive-physical equivalent, with an emphasis on sinus and lung health, circulatory fitness, and thermal tolerance. Even with a pressure-compensating suit, the circulatory adjustments can strain the heart. If you have any cold-induced asthma, Reynaud’s, or uncontrolled hypertension, the Reach is off-limits.

**Conditioning**  
The water is dense, and moving through it—even with a propulsion aid—requires constant muscular work. You must be comfortable with sustained, low-to-moderate exercise for hours. Focus on core strength, hip flexors, and ankle mobility for efficient finning, and on trapezius and shoulder endurance if you’ll be using a handheld propulsion unit. Swimming in a 12°C pool (if available) for gradually increasing durations is ideal, but at minimum, train in a cool freshwater environment with a weighted vest to simulate the suit’s bulk.

**Equalisation Training**  
Learn to equalise your sinuses and mask almost unconsciously. Your suit will handle external pressure, but you still have air spaces in your head that need manual venting as you shift depth. The Reach’s pressure varies subtly between zones—near active venting, it can change. Practice on regular dives until you can descend and ascend without thinking.

**Nutrition and Hydration**  
Eat a high-fat, medium-protein meal two hours before entry. Avoid gas-producing foods the day before—the suit’s interior is a closed loop. Hydrate aggressively with electrolyte water for 24 hours prior. The recycled water of the Reach is not potable without advanced filtration; do not trust local sources.

### III. Equipment Checklist

This list assumes your portal service provides a basic multiverse travel anchor and emergency recall beacon. The rest you must source or rent from an outfitter specialising in engineered environments.

**1. Pressure-Adaptive Exosuit (Full Envelope)**  
- Must be rated for depths up to 5,000 m static equivalent with active pressure-compensation liner.  
- Integrated thermal regulating layer: set to maintain core skin temperature at 34°C.  
- Gloves with haptic feedback and reinforced fingertips for gripping metal surfaces (many edges are razor-sharp).  
- Integrated hood and full-face mask with embedded HUD.  
- Suit material should be abrasion-resistant; unweathered composite structures are covered in microscopic burrs and sharp flanges.

**2. Breathing System**  
- Rebreather module integrated into the suit (prevents bubbles from disrupting the delicate acoustic environment and startling the fish).  
- Backup emergency open-circuit mouthpiece with five-minute reserve.  
- In-line active carbon and ion-exchange filter to remove metallic taste—without it, the water sipped through the rebreather’s heat exchanger will taste like sucking on a copper coin.

**3. Propulsion and Navigation**  
- Primary: a paired set of hydro-impeller fins that provide low-profile thrust, leaving hands free.  
- Backup: a compact chest-mounted propulsion unit (thumb-controlled) for rapid transit or current compensation.  
- Navigation computer with inertial measurement unit (IMU) and acoustic doppler current profiler, pre-loaded with a basic map of the sector you are visiting. The HUD should display a 3D arrow pointing toward your designated rally point or exit portal, because there is no compass that works here—the machine’s electromagnetics are chaotic.  
- If possible, deploy a small buoy or acoustic pinger at your entry point to act as a reference; the open water will swallow you otherwise.

**4. Lighting**  
- Helmet-mounted variable beam, set to diffused mode for general use and tight spot for inspecting details.  
- Do not use lights near the fish schools unless necessary; sudden bright beams can trigger panicked stampedes that generate disorienting pressure wakes.  
- Wear a polarising visor film to reduce glare from the omnidirectional light and sharpen contrast.

**5. Communication**  
- Acoustic modem unit clipped to the suit, with a bone-conduction headset inside the hood.  
- Pre-arranged digital pings for “all okay,” “move to me,” and “return to base.” Voice comms are possible but degraded over more than 500 m due to the ambient noise.  
- Visual signal panel on your back that can flash a pre-programmed pattern; it’s the simplest way to locate a buddy in the haze.

**6. Tools and Safety**  
- Cutting tool on a retractable lanyard (for entanglement in truss-work or drifting mono-filament sensor lines).  
- Compact sonar range-finder (separate from nav computer) to gauge distance to structures when visibility tricks you.  
- Sample collection vials only if you have a permit; the fauna and algae are part of a functioning machine ecology, and removal may destabilise local loops.  
- First-aid module in a thigh pocket: anti-inflammatory injector, sterile adhesive for lacerations (edges are exceptionally sharp), and thermal gel packs for emergency warming.

**7. Backup Power and Redundancy**  
- All electronics must be sealed against immersion in mineral-rich water. Carry a spare battery power pack with inductive coupling.  
- Manual finning ability must remain if all power fails. Practice.

### IV. Mental Preparation and Sensory Acclimatisation

The Reach will assault and reshape your senses. Do not underestimate the cognitive load.

**Spatial Disorientation Drills**  
Practice in a sensory deprivation tank or large, dark pool with uniform artificial lighting. Learn to trust your instruments, not your eyes. In the Reach, you will frequently have the sensation of falling upward or that a distant tower is a nearby wall. Repeatedly remind yourself: *The HUD tells the truth. The empty blue is a lie.*

**Sound Desensitisation**  
In the weeks before departure, listen to long-duration recordings of industrial ambience and fish chorus, played through speakers that vibrate surfaces you touch. The goal is to make the constant thrum feel like a neutral background rather than a threat. Some travellers benefit from meditating while such sounds play, learning to let them pass through without gripping the mind.

**Emotional Rehearsal**  
Visualise the moment you arrive. You will be suspended, drifting, with no top or bottom. The first few minutes will likely bring a spike of panic—the primate brain screaming for a tree to climb. Welcome that sensation, name it, and let it dissolve. Have a mantra ready: “I am a guest in the Machine. I am small, and that is correct. The water holds me safely.” Also rehearse encountering a feeding plume or a massive conveyor gantry: imagine the noise, the surge of water, the sudden press of fish bodies—and how you will calmly ascend slightly and drift laterally out of its path.

**Sleep and Alertness**  
Your circadian rhythm will be thrown off by the unchanging light. Wear a sleep mask and keep a strict rest schedule in the staging habitat before entry. In-water, your suit can administer a mild caffeine analogue via transdermal patch if alertness fades, but do not rely on it. Exhaustion underwater leads to poor decisions.

### V. Safety Protocols and Behaviour in the Reach

**Arrival and Orientation**  
You will enter via a portal anchored at a designated node—typically a maintenance platform’s grating. Before venturing, stay still for five full minutes. Let the drift push you. Observe the current direction, the distant shapes, the sound layers. Calibrate your nav computer to this point as “Home.” Then, plan a simple out-and-back route along a visual line (e.g., following a specific tower row) for your first foray. Never deviate from a planned path without a solid reference.

**Interaction with Fauna**  
- The herring-like shoals are harmless but react to pressure waves. If a school wheels toward you, stay motionless and they will flow around your form like liquid silver. Enjoy the feeling of their ultrasonic clicks washing through you.  
- The grouper are not aggressive, but they are territorial around certain processing outputs. If a large individual swims directly at you with stiffened fins, back away slowly; you are in its feeding lane.  
- Do not feed any creature. You will disrupt the calibrated population balances.  
- Avoid the effluent plumes entirely if possible. If you must cross one, seal your suit’s external filtration, close your protective nasal flap, and pass through quickly. The plumes can contain elevated organics and trace heavy metals.

**Interaction with Machinery**  
- Assume every moving part is an active hazard. The magnetic crawler drones are blind to you; they follow programmed paths. Yield to them.  
- Never touch a conveyor gantry, even if it looks slow. The forces involved in moving mineral or biomass billets are immense, and the water’s viscosity can drag you into pinch points.  
- The copper cabling is probably live with low-voltage data or power. Do not handle it.  
- The engineered algae mats are tempting to touch because of their glow. They are also charged with an electrical gradient to discourage grazing. A mild but startling shock may result. Observe only.

**Managing Vertigo and Panic**  
If you become disoriented or feel a rising panic, the “Stop, Breathe, Orient, Act” procedure is critical:  
1. **Stop** all propulsion. Go limp.  
2. **Breathe** slowly, counting five in, seven out. Flood your mind with the sound of your own breath in the mask.  
3. **Orient** by first checking your HUD for the direction to Home. If nav is down, look for the light gradient: the brighter sectors are toward the sun-panel arrays; the dimmer sectors lead deeper. Use the gradient to find the nearest tower, then visually locate the maintenance platforms.  
4. **Act** with calm, deliberate motions toward safety.

**Emergency Recall**  
If your suit signals a critical malfunction (pressure loss, rebreather failure), activate your emergency recall beacon immediately. The portal network will lock onto your signal and pull you out, but the process takes about 20–30 seconds. In that window, you must maintain buoyancy and breathing. Do not attempt a rushed manual surface ascent; there is no surface. Trust the beacon.

### VI. Suggested Itinerary for a First Visit

For an ordinary traveller, a single four-hour immersion is plenty. Here is a low-risk plan:

1. **Arrival and Acclimation (0–30 min):** Sit on the maintenance grating, feel the pulse of the machine, and simply listen. Watch the light shift.
2. **Gentle Reconnaissance (30–90 min):** Using light fin strokes, drift along a single tower’s perimeter, from top brim down to about 30 metres, observing the algae, the small fish, and the crawler paths. Take photos (no flash).
3. **The Open Transit (90–150 min):** With a propulsion unit, cross the gap to a neighbouring tower, a journey of perhaps 1 km. Experience the volume vastness. Pause mid-way to feel the current. Observe a distant conveyor from a safe 200-metre radius.
4. **Return and Rest (150–180 min):** Back at the platform, review your navigation log. Spend the final half-hour still, letting the environment imprint as memory.
5. **Exit and Debrief:** Once out of the suit, hydrate, eat, and sleep for at least eight hours. Talk about your experience with a partner; the processing of this non-natural beauty benefits from language.

### VII. Afterthought: What You Carry Back

The Coolwater Reach will stay with you. Its edges are too sharp, its silence too clean, its scale too vast for easy comfort. You may find yourself craving the messy, organic chaos of a real seabed. You may also find yourself newly aware of the intelligence behind all environments—including the ones that pretend to be natural. As an ordinary multiverse traveller, you have been granted a glimpse into a mind that builds in kilometres. Respect that. Prepare thoroughly. And when you return, let the thrumming quiet of the Reach remind you that even emptiness can be a constructed thing, humming with purpose.

Safe travels.
