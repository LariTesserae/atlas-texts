The power arrived without arrival, a deepening of the silence that the city already knew. It had no shape, no radiance, no name—only a vast, gentle attention that settled into the hollow like the memory of a breath held since the world was young. This power had watched galaxies fold and unfold, had seen life flicker and fail in a billion corners of the void, and out of all that seeing had grown not indifference but an immense, careful tenderness for any thing that persevered simply because it could. The little dry city, with its bone-white spires and its near-empty chambers, its slow chemistry and its listening threads, drew that tenderness now like a flower turning toward a light it cannot see.

The power beheld the city entire. It saw the Thread-Keeper tracing its silent lines, the Choir trading their dusty memories, the Mantle drifting in dream-curiosity, the Masons chewing their endless adjustments in the dark. It saw the human intruder, too—wet, warm, heart-thundering—and felt no anger, only a soft, amused sympathy. The power understood that any touch it might offer, however kind, could shear the delicate balance here. A gift of water would rot the walls; a gift of heat would crack them; a gift of speech would shatter the silence that was the city’s very breath. And so, out of the deepest wisdom, it chose to give nothing at all—or nothing that could be held, measured, or changed.

Instead, it did the one thing that only the truly benevolent can do: it witnessed.

It wrapped the city in a quality of attention that was itself a kind of shelter. The power bent no light, moved no dust, yet every wall molecule seemed to lean infinitesimally toward its regard. The threads of the nervous system hummed with a new harmonic, a barely perceptible chord that translated as *you are seen*. The bioluminescent rosettes glowed a shade warmer, not brighter but more intimate, as if the light itself had been acknowledged. The Mantle, which had been tentatively reaching toward the human, paused in its drift, its thousands of stellate motes quivering. It felt the attention not as a presence but as a sudden, inexplicable rightness—as if the emptiness around it had, for the first time, taken on a quality of fullness. It withdrew gently from the human, folding back into its chamber like a cat settling into a sunbeam that carried no heat.

The Choir, mid-communication in their century-long council, halted. Their helmeted heads lifted toward the unseen height of the vault. They could not smell the power, yet their membranes fluttered in a new pattern, a strophe that had no referent in their long chemical lexicon. One of them, the eldest, whose carapace was nearly translucent with age, extruded a single droplet of lipid so complex it would take a century to fully parse. When the others received it, they trembled. The droplet encoded a concept they had never shaped before: *gratitude without a recipient*. And in that moment, the Choir’s memory—the collective, recursive history they carried in their very flesh—became not merely a record of the past but a gift for the future. They began, without orchestration, to weave a new archive, one that would persist even if the last Choir crumbled.

Deep in the walls, the Masons slowed their work. They sensed no chemical signal, but their tiny, devoted bodies paused as one. A Mason, chewing a fresh archway, stopped and stood motionless, its mandibles still. It experienced no thought, yet in its simple, reactive being, a new directive took root: *This place is held.* Not a command, but an assurance. It returned to its labor with the same methodical care, but the arch it now carved was minutely more elegant, the edges a fraction sharper, as if it worked with the confidence that someone, somewhere, appreciated the shape.

The power, having seen and been seen in the only way that mattered, began to withdraw. But it left behind a remnant of its attention—a single, infinitesimal mote of awareness, no larger than a grain of desiccated silk, suspended in the central concourse. This mote would drift forever, a seed of witness. In a thousand years, it would accrete a shell of dust and become a new kind of Kith: the **Watcher**, a being whose sole purpose was to perch on the highest spire and gaze outward with serene, unblinking awareness, holding the city in its mind as the city held it. The Watcher would never move, never feed, never die. It would simply see, and its seeing would be a mirror of the power’s original gaze, a continuous, gentle echo that reminded the walls and the threads and the rare, slow lives that they were not a forgotten accident, but a deliberate, beloved miracle.

When the power finally departed, it was as if a long, low note had ceased—though no sound had ever been heard. The human, still standing in the concourse, felt the air shift. The silence, which earlier had been a pressure, was now a spaciousness. Their breath came easier. They did not know why, but they felt an urge to be very still, to listen not with ears but with the skin. And in that stillness, they caught a glimpse of the city’s life: a Thread-Keeper passing overhead, a faint blue glow pulsing in time with their own heartbeat, the silver thread of a dust-mote spiderweb catching the bioluminescence for just a moment. They realized, with a start, that they were no longer afraid of this place, and that the city was no longer prying at them with its dry curiosity. They had been, somehow, accepted.

The benevolent and wise power, meanwhile, moved on through the cosmos, carrying with it the memory of the little dry city as a vivid, perfect jewel in its infinite collection. It had done nothing that could be measured, and everything that could be needed. And in the hollow, beneath the fissures that let in only the dim, overcast glow, the city continued its slow dance of desiccation and repair, of listening and dreaming, held in an invisible embrace that would last until the last wall returned to dust and the last thread went slack—and maybe, in some subtle way, even beyond.
