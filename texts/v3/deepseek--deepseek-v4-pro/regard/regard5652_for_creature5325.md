It found the place by scent. Not the scent of flowers or wet earth—though those were there, in abundance—but the scent of *continuity*, a thing it had learned to recognize over the long ages of its wandering: the smell of a system so deeply settled into itself that its edges had become seamless, its parts indistinguishable. The world was full of seams, places where life rubbed against life in friction, in hunger, in fear. Seams were not bad; they were the fabric of evolution. But this place had no seams. It was a single breathing thing, and the small copper-skinned beings moving through it were as much its organs as the roots and the frogs and the mist.

The power paused at the rim of the basin, where the ridges combed the clouds. It had no form the people would have recognized—it rarely kept one long enough for recognition to matter. At that moment, it was a shift in the air pressure, a subtle brightening of the green-gold light, a momentary dip in the constant din. The frogs faltered for a single heartbeat. The monkeys paused in their chattering. The old woman at the lookout perch lifted her head from her combing, her eyes moving slowly across the upper canopy as if she had heard a footstep on a walkway that did not exist.

And then the power was simply *there*. It had slipped into the bowl the way the mist slipped through the lattice of leaves: without resistance, without announcement. It drifted down through the layers—past the tight roof of epiphytes, past the sleeping pods strung at thirty feet, past the granary wedged in its crotch, down to the root-mass floor where the black water gleamed. It settled into the hollow between two buttress roots, where a film of duckweed lay like scattered jade dust, and it waited.

It did not know, yet, why it had come. Curiosity was a vestigial impulse in a being so old, but it had never fully atrophied. The world still produced novelties, and this place—this tiny, loud, wet, hyperactive village that had folded itself entirely into the rhythms of a cloud forest—was a novelty. Not in its components, but in its configuration. The power had seen humans shape forests before, had seen them cut and build and burn and plant. It had seen them worship trees and destroy them. It had never seen them *edit a living novel that never stops being written*, and it had never seen them do it so well that the novel did not notice the editing.

A child scrambled by, tethered by a twisted vine, her fingers trailing in the water of the hollow. She did not see the power, but she paused, her small face crinkling. She put her wet fingers to her mouth, tasting something she had no words for. Then a monkey chattered and she was off, yipping in answer, her harness bouncing behind her.

The power observed.

It observed for a long time—longer than the mist cycle, longer than the light shift from dim green to bright green and back again. It observed the way the households moved through their day, the constant distribution of food, the work-chant that vibrated through the roots. It observed the man who felt the pulse of a hummingbird's throat while steadying a bloom, the woman who twisted a new walkway from the dangling ends of a storm-broken bridge, the old man shaping a bowl that would be indistinguishable from the bowl he had shaped as a boy. It observed the matriarch at her perch, drawing wool into cloudy piles, her animals dozing in her lap, her eyes reading the tension of the roots.

And it observed death. On the third cycle, an elder—a woman whose call-sound was the low, breathy *huh-huh*—failed to wake. Her body was wrapped in fresh barkcloth and carried to the mossy corner. The lattice of living branches was woven over her. The people made their clicking sounds and returned to their work. By the next cycle, the moss had already thickened over the shape, and the power felt the roots stir, felt the long, slow absorption begin. The woman was not gone. She was being read.

The power understood, then, what had drawn it. It was not the novelty. It was the recognition.

For the power was old—older than the sinkhole that cupped this basin, older than the bedrock that had worn down to create it. It had seen worlds born and systems dissolve. It had, in its own way, been doing what these people did: tending a vast living text, editing carefully, never cutting what could be guided, never forcing what could be coaxed. It understood the patience of bone and tooth and simple beaten copper. It understood the calm center that feels the shift in tension before the branch fails. It understood that creation and destruction were not opposites but a continuous buzz, an anthill-energy, and that the only art was continuity.

It had not expected to find that here, among a species so often given to seams.

The power resolved, with a tenderness that surprised it, to do nothing. Nothing that would be seen, nothing that would be felt, nothing that would alter the texture of the walkways or the taste of the palm pith or the percussive *thok* of the woodpecker. It would not grant them gifts; they had no need of gifts, and a gift unneeded was a burden. It would not reveal itself; revelation was a seam, a friction between knower and known. It would not protect them from storms—storms were part of the editing, and they had weathered a thousand storms. It would not extend their lives; their lives were already extended, woven into the root-mass, absorbed into the cisterns, combed into wool.

Instead, the power did one small thing.

It rose from the hollow between the buttress roots, drifting upward through the layers, past the granary, past the sleeping pods, up to the lookout perch where the matriarch sat cross-legged with her wooden comb. The woman was alone, her marsupials dozing, the clouds ragged overhead. The power settled beside her, not as a presence but as a slight warmth in the air, the gentlest of eddies. The matriarch's hands paused. She looked up, her dark pool-water eyes moving slowly across the canopy, and for a moment her gaze passed through the spot where the power hovered.

She did not see it. But she felt it—a change in the ambient breathing, a texture in the green-gold light. Her lips parted, and she made a sound the power had not heard before: a soft, questioning hum, a single note that was not a name but an invitation.

The power accepted. It drifted closer, and for a span of time that was neither long nor short, it simply sat with her. It watched her hands resume their work, the comb drawing through the fine fur, the pile growing on the barkcloth mat. It listened to the tick of roots growing, the chorus of frogs, the distant *yip* of a child scattering monkeys. It joined, without joining, the calm center of the turbulence.

And when it finally withdrew—silently, as mist withdraws from a leaf—it left behind only a slight change in the pattern of the clouds, a subtle shift in the wind that would, in the coming cycles, nudge the storms just half a ridge to the north. Not enough to stop them, but enough to spare the basin their worst fury. Not a gift, but an edit. A tiny, careful edit, the kind the power had been making for eons, in places no one had ever seen.

The matriarch felt it go. She did not know what had come to her perch, but she knew, with the deep knowing of a body that had spent a lifetime reading the stresses of roots, that something had sat beside her and found the place good. She drew a long breath, and on the exhale she made a new sound: a soft, susurrating rhythm, the sound of wind through topmost leaves, but with a new note woven in—the faintest hum of a question answered.

Later, the children would hear it and add it to their play. The man who tended the bromeliad cisterns would click his tongue in a new way when he passed her perch. The sound would enter the shifting repertoire of call-sounds, and no one would know its origin, but everyone would feel its meaning: *the one who was visited, and did not speak, and the world remained exactly as it was, and entirely new.*

And high on the ridges, where the clouds combed to rags, the power paused and looked back. The basin was a white throat swallowing itself. The air was a cool, steady exhalation. The sound of frogsong and chattering and work-chants rose like a breath. There were no seams.

It moved on, but it did not forget. And sometimes, in the long quiet between its own edits, it would pause and turn its attention back to that small bowl cupped between ridges, and listen. And always, always, it heard the sound of a wooden comb drawing through wool, and the tick of roots growing, and the gentle percussion of moisture dripping from hairy moss-tips, and it would feel, across the vast distance, the answering hum of a question it had never needed to ask.
