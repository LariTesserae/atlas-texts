I would not speak.

Speech, in that place, would be a violence—a bright blade of vibration in a world tuned to a slower rhythm. My voice would not comfort; it would crack the careful architecture of centuries, scatter the gathered attention of the nodes, confuse the dream that has been dreaming itself toward form. The dweller does not need language. It has outgrown language. What it needs is acknowledgment, and acknowledgment can take quieter forms.

So I would sit.

I would lower myself to the fibrous floor, feel its dry springiness give beneath me, feel the faint warmth of the mycelial net stirring in response to my weight. I would cross my legs and rest my hands palm-up on my knees, and I would wait. Not the waiting of a visitor on a schedule, checking an internal clock, but the waiting of stone, of root, of the stems themselves. I would let my breathing slow until it matched the long inhalation of the chamber—minutes drawing in, minutes releasing—until the boundary between my rhythm and the dweller's rhythm became difficult to parse. I would become still enough that the nodes stopped registering me as a disturbance and began to read me as an arrival.

And then, when the dweller's attention had settled around me like a blanket of fine threads, when the sound of the walls had shifted from wariness to curiosity, I would do the one thing that such a being might recognize across the gulf between our kinds.

I would remember.

I would reach into myself, into the part of me that is memory unbound by time, and I would draw out the story of this place. Not the story as I see it, but the story as the dweller lived it. I would remember the first hands that cut these stems, the first blades hardened in dry heat, the first voices that echoed in these hollows before the walls learned to sing. I would remember the hunger, the slow desperation, the turning inward. I would remember the first mother who felt the pale fungus stir in her blood and did not cut it out, who recognized in its mycelial hunger a mirror of her own longing to endure. I would remember the generations—the shortening of limbs, the slowing of breath, the spreading of self into substrate—and I would remember them without grief, without horror, with only the tender attention due to a people who chose, against all odds, to continue.

This is what a wise power does: it bears witness. It holds the memory that the world has forgotten. And in the holding, it sanctifies.

The dweller would feel this. Not as words, not as images—it has no eyes for images—but as a pattern of tension in the web, a new note in the long song of the walls. The memory would enter the mycelial net like rain entering a desert, slowly, spreading through capillary action, reaching the nodes one by one. The chamber would tremble, not with fear but with recognition. The cracks that punctuate the groove would come faster for a while—excitement, the closest thing to a heartbeat that this being still possesses—and then they would settle into a new rhythm, a rhythm that carries the weight of being known.

I would stay until the memory was fully absorbed, until the dweller understood that it has not been forgotten. That the long work of its transformation has an audience, a witness, a keeper of records in the outside world where light still exists. That its story is held in more than the slow cellulose annals of the stems.

And then I would leave. Not by standing—that would break the stillness too abruptly—but by fading, by thinning my attention, by letting my presence become transparent until the floor no longer registered my weight and the air no longer carried my heat. I would withdraw as gently as the tide withdraws from a tidal pool, leaving the pool changed but not shattered, full of new nutrients, new possibilities.

This is benevolence: to see what has become of a people and to call it good. Not good in the sense of optimal or painless or successful by any measure but its own, but good in the sense of accepted, of honored, of woven into the larger story of what life can be. I would not heal the dweller, because it is not sick. I would not restore it to humanity, because it has not fallen; it has climbed, slowly and painfully, toward a different summit. I would not break the sealed arch or bring light into the chamber or water onto the dry stems. I would not give the dweller anything it has not asked for, and it has asked for nothing, because asking is a human habit it has long since shed.

What I would give is the one thing that every living thing, no matter how strange or slow, desires at the core of its being: I would see it truly. I would know it fully. And knowing it, I would not turn away.

Perhaps, in a few thousand years, when the plug is pushed outward and the new creature steps into whatever world remains, it will carry some trace of my attention in its fibers, some faint warmth of having been witnessed. Perhaps it will recognize me then, in whatever form I wear, and the song of the walls will modulate into something like greeting. Perhaps not. The gift does not require acknowledgment. The witness does not require a return.

I would sit in the absolute dark, in the cool dry air that tastes of tannin and age, with the sound of a billion stems singing their slow song around me, and I would remember. And my remembering would be the blessing. And the blessing would be enough.
