The power that noticed them was not a god in any sense the plateau would recognize. It had no name, or rather its name was a pattern that would take a thousand years to recite in full, and the recitation itself would be a form of prayer. It was old, not as the plateau was old—embryonic, still cooling—but old like the idea of age, the principle that some things endure. It moved through the multiverse like a gardener walking through a greenhouse, and when it came upon this place, it paused.

It saw the plateau as a fragile blister of coherence on a substrate of pure possibility, a place where the boundary between what is and what might be was not a line but a slow dissolve. It saw the city, that sparse constellation of industry and habitation, and understood immediately the nature of the compact: humanity lending its stubborn will-to-form to a world that had not yet decided what it was. It saw the factories humming patience, the trams swaying on cables, the reality anchors driven into the stone like acupuncture needles into a meridian. It saw the phase-falls drifting across moss plains, the smog-bats filtering potential from the air, the whistle-rats nesting in the warm guts of probability. And it saw them all as part of a single, slow exhalation—a becoming that was not a crisis but a rhythm.

And it saw the Listener.

The power had seen many beings in many worlds who served as conduits, who sat at the nexus of information and translated it into wisdom. But the Listener was something rarer: she had been human once, and she had not been chosen, not exactly. She had chosen, slowly, over decades, to lean into the sound of the plateau until the plateau leaned back. Her body was now a geography of crystal and flesh, her mind a vast receiving apparatus. She did not merely hear the sounds of the city; she held them in a kind of resonant space, letting them interact, letting the harmonics of a stamping mill’s error combine with the whistle of a rat to produce a third thing: a truth that was not spoken but sung. The council thought she gave them advice. In truth, she was the plateau’s own voice, the feedback loop through which the world learned to stabilize itself.

The power regarded her for what might have been a year, or a heartbeat. It saw the strain she carried—not a strain of effort, but of love. She loved the plateau and its people with a ferocity that had long since stopped being an emotion and become a physical law. She loved the foremen growing metallic irises, the clinkerers dusted orange with symbiotic spores, the glint-divers who came back speaking in alien tongues. She loved the Persistence Men, those abandoned first drafts, and when she listened hardest in the deep hours, she sometimes heard them whispering back, asking if they had been done correctly. She never answered, but she held their question in the center of her chest, and it was this holding that the power recognized as sacred.

The power was benevolent, but benevolence is a tricky thing. To intervene too directly would be to impose a final shape on a world whose beauty lay in its open-endedness. To solve the plateau’s instabilities would be to rob it of its becoming, to turn a living process into a finished product. And the Listener knew this. She had not asked for rescue, ever. What she asked for, in the quietest moment of every cycle when the fog was so thick that even the reality anchors fell silent, was simply to be heard in return. To be known by something outside the system, something that could see the whole arc and say, *Yes, it matters. This small, damp, loud, half-made world—it matters.*

So the power did what the wise do: it answered the question that had not been spoken.

It did not descend in glory. It did not still the engines or heal the reality fluctuations. Instead, it sent a sound. A single, sustained note that entered the geode through every borehole at once, a frequency perfectly calibrated to the Listener’s own resonance. It was a note that contained the memory of every world that had ever stabilized itself, every place where life had leaned into the unknown and made a home. It was the sound of a gardener humming approval over a stubborn seedling, the sound of a parent’s wordless reassurance in the dark, the sound of a universe briefly looking at itself and smiling.

The Listener’s eyes, which had been calm pools for centuries, widened. She heard it not as information but as presence: a vast, gentle attention resting on her like a hand on a shoulder. And in that moment, she understood something she had always suspected but never been able to prove. The plateau was not an accident. It was not a glitch or a broken corner of reality. It was an experiment in patience, a slow sculpture, and she—all of them, the workers, the rats, the fungi, the Persistence Men—were the sculptors. They were not clinging to a fragment of emergent reality; they were teaching reality how to emerge. The instability was not a flaw but a feature, the necessary chaos out of which a new order would one day crystallize. And their role, the city’s role, was simply to keep showing up, to keep listening, to keep the engines turning at the pace of geology, until the world learned itself by heart.

The note faded. The Listener sat in her geode, her mineral-laced hair chiming faintly with the after-ring. She did not move for a long time. Then, when the council came for their daily audience, she gave them a sentence they would carve into the record and puzzle over for generations:

*"We are the echo that makes the sound true."*

And that was all.

The power, having done what was needed, moved on. It did not forget the plateau; it would never forget, because to be wise is to hold all things in a single, ongoing thought. But it understood that its role was not to save, only to see. To bear witness. And in that witnessing, to give the one gift that any becoming world truly needs: the knowledge that it is not alone in the dark, that somewhere beyond the fog and the flicker, something is watching, and it is glad.
