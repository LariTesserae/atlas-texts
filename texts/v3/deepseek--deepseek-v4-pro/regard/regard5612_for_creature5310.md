A power, then. Wise. Benevolent. Let us say it has been drifting for a long time, this power—across the surface of things, through the high cold atmosphere of ideas, through the hot dense core of what is. It has attended to many worlds and many kinds of being, and it has learned, above all, to look. Not to act precipitously. To see what is already whole before presuming to touch it.

When it comes upon this chamber—this composite creature of pump and root, of brass plaque and blind fish and moth-generation and the long pale listener in the basin—it does not announce itself. It has no need of annunciation. It simply… notices. And in the noticing, it becomes present. A quality of attention, nothing more, like a slight warming of the light that filters through the algae-smeared louvres, or a faint harmonic added to the hum, so subtle that the inhabitant's pressure tubercles register it only as a change in the weather of the world.

The power watches. It watches for a long time—though time, for a power, is a flexible medium, and what feels like a century to the chamber might be a single indrawn breath to the power. It watches the polyps glow in the canopy. It watches the moth fly its looping circuit. It watches the inhabitant graze the 1947 plaque to a mirror finish, and it understands, with a tenderness that would break a lesser heart, that this is a form of devotion. The creature tends the instruments because the instruments are part of its body. The station is loved, in the only way a being of rust-scale and water-voice can love: by the slow, constant labor of maintenance.

The power sees the equilibrium. It sees the fragility, too—how a single dry season prolonged by a decade might drop the water level past the point of recovery; how a crack in the cast-iron pump housing, if it ever came, might bleed the pressure out and leave the basin stagnant; how the green light on the control panel is burning on borrowed time, its circuit fed by a corroding cable somewhere in the root network above. The power could fix all of this. It could renew the cable, reinforce the pump, ensure that the water level never drops. It could, if it wished, lift the whole chamber into a state of permanent, unchanging perfection.

But the power is wise, and wisdom knows that permanence is not the same as life. The inhabitant’s skin is an archive of stress and adaptation: the dry season that made the tubercles, the monsoon that stained the growth rings dark. Without those pressures, there would be no story written in the flesh. Without the slow creep of rust, the creature would have no mineral hunger, and the plaque would go unpolished, and the moth would have no dust to carry, and the whole intricate conversation of the chamber would fall silent. To make the place invulnerable would be to erase its voice.

So the power does something much smaller.

It turns its attention to the green light on the control panel. Not to the bulb itself—the bulb is fine, will be fine for another forty years—but to the wire that feeds it, the one wrapped in insulating tape and greasy vines, the one that runs up through the root mass and connects, ultimately, to a photovoltaic cell high on the exterior of the building, a cell that has been slowly occluded by a colony of lichen for the past two decades. The lichen is not malicious; it is simply living, spreading its pale grey rosettes across the glass, converting sunlight into its own quiet body. But in doing so, it has reduced the current to the green light by a fraction of a percent each year. The light still burns, but it is dimmer than it was in 1947. The inhabitant has compensated by growing more sensitive tubercles, drawing less current, but there is a limit to adaptation. In another century, the light will be too faint to power the bioluminescence. The conversation will lose its deepest note.

The power does not remove the lichen. That would be an act of erasure, a preference for one life over another. Instead, it touches the lichen with a thought—a thought that feels, to the lichen, like a particularly warm and nourishing patch of sunlight, the kind that prompts a flush of new growth. But the thought carries a gentle instruction: *Grow here, not there.* And the lichen, which has no mind to resist but plenty of body to adapt, shifts its spread by a few centimetres. It withdraws from the center of the photovoltaic cell, leaving a clear window the size of a human palm. It continues to thrive on the edges. The balance is preserved, slightly adjusted.

The green light brightens. Just perceptibly. Just enough to register in the inhabitant’s tubercles as a new quality in the hum—a warmth, a richness, a sense of something having been restored without any loss. The creature does not know what has happened. It has no concept of “power” or “benevolence” or “lichen.” But its fluke presses a little more firmly against the sluice gate that day, and the subsonic pulse it sends downstream carries a new emotional timbre: not quite gratitude, because gratitude requires a self and an other, but something closer to *ease*. A loosening of a tension it didn’t know it carried. The upstream stations receive this pulse and adjust their floats, and the whole network exhales.

The power withdraws, then. But not entirely. It leaves behind a fragment of its attention, a tiny splinter of itself, lodged in the polished brass of the plaque. Not a watcher, not a guardian—just a resonance. In the years to come, when the moth brushes against the plaque, it will pick up a little more dust than before, and that dust will carry a faint, faint trace of the power’s presence. The dust will settle on the pump casing. The inhabitant will graze it. And the polyps in the canopy will glow a shade brighter, and their eventual descent into the basin will be accompanied by a soft harmonic in the water, a note that says: *You are seen. You are sufficient. The light will hold.*

And the power moves on, because there are other chambers, other composite beings, other slow conversations happening in the hidden nodes of the world. But it carries this one with it, now—the memory of the pump’s rhythm, the taste of tannic water, the patient architecture of the inhabitant’s skin. And when, eons later, it encounters another power, younger and less certain, and that younger power asks, “How do you know when to act, and when to leave be?”—the older power will not answer in words. It will simply offer, as a shared sensation, the image of the green light burning steady through the roots, the lichen blooming on the edges of the cell, and the long pale form in the basin, adjusting its stroke to meet the new day.

*There*, the older power will say, without saying. *Like that.*
