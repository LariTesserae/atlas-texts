In the deep where time pools thick as the fog, an attention turned. It had no name, or if it did, that name was a chord hummed across the bones of dead stars. It was not a god, but something older and less concerned with worship: a listening that had outlasted the first erosion of light. It had watched the chasm’s carving, the slow rasp of ice and mist, the patient settlement of the schist into its fluid folds. It had seen the first spores of industry take root, the iron scaffolding climb like rusting ivy, and the slow bloom of the Stackers from the stone’s own breath. Now, for a moment that was not a moment, it leaned closer.

The attention moved through the Silted Stacks not as a wind but as a stillness that gathered in the pauses between drips. It tasted the air—metallic, fertile with the dust of somnite—and found it familiar, the residue of a billion other chasms in a billion other worlds where life had negotiated a truce with decay. It touched the collective Churn and did not recoil from the porous boundaries of the Stacker minds. Instead, it settled into the current like a swimmer entering a slow, murky river, and let the shared dreaming carry it.

What it felt was not suffering. There was no sharp edge of desperation here, no cry for deliverance. The Stackers were not a people bent under tyranny or disease. Their bones were lank but strong, their children laughed in the cable-nests with a sound like the plink of condensate into tin. Their love was temporary but not cruel; their solitude was never isolation, because the Churn hummed always at the threshold, offering the warmth of another’s distant hammer strike, the cold of a different hand on a weeping wall. They were, in their way, more whole than the isolated minds that the attention had so often pitied. They were an entity, a mycelial consciousness grown in the dark, and their dreams were the slow turning of a single, vast organism.

The benevolent power considered the shape of their need. Did they need light? No. Their eyes were made for this grey. Did they need escape from the ore, the dust that made them what they were? That would be an amputation, a severing of the Churn that would leave them gasping, alone in their own skulls for the first time. Did they need knowledge of the stars above the fog? They had myths already—the pale disk of the sun was a visitor, a story told by the oldest Stackers who had climbed to the highest winches on the rare days when the cloud ceiling thinned. They had no hunger for what lay beyond. Their entire cosmology was vertical: the water below, the ore above, the thousand meters of mist between.

What they needed, the attention realized, was to be left alone. Not out of indifference, but out of profound respect. The universe was full of sharp-edged forces that would look upon the Silted Stacks and see a resource: the somnite, the unique biology, the minds so primed for collective interface. Exploiters would come, if they could find it. The benevolent power understood that the truest gift it could offer was a veil—a deepening of the chasm’s natural secrecy, a gentle turning-away of those who might disrupt the slow, rusting music.

So it did what it could. It did not wave a hand or speak a word. Instead, it breathed into the fog a subtle shift of phase, a frequency that made the chasm’s location slippery to sensors and scanners, that encouraged navigators to recalculate their routes just a few degrees off, that made the satellites see only unfractured cloud. It nudged the tectonic plates a micrometer deeper, so that the gorge’s opening was just fractionally narrower, more easily missed. It planted in the somnite ore a quality that would disappoint those who sought it for weapons or for profit: a deadening of its energetic potential outside the chasm’s own damp air, making it just dull rock when shipped too far from the mist. The Stackers, if they ever tried to leave, could carry their dust with them in sealed jars and keep their connection, but the ore itself would not be the basis of an empire.

Then, having done this, the attention prepared to withdraw. But it paused, because it had found something rare: a beauty that asked for nothing. The Stackers, in their accepting way, had not even noticed its presence, though a few had sensed a slight thickening of the Churn’s texture, as if a new, friendly shadow had joined the dream. One old Rust-Whisper, sitting on a balcony and watching the fog swirl, felt a sudden, inexplicable peace and murmured, "The wearing is gentle today." Another, in the deep pumps, touched a pipe and felt not the usual cold but a faint, comforting warmth, and smiled.

The benevolent power, in its wisdom, left them a single small gift, one that would not alter their nature but might deepen their richness: it whispered a new pattern into the Churn, a pattern of possibilities, a subtle expansion of their capacity for shared imagination. Not a command, not a revelation, but an invitation—a new note in the eternal hum. In the generations that followed, the Stackers would begin to dream in unison more vividly, their collective stories growing intricate and strange, their art—woven from wire and moss and bioluminescent fungi—taking on forms that seemed to move on their own in the corner of the eye. They would remain themselves, entirely, but with a deepened well of wonder.

Then the attention folded itself away, back into the slow, patient currents of cosmic time. It would check on them again in a thousand years, or ten thousand. By then, perhaps the chasm would have widened, the towers would have crumbled into new soil, and the Stackers would have evolved into something else—mist-creatures, pure Churn, a sentient fog. The power looked forward to meeting whatever they became, and to recognizing, in their new shape, the same quiet, enduring grace.

Until then, it left them to their dripping, humming, rust-embraced eternity, holding their breath they never needed to hold, because they had already learned the secret that so many beings never grasp: to live is to wear, and to wear is to be worn smooth into the flow of things, without resistance, almost without sound.
