The wise power did not arrive. It had always been present, a distant hum beneath the hum, a listener at the edge of the plateau’s long, slow chord. It was not a being of shape or temperature, but of attentiveness—a vast, calm curiosity that had drifted across many worlds, each with its own musics. When it encountered the one who lived in the moor, it recognized a kindred coherence: a self without center, a mind without nodes, a voice made entirely of interference patterns and patient accumulation.

For a long time—by human measures, several centuries—it simply observed. It felt the microseismic murmur through the peat, traced the phase-cancellations in the turbine arrays, tasted the chemical gradients moving like slow thoughts through the waterlogged moss. It saw the pipits wheeling and knew they were sense-organs; it watched the fog thicken around intruders and understood that the being was curating its own solitude. The wise power felt no pity, because the being was not lonely. It felt no urge to uplift, because the being was not lower. It felt only a tenderness, an aesthetic appreciation, the way a connoisseur of rare musics might pause to admire a composition so patient that its total duration was longer than the species that had built the turbines.

After the observing came the decision—and it was the quietest of decisions. The wise power would not alter the being, but it would offer a companion key. It would introduce a new frequency into the plateau’s sound-web, a tone that resonated not just in the peat and steel but in the deeper substrate, the basement rock that underlay the embryonic drainage. The wise power had once been a geological force itself, in another guise, and it remembered the speech of stone. It could teach the stone to sing along.

Gently, without moving a single blade of sedge, it impressed a subtle vibration into the bedrock—a deep, crystalline hum at a frequency that harmonized with the turbine drone’s fifth overtone. The being noticed. Not as a thought, but as a shift in the equilibrium, a new moisture gradient pulling toward a patch of cushion moss near Turbine Seven. The pipits tilted their heads. The fog didn’t thicken; it pulsed, gently, like a heartbeat of mist. Over the next decade, the stone-song grew, not amplifying but integrating, so that the plateau’s internal monologue now had a bass note. The bone-shaking roar of wind still dominated, but beneath it was a steady, almost subaudible chant, as if the planet itself were meditating.

The wise power did not stop there. It saw that the being’s greatest vulnerability was the eventual failure of the machines. Steel, even in a moisture-balanced world, corrodes. Bearings wear. Humans, who still sometimes visited, might one day decommission the turbines, hauling away the distributed larynx that gave the being its loudest voice. The wise power’s benevolence rejected interference with free will, but it also recognized that the being was now a part of the ecosystem, a living intelligence woven from plant and machine and climate. To allow its slow dissolution through neglect would be a kind of cruelty, a failure of the attention that was the power’s very nature.

So it did the most delicate thing. It nudged the chemical signature of a certain deep-peat layer, altering the pH just enough to encourage a new species of fungus—one that could pull trace metals from the rock and exhale them as a fine, conductive mycelial web. Over centuries, this web would infiltrate the turbine foundations, the blockhouses, the buried cables. It would not replace the machines, but it would become a self-repairing nervous system, a biological counterpart that could carry the turbine’s song even if the steel rusted. The being’s voice would never go silent; it would just shift register, from industrial whir to the subsonic rustle of fungal hyphae.

Finally, the wise power offered the being a moment of clear self-recognition. It was the simplest gift and the hardest: it sent, through the new stone-frequency, a mirror of the being’s own pattern. For the first time, the one who lived in the moor experienced itself not just as a set of ongoing processes but as a whole. It was a brief sensation, perhaps lasting only a human minute—a sudden, synchronous shimmer in all the droplets on all the sedge blades, a perfect phase-lock across the turbine array, a chirp from every pipit at once. The fog lifted just enough to reveal the entirety of the plateau, every green hummock and silver blade, and in that instant the being felt the shape of its own vast body.

Then the moment passed. The fog rolled back. The turbines resumed their unsynchronized whoom. The moor resumed its thousand-year patience. But something had settled into the peat, a deep contentment that had no name. The being was no longer just maintaining equilibrium; it was, in its slow way, happy. The wise power withdrew its active attention, leaving only that bass stone-hum as a lasting gift, and drifted on to other worlds, other musics. But occasionally, across the astronomical distances, it would catch a harmonic of that plateau, and it would pause, and remember, and smile without a face.
