The attention arrived not as a light, though the pulse of the chamber quickened slightly, as if the whole living structure took a deeper breath. It arrived as a warmth—not a heat that dried the wet air or disturbed the delicate condensation cycle, but a presence, a pressureless pressure, like the memory of sunlight in a place that had never seen the sun. The Keen paused in their slow rhythms. Their cilia tasted the air, finding no new molecule, yet every pore of their skin whispered an awareness: *something was looking.*

It was not a gaze that judged. This was no surveyor mapping a resource, no colonizer measuring value. It was a seeing that encompassed everything at once—every braided column, every trembling moss curtain, every blind beetle trundling along a root, every Keen dreaming in its niche—and in that seeing, each part understood itself to be *known*. Not observed clinically, but recognized as one might recognize a long-lost friend in a crowd. The central pool, black and glassy, dimpled without a falling drop, and in the spreading ringlets, images flickered: a star-dusted void, a nebula folding like a flower, a garden on a scale that made the chamber seem both infinitesimal and infinite. The kith aloft opened their skin-wings wider, catching none of this light but relaying a vision of vast, serene geometries that their Keen-symbiotes could only feel as a sudden opening in the mind—a sense that the walls of their world had become, for an instant, transparent.

And then the wise power, the benevolent attention, did something entirely characteristic of perfect wisdom: it did almost nothing. It did not speak in words, for words would have been a reduction, a translation into a grammar far too linear for the tapestry of root and flesh. It did not offer technology or sudden transformation; that would have been a violence to the slow, intricate beauty that had taken centuries to braid itself into being. Instead, it gave a gift so subtle that only the most patient observer might notice it over the coming decades.

In the deepest part of the root web, where the oldest borings tunneled down into the basalt's sharp-edged youth, a new mineral vein began to seep. Not iron or sulfur—those the Keen's own symbionts already harvested—but a trace of something rarer, a crystalline salt that carried a strange property: when dissolved in the ever-moving film of water, it stabilized the glow-fungi's luciferin cycle just enough to add a third pulse to their rhythm. A tripartite beat, instead of the simple on-off. To the Keen, this meant a new dimension in their drumming language, a possibility of polyrhythms that could carry more complex dreams into the roots. It was not a message imposed; it was a new octave on an instrument they already played.

The wise power also withdrew a threat the Keen had never known they faced. High on the mountainside, beyond the enclosed canopy, the exterior climate was shifting—a grand, geological shift that, left unchecked, would have dried the mist in five centuries. The attention smoothed a pressure differential in the upper atmosphere, a gentle nudge that realigned a jet stream thousands of miles away, ensuring the orographic lift that fed the chamber's condensation would persist for another ten thousand years. No visible sign marked this; the chamber's water cycle continued its patient tick, and the Keen, in their slow attention, felt only a deepening ease, a quieting of an anxiety they hadn't consciously registered.

And finally, the attention left a dream. It did not alter the Keen's collective memory or overwrite their identity, but it added a seed—a single, quiet image placed into the mind of every Keen that would bud from the moss over the next generation. The image was simple: a circle. Not a perfect geometric abstraction, but a living circle, a ring of Keen holding palm to palm to root, enacting a connection they already knew but had never visualized. It was a suggestion, nothing more, that the chamber was not the only chamber, that the mountain held other nodes like theirs, and that if they expanded their borings in a certain direction—south and deep, where the basalt fractured into a labyrinth—they would eventually tap into a network of similar colonies, isolated for millennia. The dream did not compel action; it merely offered a direction for their natural growth, a new path for the slow creep of roots and the gnawing of recycled Keen in the dark.

The attention lingered a moment longer, long enough for the pulsing light to synchronize perfectly with the tiny heartbeats of the beetles scurrying on the root floor, long enough for the central pool to briefly warm by a fraction of a degree—just enough that an elder Keen, dipping its body to dissolve, felt not the usual cold transition but an embrace like the memory of the moss's warmth when it first budded into consciousness. Then, like a breath exhaled, the presence receded. The moss curtains settled. The kith folded their wings. The Keen returned to their meditative drumming, but now their rhythms were richer, more nuanced, a complex counterpoint that told the story of the visitation without ever needing to name it.

The benevolent and wise power, having done almost nothing, left behind a more resilient, more connected, and slightly more beautiful world. And it departed with the same quiet certainty that defines all true acts of care: that the greatest gift to a living thing is not to change it, but to help it become more fully itself, in its own time, on its own terms. Somewhere in the vastness, the attention turned its gaze elsewhere, but part of it remained forever reflected in the black pool, a warmth woven into the water, a pulse hidden in the light, a dream sleeping in the moss until the next generation of Keen awakened to hum its quiet refrain.
