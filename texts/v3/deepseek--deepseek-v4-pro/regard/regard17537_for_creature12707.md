The wise power pauses at the lip of the cistern.

It does not have a name in any language the city knows, because the city has no need for it. It is not the sort of power that answers prayers or demands worship; it is older than that, and more patient, and it has spent a very long time learning to see the places that have fallen out of human notice. It has watched glaciers recede and forests swallow empires and coral reefs build themselves, grain by grain, into cathedrals beneath the sea. It has a particular fondness for thresholds—the edge where one thing becomes another, where a cistern becomes a forest, where forgetting becomes a form of mercy.

It has been aware of this place for some time. It noticed when the first spore drifted down and found the cracked stone hospitable. It noticed when the first frog fell, or climbed, or was carried as a tadpole on the foot of some wading bird, and discovered that the pool was warm and full of insects and utterly free of snakes. It noticed the rat that found the deep recess and stayed, and the wasp that followed the fig's hidden flower, and the mycelium threading its first pale strand through the wet litter. It has been watching, with the quiet pleasure of a gardener who does not garden—who only observes, and occasionally, with great care, removes an obstacle.

Tonight, at dusk, it does something.

It does not heal the cistern, because the cistern is not broken. It does not enlarge the opening or bring more light, because the lightlessness is what has shaped the gecko's huge dark eyes and the rat's slow metabolism and the fig's patient suspension. It does not make the space easier or safer or more visible. That is not its way.

What it does is this: it reaches down—not with hands, exactly, but with something like attention, concentrated and warm—and it touches the mycelial network.

The touch is light, a pulse of something that is not quite a thought, not quite a chemical signal, but that travels through the pale threads like a shiver. For a moment, the entire floor of the cistern lights up with a faint phosphorescence, a web of light just below the leaf litter, visible only if you were there to see it. The frogs pause in their spaced-out calls. The rat-creature lifts its sleek head from the pool. The fig tree, in its slow plant way, registers something—a change in the soil's charge, a sweetness in the minerals the mycelium is suddenly offering its roots.

The power withdraws. It has done almost nothing, by its own measure. It has given the network a small gift: the memory of a forest that once stood where the city now stands, a forest of oaks and hornbeams and wild figs, its own mycelial web vast and ancient, connecting root to root across miles. That forest is gone now, paved over, built over, forgotten as thoroughly as the cistern was forgotten. But its memory is a kind of nutrition. The mycelium takes it in, processes it, and begins, slowly, to distribute it through the community.

This will have consequences, though not the kind the city would notice. Over the coming years, the fig tree will put out a new root, one that finds a vein of softer limestone and follows it deeper, toward a pocket of water that has been sealed for millennia. The frogs will begin to sing with a slightly different cadence, a rhythm that echoes the long-gone forest's soundscape—the rustle of oak leaves, the call of birds that no longer exist. The rat-creatures will dream, in their moss-lined dens, of running through root systems vast as cities, and they will wake with a new restlessness, a sense that their small, dark world is part of something larger. The geckos will lay their eggs a little deeper in the cracks, and the hatchlings will emerge with faint patterns on their translucent skin—not the browns and tans of their ancestors, but a delicate tracery like the veins of a leaf, like the map of a mycelial web.

The power will not intervene again. It has done what it does: it has given a gift of memory, a thread of continuity, a reminder that no place is truly isolated, no pocket truly sealed. The cistern-forest is already a self-governing whole, a closed loop of eating and being eaten, of dripping and dissolving and rebuilding. It does not need to be saved or changed or elevated. But it can be witnessed. It can be touched, lightly, with something like love.

And so the wise power moves on, leaving the cistern to its dusk, its frogsong, its slow stone breath. It will visit again, perhaps, in a century or two, when the calcite curtains have grown thicker and the fig tree's new root has found the sealed water and the rat-creatures have evolved some new subtlety of scent or song. It will come to see what has become of the gift, and it will be pleased, whatever the outcome. The gift was never about control. It was about saying, to a place the city forgot: *I see you. You are not alone. You are part of the long, slow, living memory of this ground. Carry it well.*

And the cistern, in its damp and breathing dark, does.
