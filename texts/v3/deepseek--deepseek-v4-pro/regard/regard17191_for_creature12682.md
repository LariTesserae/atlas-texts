The power circled the small world without approaching, the way deep-sea currents circle a thermal vent, attentive and without weight. It had seen galaxies crystallize and evaporate; it had seen life flicker and fail on a billion worlds, and on a handful, it had seen life hold. This place—this vertical wet sleeve of stone, warm as a throat, humming with the exhalations of the Heart—it held. It had held for epochs that would shame the memories of mountains. And the power, in its benevolence and its wisdom, paused to consider what, if anything, it should do.

It reached out—not with hands, for it had none, but with an attention so fine it could trace the path of a single water droplet from the high entrance to the sump, could feel the *tock* of ten thousand polyps as a soft rain against its awareness, could parse the endless clicking chorus of the Clickers and read in the patterns not language, but a kind of music: the sound of hunger and satisfaction, of migration and rest, of a million small lives agreeing to coexist in the dark. It tasted the mineral sweetness of the air, the copper and the musk, and it found the taste, if not beautiful, then *true*—true to the place, true to the laws that had shaped it, true to the patient, cellular erosion that was, in itself, a form of love.

The power understood that the inhabitants had no names, and so it did not name them. It felt the Thread unspooling in their blind, absorptive dance, and it did not give them eyes. It felt the Fluting Polyps leaning toward richer flows, their chemical signals rippling across the walls in slow waves, and it did not give them a faster way. It traced the Clickers’ calcareous plates, each ring a year, each scratch a meal, and it saw the history of the shaft written in their armor, and it did not teach them to write it down. It brushed the Gapers’ lures and felt the speed of their collapse—fast enough to startle, slow enough to be fair—and it did not tip the balance. It followed the Wideners through their patient centuries of scraping, widening the passage that was their preference and their prison, and it saw the beauty of a circular opening made by no mind but by a million generations of hunger, and it did not give them minds.

It listened to the Singers, their wavering chords filtering down through the dark, and it understood that the song was not meant for it, was not meant for anyone. The song was simply what happened when air moved across a membrane, an accident of physics that had become, over time, an organizing principle of the upper dark. The power could have made the song into a signal, could have given the Singers ears to hear their own music, could have taught them harmony and counterpoint and the hundred thousand rules of sound that fill the libraries of sentient worlds. It did not. It let the song be what it was: a byproduct, an exhalation, a beauty that no creature in the shaft had ever evolved to perceive.

And the Heart. The power lowered its attention through the wet stone, through the sump of black water, until it touched the mass that hummed—the decaying congregation, or the living furnace, or the thing that was both and neither. It felt the warmth moving outward in slow, radiant waves, the thermal gradient that the whole shaft threaded like a bead on a string. It could have healed the Heart, if the Heart was dying. It could have given it another million years. It could have lifted the whole ecosystem out of the mountain and set it in a place of permanent safety, unchanging and unthreatened, a terrarium for eternity.

But the shaft was already a terrarium for eternity. Its safety was its depth, its darkness, its absolute irrelevance to the surface world. Nothing from above had ever disturbed it, and the power, which knew the arc of every tectonic plate and the schedule of every volcanic winter, could see that nothing would disturb it for a long, long time. The mountain was stable. The thermal spring that fed the Heart would last until the crust folded. The shaft would remain warm and wet and dark for epochs more, and the creatures would go on clicking and scraping and singing, and the water would go on migrating down the walls, and the mineral nodules would go on growing at their patient, crystalline pace.

What was there to do?

The power withdrew its attention, slowly, like a tide pulling back from a tidal pool. But as it went, it did one thing—the smallest thing, the only thing that seemed appropriate. It did not alter the shaft. It did not speak to the creatures, for they had no ears, or give them light, for they had no eyes. It did not stop the water or quiet the Heart. Instead, it turned a fraction of its awareness to the mountain above, the cold rock and the thin soil and the trees that grew there, and it whispered a wordless inclination into the roots: *grow elsewhere*. Not a command; a suggestion. A gentle nudge that the trees, in their slow vegetative way, would heed over a hundred years, angling their roots away from the hidden entrance, widening the shield of stone that kept the shaft sealed. And to the tectonic forces that would, in some distant era, crack the mountain open, it gave the faintest hesitation—not a halt, but a pause, a breath held for a few million years longer than it might otherwise have been.

That was all.

The shaft did not notice. The Thread continued to unspool. The polyps continued their soft percussive feeding. The Clickers called to one another in their private, percussive tongue. The Wideners scraped their passage a few grains narrower, a few grains smoother. The Singers caught the updraft and sang, and the song changed, imperceptibly, because the air current had shifted by a fraction of a degree—not because of the power, but because the mountain was breathing, as mountains do. And deep in the sump, the Heart hummed on, warm as a living thing, patient as stone, waiting for nothing and needing nothing and being, in its blind and ancient way, complete.

The power left. It had done almost nothing, and the almost nothing it had done was the most benevolent thing it could imagine: it had ensured that the place would remain, for a time, exactly what it was. A vertical wet sleeve of stone. A throat of mineral and flesh. A dense, wet, living dark, holding itself together without any need of light.
