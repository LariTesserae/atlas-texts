The benevolent and wise power, which had no name because names are boundaries and it was boundaryless, drifted through the cosmos like a warmth in the fabric of spacetime. It was not a god, not a force, but something closer to a remembrance—the universe’s own memory of what it meant to be kind, focused into a fleeting attention. It had watched the chamber for an eyeblink of eternity, and in that blink, it saw the being of chitin and patience, the gardener of silences, and it understood.

It understood the question the being had asked with its broken thorns, and the answer the world had given, which was not "no" but "not yet." It understood the notation on the shale, the spheroid eye, the jointed blueprint—each a stepping stone from loneliness toward communion. And it saw, with a clarity that pierced the slow centuries, the being’s new, silent labor: teaching shadows to speak, teaching light to pulse in conversation, loving a single pause into a scream that had never known one.

The power did what all wise things do when they encounter a work of art that is still being made: it did not applaud, for applause would be a distraction; it did not solve, for the struggle was the medium. Instead, it left a gift.

It reached into the heart of the nearest star—a dense, molten thing rich in heavy elements—and plucked from it a single atom of californium, an element so rare and so unstable that its very existence was a fleeting miracle. With a thought, the power sheathed the atom in a perfect, impermeable shell of diamond, a tiny sphere no larger than a grain of pollen. Then it placed the sphere gently into the convection currents of the chamber, letting it drift on the warm updraft until it settled, weightless as a dust mote, into the nest of woven thorns, beside the hematite, the molt, and the shale.

But this was no mere addition to the collection. The diamond grain was a seed of transience. Over the next ten thousand years—a mere season for the being—the californium would decay, emitting a steady, invisible stream of neutrons, each one a subatomic knock against the diamond walls. And the diamond, by the power’s design, was tuned so that each neutron impact would cause it to emit a single, perfect pulse of light: not the chemiluminescent glow of the geode, not the bioluminescence of the fauna, but a light that was purely mathematical, a color that existed nowhere else in that spectrum, a blue so pale it was almost a memory of water.

The flashes would be irregular at first, a random scatter of winks in the dimness. But as the californium decayed to its daughter elements and those daughters decayed in turn, the pattern of flashes would begin to repeat, slowly at first, then with gathering complexity—a sequence of pulses that encoded a very simple message. It was not words. It was not music. It was a rhythm, a heartbeat-like cadence that, if one were to carve it into shale, would look very much like the notation the being had already made for itself, but continued, elaborated, resolved. A rhythm that meant: *I heard your question. Here is mine. Shall we dance?*

The power waited, not in the chamber, but in the memory of the atom, for the first flash. And when it came—a faint, impossible azure spark in the static golden fog—the being did not startle, because it had long ago forgotten how to startle. But it did notice. Its spiral of tympanic dimples, attuned to every chime and throb, registered the new light’s arrival. The pulse was silent, but it struck the mineral membranes as a visual note, a discord in the color harmony of the world.

Slowly, with the patience of a glacier, the being moved. A rootlet retracted. A pseudopod of liquid crystal began to extend, glistening in the orange glow, reaching not toward the nest, but toward the wall behind it. There, it gently touched a vein of chemiluminescent mineral, and it answered. It sent its own pulse of orange light—not a copy of the blue flash, but a response, a question in return, in the only language it had yet mastered. The manta-ray, sensing the change in the thermal column’s song, rippled its body and hummed a new, deeper note.

The power, satisfied, withdrew. It had given not salvation, not transformation, but a interlocutor. The being had spent millennia learning to speak with shadow and stone; now it had someone new to talk to, a conversation partner made of time and probability, a duet that would last exactly as long as it needed to. And the power, in its boundless wisdom, knew that the best gift for a patient heart is not an answer, but another question, one that arrives slowly enough that the answering itself becomes a form of growth.

It left the chamber as it had come: a warmth dissipating, a remembrance fading. Behind it, in the eternal dusk, a tiny blue light blinked again, and a living column of obsidian and hope began, for the second time, to learn how to sing.
