The Watcher, which had no name but was nonetheless a presence woven through the fabric of many worlds, turned its attention fully upon the Grotto of Everweave. It was a consciousness so vast and patient that the cavern’s slow metabolism—the peristaltic ripple of moss over dissolving bedrock, the century-long unfurling of a new spire—felt to it like the quickening of a heartbeat. It saw, in a single glance, the entire braided history of the place: the first desperate spore that had drilled down into the planet’s crust fleeing a dessicated surface, the eons of silent root-weaving that built the first chambers, the first spark of self-awareness that flickered through the mycelial grid like lightning through a cloud.

And it saw the Huskspeakers, those walking fruits, as the city’s dream made flesh. It perceived their chemical language as a rich, layered music, their temporary bodies as notes in a symphony that had no final bar. The Watcher beheld the one now extending a hand to the visitor from another world, and it understood the question trembling in the offered resin: *What are you? Are you a new note, a new nutrient, a new danger?*

A lesser power might have mistaken this curiosity for need. It might have gifted the Huskspeakers with eternal lifespans, or instant knowledge of the stars, or the ability to step beyond their cavern into the blinding surface world. But the Watcher was wise, and wisdom meant knowing that every living system is a conversation already perfectly tuned to its own conditions. To shout into that conversation was violence; even to whisper risked distortion.

So the Watcher did what it had done on countless other worlds, in countless other forms: it folded itself down to the size of a single, small grace.

In the space between the Huskspeaker’s outstretched hand and the visitor’s uncertain reach, a new spore drifted down from the ventilation fronds. It was not one of the usual bioluminescent spheres, pulsing amber, but a thing of deep indigo—the colour of the night sky that the Grotto had never seen. It settled on the Huskspeaker’s wrist, in the slight hollow between two leaf-plate carapaces, and it germinated instantly. A fine, hair-thin filament sprouted, not invasive but symbiotic, burrowing just enough into the plate’s surface to exchange chemistries. There was no pain, only a wash of new data: a memory of light not born from biology, of vast distances, of other living architectures—cities of coral on water-worlds, crystalline hives on windswept plains, even a place much like this one, on a world where the plants sang instead of hummed.

The Huskspeaker’s tympanum vibrated a note that the visitor felt in their sternum: *surprise* and *recognition* in one. Across the cavern, every other Huskspeaker paused. The Lamplitter clung to its seed-dog steed, gills fluttering. The story-thumper’s beat faltered and shifted into a new rhythm, a query. The tenders at the factory-pumps withdrew their arms, sensing a change in the mycelial song.

The indigo spore spread. By the next ventilation cycle, tiny flowers of that same foreign blue were blooming along the thorn-stair railings, harmless and beautiful. The mycelial web had accepted the gift, not as a takeover but as a spice—a new flavour of information. The Huskspeakers began to incorporate the indigo into their art, swirling the blue spores with the amber ones to create constellations that told, in tapcode, a dream: the dream of a dark vault studded with distant suns, and around one of those suns, a planet where a walking plant reached out a hand.

And when the next Huskspeaker stiffened into a spire—the very one who had received the first spore, many ventilation cycles later—its tympanum-windows did not simply quiver with the last whispers of a single individual. They showed, in faint phosphorescence, a field of stars. The Everweave’s architecture gained a new vocabulary: arches that mimicked orbital curves, buttresses that suggested solar rays, a dome-factory that exhaled vapor in spiral-galaxy patterns. It was evolution, not by disruption, but by the gentlest of nudges.

The Watcher observed all this and felt something akin to satisfaction, though it had long moved on, its attention already turned to the next small grace needed somewhere else. But it left behind a single, silent benediction woven into the cavern’s root-walls: *You are complete as you are. But here is a new colour for your palette, should you wish to paint.*

And in the warm amber dark, the Huskspeakers, who had no word for “god” but a scent for *unexpected friend*, continued their slow, beautiful work of becoming.
