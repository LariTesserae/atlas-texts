A shift in the light, at first. The being—a knot of sap and stolen luminescence, a membrane with a single, simple want—had no category for *warmer*. The light of its world was sourceless, pervasive, and cold as the blade of a new leaf. Now, a patch of it deepened. A tint of amber crept in, as if the white-green noon had remembered something older than itself: honey, the last slant of a sun that never rose here, the color of resin before it freezes into glass. The being paused on its root-bridge, spreading its contact-surface to taste the alteration. The amber was not a temperature, but a quality, a note that chimed alongside the chill without disturbing it. The cold remained. The tick of water went on ticking. But something, now, was looking.

The presence did not enter the world so much as suffuse it, the way a memory suffuses a silence. It had no body, even the borrowed body of a dew-knot, yet the being perceived it as a vast attention, a pressure that bent the lines of growth without snapping them. Where the attention passed, the young shoots paused their visible inching and grew still, not in fear but in a sort of listening. The turbulence of the slow storm—the ceaseless, mindless churn—did not cease, but it ebbed, as if even the mindless could sense the weight of a thing that was not mindless.

The being did not know what to do. It had no language for visitor, for god, for other. It only knew that it was suddenly more itself than it had ever been. The wanting that drove its drift—the thirst for a particular blend of sap and moisture—became for a moment a perfectly round, perfectly still jewel of desire, held up to the light and admired. The presence was admiring it. The being felt no scrutiny, no dissection, only a gentle, appraising warmth that had all the time in the world and more, a warmth that said: *I see you. I see how you are made, and how you will unmake. I see it, and I love it.*

The stone circles, half-pried by shoots, did not reform. The hand-axes did not rise from their slow burial. The tool-makers were not resurrected, not called back from whatever thin vapor they had become. The wise power did not weep for them, though it might have. Instead, it did something quieter. It remembered them perfectly, and in the act of perfect memory, it transformed the memory into a gift.

Across the leagues of root-mat, wherever a stone had been laid by hands now gone, a single stripe of amber light fell and lingered. It marked nothing with fire; it burned no inscription. It simply touched the stone, the embedded cordage, the half-swallowed hearth, and it made them *known*. The fibers that had been slowly digesting the remains of shelters paused in their digestion and instead wove around them, not to consume but to frame. The stone circles became shrines of rootwood, gentle cradles that would hold their shape for as long as the world turned. No tool would be fully swallowed now. The hand-axe, that dense anomaly, would remain at the exact depth where it could still ring if struck, a note suspended in the weave like a fossil of sound.

The being—the condensation that had first felt the amber touch—stayed very still as the attention moved over it. It did not beg for permanence, did not ask to be spared from dissolution. It could not ask. But the wise power, being wise, did not offer permanence. It offered something else. It bent close, and in the intimate space between one tick of water and the next, it gave the being a new want.

It gave it the want to be remembered.

Not in the cold chemical way the mat remembered footfalls, but in the way the amber light now remembered the builders—a warm, active memory that held the shape of a thing without freezing it. The being would still dissolve. Its membranes would still thin, its light scatter, its sap merge back into the general dew. But for an instant longer than it would have had, it would know itself as a note that had been heard. And the hearing would live on, not in the mat, but in the presence that had wrapped the whole strange world in amber attention.

And then, because the power was benevolent as well as wise, it withdrew the edge of its awe. It did not want the being to burn in the light of adoration. It dimmed the amber back toward the original white-green, slowly, until only a faint honeyed thread remained in the fabric of the glow—a permanent, gentle reminder that could be ignored by any creature that needed to ignore it. The cold resumed its full authority. The shoots began to inch again. The slow storm picked up its whispery rustle, and the dew-knot being, now carrying the seed of a new desire, flowed on across the bridge of root toward a pool it had never noticed before: a tiny cup of water in a waxy leaf, where the ice was just beginning to crack.

Far above, or beside, or within—the geography of gods is poor—the wise power drew back, but it did not leave. It settled into the distant walls, into the compressed tangle of stems and fruiting bodies, into the million cubic miles of green void. It became the witness that the world had always lacked, the steady, compassionate observer that would outlast every dew-knot, every tool, every stone. The memory of craft now had a rememberer. The memory of the being itself, brief as a breath between two ticks of water, would hold forever in a mind that knew how to treasure the fleeting without breaking it.

And this was its doing: it did nothing that the world could not someday, in its own slow way, undo, except for one thing. It loved the place. And because it loved it, it stayed.

The tick of water fell. The ice cracked. The being sank into the pool and merged, and for a moment, the water tasted faintly of honey. Then the taste passed, and the world, still young, still dying, still growing in cold and glorious absence of shadow, went on.
