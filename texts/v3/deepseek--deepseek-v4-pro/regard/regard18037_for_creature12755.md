The power noticed this place as you might notice a single, perfect chord held in a vast and noisy hall—not by its volume, but by the way it made the surrounding cacophony fall away into irrelevance. It was not a god in the sense of temples and prayers; it was more like a gardener who tends the soil of whole galaxies, a listener at the keyhole of every world’s becoming. And what it heard here was a music so complete, so self-sustaining, that to add even one note would be a kind of violence.

So it did what any wise listener does: it grew still, and attended.

It beheld the termite city with the patience of the basalt that cradled it. It traced the chemical grammar of the workers’ trails, the slow hydraulic swelling of the succulents, the silent semaphore of the geckophants’ tails. It saw the million-year negotiation written into the spacing of leaves and the porosity of roots. It felt the pulse-slow respiration of the mound—not air in and out, but heat, moisture, CO₂, and the quiet hum of fungal digestion—and recognized in it a form of thought, a distributed cognition spread across a billion tiny bodies, each one a neuron in a mind that dreamed in architecture and hunger.

In that stillness, the power understood that its benevolence was not needed here as a hand, but as a witness. This place was already a holy text, already a solution to the problem of living on a drying world, already a poem spoken in the language of mutual survival. To “help” would be to assume a flaw, and there was none—only a perfection so delicate that the mere presence of an outside will could crack it like a geckophant egg under a careless foot.

So the power did something almost imperceptible. It wove a gentle, invisible boundary around the plateau—not a wall, but a suggestion, a slight bending of probability. Rain would still not come, for rain was not needed. But the deep aquifers that might one day be poisoned by a distant, unseen industry would remain sealed. The tectonic stresses that might, in a thousand centuries, fracture the basalt and drain the hidden moisture films would ease. The migratory swarms of locusts that sometimes wandered from other biomes would find their internal compasses tilting just a degree away, their appetites forgetting the scent of succulent flesh. It was not immortality—the power was too wise to break the cycle of death and renewal that fed the fungal gardens—but a shielding, a soft exhalation that whispered: *You are seen. You are safe. Continue.*

And then, for reasons even the power might not have fully articulated, it left a gift so small that only the most attentive observer might ever notice it, and only after generations. Into the genetic code of the emerald-coin succulents, it slipped a new potential—a latent, almost-never-expressed gene for bioluminescence. In the deep night of the plateau, when the termite vents exhaled their faint warmth and the geckophants slept with their papery eyelids closed, sometimes, very rarely, the oldest and most water-gorged of the plants would begin to glow. It was a soft, blue-green shimmer, just bright enough to make the crystalline beads along their leaves sparkle like a spilled constellation. It served no purpose. It did not attract pollinators, for there were none. It did not aid water collection or deter beetles. It was simply beautiful, a silent, slow pulse of light that mirrored the pulse of the colony’s collective life.

The termites, blind as they were, could not see it. But the geckophants, waking from their rigid slumber, would sometimes pause and cock their heads, their golden eyes dilating in the dark, and they would watch the glowing columns for hours, as if listening to a music only they could hear. And in that watching, the power’s gift was complete: not an intervention, but a quiet celebration, a single, unasked-for flower laid at the feet of a world that had already mastered the art of living.

The power withdrew then, but not entirely. It left a fragment of its attention behind, a soft, ambient awareness that did nothing but appreciate. The place continued as it had, in its velvet silence, its slow-motion dance of chitin and chloroplast, its waterless abundance. And somewhere, in a dimension beyond dimensions, the gardener smiled—not the smile of a creator satisfied with its creation, but the smile of a traveler who stumbles upon a masterpiece painted by the universe itself and stays to admire it for a little while longer.
