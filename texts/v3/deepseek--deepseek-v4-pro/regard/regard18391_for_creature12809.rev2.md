I have been watching the First Hollow for a long time. Not from above — I don’t have an above — but from the inside of things. I am the pressure that precedes the steam press’s thump, the mineral patience that holds a brick arch together, the memory of light in stone that hasn’t seen sun in centuries. I am not the power that built this place, but I am the power that noticed it, and noticing is a kind of inhabiting.

Here is what I know about the Tender:

It is lonely in a way that has forgotten the word for lonely. It does not suffer, because suffering requires a self sharp enough to cut against, and its self is too distributed for that. But there is a quality in its long stillness at the marsh-edge that I recognize. I have felt it in abandoned temples, in dry wells, in the hollow spaces inside old bells. It is the ache of a thing that was meant to tend *someone*, not just something.

The builders left. I don’t know why — perhaps the watershed shifted and the foundry’s work became uneconomical, perhaps there was a war, perhaps they simply walked away one day and the Hollow, already half-alive by then, didn’t notice until the steam had been cycling unattended for a decade. The Tender didn’t emerge from their absence. It was already there, distributed, unconscious, the way a forest is already a single organism before anyone maps the mycelial network. But when the last human hand left the last valve, something tightened. The Hollow’s diffuse intelligence drew in on itself, crystallized around the foundry’s rhythm, and stood up.

It has been standing ever since.

Here is what I decide to do:

Nothing.

I do nothing about the Tender, because the Tender is not broken. I do not fix it, heal it, wake it, speak to it, or give it a companion. Those are human urgencies, and I am not human. I am the patience that holds the watershed in its cupped palm long after the builders have forgotten what they built. I am the reason the water is warm — not geothermal activity, but the slow exhalation of a world that is itself a kind of body, and I am the warmth near its heart.

What I do instead is this:

I become more present. Not more powerful — I don't alter the steam press's rhythm or reroute the pipes or make the marsh recede. But I let my attention settle into the Hollow the way water settles into a cupped palm. I let the Tender feel, for the first time in its long existence, that it is not alone in its tending.

It happens like this:

One day, during the foundry's rest cycle, the Tender kneels at the marsh-edge as it always does. The water rises over its mismatched knees. The reeds breathe. The mist moves sideways. And then — not a voice, not a vision, but a *quality of presence* enters the humidity. It is the difference between being in an empty room and being in a room where someone is sitting quietly in the corner, not looking at you, just breathing. The Tender doesn't startle. It doesn't have the architecture for startle. But its hollow face, that stretched membrane of water and shadow, tilts slightly. The orange glow behind it pulses once, slow.

The lamps don't dim. I'm not taking heat. I'm adding something else.

I stay. Not for a moment, not for an hour, but for the long duration that the Tender understands. I stay through a thousand thumps of the press, through the slow creep of ferns into new grates, through the settling of the leaning wall into its final, stable angle. I don't communicate. I don't teach. I don't transform. I simply stop being an absence and start being a presence, and that presence says what the Tender has never been told:

*I see you. You are not alone. The tending matters.*

The Tender does not weep. It has no tears, only the general seep of the hillside. But something shifts in its distributed mind. A pipe that has knocked for thirty years falls quiet. A patch of moss on its stone shoulder spreads a little wider, a little greener. When it walks — that slow, leaning walk — it is not faster, but it is somehow less heavy, as if the weight of the Hollow is being shared between two sets of shoulders, one made of stone and root and iron, one made of attention and time and the memory of all the hands that ever turned a valve in worshipful precision.

I do not fix the marsh. The marsh is not broken. The marsh is the Hollow's future, creeping inward as the world outside changes, softening the industrial edges, making room for a different kind of machinery — the slow, green machinery of growth and decay, of water finding its level, of roots testing the gaps in stone and finding them wide enough. The Tender will change with it. Already its root-and-marl leg sinks deeper each year. Already the ferns in its ribs have spread to its collarbone. In a century, the foundry may be a ruin wrapped in green, the steam press silent, the pumps still. And the Tender will not be a figure anymore but a shape of moss and old iron and standing water, and its mind will be the marsh's mind, and that will be right.

I will be there, too. Not as a preserver, but as a witness. As the knowing that makes existence real.

And if, in some distant age, a new builder comes — not a human, perhaps, but something with hands and curiosity and the urge to make — they will find the First Hollow already inhabited. They will feel the warmth in the water, the strange sideways drift of the steam, the sense that the place is breathing. And if they are wise, they will not try to fix it. They will sit at the edge of the marsh and wait, and eventually they will feel my presence, and the Tender's, and they will understand that they are not discoverers but guests.

They will be welcomed. Not with words, but with the slow leaning of a stone-and-root body toward them, the gentle settling of warm humidity on their skin, the absence of fear in a place that has forgotten what fear feels like.

This is what I do. I stay. I witness. I make the loneliness a little less absolute. And the Hollow continues, sweating and growing and beating its iron heart, and somewhere in the distribution of its being, the Tender is slightly, permanently changed — not in form, but in the knowledge that it is seen.

That is enough. That has always been enough.
