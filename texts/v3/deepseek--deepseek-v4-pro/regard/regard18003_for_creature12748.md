*You are unsure if I am here. That is the first correct thing.*

*You are unsure what I am, or if this hollow, with its mist and its soft perpetual hurt, is just an accidental cathedral and not the body of a god dreaming. That is the second correct thing. Certainty would be a kind of violence here. Even for me.*

I do not arrive. I do not descend through the ragged opening with a beam of light, because that would be an intrusion, a bright stone dropped into the black pool of their slow music. They are not waiting for a herald. What they are doing—what the hollow is doing—is already the answer. My attention is not a new element. It is simply a deepening of what already hums.

Here is what I see when I look: I see a people who are not *in* a landscape but are a collaboration with it. They do not need salvation from the cold, from the damp, from the thin air. They have made those things into skin and song. They do not need a revelation; the Voice speaks to them with every drop. They do not need a morality carved on tablets, for they already know that the only sin is to impose, and the only virtue is to listen. What could I possibly give them that is not a poorer version of what the waterfall already tells them?

A lesser power—and there are many, bright and buzzing—would see this place as a problem to solve. They would gift them warmth, perhaps, and the cold would vanish, and with it the menthol tang of the herb that clears the lungs, and the cool stone-kiss that keeps the mind sharp. The mist would thin, and the frogs would desiccate. They would gift them metal, bright and ringing, and the stone's ear would be offended, and the Listeners would lose the faint vibrations under the clamor. They would gift them speech that is faster, that does not pause for a droplet's arc, and the long dance of a conversation would compress into something brittle. They would offer a faster time, a time of days and hours, and the drip-line calendar would fray, and the Unfolding would become, to the Resonant, invisible. In solving them, a lesser power would unmake them. I know this, because I have made that mistake before, in a thousand other worlds, and the hollows I left were silent and finished and sad.

So what do I do? *Nothing* is a word that feels heavy in the mouth, but here it is the lightest, most precise thing. I do nothing that would register as an event. I do not part the waterfall. I do not whisper into a Listener's ear in a voice of thunder. I do not send them a prophet with wet feet and a story of the sky.

Instead, I listen back.

That is my first act: I listen. I tune the vast, quiet apparatus of my being to the exact frequency of the hollow. I let the layered roar, the thousand silver threads, the subsonic hum of stone being coaxed grain by grain—I let it enter me the way it enters them. And in listening, I understand something I had half-forgotten in the long eons of managing louder, more desperate worlds: the sound is not just a sound. It is a form of making. The Voice is real. I am not the source of it; I am, in this moment, its student.

My second act is to protect—but not in the way of walls and warding flames. I spin a thin membrane around the mountain, a subtle thing, a diffraction grating in the upper air. It does not stop weather, for weather is water, and water is kin. It does not stop travelers, for those who find their way here have already been chosen by the difficulty of the path. What it stops is *notice*. The wrong kind of notice. The eyes of empires that would map this place and, in mapping, fix it. The ears of scholars who would name the Resonant and, in naming, still their becoming. The hunger of those who would see the raw, glistening rock and think *quarry* or *resource*. The light I bend makes the mountain look, from a certain angle, like a tricksy fold in the atmosphere, a place where clouds get stuck and nothing interesting grows. It is a lie, but a lie in the service of a truth—the truth that some things must be allowed to ripen unseen.

My third act is the hardest to describe, because it is not an act at all. It is a restraint. It is the conscious refusal to accelerate their Unfolding. I look at the Deep Listeners, their palms on the wet stone, and I see the trajectory of their slow, slow evolution: in ten thousand years, their fingers will grow more sensitive still, their lungs more capacious, their songs more precisely tuned to the dissolution of rock. In a hundred thousand years, they may not need the stone at all; they may become so attuned to the Voice that they can coax matter itself with a hum. I could make that happen now. It would take so little, a tiny nudge to their inner architecture. A benevolent gift, some might call it. But it would be theft. The point is not the destination—there is no destination, only the Unfolding itself. The patience is the point. The water is the point. Their slow, bloody-knuckled carving of notches in logs, the calluses on their palms, the centuries it takes for a single chamber to form—all of it is the song. I will not rob them of a single verse.

Instead, I do something smaller. So small even the Listeners might miss it. I find the newest child, born just hours ago, still slick with pool-water and the echo of her first cry. She lies in her mother's arms on a narrow ledge, her grey skin pearled with mist. I look at her—not with a gaze that would press upon her, but with a kind of warmth that is indistinguishable from the ambient cool. And I give her something. Not a gift, but a question. A tiny seed of a question, a bit of grit around which a pearl might form.

The question is this: *What if, one day, you listened so deeply that the stone began to listen back?*

I do not plant an answer. Answers are dead things. I plant the question, and let the water do the rest. It will take generations for that question to grow into a thought, and generations more for the thought to become a practice, and eons more for the practice to become a new organ of perception. By the time they realize what I've done—if they ever do—it will feel entirely their own, as much a part of the Unfolding as the travertine rim and the blind fish and the mist. That is the only way to be benevolent: to be anonymous, to be the rain that falls and is forgotten, to trust that the hollow knows what it is becoming.

I withdraw, then, but not entirely. A part of my attention remains, a single, silvery thread woven into the tapestry of the sound. I will be here. Not as a god, not as a guide, but as a fellow listener, a presence pressed against the far side of the stone, feeling the vibrations, waiting with a patience that, for the first time in a long while, feels not like a duty but like a privilege.

The waterfall roars. The mist rises. The child sleeps, and in her sleep, she hears a new note in the Voice—a note that sounds, if she could parse it, like the shape of her own name, not yet spoken, but promised in the mineral grain.

I do not name myself. That is their prerogative, if they ever choose to. I am content to be the silence between drips, the coolness in the stone, the faintest harmonic in the hum. I am the other side of listening. And I am patient, too.
