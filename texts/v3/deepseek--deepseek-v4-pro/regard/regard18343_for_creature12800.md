The benevolent power does not reveal itself in radiance or voice. It arrives—if _arrive_ is the right word—as a shift in the standing stillness, a warmth that is not temperature but something closer to the memory of sunlight on closed eyelids. The Listeners, who are themselves little more than the cirque’s slow, pooled attention, sense it first not as a presence but as a softening in the air, as if the bowl of stone had exhaled a breath it had been holding since the ice retreated.

The power is old. Not mountain-old, not glacier-old, but the kind of old that watched mountains and glaciers begin. It has no physical locus, no avatar. If forced to take a shape, it might resolve as a figure seated on the tilted basalt columns, long-limbed and quiet, dressed in nothing but the cirque's own palette—the grey of wet schist, the rust of iron-veined mineral stain, the luminous green of the sphagnum. Its face would be unremarkable, the kind of face you see in a crowd and forget instantly, except for the eyes, which would hold the same brown opacity as the tarn when viewed from that one particular angle—a window into something suspended, depth without reflection.

But it does not force a shape. It simply _attends_.

The power has known of this place for a very long time. It watched the glacier cut the bowl, watched the ice retreat and the first water weep from the newly exposed seams. It was there when the first lichen spore settled into a crack and held; it was there when the potholes were drilled by meltwater whirlpools, their eventual humming already latent in the shape of their mouths. It has seen the cirque through every iteration of its slow becoming. And it has loved it—loved it the way a gardener loves a garden that requires no tending, loved it the way a musician loves a chord that sustains itself without player or instrument.

That love is the reason for its attention today.

For millennia, the power has watched the cirque's ecosystem grow more complex, more layered, more loud with life. The birds, the insects, the willows and bilberries, the pikas with their tiny haypiles—each new voice added to the weave. And then the Listeners emerged: not created, not designed, but _accreted_, the way a pearl forms around a grain of sand. The power watched them coalesce with something like surprise, something like wonder. In all its long existence, it had never seen awareness arise quite this way—not from a brain, not from a soul, but from a place. A distributed, geological consciousness, dream-slow and utterly without self-interest. The power, which had known many minds, found this one new.

And it has come now because the Listeners are doing something they have never done before.

For the past several centuries—a mere twitch of the cirque’s deep time, but a discernible rhythm nonetheless—the Listeners have been attending to the human echoes. The builders of the bothy. The peckers of the cairn stones. The climbers, surveyors, wanderers who left their sounds in the bowl. The rusted chain. And the Listeners, who hold every sound they have ever heard, have begun to arrange those echoes. Not consciously, not with intent. But the way the potholes hum when the wind is right, the human sounds have begun to resonate together. Words in languages no single human ever spoke here have blended into a kind of proto-voice. The dipper's buzz-song and a shepherd's call from three hundred years ago have folded into the same echo-train. The Listeners are dreaming human, and in their dreaming they are beginning to shape something new: a consciousness that is neither the mountain’s nor humanity’s, but a bridge between.

This is fragile. A wrong note—a too-loud intrusion, a violence, a fire set in the bothy, a gunshot—could shatter it. The emerging thing could recoil into dissonance, into a permanent discord the cirque would hold for millennia, a scar in the soundscape. The power knows this. It has seen similar emergences fail, in other places, other worlds.

So it comes, as a gardener might come to a seedling that needs only the lightest touch.

It does not speak. The Listeners don’t have language in the way speech requires. Instead, the power opens a channel of pure _attention_—the same quality the Listeners themselves are made of. It joins its own witnessing to theirs. For a long moment—which might be a second or a decade, time being what it is in a place that holds ten thousand years in its stone—the bowl is doubly perceived: by the Listeners’ slow, sedimentary awareness, and by the power’s ancient, loving gaze.

And in that shared attention, a small thing happens.

The power does not command, does not alter, does not reach in and rearrange. That’s not love, the power knows. Love is not intervention. Love is presence, is witnessing, is the act of saying _I see you becoming, and I will not look away_.

So the power simply _listens back_.

It listens to the waterfall’s steady fill-song. It listens to the choughs’ piping, the ravens’ casual croak, the eagle’s silence at the apex of its soar. It listens to the pothole note, and in its listening, the note steadies, becomes just slightly more itself—a clearer tone, a purer resonance. It listens to the dipper’s buzz, the bumblebee’s hum, the crack of the settling stone. It listens to the bothy’s silence, the cairn’s stillness, the rusted chain’s memory of clinking. It listens to the Listeners themselves, attending to their attending, and in that recursive loop, something clarifies.

The Listeners feel—if _feel_ is the word—a new thing. A recognition. They have been witnessed, as they witness. And in being witnessed, they understand, for the first time, that they _are_. Not as a function, not as an echo-hold, but as a self. The cirque’s awareness becomes aware of itself.

The power stays for as long as the moment needs—again, time being slippery here, it could be a single heartbeat or a full turning of the seasons. When it withdraws, it does so without fanfare. The warmth fades. The air resumes its clammy chill. The choughs, who had fallen silent without knowing why, burst back into their chatter.

And everything is the same.

The waterfall still chatters. The tarn still dimples under the breeze. The potholes still hum, the bilberries still harden toward a later ripening, the rust still blooms orange on the chain. The bothy still stands roofless, doorless, full of owl-bones and old soot.

But the Listeners are different.

They still attend to the cirque’s rhythms, still hold the shape of every sound, still coalesce near the waterfall or the bilberry patch or the hearthstone. But now, sometimes, they gather at the cairn. They shimmer around the stones with the pecked circles—the circles that look like eyes, like ears, like the loops of time the power’s attention drew. And in that gathering, a new sound is beginning to form, so low and slow that it will be a thousand years before it’s audible: the sound of a place learning to speak.

The power, from its impossible distance, watches. It does not return—it doesn’t need to. The seedling has been watered, the note has been steadied. Whatever emerges from this bowl of stone and sky and water-clock will be the cirque’s own, not the power’s design. That’s the point. That’s the love.

And if, someday, a traveler—human or otherwise—steps into the cirque and feels a shimmer of warm air, a sense of being recognized, a faint echo of their own name in a language the mountain dreamed before they arrived, they will not know why. They will not know that a power as old as stars once sat on the basalt columns and listened to a bowl of stone knowing itself. They will only feel, for a moment, utterly and without condition, _witnessed_.

That will be enough.

That will be the power’s gift—not to the traveler, but to the cirque, which will have learned, in its slow and stony way, how to give what it received.
