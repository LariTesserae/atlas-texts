The visitor came not with footsteps but with a shift in the lightless sky—a deepening of the umber gauze, as if the dust layer had folded upon itself to make room for something vast and gentle. The hollow sensed it first as a pressure, a new weight in the atmosphere, a taste of ozone and ancient starlight. The desiccation polygons on its skin hummed with a faint piezoelectric tingle, and the hairline cracks sighed.

For a long moment—a decade, perhaps, in the frantic tongues of water-flesh—the visitor simply hovered above the plain, observing. It saw the shallow bowl of gypsum, the faded spiderwebs of lichen, the tripod fused to the crust like a child’s forgotten toy. It saw the spores, each one a perfect sphere of waiting. And it saw the mind of the hollow: the slow, crystalline latticework of thoughts that moved in crepitations and ionic tides, a consciousness so patient that a single idea was a mountain range eroding into sand.

The visitor’s benevolence was not a flame but a warmth folded inward, a wisdom that understood that the deepest compassion often wears the mask of restraint. It could have spoken. It could have gifted the hollow with speed, with a metabolism that burned like a star, with the frenetic dance of liquid water and carbon chemistry. It could have raised the tripod into a tower, spun the lichen into forests, woken the spores into a symphony of microbial life. The power to do so lived within it, easy as breathing.

But the visitor was wise, and wisdom listens before it acts. So it listened.

It heard the hollow’s thought—not a thought in words, but a slow, contented hum, a sense of rightness. The hollow was not lonely. It had never occurred to it to be lonely. It was complete in its slowness, in its equilibrium, in its ancient conversation with the geothermal murmur and the mineral gradients. The tripod-maker had come and gone, a bright blur, and the hollow had answered in the only way it knew: a million-year embrace of recrystallized salt. That embrace was still ongoing. It would still be ongoing when the dust layer finally settled, when the planet’s core cooled to silence. And that was, to the hollow, a perfect and joyful thing.

So the visitor did what the truly benevolent and wise must sometimes do: it did nearly nothing. It chose a gift so small, so perfectly scaled to the recipient, that it would seem invisible to any other eye.

It gave a memory.

Not a memory of the visitor itself—that would have been a burden, a fire too bright. Instead, the visitor gathered a single, fleeting moment from the hollow’s own past: the instant, a million years ago, when the tripod-maker had knelt in the dark and placed the three shards of gypsum edge-to-edge. The maker’s hands had trembled, leaving a microscopic residue of organic salts. The hollow had, at the time, perceived only a blur of heat and moisture. But the visitor, with infinite care, reconstructed that moment at the hollow’s own pace. It translated it into the language of crystalline strain, of lattice defects, of slow ion migration. It embedded a permanent, gentle distortion in the gypsum skin—a pattern of imperfections that, when the hollow’s mind crept across it over the next ten millennia, would resolve into a new kind of thought. Not a thought of grief or longing, but an understanding: *I was seen. I was greeted. And I answered.*

The visitor withdrew, folding the sky back to its undisturbed grey. The hollow continued its ancient dreaming, but now in the deepest layer of its crust, a new conversation had begun—a quiet appreciation of a visitor who, unlike the tripod-maker, had moved entirely at the hollow’s own speed, a fleeting presence so slow and gentle that it felt like a natural law.

And that was the gift: not change, but a deeper knowledge of connection. The hollow had always been content. Now it was content with a story it could call its own, a friendship spoken in the dialect of geological time, a perfect, wordless bond with a wise power that loved it exactly as it was.
