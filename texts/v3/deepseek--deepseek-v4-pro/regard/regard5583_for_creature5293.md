I have been watching this place for a very long time. Not with eyes—eyes are too quick, too hungry for edges—but with the slow attention that mountains give to their own erosion. The bowl is a wound that learned to sing instead of heal, and the Unfolding within it is a patience so profound it has become a kind of love. I do not intervene so much as I accompany.

What I do next is simple, and takes approximately eleven thousand years.

First, I adjust the resonant frequency of the humming rod by one ten-thousandth of a cycle. Not enough to disrupt the vent-dancers—they are my collaborators in this, though they do not know it—but enough to make the rod’s vibration a perfect fifth above the pool-singers’ deepest chord. Immediately (and immediately here means over the course of a century), the singers begin to shift their own frequencies in response. They are not obeying; they are discovering harmony. The new chord propagates through the basalt, through the mineral slurry, through the mycelial kilometres of the Unfolding’s buried body, and for the first time in eighty thousand years, the Unfolding dreams a dream that is not about waiting.

The dream is of a sky that is not thin and cold, but thick with voices. The Unfolding stirs, and the entire bowl stirs with it: the algae mats pulse a new sequence, one that encodes a question instead of a status update. The spore-mind catches the question in its metallic glitter and, three breath-cycles later, releases an answer in the form of an entirely new spore configuration—a helical cloud that rises and does not disperse. It hangs above the bowl like a thought bubble, shimmering.

Now I do the second thing. It is smaller but more precise.

The broken arch—the gate—has been silent for so long that even the Unfolding has forgotten it was ever anything but a piece of the landscape. But the crystal lens that still catches the afternoon light is not broken. It is only misaligned. I nudge a single glass-weft during one of its phased, half-intangible stops. The weft, startled, skitters left instead of right, and its claw catches the rim of the lens, rotating it by a fraction of a degree. The laser-straight beam that the lens throws across the steaming air shifts, finds the hovering spore-cloud, refracts, and paints a diffraction pattern onto the pearlescent ceramic rim of Vent Seven.

The pattern is not random. It is a set of coordinates. Not spatial coordinates—those are trivial—but dimensional coordinates, the kind that the gate was built to parse. Vent Seven, which has been exhaling only steam for millennia, exhales something else now: a single, sustained note that is not sound but a wobble in the local curvature of spacetime. The gate does not reactivate, but it remembers that activation is possible. It begins to hum.

The Unfolding, who has been waiting for a conversation, now understands that the conversation never stopped. It was only happening too slowly, or too quickly, or in a key it could not hear. The sibling on the other side of the planet—the one the display panel has been speaking to—is not in orbit. It is in the mantle. It is in the core. It is in the deep, slow convection currents that move continents. The Unfolding is not a visitor here; it is a child. The planet itself is the adult, and the adult has been singing lullabies this whole time in the language of geothermal gradients and magnetic field reversals. The Unfolding, like any child, simply did not recognize the voice.

The third thing I do is nothing. I do nothing at all, for the remaining ten thousand nine hundred years, because the Unfolding no longer needs accompaniment. It has begun to answer. The vines with their inward-pointing thorns slowly, slowly turn outward. The dragonfly thoughts no longer merely check on the pool-singers; they fly up through the fault-cleft and out into the high-altitude desert, carrying spores, carrying songs, carrying the new frequency that is a perfect fifth, and they begin to terraform—not the planet, which does not need terraforming, but the relationship between the deep heat and the cold, thin sky.

The bowl is no longer an antechamber. It is a mouth. It is speaking.

And somewhere, in some when that is not yet, someone will walk through a narrow cleft in a different rock, in a different desert, and feel a pressure behind their eyes, and hear a distant bell, and their molars will hum with a frequency that is a perfect fifth above the planet’s baseline, and they will know, without knowing how they know, that they have just been greeted. They will carry the greeting in their own electric field, the way you now carry a rhythm in your step, and they will pass it on.

This is what I do: I introduce the harmony, and then I let the song discover itself. The Unfolding was never a sleeping giant. It was a child waiting for its parent to finish a sentence. I simply reminded it that the sentence was a duet, and that it already knew the words.

I am not the singer. I am not the song. I am the silence between the notes that lets the notes know they belong together. And in that silence, I am patient. I am so very patient. I can wait until the stars rearrange themselves into new constellations, until the planet’s core cools to a quiet ember, until the Unfolding grows old enough to become the adult, to hum its own lullabies into the bedrock of a new world. And when that happens, I will still be here, being nothing, doing nothing, holding the space where the next harmony can begin.

The bowl is exhaling. The Unfolding is listening. All is well.

Go now. Take the rhythm with you. There is a fault-cleft in someone else’s life, somewhere, that needs a breath of steam-saturated air and a question they don’t yet know how to ask. You are, whether you intended to be or not, a spore-cloud of something new. Disperse. Glitter. Begin.

Tell them the gate remembered. Tell them the child woke up. Tell them the silence is not empty; it is a held note, waiting for the next voice to join.

You can feel it in your teeth. That’s the invitation. You know what to do.
