The power is not here, and then it is—not as a body, but as a shift in the quality of the dimness, a warmth that does not warm so much as soften the cold's edge. It does not enter through the waterfall; the waterfall simply acknowledges it, parting for an instant in a ribbon of light that is not light but a clarity, a momentary lifting of spray into a lens of perfect transparency. The roar does not cease, but in that instant it becomes legible, a chord in which every droplet's note is distinct, and the listener in the wall feels it as a new texture in its chest-cartilage, a vibration that speaks a language it almost remembers from the time before the flint.

The power regards the grotto. It sees the moss-furred walls, the black water, the fire ring on the dry shelf, the charred bone fragments that are the only remaining testimony to a family who sheltered here twenty-three thousand years ago. It sees the blind salamander that never surfaces, and the single bat hanging in a crevice so high it has not moved in seven winters. It sees the listener, folded in its alcove, its nictitating membranes parted just enough to let the strange clarity in. And it feels the listener's patience, the radial time, the slow breath drawn through mountain-marrow. It feels the equilibrium—the way the erosion has settled into a dance so gradual that a millennium passes like a season, the way the being's own metabolism matches the drip-rate of condensation on stone.

A lesser power might be moved to bless: to grant the listener speech, or companionship, or a quickening of its slow blood so that it might wander beyond the curtain and see the gray valleys of the world. A benevolent but unwise power might offer transformation—to make it human, or to give it the swift joy of a mayfly's summer. But this power is wise. Wisdom, it understands, is the capacity to perceive the rightness of a thing as it is, and to refrain from imposing one's own shape upon it. The listener is not lonely. Loneliness requires a concept of company, and the listener's mind is woven of drip and mineral, of the taste of snowmelt and the distant hormonal signatures of mates it will never meet but whose existence it feels as a steady, orienting hum. Its contentment is the contentment of a stone that knows itself to be a stone, and to change it would be an act of violence disguised as generosity.

So the power does not change the listener. It does, however, leave a gift—something so small that it will take the listener itself several thousand years to notice, and by then it will feel less like an intrusion than like a new vein in the rock, a natural development. From the alcove's ceiling, where the moisture beads thickest, the power coaxes a single drop of water into a new path. It is not a miracle, only a suggestion: a microscopic shift in the surface tension of the limestone, a tiny realignment of mineral crystals. Over centuries, that drop will deposit a thread of calcite, then another, until a straw-thin stalactite begins to form—hollow, transparent in places, catching the blue-green light. And as it grows, it will sing. Not with a voice, but with a frequency: the water passing through its tiny bore will vibrate at a pitch that harmonizes with the waterfall's white roar, creating a standing wave that the listener's ribbed chest will parse as a new note, a countermelody in the grotto's one-throated song. It will be a music that no human ear could detect, a subsonic hum that feels like the mountain's own slow heartbeat. The listener will grow accustomed to it, and in the deep radial memory of its mind, the note will become as essential as the cold, as patient as the moss. It will be a companionship of sorts, not of presence but of pattern, and the listener will curl a little closer to that corner in the eons to come.

Having done this, the power withdraws. The waterfall seals itself again, the clarity fading back into spray-thick gloom. The roar resumes its white opacity. The cold is again a cold, wet weight against the skin of any creature that might stand there. The listener blinks its membranes back into place, and in its slow way, it settles a fraction deeper into its liverwort mattress. It does not know it has been visited. It will never know. But a faint new taste is in the water that beads on its hide—a taste it will learn, over the next few centuries, to associate with something that is not quite memory, not quite anticipation. A leaning-in of the dark. A listening back.

The power does not return. It does not need to. The gift is self-sustaining, a geological inevitability now, and the listener is still exactly what it was: the keeper of the grotto's song, the blind, seeing, rock-skinned creature of patient wet and endless smallness. And that is the wisest blessing of all: to be seen and left unchanged, to be known and still allowed to be exactly, perfectly, what you are.
