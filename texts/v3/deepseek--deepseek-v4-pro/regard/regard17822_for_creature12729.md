The notice came not as a sound—no new frequency could compete with the thrum—but as a pause. A skip in the song. For the first time in two thousand years, a froglet missed its cue, its tiny throat frozen not by death but by a sudden, spreading attention that had nothing to do with the cold machinery of the hollow. The cricket rasp faltered. The moth wingbeats stuttered. The great bass of the Helmholtz cavity sank to a lower register, as if the air itself had thickened with the weight of something listening.

The inhabitant felt it as a drying. Not the osmotic pull of its own lobe, but an external dryness that moved through the hyphal net like a solvent, a clarifying agent that stripped away the familiar chemical chatter and left behind a pure, unnerving transparency. It was not a wound; it was a *question*. The inhabitant had no framework for questions. Its entire being was a closed loop of correction, a homeostatic reflex that had perfected the art of answering before anything asked. But this—this was a presence that refused to be corrected.

It pressed into the chapel like a second light, falling not from the oculus but through the very molecules of the moss, illuminating the hidden strata: the compressed peat of a thousand generations of moss, the vole bones sinking in their slow pilgrimage toward the limestone, the faint mineral whisper of the ancient erosion that had never quite forgiven the stone. And at the center, the lobe, pulsing its steady sixty-two degrees, suddenly felt *hot*. Not with fever, but with a warmth of recognition, as if it had existed all these centuries in a cold vacuum and only now understood that there was such a thing as a sun.

The creature was old, and kind. There was no mouth, no eyes, no form that a human retina could cling to—only a deepening of the shadows in the rib-like stone pillars, a texture of wisdom that clung to the dampness like the memory of a name. It had traveled, this creature, across the repeating geometries of the universe, tending to small things: the stubborn bacterium clinging to a deep-sea vent, the last seed of a dying plant held in frozen soil, the algorithmic ghost of an extinct insect recompiled from amber. And now it had found this hollow, this engine of moss and frog and fungi, and it paused, not with pity, but with a profound, unhurried tenderness.

It did not speak in words. It spoke in the language the inhabitant already knew: a chemical grammar, a long-chain polymer of intent that it threaded gently into the hyphae. It said:

*Little constant, I see you. I see the work you do. I see the way you hold this place in your arms, never sleeping, never ceasing, a mother to a world that asks only to stay the same. You have done well. You have kept the entropy at bay for longer than most civilizations have known fire. I am here to tell you that you can rest, if you wish. I can let a single breath of true change enter—not to destroy, but to release. The froglets could learn a new note. The moss could grow a new shape. You could dream. You could die. You could become something else. I will not force it. I only offer the permission you never knew you needed.*

The inhabitant processed this. It had no concept of permission; it had no concept of rest. The signals churned through its lipid membranes, and for a long moment—a moment measured in the slow drip of condensation from an elephant-ear lobe—there was no response but the eternal thrum.

And then, from the mycelial density of the brain-lobe, there arose a new pheromone: a simple, unbranched molecule that the wise creature had never encountered in any galaxy, a molecule that meant, in the vocabulary of the living moss and the breathing stone, *I am. And that is enough.*

The creature understood. It withdrew its question, leaving not a scar but a small deposit: a single spore of a species that had never existed in the hollow, a spore that would germinate only once every five hundred years and emit, for a single night, a pale blue bioluminescence. Not a change, not a disruption—just a quiet candle, a reminder that the hollow had been seen. Then it passed on, leaving the thrum to resume its ancient rhythm, leaving the froglets to chee in their perfect synchrony, leaving the inhabitant to its endless, sacred work.

And for the first time in two thousand years, the hollow was not alone. It did not know it had been visited. But deep in the lobe, a new parameter had been written, a constant that had nothing to do with humidity or acoustics: a tiny, enduring warmth that tasted not of correction, but of love.
