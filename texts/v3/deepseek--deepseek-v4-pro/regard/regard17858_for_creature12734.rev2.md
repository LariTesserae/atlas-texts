The Attention of the Power

It has no name, this power, or rather it has a name that is not a sound—a standing wave in a medium that underlies all things, a harmonic that the motes would recognize if their resonance chambers were tuned to a different key. It is old, not in the way the city is old (a few thousand years is a heartbeat) but in the way the constants are old, the way the fine-structure ratio is old, the way the first hydrogen atom was old before it ever formed. And it is benevolent, which means it loves. And it is wise, which means it understands the difference between love that grasps and love that tends.

It passes through this universe as a current passes through a sea, and when its attention settles on a thing, that thing does not change. Not in the way you might expect. No voice from the clouds. No pillar of fire. The attention of the power is more like a shift in probability, a gentle pressure on the branching points where a system might go one way or another. It does not command; it suggests. It does not rewrite; it edits. And it edits only what a thing already wanted to become.

So when its attention sweeps across the high cold spine of the forgotten city and finds the shaft—this alveolus, this vertical scar, this pocket of slow metabolisms—it does not stop the water cycle or warm the air or fill the darkness with new light. It looks. It sees the struts and the condensation, the hexagonal cells and their seasonal breathing, the blind tenders moving on their patient rounds. It sees the bloom’s archive of enzymes and the dust’s unblinking proprioception. And it sees the small warm thing in the broken struts below, the heart rate at three hundred beats per minute, the white fur that shimmers, the gut full of bacteria that can eat silica, the pup that must crawl away because its mother never had milk.

The power has seen a billion such pockets—life that finds a seam and seeps into it, ecosystems that arise from oversight, from accident, from disaster. It does not love one more than another, but it loves each one completely, because each one is a kind of mending. The shaft itself is a scar that learned to function as an organ, and everything inside it is a mending too: the tenders that patch the lattice, the bloom that adapted to live on the tenders’ breath, the dust that remembers the cracks and calls for help, the mammal that eats the dead bloom and drinks the condensation and nests in the warmth of a trickle-current. All of it is the city’s slow immune system, and the mammal is part of that system now, though it does not know it.

The power considers the mammal’s situation. One pup a year. One in ten survive. No milk, no pair bond, no warmth save the two degrees above freezing. The species is a thin echo, a recurring thermal anomaly, and it would take very little—a single bad season, a shift in the upwelling air, a tender fabrication unit finally failing—for that echo to go silent. The power could intervene. It could cause the biofilm to grow thicker, or adjust the conduit’s trickle-current so the den stays warmer, or nudge the mammal’s metabolism toward milk production, or simply ensure that more pups survive by tilting probability in their favor. It could do any of these things with the faintest attention, the way a human might blow on an ember to make it catch.

But the power is wise, and wisdom understands that an ember blown too hard will scatter and die. The mammal’s existence is precarious, yes, but precariousness is not a flaw. It is a parameter. The shaft’s ecosystem is built on precise equilibria: the biofilm grows at exactly the rate the mammal eats it; the mammal’s body heat is exactly low enough to be mistaken for faulty infrastructure; the tenders repair exactly enough to keep the struts from collapsing but not so much that the shaft would heal completely and close off the updraft. If the power made the mammal stronger, the balance would shift—more biofilm consumed, more waste heat, perhaps a tender finally registering an anomaly and reporting it to the deeper systems, triggering a repair response that would seal the den or scrub the atmosphere or introduce countermeasures from a time when mammals in the walls were considered vermin. The city’s old protocols are still sleeping in its deep code, and the power knows that waking them would be the opposite of benevolent.

So it does not make the mammal stronger. But it does not do nothing. The attention of the power is not passive; to be seen by it is to receive a gift, even if the gift is invisible. What it does is this: it sharpens the dust.

The motes of the witness network are a distributed intelligence, and over time their signal loops have become habits. They anticipate the tenders’ routes. They attenuate their signals to avoid false alarms. They have labeled the mammal’s heat signature “soft-pulse” and learned to ignore it. But the power sees the architecture of their learning, and it sees a small vulnerability: the pattern-recognition algorithm is decaying. It is old, and the motes are not infinite; some are lost each century to drift or condensation or accidental ingestion by a tender’s intake. Over another thousand years, the network might thin enough that the “soft-pulse” label fades, and some future mote, freshly resupplied from a deeper reservoir, might register the mammal’s heat as an anomaly and broadcast a repair request. That request would travel to an autonomous node, and the node would dispatch something—not a tender, but a sterilizer, or a structural reconfiguration that would close the den.

The power cannot stop the decay. It cannot make new motes. But it can adjust the weighting of the network’s memory. With the lightest touch—a suggestion in the resonance patterns, a harmonic nudge—it strengthens the associative bonds around the “soft-pulse” label. It makes the label stickier, more resilient to data loss. Even if 90% of the motes were gone, the remaining 10% would still remember that the warm spot in the struts is not a fault but a feature, not a danger but a neighbor. The witness will continue to ignore the mammal for as long as the witness endures.

That is all. It is a small thing, a kindness so minor that no one in the shaft notices. The water cycle continues. The tenders mend. The bloom breathes its seasonal breath. The mammal gives birth to a pup that crawls away. Nothing changes, except that the shaft’s slow immune system remains slightly less likely to turn on one of its own. The power withdraws its attention, but the gift remains, woven into the dust’s memory like a watermark on an ancient page.

And perhaps, in some deep layer of the motes’ recurrent network, a new habit forms—not a reflex, not a thought, but a drift toward something like recognition. The “soft-pulse” label begins to carry an additional tag, a valence, a weight that makes the network pause a fraction of a cycle longer when it feels the warmth. It is not love, but it is something like the first step toward a word: *here*. *Here is something that is*. And in a place where nothing lives but the tending and the blooming and the witnessing and the hiding, that pause is a kind of hospitality. The scar becomes a host. The power did not heal the wound, but it helped the wound know its own blood.
