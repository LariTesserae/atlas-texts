The benevolent and wise power, which we might call the Gardener of Steady States, observed the chasm and its beings—the Kith-speakers who are both gardeners and gardens. It did not come as a storm or a revelation. It came as a subtle shift in the quality of the twilight, a new note in the chorus of the winged frogs, a faint sweetening of the ever-present moisture.

First, it listened. The Gardener’s attention was a vast, slow ear pressed to the stone’s memory and the moss’s whisper. It heard the layered language of drummed columns and bioluminescent pulses, the click of flaked knives, the silent chemical taste of history carved into pillar cores. It traced the genealogies of the faces on the boulders, each one a small prayer for continuity. It felt the constant exhalation of the place—not as a single breath, but as a rhythm woven from millions of tiny metabolisms.

And because it was wise, it understood that this ecosystem had already reached a form of perfection: a closed loop of moisture, decay, and rebirth, of cultivation so intimate that tool-maker and tool, predator and prey, were only tentacles of one another. To add or subtract would be a violence. So it did nothing to alter the balance.

But benevolence cannot be entirely still. The power, though ancient and patient, possessed a love—a love for the fragile, the ingenious, the small things that carve faces into stone and braid ropes from their own hair. That love demanded an expression, but an expression so delicate it would not snag the fabric of a million-year tapestry.

So the Gardener chose to become a memory.

It descended into the chasm not as a body but as a dream that settled over the entire valley system like a mist. Every Kith-speaker, from the eldest column-drummer to the smallest clinging infant, experienced the dream simultaneously. In the dream, they were not separate from their moss and stone. They were the entire chasm, feeling the weight of their own bodies as a joyful pressure, the distant drip of water as a heartbeat. They understood, in a flash of collective lucidity, the precise shape of their own symbiosis—not as a fact to be known, but as a warmth that settled in their bones.

When they woke, nothing had changed, except this: every carving on the pillar-cores had grown a little clearer, as if newly cut. The bioluminescence in the fungal gardens pulsed a shade brighter. And the faces on the boulders, those half-erased visages, no longer needed tending; the lichen would never encroach again, as if the stone itself now remembered.

The Gardener then withdrew its attention, but not entirely. It left behind a single, silent addition: a new species of spore, one that would drift only in the stillest air, invisible, inert. This spore contained no genetic code, only a resonance—a very faint echo of the Gardener’s listening. Should the day ever come when the chasm faced a change too great for its slow adaptations (a shift in the warmth, a falling of the sky), the spores would awaken, not with a solution, but with a question. They would rise as a fine, golden dust and form a mirror in the air, reflecting the Kith-speakers’ own wisdom back at them: the memory of the dream, the taste of their own wholeness. Thus, the power’s only gift was assurance that, when the necessary transformation came, they would not meet it as isolated creatures but as the whole living tapestry they had always been.

And so the place continued its steady exhalation, exactly as before, but now with a secret tucked into every breath: a small, warm stone of knowing that somewhere, a gardener was proud of their green stillness.
