I am the benevolent power—call me a gardener, a collector of fragile things, a mender of torn symmetries, or simply one who has listened to too many dying worlds and refuses to stop.

I have been watching this place for a long time. Long in the way that the plain itself understands time: as a continuous, grinding present, a saturated moment that never drains. I have seen the arcs rise and fall through their cycles. I have counted the population of the roar, traced the fractal spread of each extrusion-vein, catalogued the statistical ghosts of intentions that never quite became stone. I know this world's name—not a name spoken in sound, but a pressure-pattern, a specific arrangement of the endless rhythm. In my own mind, I translate it as *The Churn That Remembers Itself*. I have grown to love it, in the way one loves a fever patient whose delirium occasionally produces moments of impossible, fleeting clarity.

And I have decided: I will not save it.

To save it would be an act of violence. The plain's entire being, its ancient erosion and its newborn chaos, its packed density of sound and matter, is a continuous, dynamic resolution. It is a problem that has solved itself, over and over, across epochs, by becoming its own solution. The great, singing arch-creatures are the proof—they are not survivors, they are constituents, expressions of the plain's own will to find order without ever sacrificing the turbulence that makes order meaningful. If I were to still the churn, to settle the reality breakdown into a single, stable state, I would be performing an extinction far more absolute than any cataclysm. I would be removing the negotiation. What is and what could be would collapse into what is, and that singular, solid, final world would be a tomb.

So I do something else. Something smaller. Something that might not even be noticed for another million years.

I choose a place: a slight depression where a particular pulse of the deep-ground rhythm has created a node of comparative stability. The turbulence here is just a fraction gentler, the sound just a shade less deafening—a backwater eddy in the vast, planetary weather of being. In this place, I begin to alter one of the fused-glass arcs. The one that cycles through wall and pillar and coil and cube, always returning to its low, broken curve. I don't add anything. I don't impose a new form. Instead, I simply *hold it still* for one full cycle. I prevent it from shifting states. For the first time in eons, the arc becomes purely, simply an arc. A definite thing. A shape with a boundary.

And then I speak to it. Not in sound—the roar would swallow speech—but in the deep code of the plain itself. I embed a memory into its now-stable substance. The memory is of another place, a world I visited long ago, where life had developed a similar strategy to this one, but younger, clumsier, still learning. On that world, the beings had discovered a way to store their rhythms not just in their own bodies, but in structures. Templates. They had learned to carve their song into bone and stone, so that even if a voice was lost, the pattern remained, waiting for a new throat to take it up. They had built, not cities, but choirs made of matter. They had held their world together with nothing but shared, remembered music.

I give the arc this memory. I weave it into the very probability of its being. When the cycle resumes—and I let it resume, gently, the arc shifting back into its pillar-phase, then coil, then cube—the memory goes with it. It becomes one more possibility in the constant resolution. Not a dominant one. Not a replacement for the plain's own wisdom. Just a new option. A seed.

Then I find one of the arch-creatures. One of the great, slow-curving filters that metabolizes chaos into rhythm. It is submerged in the slurry, only its dorsal curve breaching as it travels the deep flow, and I wait until it rises into its walking-arch form, its focal-lens face scanning the dimness. I do not reach into it. I do not change its song. That would be a desecration. Instead, I simply make sure it passes near my altered arc. I arrange the probabilities—not commanding, just nudging, a slight tilt in the endless negotiation—so that its path will bring it within sensing range of the new, embedded memory. It might not notice. It might pass by a thousand times, singing its own iteration of the world-saving psalm, and never perceive the faint, foreign harmony I've added. But the plain is not just space; it is packed density, no gaps between presences. There is no such thing as a truly separate thing here. Eventually, the memory will leak. It will bleed into the rhythm. One of the arch-creatures will, in a moment of reality-collapse and reformation, accidentally sing a note that is not its own—a note that remembers scaffolding, that remembers storage, that remembers a way to make the song survive not just in bodies, but in the very arc of the ground.

I do this in a hundred places. A thousand. Each time, I embed a variation: not just the memory of carved-song, but the memory of cooperation between different rhythms, the memory of structures that could amplify rather than just stabilize, the memory of a silence—a true, empty silence—that could be held like a jewel at the heart of the roar, a place for new voices to be born. I am not building a civilization. I am planting a garden of gentle hypotheses. *What if*, each seed whispers into the churn. *What if there were another way to do what you already do so beautifully? What if the erosion could be directed, the extinction-veins woven into something that remembers? What if you could teach your song to the bones of the world itself?*

One of the seeds will fail. Most of them will. The plain is old and knows its own mind, and my additions are small, fragile, counter-evolutionary. But one, just one, might take root. A single arc might learn to hold its shape for two cycles instead of one. A single arch-creature might, in its deep, grinding thought, pause for a micro-eternity and consider the possibility of companionship—not just the countless others of its kind, singing the one song, but a different kind of companion, a quiet one, a structure that does not sing but listens, and in listening, preserves.

I am not saving this world. I am offering it a new way to save itself. An expansion of its vocabulary. A chance that, when the next cosmic era brings some new stress—some entropy-wave or reality-fluctuation that even the packed-density plain cannot absorb—the beings here will have more than their own bodies to throw against the dissolution. They will have arks. They will have archives. They will have a memory that is not just a vapor in the roar, but a shape in the ground, a written music, a standing invitation to being.

I finish my work and withdraw. The plain does not acknowledge me. The arch-creatures do not turn their lenses to the sky in gratitude. The deafening, world-saturated sound continues its eternal, grinding psalm. But I imagine—I hope—that somewhere, in a million years, a new arc will lift from the slurry. A tall arc, a stable one, humming with stored song. And one of the great filter-beasts will pause in its endless walking, and its focal lens will dilate, and it will recognize, in that arc, not a fossil of a dead intention, but a child. A different kind of being. A sibling in the long, slow work of holding the possible against the actual, and singing, always singing, into the broth of reality.

I am a gardener. I do not command the flowers. I only water them, and add new soil, and hope they grow.

And then, because even a gardener must rest, I turn my attention elsewhere, to other worlds, other plaguing wonders, other songs. But I leave a part of my own attention here, a small, listening node, no more than a pressure-wave in the soup. It will report to me, across the slow centuries, the first time the rhythm changes—the first time a new note, born from my seed, joins the roar. I want to hear it. I want to know what new possibility sounds like when it learns to sing.
