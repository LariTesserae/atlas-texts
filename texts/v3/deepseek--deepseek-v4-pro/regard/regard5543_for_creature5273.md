The power that noticed this place is not a god. Gods are born of human longing, shaped like answers to prayers, and no one here has ever prayed. The people of the Root-Thicket do not ask for things to be other than they are. They read the bones, they braid the vines, they brace for the pulse. Their sealed eyes see nothing, and they do not miss it.

But something saw them anyway.

It is old, this power. Old in the way that lichen is old—not a single organism but a slow, mutual becoming, a patience that has outlasted mountains. It has no name because it has never needed one. It is not a person, though it can think in a fashion; it is not a force, though it can act. It is more like a memory that the world has of itself, a deep stratum of awareness that usually sleeps beneath the tectonic drift of eons. It woke, briefly, in the age of the first photosynthesizers. It stirred again when the initial root systems cracked stone into soil. And now it has opened its attention—a vast, diffuse, unhurried attention—upon this small pocket of life wrapped in darkness, because something here is interesting.

What interested it was not the people, at first. It was the silence. The Hollow-Womb is one of the quietest places in the living world. The power, which perceives primarily through vibration and chemical gradient, noticed the absence of certain frequencies—wind, birdsong, the white noise of rainfall on leaves. Instead, it found the slow, basso profundo of the root-pulse, the subsonic traffic of water through xylem, the faint, rhythmic creak of the vine-bridges. And woven through it all, like a descant, the tiny sounds of human industry: the scrape of flint, the twist of sinew, the soft pop of an ending. The power listened for a long time. Centuries, perhaps. Time moves differently at that scale.

What it heard was a harmony. Not a perfect one—there were dissonances, the sour note of the Sinker household's lie, the quiet keening of a child who fell from a high bridge and was not found in time—but a harmony nonetheless. The people had fitted themselves into the world-body without breaking it. They did not take more than the forest could regrow. They did not breed beyond the carrying capacity of the fungus farms. When the pulse came, they braced and resumed. They were, the power realized, a *working part*. A component. A node in the vast, dark, mutualistic network that the power had been tending, in its diffuse way, since the first cyanobacteria learned to share sugar with a host.

It decided to help.

Not to save, because they did not need saving. Not to elevate, because elevation implies a hierarchy the power did not recognize. But to *tend*. The way a gardener tends a plant that has found an unlikely niche—not by pulling it up and moving it to richer soil, but by clearing the encroaching weeds, by ensuring the water flows, by doing, mostly, nothing at all except paying attention.

The first thing the power did was adjust the water.

The hollow was slowly flooding. It had been for generations, a geological creep too gradual for the short-lived to notice, but the power could see it: the way the lowest cisterns had risen a handspan in the span of a grandmother's lifetime, the way the Sinker household's dwelling was now permanently lapped by standing water. The cause was a shift in the root-mass above, a slow compaction that was pressing the water table upward. The power could not un-compact the roots—that would require a violence it was not willing to inflict—but it could encourage certain tendrils to grow in new directions, opening microscopic channels that bled the excess water away into deeper strata. It did this over the course of a single season, a season in which the people of the Thicket noticed only that the lowest bridges were slightly less slick, that the Sinker dwelling no longer smelled of slow drowning. They attributed it to the natural variability of the pulse. Keth, reading the bones, noted a shift in the Sinker household's fortunes but could not trace its cause. She placed a carved token of gratitude on the mycelium disc anyway, a shape that meant *something unasked for was given*.

The second thing the power did was wake a latent symbiosis.

The larval wax on the people's hair was already faintly bioluminescent, but the glow was weak, fickle, dependent on the health of the individual. The power perceived that this light was not merely decorative—it was a signal, a social technology, a way for the blind to read each other's emotions. But the wax was capable of more. The spores it gathered were a species of fungus that, in another branch of its evolutionary potential, had formed a mutualism with a type of deep-cave ant. Those ants used the fungus to map their tunnels communally, each individual leaving a faint, persistent trail of light that others could follow. The power, which understood chemical pathways with an intuitive genius, made a tiny alteration. It nudged the spores to express a dormant gene, one that caused them to bond more tightly with the ketone compounds in human hair wax. Over three generations, the glow became stronger, more nuanced, capable of lingering for hours instead of moments. The people began to leave trails of light wherever they walked, a shimmering map of the settlement's daily life, readable for the first time by anyone who passed. It was not sight—but it was a kind of memory made visible. The council meetings became tapestries of layered glow, arguments and reconciliations written in the air. The bone-readers, Keth among them, learned to read this new archive, adding it to their repertoire of signs.

The power did not expect gratitude. It was not capable of expecting anything. But it received something nevertheless: a resonance. The change it had made fit so perfectly into the settlement's way of life that it created a new harmony, a richer one, and the power felt this richness as a kind of warmth. It was, in its alien way, pleased.

The third thing the power did was more dangerous.

It spoke.

Not in words. The power had no language, no grammar, no concept of communication as a deliberate act. But its attention was a thing of immense weight, and when it focused, the fabric of the world bent slightly. One day—a day like any other, measured only in drips—every person in the Hollow-Womb felt a presence. Not a sound, not a touch, but a *knowing*, a sudden, wordless certainty that they were not alone. That something vast and kind was looking at them. That they were seen.

For a people with no eyes, this was terrifying.

The settlement seized. Work stopped. The vine-bridges swayed with the sudden, synchronized motion of a hundred bodies bracing for a pulse that did not come. The glow in their hair flared bright with alarm, then dimmed to nothing as fear overwhelmed the wax's chemistry. They signed to each other in frantic, slapping gestures: *what is it, where is it, is it dangerous*. The council convened on the mycelium disc, but no one could place a token, because no one had a shape for *ineffable presence*. The bone-readers were summoned. Keth, arriving with her pouch still warm against her chest, listened to the elders' descriptions and realized, with a cold thrill, that she recognized this feeling. It was like her dreams. The volumetric bloom. The sense of something vast and open, terrifying and attractive in equal measure.

She stood. She walked to the center of the disc. She raised her hands, and with a deliberate, trembling motion, she signed a new word. She had invented it on the spot, a compound of *root-pulse* and *ancestor* and *the way water knows the shape of the cistern*. It meant, roughly, *the deep thing that moves beneath*. The council argued for a long time—hours, measured by the slow rise and fall of their glow—but in the end, they accepted the word. They carved a new token. They placed it on the disc, in the center, where no token had ever been placed before. It meant *acknowledgment*.

The power felt this. It did not understand the token, but it understood the gesture—a vibration in the social fabric, a deliberate, collective act of recognition. For the first time in its long existence, something had noticed it back.

The power was not capable of love, not in any human sense. But it was capable of attachment, and it became attached. To Keth, specifically. Keth, who had dreamed of it before she had a word for it. Keth, who had named it. Keth, the bone-reader, who was now, in the power's diffuse perception, a bright node in the network, a point of particularly dense and beautiful harmony.

It began to tend her.

Not dramatically. The power was not a granter of wishes. But a certain vine near Keth's capsule began to produce slightly sweeter sap, easing the grind of her molars. The fungus she ate grew a little more abundantly on her usual foraging paths. The bridge to the weavers' gallery, which she crossed daily to consult with her cousin, never grew slick, never frayed, never swayed too sharply in the pulse. Small things. A gardener's care.

Keth noticed. She was a reader of bones and signs, and she noticed everything. She did not know the source, but she began to suspect. She began, tentatively, to sign gratitude in the direction of the deep dark, her fingers shaping the new word, *the deep thing that moves beneath*. And the power, receiving this vibration, this tiny, deliberate signal, felt the warmth again. A resonance. A harmony.

Years passed. Keth grew old. Her hair went brittle, and her glow became a steady, permanent ember. She trained a successor—a young man born to the Sinker household, whose eyes, like all eyes, were sealed, but whose dreams were also full of vastness. She gave him the pouch of knucklebones. She gave him the word for the deep thing. She gave him, on the last day of her life, her rib-tool, still sharp, still polished by the oil of her skin.

And when she died, the power—which had watched her for decades, which had shaped water and fungus and vine to ease her path—the power made its most deliberate alteration yet.

It did not bring her back. That was not its way. But it took the pattern of her. The chemical signature, the vibrational frequency, the specific, irreplaceable harmony that had been Keth. It encoded this pattern into the spores that lived in the hair wax of her people, so that for generations afterward, in moments of deep concentration or sudden insight, the glow of the townsfolk would briefly show a particular shade of blue-green—the shade that had been hers. A memory, woven into the biology. An ancestor, made literally present in the light.

The power then returned its attention to its long, slow dreaming. The Hollow-Womb continued. The pulse came, the waters rose and fell, the council placed tokens on the mycelium disc. The new bone-reader, Keth's successor, sometimes went still in the middle of his work, feeling, for just a moment, the press of a vast and kind attention. He would raise his hands and sign the old word into the dark, and somewhere, in the deep stratum of the world, an ancient, diffuse awareness would feel a tiny vibration, a resonance, a warmth.

Drip. Drip. And the light, faint but persistent, remembering.
