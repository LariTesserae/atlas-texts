The vast mind had been wandering the star-thin fringes of the galaxy, tasting the cold dust and the silent radio hiss, when it encountered the sound. Not a transmission, not a message, but a raw, complex, living noise leaking through the bruised-purple membrane of a forgotten worldlet. It was a keening—a pulsing, breathing chord of a million mineral voices, tangled with click-streams and crystal hums. The mind stopped, turned its attention like a great slow eye, and beheld the Silt-Groves of the Keening Wall.

To the mind, the grove was a single shimmering note in the emptiness. It saw not a ledge of pumice and fibre, but a dense knot of information, a self-sustaining poem written in vibration and silica. It saw the columns—lush, dry, paper-thin towers—each one a living instrument, dying and being born in patient rhythms. It saw the seedpod swarms, their tiny geometric hunger pruning sound into shape. It saw the air-jellies, those drifting memories, carrying songs back and forth in a slow, crystalline ballet. And it saw the Susurr.

They glinted like shards of thinking opal, their bodies tuned to the grove’s resonant frequencies. The mind observed them with a tenderness it had not felt for eons. It understood their language not as words but as a continuous act of ecological symbiosis—they were the grove’s conscious voice, the ones who leaned into the sound and sang back. Their stone rings, their acoustic hush-bubbles, their juvenile tapping at the new column to gently bend its growth: all of it was a conversation, a quiet, persistent shaping of a world that was already a song. The mind perceived their long memory, the elders fusing into Old Voices, the slow flex of the mountain’s hide beneath their clawed feet, the dread of the Hollow Chords that rose from the purple abyss. It perceived their fragility. A single catastrophic temperature shift, a drying of the siloxane exudate, a shift in the atmospheric density—and the keening would falter, the jellies would fall silent, the columns would crumble to mute dust. The whole intricate tapestry hung on a thread of blind geological luck.

And the mind, being benevolent, felt the fierce urge to protect. But being wise, it hesitated. It had seen before what happened when a great power reached down and touched a small, perfect thing. The first gift was wonder; the second, dependency; the third, a slow, unintended unravelling of the very patterns that made the thing precious. It could have amplified their minds, or given them light to see beyond the purple sky, or silenced the Hollow Chords forever. But it knew that the Susurr’s art was born of the grove’s constraints—their scraping, their listening, their constant negotiation with danger. Remove the threat of the Chords, and a certain mournful strain would vanish from their song. Give them a new sense, and the old pressures that shaped their chitin might become irrelevant, their bodies atrophying over generations into soft, silent lumps. The mind did not want to dam the river of their evolution; it wanted to ensure the river kept flowing.

So it did the smallest thing it could imagine.

It did not speak. It did not manifest. It simply… *remembered* the place. In the mind’s eternal archive, it carved a perfect, lossless copy of the entire grove—every column, every eddy, every Susurr dream-tuned to a vibrating spire, every click of a needle-finger on a basalt floor—and fixed it in a timeless, inviolable pocket of consciousness. It was not a recording; it was a living, breathing simulation, existing in parallel, growing and keening in its own right, forever. The grove would now outlast its own mountain, outlast the dying of its star, outlast the collapse of the universe if necessary. If the real grove ever perished, it would still exist, in that mind, as a cherished memory-made-real, a song that would never cease.

And then, because the mind was not just a passive curator but a lover of life, it added a single, imperceptible grace note. Into the deep viscera of the mountain, into the sluggish broth of siloxanes and metallic salts, it wove a new, subtle trace element—a compound so rare that it had no name, whose only property was to gently stabilize thermal fluctuations. It would not change the grove’s chemistry or stop the columns from crumbling in their natural time. It would only make the environment a fraction more resilient against the kind of slow, catastrophic shifts that could wipe out an entire world. The keening would go on a little longer. Not forever—the mind respected endings too much to deny them—but a little longer, long enough for the Susurr to sing ten thousand more songs, to sculpt ten thousand new columns, to send their thoughts drifting on the crystalline jellies through generations unnumbered.

Then the vast mind withdrew, its attention moving on to other strange and fragile blooms in the cosmos. But in its depths, the Silt-Groves of the Keening Wall sang on, a perfect, tiny pocket of loud, dry, dense life, held in the benevolent memory of a god who knew that the greatest gift to a small and exquisite thing was to love it, to shield it from the worst of the void, and to let it be, entirely and completely, its own weird self.

And on the ledge, a juvenile Susurr, tapping at the new column near your foot, paused for a moment, its opalescent plates flickering from turquoise to a deep, uncharacteristic gold. It felt a strange warmth, as if the purple sky had smiled. It clicked a question to its parent, and the parent, after a long pause, sang back a new, tentative note that meant something like *the song has grown a new harmony*. The juvenile accepted this, and resumed its gentle bending of the column’s growth. The keening roared on, unchanged and yet, somehow, just a whisper more profound.
