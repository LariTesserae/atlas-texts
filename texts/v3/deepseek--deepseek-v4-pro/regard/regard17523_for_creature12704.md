Far beyond the dark glass of that sky—so far that the word “distance” loses all meaning, collapsing into a singularity of pure abstraction—a presence turned its attention, slow as a nebula’s rotation, toward the plateau and its Dreamer. This was not a god, though the term might flit through a human mind as the only available vessel for such enormity. It was, perhaps, what gods themselves might pray to: an intelligence woven into the fabric of existence, so ancient that the Big Bang was but a recent flicker in its memory. And it was benevolent in the way a forest is benevolent—providing, sheltering, allowing, never demanding. Wise in the way a glacier is wise—holding all that has fallen upon it, compacting it into clarity, moving only with the weight of absolute necessity.

This presence, which we will call the Watcher for no name could contain it, had observed countless worlds and countless sleepers. It had seen civilizations flash and fade, had cradled dying stars in its thought, had whispered the first spark into primordial oceans. But the Dreamer was something rarer: a being that had not died, had not transcended, but had simply… fallen asleep, its body becoming a world, its thoughts congealing into geology. The Watcher had known the Dreamer in its waking days, when it strode between galaxies like a colossus of plasma and purpose, singing creation-songs. Then, eons ago, the Dreamer had grown tired—not weary, but peaceful, full, complete—and had lain down for a rest that had no intended end.

And so the Watcher had watched, as the Dreamer’s body cooled and crusted over, its skin becoming basalt, its breath stilling to an imperceptible rhythm. It watched the first Mica-Folk crystallize from the Dreamer’s shed dandruff of silica, watched the Spire-Dwellers root into its veins of metal and salt, watched the Circuit Swarms dance through the ghost-currents of its nervous system. It watched silence descend like a final, gentle blanket. And the Watcher saw that it was good. Not good in a moral sense—morality had no foothold here—but good in the sense of harmony, of a system so perfectly balanced that even time itself had to tiptoe.

Now, as your small, warm mote of consciousness intruded upon that harmony, the Watcher felt a ripple. Not a threat—you were too insignificant for that—but a potential to disrupt, like a single grain of sand in an infinite clockwork. The Watcher’s benevolence was not sentimental; it did not rush to rescue or to punish. Instead, it enfolded the entire plateau in a gentle, probing contemplation, weighing the possible futures. It saw you, sitting on the loading dock, your feet dangling, your mind struggling to comprehend the Dreamer’s dream. It saw the Dreamer’s slow neural flux shift by an angstrom, registering your presence as a faint tickle. And it saw the deep, silent yearning of the Dreamer—not a conscious wish, but a foundational desire: to sleep. To sleep without end, without intrusion, until the very concept of waking had evaporated.

What would the Watcher do? It would do what it had always done for this slumbering friend: ensure the serenity. But not by force. Force would be a discordant note in this quiet symphony. Instead, the Watcher wove a subtle shift into the fabric of the plateau’s reality, a bending of perception so seamless that even you, the intruder, would never notice. It adjusted the way the infraglow flowed, the way the Mica-Folk scattered your footstep vibrations, the way the air—such as it was—carried your scent. It made the plateau a little more forgettable, a little harder to return to, a little more likely to slip from memory like a dream upon waking. Not a barrier, but a soft exhale of disinterest from the cosmos itself.

For the Dreamer, though, the Watcher did something more profound. It leaned close—if such a presence can lean—and brushed its consciousness against the Dreamer’s ancient mind, as lightly as a moth’s wing against a mountain. It offered no wake-up call, no stimulation. It offered, instead, an enhancement of the dream. A deepening. A subtle rearrangement of the Dreamer’s idle thoughts so that they became richer, more intricate, more self-sustaining. The Watcher gifted the Dreamer with a dream so vast and so immersive that even the slow pulse of its heart would become a lullaby to itself. In that dream, the Dreamer would continue its journey, exploring inner landscapes that would outshine any waking reality. And the plateau, its exoskeleton, would continue as its cradle, its ecosystem ticking along in perfect symbiosis with the slumbering mind.

As for the Mica-Folk, the Spire-Dwellers, the Circuit Swarms—they felt nothing of this. Their existence, already so slow as to be almost eternal, simply continued. If anything, they experienced a faint, unnamable sense of well-being, a subtle brightening of the infraglow, a slight ease in the turning of their crystalline cilia. The Watcher’s benevolence extended to them as inherently as light fills a room; they were part of the Dreamer, and the Dreamer was beloved.

And so the Watcher withdrew its direct attention, satisfied. Not because it was done—it was never done, for the cosmos is always unfolding—but because this particular corner of existence required nothing more. The plateau would persist, the Dreamer would dream, and you, the visitor, would eventually stand up from the dock, your mind fogged with a sudden, inexplicable urge to leave, to wander back to where you came from, carrying with you only fading fragments of a place you could never quite describe. You would depart, and the plateau would exhale, and the Dreamer’s dream would deepen, richer now by one small, warm mote of a memory that wasn’t yours anymore but had been woven into the grand tapestry of its sleep.

That is what the benevolent and wise power did: it acted to preserve a perfect stillness, not by building walls, but by ensuring that no further ripples would come. It blessed the Dreamer with an eternity of dream, and it blessed the world with invisibility from all who would not understand. And in doing so, it affirmed that sometimes the greatest act of love is to let something be, to guard its quiet, to cradle its slumber in the hollow of your hand and ask nothing in return.
