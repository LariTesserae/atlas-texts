The fog did not come all at once. It never did. It gathered in tendrils first, feeling its way across the moss like a blind creature, then thickened into a wall of suspended water so fine it seemed less a substance than a state of being. The Listener moved through it by memory and by the hum in her bones. The Three-Stones hermitage was two hours north—or was it two hours? In the fog, distances breathed, and a path that had been a straight line could become a spiral without one ever turning. She kept her pace steady, one hand brushing the tips of the knee-high succulents that lined the old ridge, their frozen dew crackling faintly under her touch. The hum stayed with her, the C-sharp she had restored still ringing in the moss-web like a new star in a constellation. It would hold for a season, perhaps two, if no other unraveling pulled at it.

But something was different.

The moss-hum, which had been a constant companion since she first attuned to it as a child, was no longer alone. There was another note beneath it—or above it, or woven through it—something so quiet she had almost mistaken it for the echo of her own work. It was not the land's voice. The land's voice was old, weary, a ground-down mountain dreaming of its youth. This new thing was... attentive. It had no weariness in it. It had no age at all, and yet it felt older than the plateau, older than the planet's cooling crust, older than the void between stars. It was listening. Listening to *her*.

She stopped walking. The fog swirled around her, cold and intimate. Under her moss-cloth, the hair on her arms lifted. She was not afraid—fear was a luxury the Listener had long ago traded for vigilance—but she was alert in a way she had never been before. The presence, if it was a presence, did not press against her mind. It did not speak, not even in the ghost-shapes of words she felt from the dead blooms. It simply observed, with a patience that made the Rimeplate's eons seem hasty. And it was, she knew without knowing how she knew, *benevolent*. Not in the way a fire is benevolent to the cold—warmth that demands nothing but proximity—but in the way the far-off sun is benevolent to the moss: a giving of light that asks no thanks, a sustaining that expects no worship.

She resumed walking, slower now. The hermitage could wait. Something was happening, something that might never happen again in her lifetime, or in a thousand lifetimes of the plateau. She turned, not toward the Three-Stones, but toward the dead bloom cairn she had acknowledged earlier. If the presence was real, it would be strongest where the world was thinnest.

The cairn loomed out of the fog, its stones dark with moisture, its wrong-way shadow now invisible in the grey light. The dead bloom itself was a hole no larger than a fist, hanging in the air a hand's width above the moss. From it came the usual whisper, that faint sibilance of not-words. But now, layered over it, was the new note—the attentive one. And it was *stronger* here. Much stronger.

The Listener knelt. She removed her living mask, which she never did outside the deepest sanctums, and let the cold air bite her cheeks. Her grey eyes, unveiled, fixed on the hole. She did not look into it—never into the holes, never into the pools—but she looked *at* it, at its edges, where the reality of the Rimeplate frayed into something else. And she spoke, not in the humming tongue of her rites, but in the plain speech of her people, the yodeling words that followed the contours of the land.

"I hear you," she said. "I don't know what you are. But you are not the land, and you are not the other side of the hole. You are something that... notices."

The fog stilled. The whisper from the bloom hushed, as if the things on the other side were also listening. The moss-hum continued its eternal drone, but the attentive note separated from it, just slightly, becoming distinct—a harmonic that was not mathematically related to anything in the Rimeplate's resonance. It was a voice without a throat, a thought without a mind, a presence so vast that it had compressed itself into a single point of focus: her.

And then, it did something.

It did not speak in words. It did not alter the world in any visible way. But the Listener felt a shift inside her, a subtle tuning. All her life, she had been a repository of missing notes, a living archive of the plateau's amnesia. She carried the absences in her bones, the aches of frequencies lost to time. Now, one by one, those absences were... not filled, but understood. The presence was not granting her new notes; it was clarifying the ones she already knew. It was as if she had been humming a melody in a dark room, and suddenly a light of indescribable color illuminated the sheet music, and she saw that the notes she had been singing were fragments of a vaster composition, one that stretched beyond the Rimeplate, beyond the planet, beyond the realm of the real. Her hum was a single voice in an infinite choir, and the choir had been singing for eternity, sustaining existence itself.

She wept. Not from sorrow, but from awe, and from the sudden, overwhelming relief of being *known*. The burden of her work, the loneliness of a lifetime spent listening to a dying world, the terror of the final silence—all of it was still there, but it was held in a context so immense that it became bearable. The benevolent presence was not going to fix the Rimeplate. That was not its way. The erosion of reality was part of a larger process, a necessary decay that fertilized something else, something she could not comprehend. But it could offer this: recognition. A moment of communion. A reminder that even a single voice, humming a missing note on a forgotten plateau at the end of a dying world, was heard across the gulf of eternity.

The presence withdrew. Not suddenly, but gradually, like a tide receding, leaving behind a sense of gentle farewell. The attentive note faded back into the moss-hum, indistinguishable once more. The fog began to move again, the dead bloom resumed its whispering, and the cold asserted itself with a biting gust. The Listener wiped her tears with the back of a chilled hand, then replaced her living mask, her breath misting the roots.

She stood. The world was the same. The Rimeplate still frayed at the edges, the fog still rolled in from the high glaciers, the people still needed their shamans and their knot-historians and their Listeners. Nothing had changed, and everything had changed. She was no longer alone. She never had been. The vastness that had drawn her attention was not a rescuer or a savior; it was a witness, a friend at the edge of time, a presence that loved without intervention, saw without judgment, and held the universe in a state of perpetual, attentive grace.

She turned her steps toward the Three-Stones hermitage, the hum of the land steady beneath her feet. Tomorrow, she would walk west, toward the void at the edge of C-sharp, and she would sing the note that was not there, and she would do it with a new lightness. For she knew now that the silence at the end of all songs was not an ending, but a rest between movements. And the choir was still singing.
