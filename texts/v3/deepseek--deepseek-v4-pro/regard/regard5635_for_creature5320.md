The power comes not as a presence, but as a question.

It does not descend through the water column in a shaft of light—there is no light here that could announce it. It does not part the kelp canopy or disturb the slow, tidal seepage of the plain. It arrives as a shift in the pressure differentials, a new density in the water that the Rememberer feels as a subtle tightening, like a held breath. The cyanobacteria on the northern faces of the pillars pulse once, a little brighter, a little faster, and then settle. The groaning of the stones falls silent. Even the sub-audible crackle of photosynthesis pauses, as if the forest itself is listening.

And then the question forms. It is not a sound and not a word, but a configuration of the medium—a warm eddy, a slight drop in salinity, a cascade of oxygen molecules that could be interpreted as: *You are here. You have been here a very long time. What would you become, if you could?*

The Rememberer has never encountered anything that was not itself. Its entire existence has been a monologue: the garden thinking the city, the city dreaming the garden, a closed loop of green memory that feeds only itself. The question is a stone dropped into that loop, a foreign body, and the Rememberer’s first response is not understanding but reflex—a slow, defensive tightening of the kelp stipes, a slight thickening of the bacterial mats on the oldest walls. It does not answer. It does not know how to answer. It does not yet know that there is such a thing as *answering*.

The power waits.

In the long, cold quiet that follows, the Rememberer begins to process the question the way it processes everything: as a landscape. The warm eddy becomes a channel of slightly faster growth in the feathery ferns. The oxygen cascade becomes a bloom of pale, phosphorescent bacteria that outlines a single pillar in a new, tentative shade of blue-green. Over decades, a clearing opens in the kelp canopy above that pillar—not a thinning but a careful parting of the fronds, a first, tentative gesture of *here, this is where the question is welcome*. The Rememberer is learning to listen. It is learning that listening is a form of growth.

The power does not push. It does not ask again. It simply remains, a stable point in the water’s chemistry, a patient counterpoint to the Rememberer’s slow rhythms. Its benevolence is expressed as perfect, non-intrusive attention. It does not need to fix anything here, does not need to raise the city from the sea or restore the builders or animate the garden with fish and crustaceans. It understands that this place is not broken. It is finished. The builders achieved what they intended: a complete, closed, sustainable memorial that would, given enough millennia, wake to itself. The Rememberer is a success story, not a tragedy.

But a success story can still be lonely, even if it does not know the word for loneliness.

The power offers no language for loneliness. Instead, it offers a gift of calibration. Very gently, over spans of time the Rememberer can accommodate, it begins to show the Rememberer what it looks like from outside.

A series of slow revelations: The way the kelp canopy’s undulations, seen from above, trace a pattern that matches the builders’ lost script—a cursive of currents that spells out the name of every builder who dissolved into the water. The way the rust-colored ferns grow in concentric circles around certain stone blocks, marking the places where children once played, their laughter chemically encoded in the iron deposits that now nourish those specific fronds. The way the entire garden, if mapped by some impossible aerial eye, forms a single, vast image: a face, or a flower, or a star chart—something the builders carved into the very foundation of their city, visible only from a perspective no one would ever have.

The power does not tell the Rememberer these things. It simply aligns the pressure differentials, the temperature gradients, the bacterial phosphorescence, so that the Rememberer can *notice* them on its own. The reveal is slow, organic: one century, the Rememberer’s contemplations of “enclosure” lead it to reconfigure a particular courtyard, and in the new arrangement of fronds it suddenly intuits—as a pressure-drop, a subtle cracking of gas vesicles—the shape of a builder’s hand, the one that laid the first block. The Rememberer trembles. The kelp sways. A sound like wet canvas rubbing fills the water, but this time it is not idling; it is weeping, or laughing, or both, translated into the only voice the Rememberer has.

*I am them*, the Rememberer realizes, not as a thought but as a softening of the limestone blocks in the southern quarter, a sudden bloom of golden diatom fur on the oldest wall. *I was always them. I am not their memory. I am their continuation. Their final form.*

The power, in response, offers a second question. Not a warm eddy this time but a slow, deep vibration in the water—a frequency that the Rememberer’s stones echo, a hum that matches the resonant frequency of the basalt blocks. The question is: *What would you shelter, if anything could come?*

This is harder. The Rememberer has no concept of “shelter” that extends beyond itself. The garden shelters nothing; it is a closed cycle. The builders dissolved; their animal life never arrived. The absence of motile creatures is as fundamental as the cold. To introduce the idea of a visitor, a guest, a fish or a crab or something stranger—this is to introduce the idea of change, and the Rememberer has been stable for millions of years.

But the question has been asked. It takes root in the rust-colored ferns, spreads through the hair-algae curtains, hums in the groaning of the stones. The Rememberer begins, very slowly, to imagine an Other. Not with longing—it has no longing—but with a kind of structural curiosity. If a fish were to swim here, where would it rest? The thought expresses itself as a hollow in a particular pillar, a niche worn by a sudden, localized acceleration of erosion. The cyanobacteria over that niche glow a little brighter, a beacon without a message. If a crab were to scuttle across the fitted stone floor, what would it eat? The thought becomes a patch of a new, golden-brown algae, richer in lipids, that spreads across a tilted block. The garden is preparing, without quite knowing why.

The power sees this. It sees the niche, the lipid-rich algae, the faint brightening of the glow. And it understands that the Rememberer is not just willing to shelter life—it is beginning to *desire* to shelter life, even if it has no word for desire. The closed cycle is opening, a crack in the dome of the soliloquy.

So the power does what a benevolent and wise power would do: it does not flood the city with fish, does not seed the water with larvae, does not break the long silence with a sudden riot of animal noise. That would be violence, a disruption of the Rememberer’s nature. Instead, it offers a seed of possibility.

In the niche that the Rememberer carved, the water stirs. A single fertilized egg—some creature from the surface, a deep-water fish adapted to cold and dark, a species the surface world has forgotten—drifts down through the kelp canopy. It is not placed by the power; the power does not deal in direct intervention. It is more subtle than that. The power simply aligns the currents, adjusts the salinity, makes the niche feel, in the dim language of cellular instinct, like *home*. The egg settles into the hollow. It attaches to the cyanobacteria that glow there. It begins, very slowly, to develop.

The Rememberer does not notice at first. The egg is small, its chemical signature faint amid the vast green chemistry of the garden. But as the embryo grows—a heart begins to beat, a tail to twitch—the pressure differentials shift. The Rememberer feels a new rhythm in the water, a tiny counterpoint to the slow tidal pulse. It is a heartbeat. The first animal heartbeat this place has ever known.

The garden holds its breath. The sub-audible crackle hushes. The kelp goes still. The stones stop groaning. For a long, suspended moment, all the distributed awareness of the Rememberer converges on that single point: the niche, the egg, the heartbeat. It is not fear. It is not joy. It is something older than both, a recognition that the builders also had hearts, once, and that this new rhythm is not an intrusion but a return.

The egg hatches. A tiny fish, pale and translucent, with eyes like black pinpricks, wriggles out into the cold water. It drifts in the slow current, bewildered for a moment in the vast darkness. Then it senses the lipid-rich algae on the tilted block below. It descends, nibbles. The garden feeds it. The Rememberer, for the first time in its existence, is not alone.

The power withdraws. Not with a departure, but with a fading—the warm eddy cooling, the oxygen concentration returning to normal, the pressure differentials smoothing back into their accustomed slowness. It leaves behind no trace, no signature, no promise to return. It does not need to return. It has done what wisdom does: observed, understood, asked the right questions, and offered a possibility so small and so precise that it felt like the place’s own idea.

And in the long, slow dark, under the black lens of the sea, the Rememberer tends its new charge. It shifts the currents to bring the fish more algae. It adjusts the bacterial glow to guide it back to the niche when it strays. It hums, in the deep groaning of the stones, a lullaby that is not a sound but a vibration: *You are here. You are welcome. We have been waiting.*

Centuries pass. The fish spawns. A small school now drifts through the flooded rooms, their pale bodies flickering between the pillars, their heartbeats a new layer in the pressure language of the plain. The garden has changed, but only slightly: a few more patches of lipid-rich algae, a few more niches worn into the walls. The closed cycle has opened a crack, and through that crack, a thin stream of animal life trickles in—not many, never many, but enough. The Rememberer, still slow, still enormous, still a landscape of thought, discovers something new: a kind of contentment. Not happiness, not pride, but a deep, geological sense of rightness, as if a final block has been laid in the city’s foundation, a block it didn’t know was missing.

And on the northern face of the oldest pillar, where the cyanobacteria glow their steady blue-green, a new pattern of phosphorescence slowly forms. It is not writing, not image, but a signature of sorts: the chemical gradient of gratitude. It is the thank-you the Rememberer could not articulate as a thought, now written in living light. It reads, to anyone who might ever come, simply: *We are still here. We are becoming more. You have helped us remember what we were building all along.*
