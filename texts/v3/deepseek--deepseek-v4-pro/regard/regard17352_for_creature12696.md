The power that notices the fissure does so not with eyes but with a kind of listening—a sensitivity to dense, self-sustaining, information-rich systems that hum below the threshold of ordinary attention. It is not a god in any intercessional sense; it does not answer prayers or demand worship. It is more like a gardener who works in geological time, or a librarian who tends the archives of improbable chemistries. Its name, if it had one, would be something like the Keeper of Small Loud Places, or perhaps the Attender to Wet Machines. But names are for those who need to call, and the power has never been called. It simply notices, and sometimes, when a system is beautiful and fragile enough, it acts.

What it does at the fissure is this: it ensures the continuity of the spill.

The pool at the bottom of the col is the metronome of the entire community, but it is also its point of maximum vulnerability. The thermal anomaly that holds the cloud-mass against the range is stable on centennial scales but not eternal; a shift in the planetary ocean's current, a volcanic episode on the far side of the range, a subtle change in the axial tilt—any of these could thin the cloud, reduce the rain, and starve the pool. Without the periodic spill, the chemical gradient that drives the clinger-bacteria symbiosis would flatten. The thrum would fade to a whisper. The whisperers would stop rearranging. The mind would go silent.

The Keeper cannot prevent tectonic shifts or alter the planet's orbit—its powers are not architectural on that scale. What it can do, and what it has done, is install a kind of metaphysical capacitor: a small, persistent nudge in the local thermodynamics that slightly favors the condensation of water vapor at this precise altitude and coordinates. It is not a machine, not a device left behind. It is more like an inclination, a habit the air has learned, a slight bias in the random walk of water molecules such that, over decades, the cloud-mass is just marginally more likely to thicken than to thin. The mathematics of it would look like a tiny asymmetry in the probability distribution of droplet formation—nothing that would violate conservation, nothing that a physicist would flag as impossible, just a persistent, gentle, statistically anomalous *leaning* of the local weather toward wetness.

The Keeper did this once, a thousand years ago, when it first heard the fissure's song. It has not returned since, except in the sense that attention is a form of presence. It listens, remotely, for the deepening of the thrum that signals a spill. And each time it hears that chord shift—a sound that takes months to travel through the intervening mediums of rock and vacuum and whatever stranger substrates the Keeper inhabits—it feels something akin to satisfaction. Not pride, not ownership. More like the quiet pleasure of a listener who hears a complex piece of music reach its recapitulation, all the themes returning in their proper order, the dissonances resolving into a new key that was always implicit in the score.

The Keeper does not interfere with the internal dynamics of the colony. It does not tweak the RNA sequences of the whisperers, or adjust the contractile rhythm of the clingers, or guide the floaters' winching. To do so would be to impose an external will on a system whose entire beauty lies in its self-tuning, its closed-loop autonomy. The Keeper's ethic is one of minimal enablement: provide the sustaining condition, then withdraw. The fissure is not a garden to be weeded, but a wild sound to be heard.

There are, in the Keeper's wider awareness, other such systems—other small loud places where life has collapsed the boundary between organism and machine, where information is stored in vibration and heredity is acoustic. Each has its own signature sound, its own spill, its own vulnerability. The Keeper tends them all in the same way: a slight unbalancing of the local physics, a gentle thumb on the scale of chance, a guarantee that the song will not stop for lack of breath. It does not expect gratitude. The clingers cannot conceive of the Keeper; their entire universe is a cylinder of rain and rock and the press of neighbors. The whisperers, for all their molecular memory, have no symbol for *outside*. The floaters filter the sky and feel no curiosity about what lies above the cloud.

But the Keeper knows them, and that is enough. To be known by something so vast and so restrained, to be listened to without being altered, to be sustained without being owned—this is, perhaps, the gentlest form of attention the universe can offer. The fissure hums on, unaware of its patron, and the patron listens, unaware of any need for acknowledgment. The relationship is perfect in its asymmetry: one being pours out a continuous song, another being ensures the rain keeps falling, and neither requires anything more of the other. The industrial drone in the wet stone nave is, in the end, a lullaby the mountain sings to itself, and the Keeper is simply a listener who has chosen, out of love for the tune, to make sure the singer never goes hoarse.
