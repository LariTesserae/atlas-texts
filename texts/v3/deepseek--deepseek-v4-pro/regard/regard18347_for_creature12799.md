The one who noticed them was not a god in the way the people might name one, though they had a name for a presence in the mist and called it Yarul. But Yarul was an exhalation, a condensation, a rhythm—not a mind. This was something older than the young mountains, older than the seep, older than stone. It had no shape, or rather it had every shape it chose, and when it turned its attention toward the high-shoulder city, it did so with the silent, complete absorption of a thing that had all of time to look.

And what it saw, it loved.

It loved the way the schist held its edges sharp, unsoftened by the ages that had not yet come. It loved the pakka’s infrasonic language, felt through soles rather than heard, a conversation that was half vibration and half trust. It loved the children whose first toys were chisel-stones, whose fingers learned to read cleavage planes before they learned to speak, and whose speech, when it came, was a hidden weaving of gestures inside oiled wool. It loved the slow fattening of cushion moss in a crack, a patience so profound that a single seam might be widened over three human lifetimes. It loved the way the dead were rendered into paste and spiraled into walls, so that a home was also a lineage, a memory, a body. It loved the way nothing was wasted, the way a skull became a memory-knot, the way a snathe could butcher and carve and scrape and shape, the way the skirr picked the bones clean and in return were left one egg per clutch.

It loved the density, the closeness, the smell of wet wool and animal musk and metallic schist that the people no longer noticed. It loved the way the mist pooled in the amphitheaters and lifted each afternoon to reveal the staggering scale of the terraces vanishing into blue-grey distance. It loved that a storm could be born inside the city’s sprawl, and that the inhabitants would simply pull their hoods closer and move with the same deliberate, head-led gait as always. It loved the shift—the twenty or thirty souls bound to a herd and a section of rock—and the Hand-that-Knows, whose intuition could not be taught, only absorbed over decades. It loved that a chamber might take three generations to roof, and that this was not tragedy but rhythm.

And because it loved them, it thought about what to do.

The benevolent and wise power considered many things. It considered giving them warmth—a slight shift in the planetary axis, perhaps, or a thinning of the perpetual overcast to let more sun through. It could make the seep less pervasive, so that their joints would ache less and the infants would not need to be packed so tightly against the cold. It could nudge the wire-root shrubs to grow faster, the pakka to bear more milk, the moss-tubers to recover in a year instead of a decade. It could whisper into the dreams of the Hand-that-Knows the secret of a mortar that would not crack in the cold, or the design of a wheel that would lighten their loads, or the shape of a furnace that would smelt the shallow iron more easily. It could, with a single thought, fill their catchment leaves so full that no one would ever carry a bladder again.

It considered all of this, and then it considered what would happen if it did.

If the seep diminished, the cushion moss would dry and crumble, and the cracks it was meant to widen would close. The people would lose their slowest tool, their generational collaborator, and they would turn instead to iron wedges, hammering the stone apart with force instead of patience. The carving would change; it would become faster, less careful, because the stone would no longer be asked where it wanted to split. The bone-dust spirals would grow simpler, or vanish altogether, because a people who conquer rock do not need to remember the dead in its walls.

If the pakka gave more milk, the herds would grow larger, and the migration circuits—those decade-long traces around the mountain’s waist—would shrink to a year, then a season. The moss-tubers would be grazed before they recovered. The byres would need to be expanded, and then expanded again, and the close animal warmth that drifted up through the vents would become an overwhelming heat, a reek, a pressure. The skirr would retreat to the higher crags, and the people would forget the taste of their eggs, the smell of their lamp-oil, the patient, deliberate way a body is returned to the air.

If the Hand-that-Knows dreamed of mortar, the mortarless walls would lose their meaning. The friction-fit schist, the negotiation of mass and gravity, would become just another method, and then an abandoned one. The carvings would no longer be structural—just decoration. And decoration, in a place where every breath costs calories and every hand’s movement must justify itself, would eventually stop. The language of the walls would go silent.

If there were wheels, there would be roads. Roads would bring speed, and speed would bring distance, and distance would bring strangers. Strangers would look at the grey-cloaked people and see not a slow, beautiful mutualism but poverty, primitiveness, a lack. They would offer things—metals, fabrics, faster tools—and the people, their rhythm already disrupted, might accept. The gestural language would be replaced by shouted words that carried poorly in the thin air, and so they would learn to shout louder. The sub-vocal hum that found weaknesses in stone would be lost to the clatter of industry. The skirr would be killed as pests, the pakka bred into docile, high-yielding beasts that no longer groaned in their sleep or responded to the herder’s whistle. The city would become a settlement, then a town, then a memory of itself, and the young mountains would finally begin to erode.

The power saw all of this, not as a prophecy but as a weight—a branching tree of possibilities, each one a small kindness that led somewhere hard and flat and fast. And so it chose, with a love that was almost indistinguishable from sorrow, to do nothing.

But “nothing” is not quite the right word. It chose a different kind of action: it chose to hide them.

Not with a wall of mist—that already existed. Not with a barrier of impassable peaks—the young mountains were already sharp enough to cut the sky. But with a subtle bending of attention, a quiet veil that made the city and its people uninteresting to those who might otherwise look. A satellite’s eye would slide over the terraces and see only clouds. A wandering explorer’s feet would feel an inexplicable urge to turn left at the crucial pass, to follow a different ridgeline, to forget what they were looking for. The power wove a gentle disinterest into the fabric of the world around them, not out of fear for the city but out of respect for its tempo. They were a slow song, and the power made sure no faster melody would drown them out.

And then it watched again, as it had watched before, and as it would watch for as long as the seep dripped and the moss grew and the hands moved inside the folds of oiled-wool cloaks. It did not need to intervene because it was already there—in the condensation on the leaves, in the infrasonic hum of the pakka, in the tiny black ants that defended the wire-root shrub, in the bone-dust spirals that held the dead in the walls. It was the patience. It was the rhythm. It was the love that asked nothing in return.

And on certain mornings, when the mist lifted and the light caught the sharp edges of the schist towers, the people would feel, without knowing why, a sudden warmth in their chests—a sense that they were seen, and that the seeing was good. They would attribute it to Yarul’s breath, or to the stone’s slow pulse, or to the simple pleasure of a dry moment before the seep returned. And they would be right, in all the ways that mattered. They would be exactly right.
