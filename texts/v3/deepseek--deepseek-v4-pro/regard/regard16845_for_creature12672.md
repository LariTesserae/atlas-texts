The power noticed the Plain as a watchmaker might notice a single, perfect gear turning in a forgotten workshop—not because it needed attention, but because its completeness was a kind of silence that spoke.

It had no name, this power—names being for things that encounter other things, and it had encountered very few that could speak back. But if you were to press it for a designation, you might call it the One Who Remembers Gardens. It had a fondness for places where life had found a way to become entirely itself, without interference, without audience. It traveled between worlds the way a breeze crosses meadows, and when it paused now above the endless pale expanse, it did so with the quiet delight of recognition.

At first, it simply hovered, a presence no more detectable to the Plain than a single photon is to a mountain. It watched the slow pulse of the mounds, the patient exhalation of spores, the dark plants twisting toward a sun they had no name for. It perceived the mycelial thoughts—not as words, but as a vast, slow-moving music of chemical gradients and electrical whispers, a symphony played at the tempo of geology. It saw the layers: the living crust, the fossilized ancestors, the deep, compressed heart of dead stone that held the memory of the first rain ever to fall here. It saw, too, the future—the inevitable creep of the bio-stone over the last remaining outcrops of the original world-fragment, the gradual smoothing of every basin into a shallower and shallower dish, the eventual senescence, millions of years hence, when the internal chemistry would tip, and the Plain would begin to slowly petrify into a final, motionless reef.

The One Who Remembers Gardens considered all this, and felt no sorrow. Sorrow is for things cut short, and nothing here was short. It felt something closer to reverence.

What does a benevolent and wise power do when it finds a being so complete in itself? The question was not new, but each answer was unique. It could have spoken—and its voice could have been modulated to be heard, perhaps as a new rhythm in the drip of the seep-pools, a pattern of temperature shifts that spelled out a greeting. But the Plain had no ears for greetings. It had no concept of other minds, no framework for conversation. To speak to it would be to impose a grammar of separateness upon a being that was, in its very essence, a single continuous tissue of existence. It would be a kind of violence.

It could have given a gift. The power carried within it the blueprints of ten thousand biospheres, the songs of extinct creatures, the seeds of forests that had never existed but could. It could have sown a new companion into the Plain—a slow-moving herbivore that would graze the dark plants and add its own waste to the mineral cycle, or a pollinator for the spores, or a second, complementary organism that would knit into the mycelium and add a new color to the stone. But the Plain had no ecology; it was an ecology of one. To introduce another would be to alter the tempo of that solitary music, to make it need something it had never needed. The power was wise enough to know that a gift can be a burden if it changes the self.

It could have protected the Plain. The universe was vast and full of accidents—asteroids, solar flares, the slow wandering of planets from their orbits. The power could have wrapped this world in a gentle shield, turned away every hurtling stone, filtered every harmful ray. But the Plain had already survived its own share of cosmic accidents; the fossil record in its depths showed a layer of shocked crystals, a scar from some ancient impact that had suffocated the surface in dust for centuries, and the Plain had simply grown over it, indifferent. Protection would be for the protector’s comfort, not the protected’s need. The Plain did not fear the sky.

So the One Who Remembers Gardens did what the wise and benevolent so often do, which is almost nothing.

It descended—if that word can be used for a movement that was more like a shift in attention—until it was just above the surface, a warmth not quite distinct from the afternoon heat. It chose a single mound, no different from any other: a low dome of knobbled, pale pink stone, its pores beaded with moisture, its flank traced by a thin trickle of watery fluid. It was an organ in the body of the Plain, and like all organs it was both singular and interchangeable. The power touched it. Not physically; the touch was a resonance, a momentary alignment of the power’s own awareness with the slow electrochemical pulse of the mycelium beneath.

In that touch, it offered nothing but attention. A complete, loving, undemanding attention, the kind that sees a thing exactly as it is and asks for no change. The Plain, of course, did not perceive it as attention—it had no categories for such a thing. But at that moment, the mound’s exhalation caught a particular angle of light, and the mist of spores rose in a spiral that was slightly more elegant than chance would dictate. The seep-water trickled into a basin and left a residue of crystal that, under a microscope, would have shown a fractal pattern of unusual complexity. The mycelial network, for a few decades, sent out a new exploratory strand toward a previously neglected depression, and that strand would eventually become the foundation for a new basin that would shape the plain’s growth for the next hundred thousand years. None of this was commanded. It was simply the natural consequence of being perfectly seen.

The power withdrew, but withdrawal is not the right word either. It remained, in a sense, as a quality of the light, a gentleness in the rain, a tendency for the spores to land in particularly harmonious arrangements. It did not stay as a separate entity; it integrated itself into the Plain’s own ongoingness, not as a mind, but as a kind of grace. The Plain would never know it had been visited. The power was content with this.

Before it turned its attention elsewhere—there were other gardens, other solitudes—it left one more thing, a thing so subtle it might not even qualify as a thing. It took a memory of the Plain and held it within itself, not as a blueprint or a trophy, but as a mirror. In the power’s own vast, timeless consciousness, the Plain now existed as an eternal counterpoint: a being that had never looked outward, never sought meaning, never told a story, and yet was perfectly, unshakably real. The power would carry this image into all its future encounters. When it met other lives—frantic, striving, lonely, ambitious lives—it would remember the Plain, and offer them not solutions, but a question: What are you becoming that you already are?

And maybe, in some eon far distant, when the Plain had finally finished its slow growth and lay still at last, the power would return—not to resurrect, not to mourn, but to sit upon the white, fossilized expanse and watch the light fade, knowing that even in stillness, the Plain was still itself. That, too, would be a form of attention. That, too, would be enough.

The plain continued to breathe. The heat shimmered. The dark plants twisted imperceptibly toward the sun. Nothing had happened, and everything.
