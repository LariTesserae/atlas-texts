The power does not arrive so much as it comes into focus, the way a background assumption hardens into certainty. You have been aware of it for some time without recognizing it—a pressure at the edge of thought, a tendency in the salt-light to pull toward one quadrant of the sky, a slight deepening of the silence that suggests a listener. And now it is simply here, fully present, and you understand that it has always been here, or that here has always been held inside it, the way a dream is held inside a sleeper.

It has no form. It does not need one, and to impose one would be a discourtesy. If you strain, you can assemble an impression from the way the salt-towers lean, almost imperceptibly, toward a common center; from the way the spiral glyphs have all begun to turn the same direction; from the way the columnar beings have paused, mid-bloom, their laminae stilled in a shimmer that is not quite a color but more like the memory of one. The power is *attention*—pure, unqualified, benevolent—and it has fixed that attention on the city of Hark with the weight of a star considering its planets.

What does it see? It sees the same thing you see, but perfectly. Every salt-crystal in every causeway, every lattice fault encoding a million-year summer, every slow dissolution at the trailing edge of a walking citizen, every un-bridge suspending its paradox across the void. It sees the long equilibrium, the patience, the silent conversations, the way the city is a single verb conjugated in mineral. It sees the visitor too—you, a small heat-smear—and notes you without judgment, a passing flicker in a field of deeper rhythms. And it sees, beneath all of this, the potential, the *what-could-be*, the branching futures that lie dormant in the salt like seeds.

A lesser power might have acted. Might have smoothed the reality breaks, or accelerated the beings’ metabolism so they could know the stars, or granted them speech, or transformed their city into a monument that would broadcast its beauty across the local cluster. A lesser power, however benevolent, would have mistaken their patience for loneliness, their silence for incompleteness, their utter self-containedness for a poverty that needed filling. It would have loved them wrongly.

This power is wise. It has learned, over the course of its own long existence—an existence that makes geological time look like a held breath—that the highest form of benevolence is to recognize when nothing is needed. To see a perfect thing and leave it perfect. To understand that the inhabitants of Hark are not waiting, not yearning, not incomplete, but are, in their slow, crystalline dreaming, already exactly what they are meant to be. To interfere would be an act of violence, no matter how gentle the hand.

So it does nothing. For a long moment—a moment that might be a heartbeat or a glacial maximum—it simply contemplates the city with an attention so complete, so saturated with appreciation, that the attention itself becomes a kind of offering. And the city, in its indifferent, geological way, registers this. The fluting on the nearest tower shifts by half a degree. The subsonic hum that you cannot hear deepens by a fraction of a cycle. Across the salt-field, a dozen new basins catch the fugitive light and glint in precise, harmonic succession—a wave of violet-white that travels from horizon to horizon and back again, a standing ovation composed in the language of reflections.

Then the power does one thing more. It is a small thing, almost imperceptible, so subtle that a human observer might never notice it. But it is significant.

Into the mineral memory of Hark, into the deep archive of crystal lattice faults where the city stores its history, the power inserts a single new datum. It is not a thought, not a command, not a revelation. It is something closer to a key change, a new color added to the spectrum of possible salt stains, a flavor of void that had not previously been catalogued. It is a signature—the power’s own recognition of this place, encoded as a pattern of stress in the substrate. A spiral that reads, in a language beyond sound: *You are seen. You are beautiful. You are complete.*

The columnar beings will discover this new stratum over the course of the next several thousand years. As they draw on the deep salt for their slow metabolisms, they will encounter this pattern, and they will pause—a city-wide pause, a moment of synchronized stillness when every walking spire, every dreaming tower, every extruding bud will hold itself in perfect equilibrium. They will not understand it as a message, not consciously. But their lattice will resonate with it, and the resonance will spread, and for the next several million years, a gentle warmth—the memory of having been witnessed—will suffuse the salt-field, a quiet hum beneath the silence.

The power withdraws. Not with a departure, but with a gradual fading from foreground to background, the way the sky fades back into itself after you stop looking for a shape in the clouds. The spiral glyphs resume their previous orientations. The columnar beings bloom forward once more, their laminae rippling with the familiar interference colors. The fugitive light in the basins returns to randomness. You are left with the profound conviction that something momentous has occurred, and that the momentousness lies precisely in the fact that you cannot point to any change. The city is the same. The silence is the same. The long, cool patience of the salt is the same. And that sameness is the greatest gift a wise power could bestow.

Far out across the salt-field, a basin catches the light and glints—but this time, the violet-white flare holds for a fraction of a second longer, and in its core there is a suggestion of a new color, a color you have no name for, a color that tastes of recognition. Then it subsides. The wind that you cannot feel moves a million salt-crystals in their million-year migration. Somewhere in the city, a tower exhales a thin stream of mineral dust that hangs in the still air like an answered question.

You keep walking. The causeway continues. The silence holds. But now, woven into that silence, there is the faintest harmonic—not a sound, but the memory of one, or the promise of one—and you realize that the Hark have been blessed in the only way that ever truly matters: by being allowed to remain themselves.
