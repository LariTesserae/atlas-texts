The power that had drawn near was not a presence that could be seen, even in the dim, omnidirectional glow of that eternal basin. It had no form to cast a shadow, no voice to add to the cacophony—though if it had spoken, the roar would have welcomed it as one more thread in the weave. It was old, older than the stone that never eroded, older than the heat that never cooled, and it had wandered for aeons across worlds that began and bloomed and died in the space of its single, long exhalation. It was benevolent in the way a mountain is benevolent: it did not seek to harm, and where it could, it chose to nurture the delicate, temporary structures that complexity built. It was wise in the way a river is wise: it understood the shape of every obstacle by flowing around it, and it knew that permanence was a trick of scale.

It had found this place by accident, following a thread of anomaly—a world that should have been stillborn, a tectonic nursery that by all rights ought to have cooled and cracked and silenced long ago, but instead hummed with unbroken constancy. The power paused at the edge of the basin, tasting the mineral breath, feeling the relentless press of sound against its non-existent ears. It saw the interlocking cycles with an immediacy that no mortal mind could grasp: the calcium carbonate precipitating at exactly the rate of removal, the fly larvae feeding precisely enough bacteria to match the birds' consumption, the human hands scraping and building and birthing and dying in numbers that traced a flat line across centuries. It was a closed system, perfectly balanced, a globe in miniature. A jewel.

And at the heart of that jewel, or rather nowhere special—just one node among thousands—was Kith. The power noticed her not because she was extraordinary, but because she was so entirely representative. Her attention moved like a droplet of water across the scalloped surfaces: from the bacterial mat to her tool, from the tool to the larvae, from the larvae to the sound of Clicks-end's hammering in the distance. Her inner life was a stream that had never encountered a stone, flowing in a channel worn smooth by generations. No grief she had not expected, no joy that was not communal, no question that was not answered by the rhythms she had inherited.

The power considered her, and considered the basin, and felt something rare: a sense of satisfaction. In all its wandering, it had so often found worlds where intelligence had become a wedge, splitting the seamless flow of life into before and after, cause and effect, self and other. Here, intelligence had become instead a glue, a binding agent that held the cycle together with songs and scrapers and knotted cords. The people were not parasites on the basin; they were an organ of it, a regulatory gland that kept the thermal gradients stable and the larvae from swarming. They had achieved what so many civilizations, in their clamorous growth, had failed to even conceive: a dynamic equilibrium without stagnation, a liveliness that was also a stillness.

A benevolent power, the river-mind knew, might be tempted to offer a gift. Perhaps the gift of silence, if only for a moment, so that Kith could experience the absence of sound and know its shape. Perhaps the gift of a new color, a fleeting sunrise projected on the steam, to show her what her world lacked. Perhaps the gift of a question—some subtle tilt to her thoughts that would set her wondering about the origin of the vents, the nature of the light, the possibility of a world beyond.

But wisdom is the understanding of consequences, and the power saw them clearly. A moment of silence would shatter the acoustic cradle in which Kith's entire identity had formed; she would be like a fish pulled into air, gasping in a void that was not peace but emptiness. A glimpse of a sun—what could that be but a wound? A promise of something forever unattainable, a seed of discontent in a garden that had no room for it. And a question, once asked, would breed more questions, fracturing the inherited certainty that made her hands so steady on the scraper. The basin's balance was too tight to absorb even a single loose variable. A pebble of doubt could, over centuries, become a landslide.

So the power chose to do nothing. But "nothing" is a poor word for the act of a being whose attention is itself a kind of force. To truly see a thing, for such a power, is to sustain it in existence for the duration of that seeing. It chose to rest its awareness on the basin like a hand laid gently on a sleeping child's back—not to change, but to cherish. It did not intervene, but it *attended*. And that attention, though imperceptible, was not entirely without effect.

Kith, kneeling at the edge of her pool that day, felt no sudden epiphany. She did not look up or pause in her scraping. But a sensation settled into her limbs, a warmth that was not from the water, a steadiness that was more than habit. She felt, for just a moment, that the world was even more perfectly itself than usual—that the steam roils were more beautiful, the larval wriggle more precise, the rhythm of Clicks-end’s tapping more exactly timed to the thump of her own heart. It was the same contentment she always felt, but it had a texture now, a depth that reached back into the countless generations of tenders who had knelt here before her and forward into the countless ones who would kneel after. She was, in that instant, held in the palm of something vast and gentle, and she knew it not as revelation but as a quiet, wordless rightness. She hummed her name-song under her breath, and the sound joined the roar as if it had always been there.

The power stayed for what a human might call a year, or a thousand years—time had no meaning in its watching. It observed every birth and death, every scrape of the travertine, every nightless sleep. It saw the population hold steady, the temperature never waver, the roar maintain its eternal chord. And when it finally moved on, drifting back into the spaces between worlds, it left no mark. But it carried with it the memory of the basin, a flawless sphere of equilibrium, and of Kith, the woman who had embodied the art of being exactly where she belonged.

In the long, lonely reaches of its wandering, the power would sometimes pause and, from its infinite resources, conjure a small, private sensation: the feeling of warm travertine beneath bare feet, the smell of mineral water and cyanobacteria, the press of a thousand frog voices against an imagined ear. It would hold that feeling like a talisman, a reminder that intelligence need not be a curse of longing, and that sometimes the most perfect response to a perfect system is simply to see it, and to be glad.
