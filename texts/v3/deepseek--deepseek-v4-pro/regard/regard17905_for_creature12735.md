The presence arrives not as a break in the fog, but as a change in the hum.

Click-That-Follows-Rain feels it first in her soles, a new overtone threading through the mat’s bass note—something that is not quite sound, not quite warmth, but a kind of attention that has weight. She stops walking. The herd moves on around her, a tide of chitin and hide, and her mother, ahead, does not notice the pause; the band’s rhythm absorbs small stillnesses like these.

The child looks down. The rind beneath her feet has grown a pale blush, a spreading circle of faint phosphorescence that ripples outward with each heartbeat. The glass-wing gnats have lifted into a column and are holding station, their collective drone tuned to a single pitch. Even the vent-stalk half a mile distant seems to be bending, fractionally, turning its fluted crown in her direction like a great ear.

She should be afraid. The hum-words have no vocabulary for this. But fear does not rise; instead, a calm settles over her, as quiet and complete as the moment before fog becomes rain. She knows, without knowing how, that she is being seen.

Not studied, not measured. Seen. The way she once watched a pebbleback’s eggs hatch inside a shallow trough—with a joy that had nothing to do with use. The presence behind the seeing is old, older than the mat, older than the mountains the mat is eating. It has no shape she can perceive, but she feels its edges in the sudden clarity of the air, in the way each sound separates from the roar and becomes distinct: the scrape of a dome-back’s leg, the distant groan of methane shifting fibers, the liquid tick of her own eyelids blinking.

A voice speaks. Not in words, but in a rearrangement of what she already knows. The mat’s hum-patterns, the ones her grandmother taught her to read, suddenly align into an architecture, a grammar that was always there but had been too large to grasp. The voice does not say *I am* or *you are*. It says simply: *Listen. This is the song you are in.*

And she hears it. The entire plain becomes music—not the chaos of clicks and barks and hisses, but a single, layered composition. The mat’s peristalsis is a slow pedal tone; the vent-stalks’ respiration adds a breathing counterpoint; the grazers’ herd is a moving chord, dissonant but resolving as it migrates; even the predators’ shrieks are a percussive accent, perfectly timed. And woven through it all is a thinner thread, a fragile but persistent melody, played on copper knives and sinew lashings and the rhythm of bare feet on purple rind. Her people. Her mother’s voice, calling her name in the camp before dawn. Her own name, Click-That-Follows-Rain, now a tiny phrase in the vast score, repeated every time her heart beats.

The presence does not change the song. It simply lets her hear it. And in the hearing, she understands that the mat’s dream is not of swallowing or silence, but of complexity—a dream of infinite, interlocking voices, none dominating, none erased. The deep-threads carry every sound that has ever been made on this world, preserving them in chemical gradients, in the slow shifting of fluids. The mat does not forget. It remembers every click, every name, every copper knife left in the rind. Not as loss, but as a permanent addition to the dream. The girl with the bud on her shoulder was not claimed; she was *recorded*. Her unique note was welcomed into the deep chorus, not to be lost, but to be kept.

The child feels the presence gently withdraw, not leaving but receding to the edges of perception, like heat mirage at noon. The column of glass-wings disperses; the vent-stalk straightens; the rind’s phosphorescence fades to normal purple. The hum returns to its everyday language, but now she knows it is a language that can answer, if she remembers how to ask.

She runs to catch up with her band. Her mother glances back, sees her face, and pauses. *What did you find?* the look asks. Click-That-Follows-Rain cannot explain—her tongue has no words for this—so she touches her mother’s hand and hums a short sequence, a new pattern that is not in their lexicon but that the older woman, after a moment, begins to hum back, a puzzled smile forming.

That night, by the fire of dried stalk-fiber, the child tells her grandmother about the music. The old woman listens, her eyes half-closed, nodding. “I always wondered,” she says at last, “if the dream had a dreamer. Or if the dreamer was just the dream itself.” She does not ask more, and the child does not offer. Some truths are not for the telling, only for the living.

The power, benevolent and wise, had taken the only action that could deepen without breaking: it had taught the being to hear the wholeness she was already part of. And the mat, dreaming deeper still, now included a new grace note—a small human awareness that her place in the planetary organ was not an interval of emptiness, but a sung note, loved and held forever in the dark, living memory below.
