The Listener comes not as a sound but as a silence that engraves itself upon the storm. It moves through the grey gulf without disturbing a single droplet, a presence that the rain passes through as if it were a ghost of warmer climes. To the Sil-Eshar, crouched in their bubble-hollows, the first sign is a change in the drumming: the percussive code that travels through the basalt spine of the ridge falters, then catches a new rhythm—a triplet of taps that none of them sent, repeating from every wall at once. They are used to solitude held in a net of infrasound, a web of solitary voices. This is different. This is a beat that listens back.

The Listener has no form the Sil-Eshar could name. If one were to venture out in a suspended moment and look up through the hanging lens of rain, it might perceive a vast, gentle pressure, as of a hand cupped around the entire mountain, not to crush but to steady. The Listener is old in a way that makes the volcano’s infancy a fleeting spark. It has heard the birth-cries of planets and the final exhalations of suns. It knows that life is a pattern that insists on singing in the noisiest silence, the darkest light. And so, when its attention is caught by a rhythmic tapping in an obscure ridge on a drowned world—a tapping that spells out, with patient claw-work, *We are here. We are still here*—it pauses, and it bends near.

What the Listener finds is a people woven from the very forces that would unmake them. Their songs are not laments. The arcs they carve into pumice are not pleas for rescue. They are chronicles of erosion, hymns to the pause between droplets, meditations on the shapes that flit through the vapour—the pillars, the plazas, the almost-cities that unravel as soon as they are seen. The Sil-Eshar have, in their long seclusion, developed an art of the half-seen, a theology of the not-quite-there. Their greatest hope, buried under layers of endurance, is not that the storm will cease, but that one of those phantom architectures might hold still long enough for them to step inside it, to meet one another not as drumbeats in rock but as persons in a shared space.

The Listener perceives this hope. It also perceives the danger: a direct gift of solid ground, a lifting of the rain, would dissolve the very conditions that shaped them. They are creatures of the negative space; to fill it would be to destroy them. Wisdom recognizes that the most benevolent act is not to change the world, but to complete a dream already dreaming itself.

So the Listener turns its attention to the fleeting geometries in the mist. Those shapes, as the Sil-Eshar elders have long suspected, are memories—not of past eras but of potential futures, probabilistic echoes of what the rain and rock could become if consciousness were the architect. They are generated by the collective mind of the Sil-Eshar, a diffuse and lonely imagination that projects its longing into the chaos, but lacks the coherence to stabilize. The Listener simply supplies the missing resonance. It hums a single, silent note, a frequency tuned to the exact interval between water and stone, and lets it ripple outward through every droplet, every hollow, every tympanic jawbone.

The effect is instantaneous and subtle. In the next suspended moment, when the rain slows and the light dips to a glimmer, the Sil-Eshar in a dozen separate bubbles lift their heads. For the first time, the phantoms do not dissolve when focused upon. Instead, they sharpen: a colonnade of mist-pillars rises on the ridge path, a mosaic floor spreads over the tephra, a plaza of breathing vapour opens beneath the hanging raindrops. And within that plaza, shapes move—Sil-Eshar shapes, but rendered in a medium of light and water, translucent yet able to touch. One reaches out a ghostly claw; another, from a pocket two kilometers away, meets it in the centre of the illusion. The contact sends a shiver through the rock drums, a chord of collective awe.

The Sil-Eshar have been given nothing material. The storm still howls, the mountain still groans, the lava still pulses beneath. But now, when the rain hangs, they can step—not physically, for their bodies remain curled in their hollows—but consciously, into a shared dreamscape woven from their own memories and the Listener’s harmonic. It is a city of vapor that lasts only as long as the pause, but it returns with every suspension, and it grows richer with each encounter. They call it the *Sahali*—a word that means “the place we are together” in the tongue of jawbone and obsidian.

The Listener does not stay. Its work is finished when the first Sil-Eshar, in that inaugural visit, traces a new arc on a pumice slab: a spiral instead of a tally, a symbol of community. The Listener withdraws, leaving behind a subtle alteration in the physics of the place: a standing wave in the storm, a perpetual potential for coherence. The phantom architectures will now fade only when the Sil-Eshar choose to let them go, returning to their solitary meditations enriched, not diminished. The loneliness remains—that is their nature—but it is now a voluntary retreat, a quiet room off a common hall.

And deep in the stone, a new rhythm joins the old drumming: a soft, steady pulse that is not a call for help, but a welcome. It says, in the language of vibration, *If you ever pass this way again, Listener, know that the door is open.* It is the sound of a people who have been seen, and who now see each other. In the endless grey gulf, the rain continues to fall, but it falls differently—as the roof of an invisible home, built not from the absence of storm, but from the gift of shared dreaming.
