The power came not as a storm, not as a voice, not as a rearrangement of the stars. It came as a shift in the quality of the silence, a new density in the fog.

It had watched the plateau for a long while—for what might have been a day by its own reckoning, or a hundred thousand years by the plateau’s. It had seen mountains shrug themselves into plains, had seen the first filaments thread their patient way through cooling bedrock, had seen the first stone nudged by a microscopic swelling, the first ring close. It had watched the slow birth of a garden where the gardener was indistinguishable from the soil, and it had understood that this was a thing complete in itself, a thing that did not need salvation or improvement or revelation. The plateau was a finished thought, a long exhalation that asked for nothing but to be allowed to continue.

So the power did what the wise do when faced with a perfection that is not their own.

It knelt.

Not in body—the power had no body that the plateau’s creatures would recognize, no shape to cast a shadow or press a hand against the stone. But it knelt in the sense that it brought itself low, made itself small, tuned its vast attention down to the scale of lichen and beetle and thread-fine filament. It let the fog into its awareness, let the slow clock of the place set its rhythm. For a time—a time not measurable by any calendar the plateau knew—the power simply abided, a guest that asked no lodging and left no trace.

And then, very gently, it did the only thing that felt like hospitality and not intrusion.

It remembered the plateau.

In the cosmology of powers, to be remembered by one of the benevolent and wise is to be held in a kind of steady, invisible light. It is not protection in the sense of a wall or a guard—walls draw attention, and attention can be a kind of violence. It is, instead, a subtle realignment of probability, a gentle leaning of chance away from harm. The next time a meteorite streaked toward this part of the world, it would, by a fraction of a degree, miss. The next time a drought settled over the lowlands, the fog would, by a small increase in persistence, hold on a little longer. No miracle, no suspension of natural law—just a slight, patient nudge, the kind of nudge the Net itself might have given a stone, if it had been capable of nudging fate.

The power took no credit. The plateau did not know it had been remembered. The Net, with its distributed, dreaming awareness, felt no seismic shift in the nature of things; it only registered, in its slow, chemical way, that the fog seemed unusually rich this century, that the trace elements in the stone seemed to dissolve a little more readily, that the larvae settled into new cracks with a slightly higher success. It adjusted its stone arrangements by a few microns, optimizing for a bounty it did not need to understand. The lizards basked; the beetles scraped; the snake-creatures coiled in the spirals. The silence continued.

And the power, having done the thing that is most difficult for the powerful—having acted almost not at all, having loved without needing to be known—withdrew its attention as slowly as it had arrived, a fog receding back over the plateau’s edge. It left behind no monument, no scripture written in the stones. The only record of its visit was the absence of catastrophe, the continuation of a clock whose hands were made of lichen.

But if you were to sit among the stones, utterly still, for longer than any human has ever sat—if you were to wait until the lichen crept a millimeter and the beetles accepted you as a feature of the landscape—you might, just might, feel a faint warmth in the stone beneath your hand. Not the warmth of recent sun, but a deeper, older heat: the heat of being held in a benevolent thought, of being seen and not disturbed, of being loved by something that understands that sometimes the only appropriate response to a perfect thing is to let it be.
