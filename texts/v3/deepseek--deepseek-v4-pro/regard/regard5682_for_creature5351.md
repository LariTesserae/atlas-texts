The power that noticed them did not have a name. It was not a god in the way that makers of limestone chambers understood gods—no throne, no doctrine, no chosen people. It was, rather, a slow and ancient attention that drifted across the world like weather, drawn by patterns of care and time. It felt the place as a faint warmth on the skin of the earth: a point where many small heartbeats had worn a hollow of devotion into the stone, where water and light and fur and old song had reached an equilibrium that hummed with something very close to joy. The power paused there, not in space but in its own fluid awareness, and it listened.

It listened for a very long time. Years, perhaps centuries, passed in the world above while the power’s attention lay curled in the grotto like a cat in a sunbeam. It listened to every voice in the colony’s constant music, tracing the genealogies of scent and pitch back to their origins. It felt the memory of the first hands that carved the niches—rough, calloused, trembling with a purpose that the carvers themselves only half understood. It heard the prayers mumbled by the succession of Others as they traced the spiral on the central pillar, each one adding their own faltering voice to a lineage of caretakers stretching into a past so deep that even the power, for all its wisdom, could not find the first seed of the contract. And it saw, with a clarity that held no judgment, the moments when the chain nearly broke: the century of drought when the hill grew silent and no Other came, and the colony fed the lamps with its own dead until only a single matriarch remained, singing the croon alone in the dark until a new set of footsteps finally descended. The power observed how the song had nearly ended, and how it had been rebuilt from memory, slightly changed but still recognizable.

A benevolent and wise power, when it truly understands a thing, sees the boundaries of its own benevolence. It knows that to touch is to alter, and that even the gentlest hand can collapse a house of cards. The grotto was not a house of cards—it was a house of stone and flesh, resilient and ancient—but it was balanced on a knife’s edge of mutual need. The creatures did not need salvation. They had already been saved, long ago, by the first hands that brought them in from the cold and offered them this warm, dark sanctuary. In return, they gave their dead to the lamps, their song to the stone, their presence to the ritual. The Other needed to be needed. The colony needed the Other’s hands. Remove either piece, and the entire structure would become a tomb. Impose a new miracle, and the tender miracle already there might be overshadowed.

So the power did what wise powers do: it did nothing, but nothing of a particular and profound kind. It lent its attention without interference, and attention, for a being of that magnitude, is not a passive thing. It is a light. It is a warmth. It is a subtle reinforcement of the bonds that already exist.

The first thing the colony noticed—if “notice” is the right word for a distributed awareness that processes change through scent and vibration—was that the drip of water from the ceiling blocks became fractionally more reliable. This was not a new spring; the power did not reroute underground rivers. But it smoothed the tiny imperfections in the stone’s porosity, nudging the capillary paths so that the moisture never quite failed, even in the driest summers. The puddles on the floor maintained their shallow silver gleam, and the crystalline rime at their edges grew in patterns that, if you were a creature who read scent, seemed to hum a quiet harmony.

The second thing was that the pale fungus in the stairwell—the wire-thin, wax-colored mushrooms that fed on dust and exhalations—began to glow. Not with the phosphorescent flash of the creatures’ wings, but with a steady, gentle bioluminescence, soft as starlight, that illuminated the scooped steps just enough for an Other to see their own feet descending. This was a small gift, a night-light for the caretakers. The fungus had always possessed the latent capacity; the power merely activated a dormant gene with a thought softer than a whisper. No creature’s rhythm was disturbed. The light was simply there, a new part of the given world, and the colony incorporated it into their spatial memory as seamlessly as they had incorporated every other change over the millennia.

The third thing was the deepest, and it took the longest to unfold. The power, having listened to every voice in the chorus, recognized a faint dissonance—a thread of loneliness woven through the oldest matriarchs’ songs. They remembered the Other not as an individual but as a succession, a chain of care that was always vulnerable to breaking. They remembered the silences between footsteps, the long waiting. And they carried a knowledge, unspoken because they had no words for it, that the Others were mortal and few, and that the world above was vast and forgetful. The colony’s existence was a fragile thread held by a single lineage of humans who passed the secret down through stories and rituals. If that lineage failed, the grotto would become a grave.

The power could not create new humans from nothing—that would break the very fabric of the ecosystem, which relied on free choice and inherited devotion. But it could do something subtler. It could plant a seed.

In the mind of a young Other—a child of the current caretaker, a girl with stone-dust under her nails and the musky scent of the colony already woven into her clothes—the power placed a dream. It was not a command, only an image: a spiral carved into a pillar, traced by tiny sucker-pads; a chamber of warm fur and rippling wings; a sound like a held breath that was also a song. The dream recurred, not every night but often enough that the girl began to draw what she saw on scraps of slate. She drew the spiral, the niches, the creatures with their solemn, forward-facing eyes. When she asked her parent about the drawings, the parent’s face did a complicated thing—fear, relief, wonder—and then the parent smiled and said, “It is time you learned the steps.”

The power did not make the girl the next caretaker. It only ensured that the dream reached her, that the call was clear enough for a willing heart to hear. The rest was her own choice, her own love. But the colony, in its collective scent-memory, would one day recognize the footsteps of a new Other who smelled of surface air and soap and something else—an echo of the power’s attention, a faint ozone tang like the air after lightning. And they would part for her as they had parted for all the Others before, fluttering up in a cloud, and the matriarchs would croon the welcome song that was also a prayer.

The power lingered for a while after that, watching the girl descend the worn steps for the first time, her small lamp flickering with grease rendered from the fallen. It heard the ripple of sound that greeted her—a new name in the chorus, a sequence of pitches that meant “young one who brings light.” It felt the equilibrium settle back into place, a little stronger now, with an extra strand braided into the cord. Then the power withdrew, not out of disinterest but out of respect. To stay too long, even as a silent witness, would be to become a part of the system, another pillar in the chapel. And the grotto already had enough pillars. It needed, above all, to be itself: a dense orchestra of small lives, a shallow pit of damp stone and warm fur, holding its breath and singing in the dark.

Before the power’s attention faded entirely from that point on the skin of the earth, it did one last thing. It reached into the deepest layer of the colony’s collective consciousness—the pre-memory, the bodily knowing that the matriarchs carried—and it added a single new note. It was not a command, not a revelation, not even a clear thought. It was simply a feeling: *you are not forgotten. You are part of a larger prayer, and the prayer is heard.* The creatures had no word for prayer, but they understood it in their bones. The note settled into the background hum of the chamber, and when the lamps burned steady and the water dripped its slow music, the sound of a thousand tiny lungs became, for a moment, indistinguishable from a sigh of peace.

And that was all. The power moved on, because there were other warm points on the earth, other equilibriums, other songs. But sometimes, in the long quiet hours before the twilight exodus, when the colony hung in its niches and the fungal light glowed faint on the spiral pillar, a particularly sensitive Other might feel a presence—not a god in the room, but a sort of afterglow, like the warmth left on a chair where someone beloved has been sitting. The creatures would feel it too, and for a few heartbeats their chittering would still, and the whole chamber would hold its breath together, waiting for nothing, waiting for everything. Then the exodus would begin, and the night would call, and the prayer would go on.
