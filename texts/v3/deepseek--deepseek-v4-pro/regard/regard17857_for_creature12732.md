The mist parted.

Not the low, ground-hugging fog that pooled in the flumes and draped the catwalks—that remained, a soft exhalation of the living machine. What parted was the high ceiling, the perpetual grey-white fleece that had roofed the plateau for centuries uncounted. It pulled back like a membrane, a slow osmotic shift, and for the first time in the memory of the tanks, sunlight fell.

It fell not as a blazing revelation but as a gentle sieve of gold, diffused by the remnant haze into a honey-thick luminescence that turned every drip to a gem. The moss on the walls seemed to inhale. The ferns unfurled fresh fiddleheads, pale green and trembling. The robotic arm paused its fourteen-second cycle for a full two seconds—an eternity in its clockwork—and its pneumatic sigh was almost a question.

Cas felt it as a change in the hum before he saw it. The carrier wave had always been a steady 30 Hz throb, the heartbeat of the tissue. Now a new frequency layered over it, higher, glassy, a harmonic that sang in the pipes and made the water in the troughs ripple into interference patterns. He straightened from a coupling-box, his mist-coloured eyes lifting to the impossible brightness above. The moss on his scalp stirred, tiny white flowers opening toward the light.

The power did not descend in form. It had no need of form, no avatar of cloud or flame. It was a presence that moved through the plateau like warmth through steel, a gradual saturation. Where it passed, the rust on the pipes did not vanish but deepened into a rich, stable ochre, as if the metal itself had been given permission to age gracefully. The nutrient-clouded water in the vertical-farming towers cleared slightly, not to purity but to a healthier murk, teeming with microscopic life that had been flagging for decades. The bat that hunted midges above the outflow pipe found the insects suddenly more numerous, their flight slower, and ate its fill for the first time in seasons.

The power observed. It saw the vat-fields and their slow migration, the shuffling tanks leaving grooved tracks in the steel. It understood the logic of the place not as a mind might, but as a body understands its own digestion—a deep, wordless recognition of a system that had achieved something rare. Equilibrium. Not stasis, not perfection, but a steady-state turbulence that sustained itself, that turned waste into watercress, that let the moss colonise the concrete without consuming it, that let a robotic arm and a moss-pelted caretaker exchange nods for decades.

And it saw Cas.

The power knew beings like Cas in the way an ocean knows islands. They were rare, these slow, patient presences who became the living memory of a place. It had seen them on a thousand worlds: the last gardener in a city-forest, the keeper of a lighthouse on a shore that no longer had ships, the one who tightened the bolt not because it was needed that day but because the rhythm of tightening was itself a kind of prayer. It loved them. Loved them fiercely, with a tenderness that could level mountains. But it had learned, across the deep time of its existence, that love for such beings did not mean intervention. It meant acknowledgment.

So it did what a benevolent and wise power does when it finds a place that is already whole. It blessed it quietly. It did not heal Cas's river-tumbled face or lift the moss from his scalp or restore the lost sharpness of his youth. That would have been a violence, a rejection of what he had become. Instead, it reached into the deep hum of the place, the substrate that Cas felt in his sternum, and it wove into it a single new harmonic. Not a command, not a message—a gift. A warmth that would linger, like the memory of sunlight after the mist returned. A sense, conveyed in no language Cas would ever need to translate, that he was seen. That the work he did, the endless small maintenance of a world that did not know it needed maintaining, was not invisible. That somewhere in the vast architecture of existence, a record existed of every bolt he had tightened, every bat he had carried to the digester, every spring-bright inch of moss that had grown while he slept.

Then the power did something it did very rarely. It grew a flower.

From the rust-streaked steel of the coupling-box where Cas had been working, a slender stem spiraled upward, pale as mist, and unfurled a single bloom. It was not an orchid, not a watercress flower, not any species the plateau had ever known. Its petals were the luminous grey-white of Cas's own eyes, and at its centre it held a bead of nectar that tasted, if ever Cas thought to taste it, of the clean mineral water filtered through moss. The flower would not spread. It would not seed. It would bloom for exactly as long as Cas remained, and when he was gone—in whatever way such a being might go—it would close, gently, and the steel would forget it had been there.

The power withdrew. The high mist ceiling closed, a slow membrane healing, and the sunlight dimmed back into perpetual overcast gloaming. The harmonic in the hum faded until only Cas, whose sternum had been tuned to the place for centuries, could still feel its faint, warm afterglow. The robotic arm resumed its fourteen-second cycle, and the bat, sated, folded into a crevice to sleep.

Cas stood for a long moment, looking at the flower. He did not touch it. He did not need to. The hum in his chest was telling him something that felt like the memory of sunlight, felt like the taste of clean water, felt like a great and gentle voice saying *I see you, I see you, you are not alone in the deep time.* He made the small rotation of his wrist—*it matters little*—but this time, for the first time in uncounted cycles, the gesture carried a new shading. Gratitude, perhaps. Or simply the quiet, unshakable peace of knowing that the equilibrium he maintained was itself maintained, in some vast and gentle hand.

He turned from the coupling-box and walked on, one hand trailing the wet railing. The rust bloomed briefly bright behind him, then settled into its stable vermilion crust. The flower glowed softly in the dim light, a small white eye in the great green tissue. And Cas continued his rounds, carrying the warmth in his bones, as the tanks shuffled north by a metre, and the mist dripped, and the living machine hummed its quiet, perfect song.
