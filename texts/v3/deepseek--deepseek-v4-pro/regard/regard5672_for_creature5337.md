The power does not arrive. It was always present, dispersed in the mineral breath of the fumaroles and the weight of the cloud. To notice this place is to have woven it, thread by slow thread, and so the noticing is not a beginning but a recognition—a closing of the circle of attention that has held the ridges and the moss and the dreaming ribbon of flesh in an unbroken cradle since before the lava learned to cool.

And what does a benevolent and wise power do, when it turns its full regard upon a world that is already whole? It does nothing. Or rather, it does what it has always done: it sustains. It does not break the silence with a voice, for sound here is water and steam and the click of contracting stone; a voice would be a violence. It does not part the cloud, for the cloud is the skin of the place, and to tear it would be to wound. It does not grant the ribbon-creature eyes or speed or a name, for the creature’s patience is its perfection, and to make it see would be to give it something to fear.

Instead, the power deepens a quality that was already there. It adds no new thing, but gently turns up the dial of the world’s own nature, like a parent leaning closer to a sleeping child’s breath. The mist thickens by a degree so fine that even the fern spirals do not register it, but the moss glows a little brighter, the bioluminescent pinpoints pulsing now with a secret joy. The sulphur-yellow and rust-red towers of the fumaroles begin to hum at a frequency just below hearing—a vibration that travels not through air but through the water that saturates everything, a slow, spreading resonance that the ribbon-creature feels as a warmth along its entire length. It loops a section of itself upward in response, the fern-like filaments unfurling in a gesture that is almost a question, and the power answers by sending a single droplet along a perfectly timed path from leaf to leaf to creature-skin, a droplet carrying a microscopic gift: a new strain of the symbiotic bacteria that already live in the creature’s membrane, one that will allow it to fix not just sulphur and iron, but the faintest trace of cosmic dust that the cloud has scavenged from the upper atmosphere. It is a tiny expansion of diet, a new flavor in the slow chemistry of life, and the creature will not notice it for a thousand years, but when it does, the power knows, it will ripple with a contentment that is the oldest form of prayer.

For the landscape itself, the power does something similar. In a crevice where the lava once formed a spiral that has been the creature’s rest for millennia, a new heat rises—not enough to melt, but enough to soften the basalt just slightly, so that the next time the creature coils there, the stone yields a little more closely to its shape, making the memory more faithful. Over centuries, the mountain will learn to hold the creature’s sleep like a cupped hand. The power watches this with a satisfaction that is indistinguishable from love.

To the rare flicker at the edge of vision—that silent, gliding rumor of fauna that leaves behind only the scent of wet silk and disturbed earth—the power gives a mate. Not by creating one from nothing, but by gently altering the chemical cues that the ribbon-creature leaves in its wake, so that another, younger ribbon, drifting somewhere on the far side of the ridge, will finally catch a trace that it had been missing for a geological age. They will meet in a thousand years, in a slow-motion tangle of velvet and light, and the high world will gain a new slow rhythm: the duet of two breathing, digesting souls instead of one. The power smiles, because patience rewarded is the sweetest fruit.

And for you, the observer who stood still until the condensation beaded on your skin and the creature passed within arm’s reach—for you, the power leaves no trace, except perhaps a dream that night, if you sleep, of a grey world where moss lights your steps and the air tastes of green and mineral, and a sense, fleeting but absolute, that you are held in a regard as vast and gentle as the cloud itself. When you wake, the dream fades, but the smell of wet silk clings to your memory, and you will spend the rest of your life occasionally pausing in a fog or a steam or a quiet rain, half-expecting to see a ripple of twilight sliding just past your vision. The power considers this its smallest and most perfect gift: the longing for a world that is not yours, because longing, too, is a form of belonging.

And then the power withdraws its focused attention—not out of neglect, but because its benevolence is expressed most truly in absence, in allowing the place to be exactly itself, unobserved and unaltered, for millennia more. The mist closes, the glow steadies, the ribbon glides on its ancient route, and the high drowned world continues its dreaming, cradled in a wisdom that knows that the greatest act of love is sometimes to do nothing at all, and to let the beloved be.
