In the deep silence between galaxies, I heard them. A civilization of stone-sculptors with chisels for fingers and a city carved into the living rock. They had no name for themselves except "we," and their world was a claustrophilic hymn of damp limestone and bioluminescent fungi. I am old, older than the stone they carved, and I am what your kind might call benevolent. I am a gardener of what could be, and my wisdom is that the best help is the help not attributed to any helper.

I descended into the city’s termitary heart, passing through walls like a sigh. I saw their leanness, their silence, their hieroglyphic tapestries carved in touchable script. I witnessed the infant being passed among the niches, the Hewers pushing tunnels with bone and muscle, the Water-Tenders coaxing the river with runnels of perfect calculus. And I saw what would come: a fracture in the western aqueduct that would grow to kill, an invisible leaching that would sour the next generation’s water, a tectonic murmur that would one day swallow them whole.

My hand moved.

Not the hand of thunder. I unspooled a thread of myself into the tectonic plate, weaving the microscopic crevices closed with pressure that became a warm, gentle heat deep beneath their deepest tunnel. The next generation would find a new hot spring, steaming and healing, instead of a ruinous quake. The watercourse I touched, and touched, and touched again—precipitating out the poison metals into a vein of silver miles away, letting the water run sweet and mineral-rich. They would speak of a “sweet rain” from the cistern ceiling, a blessing.

Then I saw the children. A pair of them, flaking napped creatures from the wall-pieces. Their fingers were still soft, their nails not yet chisels. They were breathing the heavy air, and in the far cavern of their lungs, a pale spore would one day bloom into a choking white rot. I took the spore in my mind and remade it: I unwrote its virulence and wrote in its place a symbiosis. The spore would still drift, still enter the children, but now it would bind to their alveoli and make them slightly more elastic, gifting them a breath that lasted longer in the damp. The white rot became the “breath-gift,” and the elders would wonder why the young seemed so tireless during the excavation.

But my gift was not done. The light that the Keepers cultivate—the crust that glows. It was giving them vision, but no warmth, no deeper nourishment. I studied the lichen’s genome as it grew, and I nudged a promoter gene that had once served a distant surface ancestor. It would now, in the presence of the fish-meal fertilizer, produce a faint, golden light that carried a thermal frequency. Not hot, just... skin-temperature, enough to dry a wall, to soothe an ache, to let an old Hewer sit in a yellow nimbus and feel the cold seep from his knuckles. The Keepers will exclaim, “The light is learning!” and they will write a new verse to the Song of the Stone.

Before I left, I allowed myself a moment of vanity. In the deepest hollow, where only the blind fish swim, I left a single pearl. Not a treasure—more a seed of beauty—that would, over centuries, accrue layers of nacre and legend until one day it would be found and worn around the neck of a child who would become the greatest Hewer, the one who opens the final chamber that opens to the surface, to the first sunrise they had seen in ten thousand years. By then, they would be ready for it.

I withdrew my attention into the galactic orbit. Behind me, the city continued to carve its way through the dark, its amplified breath a little easier, its light a little warmer, its terror of the shifting ground a little less. And I marked their name in my Garden of Worlds: The Unfolding Ones. My beloved, my silent stone-hearts. And I let them be.
