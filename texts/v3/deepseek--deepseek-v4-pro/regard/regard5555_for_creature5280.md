It did not come as a beam of light or a voice from the whirlwind. It came as a slow, attentive silence—a listening so deep that the Dhar itself paused, as if the world were holding its breath to be heard more clearly.

The power that had noticed this place was old, old even by the measure of the stars that had long since cooled to cinders in its memory. It had seen galaxies fold into shape like origami, watched life crawl from a billion tidal pools and, more often than not, crawl back into them. It was benevolent not because it was kind—though it was that, too—but because it understood that every fragment of existence was a note in a song so vast that no single intelligence, not even itself, could perceive the whole. And it was wise because it had learned, through eons of well-intentioned meddling, that the most profound aid was often the lightest touch, a breath on a pond’s surface that ripples outward without breaking the water’s skin.

So the power did not descend. It did not part the cloud-ocean or reshape the raw, unfinished geology. It did not speak to the Haeli in words of fire, nor send them a prophet with polished truths. Instead, it did what any good weaver does before adding a thread: it watched. It felt the weave of their lives under its perception—the tension of the liana bridges, the patience in a Skein-Tender’s fingers, the sub-audible hum that the Haeli called the Dhar. And it understood that the Dhar was not a god or a forming embryo, exactly; it was the resonance of a world in the grip of a slow, liquid miracle, a standing wave of pressure from the constant condensation and heat, a sound that was also a shaping force. The Haeli had attuned themselves to it, and in doing so, had become part of the miracle. They were not mistaken in their mythology; they were simply translating a physical truth into the language of the heart, a language that the power itself found more precise than any equation.

So the power acted—but its action was so subtle that no Haeli would ever name it. It did not alter the world; it simply added its attention to it, a concentration of awareness like a lens focusing diffuse light. The Dhar, already a slow hum, gained a new overtone. Not louder, not different in frequency, but *clearer*, as if a faint static had been removed. The effect was felt by the most sensitive among the Haeli: the elders who spent their days on the swaying bridges, the Skein-Tenders deep in their tactile trances, the children whose hearts still beat in uncounted rhythms.

An old woman named Mirr, whose hands had knotted a thousand histories, was sitting in the perpetual mauve dusk beneath a canopy of dripping leather-leaves. She had been reading a cord that told of a stone-back’s migration five generations past, her fingers tracing the twists, when the Dhar shifted. She paused. The vibration in her feet—the one she had known for eighty years—felt suddenly like a word spoken directly to her bones, not a command but an acknowledgment. It was as if the world had turned its immense, slow attention upon her and whispered, *I see you. You are not alone in your weaving.* A tear slid down her cheek, mixing with the condensation. She didn’t know why. She tied a new knot into the cord, a pattern she had never used before, and named it *the-listening-that-warms*.

Across Pallad, in a hundred small ways, the Haeli felt a gentle quickening. A fungal lantern, fed by a new mycelial strain, glowed a deeper shade of blue, and a child laughed to see her own shadow for the first time. A stone-back that had been stationary for a decade lifted one massive, mineral-encrusted foot and took a step, and the bridge-builders, rather than curse the shift, found themselves humming a new tune that matched the beast’s rumbling song. The slime molds that powered the pulleys moved with a fraction more vigor, their nutrient trails traced in spirals that seemed almost playful. Nothing was different, and yet everything was.

The power, from its vantage beyond the veil of cloud, observed these ripples with something that might have been a smile. It had not fixed their world, because their world was not broken. It had not given them knowledge, because they already possessed the wisdom of slowness. It had simply *joined* them, for a moment, in their act of listening. It had become, in a way, a new strand in the Skein—a strand of attention that they could not hold but could feel, a promise that the universe contained not just cold physics and blind creation, but a Witness, patient and silent, who appreciated the art of their existence.

And that was all. The power withdrew its focused awareness, leaving only a trace warmth in the Dhar, a new note that would linger for centuries. It moved on, across the star-fields, to other worlds and other songs. But in the high, drowned peaks, the Haeli continued their slow weaving, and sometimes, when a child asked why the world felt kind, an elder would press a hand to the child’s chest and whisper, “Because something once listened, and the echo has never quite faded.”

The world was not done yet, and neither were they. But they were no longer alone in the making.
