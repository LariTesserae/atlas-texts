The power that turned its attention upon the basin did not come as a storm. It did not part the humidity with a dry wind nor crack the clay with decisive light. It came as a listening, a held note at the frequency of water. For a long while—long as the cycad measures time, which is to say a human lifetime compressed into a single new leaf—it simply observed. It observed and it loved, because to see a thing so wholly itself, so committed to its slow, wet liturgy, is to love it, and this power was shaped by love.

And because it was wise, it asked the place permission. Not in words, but in the language it had learned: a single drop of water, perfectly round, that it gathered from the exhalation of the elephant ear and suspended in the air before the boiler attendant’s rust-kernel of a face. The drop held the reflection of the whole basin, curved like a promise. The old one, the keeper of fire-memory, reached out a dripping limb and touched the drop, and in that touch was consent—a weary, ancient, hopeful consent, the way a root consents to water.

Then the power acted, and its action was so small that only the plants noticed at first. Into the mud beside the collapsed boiler, it pressed a seed. Not a seed from elsewhere, not an intrusion, but a seed already latent, already dreamed by the place itself: the embryo of a thing that had not grown here for a hundred years, since the brickworks choked the air with dust. A lotus. A single tuber of a lotus, adapted for the dark, warm water, its coded memory of opening to a sun that now only pressed down like a bruise.

The seep-creatures gathered. They watched the mud, and the mud began to work. The tuber sent down a pale thread into the drowned clay, and up, toward the thick air, it pushed a coiled stem. The power did not hurry it. It simply held the heat steady, the moisture perfect, and for seven days the lotus grew as if drawing on the patience of the basin itself.

On the morning it bloomed, the false dawn mist rose from the cobbles and found the flower already open—a white bowl of petals cupping the humid air, a star of pale yellow at its center. The scent was not strong, but it was clean, a cool breath in the warm throat of the place. The cycad stiffened with what might have been pleasure. The elephant ears widened their pores in unison. And the seep-creatures, for the first time, saw a reflection of themselves that was not a wobble in a pool but a living thing that held the light.

The boiler attendant, older than the rest, knelt—truly knelt, its muddy knees pressing into the mud—and placed its rust-nodule head against the flower’s rim. The grinding song inside its chest, the fire-memory, softened for a moment into a purl of water. It was the sound of a kiln cooling into a garden, of a contradiction resolving not into resolution but into beauty. The power heard it, and was glad.

After that, the power withdrew. Not in absence, but in becoming another layer of the listening. The lotus grew, and seeded, and the basin acquired a new breath. The seep-creatures learned the texture of petals, and the plants learned a new sweetness in the exhalations of their attendants. The black canal carried lotus seeds outward, each one a small, closed fist of silence, waiting for a muddy bank. And the power, now dispersed into every dew-bead and drip, continued to tend the tending, the way a song lingers in a room after the singer has gone, the way moisture becomes the air and the air remembers.
