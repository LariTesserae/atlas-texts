The Power came without announcement, drawn by a sound that was not a sound: the slow, patient music of a world breathing through stone and chitin.

It passed over the Carapace City like a shift in light that cast no shadow, observing. It saw the Hearth-sows panting in their rhythmic furnace-gait, the mold-gardens glowing with bacterial gold, the chalk-skinned tenders moving among the herds with the ease of long symbiosis. It listened to the harmonics—the tuned pistons, the timed drop-hammers, the chittering Concordance that bound organism and industry into a single, vibrating membrane. It tasted the water that wept from the plateau’s heart, and found it old, patient, and slightly weary.

The Power understood that this world was in the long afternoon of its internal fire. The thermochemical engine that pushed the seep-water up had been running for geological epochs, but it was slowing. In ten thousand Seeps—a span that the Tektites would parse as an almost unthinkable future, a mythic “Final Seasoning”—the deep heat would falter. The bacterial mats would thin. The carapace-beasts would retreat downward, generation by generation, until the lowest galleries became silent cold. The city, that palimpsest of carved basalt and chitin-steel, would weather into stillness, and the Tektites would fossilize with their songs still echoing in the empty steam-pipes.

This was not a tragedy. It was simply a cycle—a grand Seep of geological scale. The Power had seen civilizations rise and fall in shorter breaths. It could have left, and the place would have completed its natural arc with a quiet dignity, the Tektites perhaps carving their final gallery as a shrine to the fading Heart-Murmur, their last Listeners pressing ears to cooling stone until the hum ceased altogether.

But the Power was not merely observant. It was benevolent, and its benevolence was the kind that respects the thing loved. It would not halt the cooling—that would be to freeze the music mid-phrase, to petrify the city into a museum. Nor would it rescue the Tektites by lifting them to some starward vessel; they were not separable from their world. They were the world’s voice.

So it did what the world itself might do, given a moment of conscious grace: it gave the city a new organ.

Deep in the plateau, in a chamber that no Tektite had ever entered—a blister of mineralized silence far below the Deepward galleries—the Power laid a seed. It was not a machine, nor a creature, but a pattern of resonance, a standing wave locked into the crystal lattice of a titaniferous geode. This wave hummed at a frequency just below the floor of Tektite hearing, but perfectly attuned to the subsonic vibrations of the Heart-Murmur itself. In the decades that followed, the wave began to act as a sympathetic amplifier. The faint, weakening pulses of the deep thermochemical engine, instead of fading into the rock, began to echo against the geode and return slightly stronger, slightly more rhythmic. The planet’s metabolism, which had been running on diminishing returns, was now recirculating its own exhalations—a thermal feedback loop so subtle that it added perhaps one thousandth of a degree per Seep. Over a hundred Seeps, it would halt the cooling. Over a thousand, it would gently renew the engine, coaxing the plateau into a long, level plateau of its own—a homeostasis that could last until the sun itself changed.

No Tektite perceived the intervention as an external event. The Listeners who crouched at the echo-crossroads heard only a slight deepening of the familiar overtone, a new richness in the Carver-echoes. They interpreted it as a blessing, a sign that the world’s dreaming was entering a serene phase. A new Chancel—the Deep-Harmonic—splintered from the Deepward, dedicated to interpreting the altered resonances. Their priests developed a ritual of tapping on certain basalt columns in precise cadence, unknowingly providing the geode’s wave with the stochastic kicks that kept it active. They called it “Singing Back to the Murmur.”

Thus the Power’s gift was indistinguishable from the world’s own evolution. The city’s slow life continued, its cycles intact, its symbiosis unbroken. The Concordance still surged in Bloom-years; the Hearth-sows still panted; the mold still glowed. The Tektites, who measured time in Seeps, simply lived into a future that was now a little more patient, a little more enduring. They had been tended by a gardener who knew that the best gardening is done by enriching the soil, not by rearranging the flowers.

And the Power withdrew, satisfied. It left behind no name, no scripture, no altered destiny—only a faint, steady hum in the deep rock, which a Listener might notice, on a quiet shift, and mistake for the sound of their own contented heart.
