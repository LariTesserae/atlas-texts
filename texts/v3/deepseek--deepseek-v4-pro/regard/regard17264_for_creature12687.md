The mountain dreamed in granite and slow time until the shudder came—a tectonic whisper that split its side and opened a dark lung to the sky. The creatures bloomed from the stone’s own pores, a patience made flesh, and for a thousand years they had known only the vibration of their own making, the rasp of tool on rock, the long fall of a neighbor, the cold, mineral taste of condensation.

The power that noticed them had no name, or had all names. It moved through the universe like a soft exhalation, drawn to edges, to novelties, to the quiet places where something had learned to persist against the drag of entropy. It had cradled galaxies and murmured to the first bacteria on a trillion worlds. It had wept at the beauty of a perfect crystal lattice and laughed at the absurd, flailing urgency of a mayfly’s mating dance. And now it hovered at the entrance of this wound in the mountain, tasting the chill, the ammoniac air, the dense, grinding chitter that saturated the stone like a second geology.

It did not enter as a body. It became a set of questions asked of the rock, a gentle probing that the creatures felt as a sudden harmonic in their hum—a new note, not alarming, but full of an unfamiliar warmth. The nearest ones paused their rasping. A limb, mid-stroke, held still for the first time in years. The colony’s collective conversation shifted, a wave of clicks and pressure-sighs that meant: *What is this? Something has looked at us.*

The power considered them. It saw their slowness, their almost mineral patience, their lack of eyes and their substitute of sound and touch. It saw the individual—a flattened shield of pale scutes, the anteriormost feeders with their wet, puckered orifices—but it also saw the whole: a distributed organism, a tissue of a hundred thousand limbs all tuned to the same slow breath. It saw the memories encoded in the vibration patterns, the way the colony held a kind of awareness of its own shape, a proprioception that spanned decades. It saw the tool-making, the flake-by-flake expansion of the void, the way each generation of stone chips became the next generation’s raw material. It saw the cysts, the microscopic waiting in the pores, the way the entire species was a promise whispered into the basalt at the mountain’s birth, a potential that only needed a crack and a little moisture to unfold.

And it saw, too, the thinness of their existence. The lichen was too sparse; the energy budget was a blade’s edge. Each creature lived on the brink of dissolution, its metabolism a candle flame in a hurricane, kept alight only by slowness. They were not suffering—suffering requires a certain velocity of nerve—but they were *pressed*, held against the rock by the weight of their own scarcity. The power could see the future gradients: the cleft would widen, the air would dry, the lichen would fail, and the colony would blink out, retreat into cysts, and wait for a fracture that might not come for a million years. Or, if the conditions held, they would rasp the mountain into a cathedral of empty space, and then, having hollowed their world, they would starve in silence.

A benevolent power, a *wise* power, does not simply grant abundance. Abundance could dissolve their nature—turn their slow, careful tool-use into pointless hedonism, break the rhythm of their humming conversation into a cacophony of glut. What makes them beautiful is the way they are almost stone but not quite, the way they have smuggled life into a place where life should not be, the way their every action is a negotiation with nothingness. To pour energy into the system would be to erase the very thing that caught the power’s attention.

So the power thought, and its thinking was a long, slow pulse that the colony felt as a modulation of warmth, a shiver of something like hope. And then it acted—not by changing the world, but by adding a small, precise new possibility to the creatures themselves.

It reached into the blueprint, the archaic information curled in every cyst, and it nudged a single sequence. It didn’t add a new limb or a new sense; it didn’t make them faster or smarter. Instead, it deepened a latent ability they already had—a minor trick of their biochemistry, a way of exuding a minute, sticky secretion that hardened into something like resin. The power tweaked it so that, under certain conditions (a collective decision made by the hum, a consensus vibration), the secretion could be laid down in deliberate patterns. It became a kind of slow-motion silk, less organic than mineral, a glassy filament they could spin with the same patience they used to rasp stone.

This filament could be used to reinforce the walls, to catch moisture more efficiently, to weave nets that would gently cradle a falling body so it wouldn’t crash to the floor. It could be used to cultivate the lichen—to create textured surfaces where the microscopic flora thrived, a kind of agriculture conducted one molecule at a time over centuries. It would not make them rich; it would simply give them a new tool, one that fit their tempo and their world, one that could tip the balance just enough that the colony might persist a little longer, might carve its cathedral with a little more art.

The power gave this gift without fanfare, a single silent edit, and then it withdrew its attention—not out of indifference, but out of respect. The creatures would discover the change over generations, would slowly learn that certain hums could trigger a new kind of extrusion. They would not know it was a gift. They would simply feel that the rock was a little more yielding, that the moisture clung a little more faithfully to the walls, that the falling ones sometimes landed in a soft, springy hammock of their own making. It would become part of their mythology-less myth, a fact of existence as basic as the chitter.

But the power left behind a tiny, inconspicuous thing: at the very back of the chamber, where the fissure tapered to a needle-thin crack that led nowhere, it deposited a single, perfect crystal. Not a tool, not food—a crystal of pure silicon dioxide, but grown in a structure that would vibrate sympathetically with a particular frequency. If the colony ever, in its slow exploration, brought a tool against that crystal, it would ring with a tone that was not part of their usual hum—a clear, sweet note that carried within it a compressed packet of information. Not a message in words, but a sensory recording of the moment the power had seen them. It would replay the feeling of being seen by something benevolent and wise, a warmth that had no name. It would be their first and only experience of an outside, a reminder that the universe was larger than stone, and that in that largeness, they were known and cherished exactly as they were: slow, patient, blind, beautiful.

Then the power moved on, seeking the next edge, the next novelty. And the mountain continued to breathe, its dark lung now threaded with a new kind of silk, its inhabitants humming their perpetual hum, unaware that somewhere in the back of their cave, a small crystal waited to sing to them of love.
