You are standing in it—though “standing” feels imprecise when the ground breathes and trickles beneath you. The sound comes first, a pressure more than a noise: a low, throaty thunder that never relents, woven from a billion strands of dripping water and the shrill, chittering rasp of creatures unseen. It presses on the ears like something physical, a texture of sound so dense you feel your bones vibrating in sympathy. You taste the air—cool, metallic, heavy with the smell of wet stone and the faint, salty tang of ancient decay.

Overhead, there is no sky to speak of, only a low-hanging mist that glows with its own diffuse, grey-blue phosphorescence. The light is dim, the kind of half-illumination that erases shadows and makes everything seem to float in a watery dusk. The mist never lifts, and the landscape unfolds in layers of soft grey as you peer out into it.

Beneath your feet is a slab of stone so old, so deeply eroded, that it no longer resembles solid rock. It is a pockmarked, spongy terrain, a honeycomb of shallow basins, curled runnels, and smooth-lipped hollows. Every surface is slick with moisture; water seeps from the finest cracks and drips from every overhang, its constant movement the foundation of the endless roar. There is no soil, no layer of living or dead plant matter. The rock is bare but for a faint, slimy bloom—a mineral-eating layer of grey-green biofilm that slicks the deeper pools and gives the ground an almost slippery resilience underfoot. This is not a place that knows plants. This is a landscape before plants, or after them.

The water is everywhere but never deep. It forms a shimmering, interconnected network of thin films and ankle-deep pools, all in slow, barely perceptible motion. Sometimes it wells up from fissures in a gentle burp; sometimes it recedes with a soft sucking sound, as if the whole stone shelf were breathing in and out. The temperature is cool—a constant clamminess that settles into your skin and leaves a chill, the water a degree colder than the air, the air cool enough to sharpen every breath.

Out in the open, the scale expands. You are in a vast, low-lying basin, ancient and almost perfectly flat, stretching for miles in every direction until it fades into the glowing mist. There are no hills, no ridges, only the faintest undulations that might once have been mountains in some epoch so distant the planet has forgotten them. This is a peneplain, a landscape worn down to its final, humbled form by eons of water and chemical dissolution. Occasionally, a harder vein of stone resists, forming a low, rounded hummock or a delicate fin, but these features are sparse—you can walk for a hundred paces between them. Much space lies between anything that rises from the level plain, giving the place a sparse, skeletal quality.

And yet, the landscape teems.

It teems not with plants, but with creatures. They are abundant, a pulsing population that scuttles, slithers, and churns through the water and over the damp rock. Pale and eyeless, their bodies are formed of chitin and calcium, the same grey-white as the aged stone. Some are smaller than your thumb, flea-like things that propel themselves across the meniscus of pools with frantic flicks. Others are as long as your forearm, segmented and many-limbed, resembling shrimp fused with centipedes. They rasp at the biofilm with grinding mouthparts, and they are the chorus: a high, continuous screech of stridulating legs and the percussion of hard carapaces clattering against stone. Larger forms navigate the deeper channels—slick, dark shapes that move with an eel’s sinuosity, occasionally breaking the surface to suck air through valved bladders that boom like deep drums. Predator and prey, their movements create a visible stir, a constant roil in the shallows.

The sound of the water and the sound of the creatures are inseparable. Together they form a wall of noise so loud it is almost blinding. It is a living, active ecosystem in deafening motion.

Yet there is always the sense that something else has been here.

Scattered across the plain, faint and rare, are the marks of building. Sometimes you encounter a low wall, no more than two or three courses high, assembled from flat plaques of stone split along their bedding planes. The edges are rounded by the same etching moisture that wears the rest of the world, and the grey biofilm has crept up their sides. The walls do not enclose anything; they run in wavering lines for a dozen yards and then stop, as if the builders had no need of roofs or rooms. Other times, you find a ring of stones, like a nest, or a single upright slab wedged into a crevice. There are also smaller things: a scattering of shaped pebbles that might have been hand-axes, or a hollowed-out basin clearly deepened by chisel marks. The technology is primitive—stone-on-stone, the simplest of tools, the earliest grasp of fabrication. Whether the creatures themselves, or some other band of beings, crafted these objects is impossible to tell. The structures are empty, and silent but for the way water trickles through them.

The light never changes, the mist never burns away, and the sound never stops. This is a landscape that has settled into its final form: an ancient, wet, mineral world where life reimagined itself without sunlight or leaves, and the only boundaries are the ones that time has almost erased.
