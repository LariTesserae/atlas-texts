The plateau has no name because no one remains to speak it, and no map has ever recorded this place with any accuracy. It is vast—so vast that standing at its edge, if you could find one, you would see the curvature of its own weather wrapping around a distance that forgets you. The air is thin and perpetually cold, a cold that does not fluctuate: no frost forms, no ice melts, only a steady, penetrating chill that settles into the metal and stone like a physical constant. Here, 5,000 meters above what was once sea level, the sky is a motionless lid of iron-gray cloud, a single unbroken sheet that never burns off, never darkens, never even shifts its shade. Light comes through it evenly, a shadowless, exhausted gray that renders everything in flat, honest detail.

The ground is young. That is the first thing a geologist would murmur if she were foolish enough to set boots here. The rock is sharp-edged, freshly fractured, as if the entire plateau had been thrust upward in a single violent spasm only a few decades ago—and then simply stopped. There are no rounded boulders, no softened ridges. Escarpments slice upward like the unweathered sides of a newly quarried slab, their faces slick with water that never stops seeping, never forms streams, never freezes. Water is not a feature here; it is a condition. Every surface weeps. It condenses out of the air in a constant dew, beads on every rail and pipe, drips from every lip of stone. The ground underfoot is neither soil nor mud but a dense sponge of saturated moss that holds the wetness like a tongue against the roof of the mouth. The humidity is a hundred percent, and it has been a hundred percent for as long as the machinery has been recording it. Puddles never grow or shrink. The air smells of wet iron and something faintly organic, a clean rot that never progresses to decay.

And onto this raw, weeping scaffolding, vegetation has claimed everything with a desperate, frozen lushness. It is a riot of greens—emerald mosses so thick you sink to your knees, feathery ferns that unroll in perfect fractal silence, sprawling mats of liverwort that coat the vertical rock faces and the sides of buildings alike. There are no trees, not at this elevation and this cold, but there are shrubs with woody stems and leathery leaves, each one dusted with condensation, each one exactly as it was when the process halted. The plants are wet and vibrant and utterly, terrifyingly alive in appearance—but they do not grow. A frond extended halfway in its unfurling will never complete itself; a patch of moss will never thicken one cell more. The vegetation is locked in a perpetual state of mid-growth, frozen at the height of its lushness without the entropy that should follow. It is a garden in a snapshot.

And then there is the city. Because this is a city, undeniably. Urban in its bones, industrial in its sinews. It stretches across the plateau in a grid that follows no human logic, a sprawling network of structures and conduits and skeletal towers that recede into the gray horizon until they become a texture, a stippling of geometry against the moss. The buildings are colossal, their walls riveted iron and blackened steel, their windows dark or shattered or never installed. Pipes as thick as houses run alongside them, wrapped in the same vivid green moss, their junctions weeping steam that does not rise or dissipate but hangs in the air like frozen breath. There are smokestacks in ranks of hundreds, each one tapering into the cloud layer, and from their mouths come constant, unvarying plumes of vapor. The vapor is white and dense, and it stays exactly where it is released, forming a stationary forest of pillars that connect earth to cloud without shifting. You could walk through them—you would find their edges cool and solid, as if the steam had been turned to aerogel in the instant it left the stack.

This city is empty. Not abandoned in the sense of a hasty departure; it was built empty. The density is a statistic that manifests as vast plazas of wet flagstone between factory shells, as streets designed for crowds that never arrived, as apartment blocks whose rooms are hollow and whose doors open onto nothing. There are machines inside the buildings—enormous iron presses, ranks of lathes, boilers the size of houses—and they are all running. They have always been running. The sound is the first thing that hits a hypothetical visitor, before the cold, before the wet: a constant, deafening noise that is neither chaotic nor rhythmic, but a single sustained chord of industry. It is the hum of generators, the clank of stamping mills, the hiss of hydraulics, the roar of furnaces, layered into one eternal drone that never varies by a decibel. You could set your watch by it if time moved here. The sound does not echo; it is already everywhere, a pressure against the eardrums, a vibration that lives in the moss and the steel and the water beads alike.

There are no animals. No insects crawl in the moss; no birds cut the stationary steam. The wind blows, because the vast scale demands weather of its own, but the wind is a constant, a steady low-pressure flow that moves always at the same speed from the same direction, carrying the same moisture and the same taste of metal. It stirs the fern fronds in a pattern that repeats exactly, a wave that flows through the vegetation and then flows again, as if the world were a few seconds of film set to loop forever. The strangeness of this place is not in any single element but in the totality of its frozen equilibrium: an industrial ecosystem that has reached a perfect, impossible stasis. The factories produce nothing, or produce the same thing over and over, or are building themselves. The plants are fake-real, too perfect, too evenly distributed. The water cycles from cloud to rock to moss to pipe without ever accumulating, evaporating, or draining. The entire plateau is a closed system that has solved entropy by simply refusing it.

It is loud, and cold, and wet, and green, and utterly still in its change. It is a monument to a process that swallowed its purpose and kept running. The scale of it breaks the heart: all this industry, all this living growth, all this water and stone and engineered air, and not one creature to breathe it in, not one moment of decay or birth. Just the moss and the machines, screaming their static hymn to a sky that never answers.
