The first thing you would notice, if you could notice anything here, is the smell. It is not a wet smell, not a green smell, but something metallic and sweet, like hot copper and burnt sugar. The air itself is thick with it, a dry, syrupy medium that coats the back of the throat and makes the eyes ache. This is a place that has never known water, not as a liquid, not as a concept. The chemistry here skipped that solvent entirely, building a living world from something else—some kind of low-temperature ionic fluid that sweats from the rock in oily beads and evaporates back into the dimness before it can pool.

You are not on a planet’s surface. You are in a chamber, a single vast room carved from the living mineral of a world’s deep crust. The scale is intimate for a geology, vast for a room: a rough dome perhaps a hundred paces across, its ceiling lost in a haze of warm, stagnant air. The walls are not walls but the raw faces of a geode, all sharp, unweathered angles of obsidian-black sulfides and veins of a mineral that glows a dull, resentful orange when you look directly at it, then goes dark in your peripheral vision. The floor is an uneven tumble of the same material, razor-edged and without a single rounded pebble. This place was torn open yesterday, geologically speaking—some gas pocket from a deep outgassing, a blister in the planet’s skin that never saw wind or rain, only this eternal, baking stillness.

And yet, it is lush.

From every surface, every crag and shelf, a vegetation erupts in a riot. But it is a vegetation of thorns. Not metaphorically—literally. The primary producers here are not leafy plants but something closer to fungal spines, chitinous towers that branch into fractal forests of needlepoints. They come in colors that have no names: a bruised violet that seems to stain the air around it, a pale, dusty orange like dried blood, a deep arterial crimson that pulses with a slow, internal light. They are dry as bone to the touch, hollow, and when the air moves—which it does, in slow convection currents from the warm floor—they sing. Not a rustle, but a true, glassy music, a million brittle chimes rubbing together, a high, crystalline ringing that never, ever stops. It is a loud place, a cathedral of insectile noise, though there are no insects.

The fauna is abundant and follows the same rules. They are chitin and copper wire, slow-moving things that navigate the thorn-forests with a kind of stately, mechanical grace. You see a millepede the length of your arm, its segments perfect hexagonal plates of burnished bronze, picking its way up a vertical face on feet that are tiny crystals of magnetite, clicking and scraping. It leaves a faint trail of etched silver on the rock. In the center of the chamber, where the warm updraft is strongest, hangs a creature like a kite or a manta ray, its body a single translucent sheet of something that is not flesh but a flexible mineral, stretched taut over a frame of hollow bones. It doesn't flap; it ripples, maintaining its station in the thermal column, its form casting a moving, distorted shadow across the ceiling. It hums, a low, subsonic throb beneath the glassy shrieking of the plants.

The strangeness coheres here: this place is a single, frozen moment of equilibrium. The not-water cycle—the way the oily fluid beads on the hot rock, is absorbed by the rootless thorns, and transpires back into the air—has reached a perfect, time-invariant balance. The millepede never reaches the top of its wall. The manta-ray never leaves its thermal column. The chime the thorns make is the exact same chord, now and forever. There is no decay because there are no microbes as you understand them, just a continuous, slow-motion crystallization of life and death into the same process. A thorn that stops growing doesn't rot; it simply becomes part of the mineral substrate, its chitinous structure a scaffold for the next generation of sharper, smaller thorns. The air is so thick with the exotic pollen—a dry, glittering dust—that the light itself seems particulate, a dim, golden fog.

And there is light, though no sun. It comes from the walls themselves, a chemluminescence inherent to the geode, and from the bioluminescence of the larger, slower fauna. It is a dim, crepuscular glow, like dusk stretched into an infinite afternoon, full of long, sharp shadows that never shift. The shadows are the only true darkness here, pooling in the clefts between the razor-rocks, and they are utterly black, sightless.

In the bowl of a particular rock formation, a shelf of obsidian that juts out like a cupped hand, there is a structure. It is a small thing, a lattice of carefully placed thorns, their chimes broken off to make a silent, woven nest. Inside, three objects rest, never moved, never changing: a spheroid of polished hematite, perfectly reflective; a length of the millepede's bronze-segmented molt, its joints still flexible; and a flat piece of shale etched with a pattern. The pattern is not a picture. It’s a systematic series of parallel scratches and dots, a rhythmic notation, a thing of intent. The maker is long gone, or perhaps was never there, moving on before the world reached this perfect, unchanging stasis. The nest remains, a single thought preserved in amber, a question to which the constant, crystalline scream of the thorns is the only and eternal answer.
