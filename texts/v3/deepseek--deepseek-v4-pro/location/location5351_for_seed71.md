The Rimeplate stretches from horizon to horizon, a vastness so absolute it seems to fold back upon itself, a green eternity suspended at the top of the world. It is a land so ancient that time has worn it nearly flat, softening every edge until the bones of the continent lie in gentle, elongated swells, their orientations a fossil memory of winds that blew when the planet was young. The air is thin and perpetually cold—a dry chill that lives in the marrow—but the ground is heavy with moisture. Every few hours, walls of freezing fog roll silently in from the invisible high glaciers to the west, soaking the moss in a near-liquid breath before retreating into a grey horizon. The dampness never wholly freezes; it beads on needle-leaves and translucent water-buds, drips from the ice-encrusted towers of slow succulent trees, and seeps into the deep carpet that is the land’s true skin.

That carpet is a paradox: lush beyond reason in this harsh, frigid clime. It is not the green of jungles but a miniature, patient luxuriance—a thousand-year-old bonsai forest spread across a continent. Mosses in every shade of emerald, olive, and rust form a springy mattress that undulates for miles, punctuated by sprawling mats of ground-vine whose bulbous nodes store the plateau’s memory of rain. Here and there, succulent towers rise to the height of a person, their fleshy facets glazed with layer upon layer of frozen dew, glinting in the pale light like living crystal. There is no animal life. No bird stirs the air, no insect whines, no worm turns the soil. The only motion is vegetal: the slow seek of tendrils, the incremental swivel of succulent faces tracking the sun’s thin arc, and the imperceptible heave of moss-breath as the whole plateau respires moisture through its diurnal cycle. This absence is not peace but a kind of held breath, an emptiness that amplifies the land’s subtler voices.

Within this still green sea, a settlement is scattered like a dropped necklace of stone. It is urban only in the loosest sense—a sparse archipelago of perhaps two hundred low structures built of hand-hammered schist, stacked without mortar and roofed with woven mats of moss-fiber. The buildings are domed and inward-turning, clustered in groups of three or ten, separated by miles of trackless vegetation. There are no streets, only the faint ghosts of footpaths where generations of quiet people have trod. These people are small, bundled in oiled moss-cloth, their faces hidden behind masks; they move with the slow deliberation of those for whom haste is a foreign concept. Their tools are stone, wood, and ancient bone—though no living memory includes the beasts those bones came from. They cultivate the land, redirecting vine growth with patient hands, harvesting water-buds and the starchy pith of succulents, and they maintain the stoneworks against a world that is rarely still. Their technology is primitive but precise, born of deep adaptation to a place where metal is absent and written language unknown. They communicate in yodeling calls that follow the land’s contours, and their history is held in knotted cords and a hymnal of slow songs.

Yet this is not a normal place. Reality has worn thin up here, eroded along with the rock. The ancient grinding that flattened mountains has also frayed the fabric of the world, and now it frays further with every passing fog. Distances are treacherous: a journey of an hour may stretch to two on the return, or the land itself may shuffle its features when you blink. The mid-morning sky sometimes develops hairline cracks of chromatic light, oozing a soft shimmer before sealing, as if the world is a vessel under strain. There are shallow pools whose still surfaces do not mirror the sky above but some other sky entirely—a realm of dual suns or perpetual twilight—and the people do not look into them, for a reflection can reach back. The vegetation itself is not wholly botanical. Some moss-choirs emit a subsonic hum when the fog is thickest, a felt vibration more than a sound. Occasionally, a succulent will bloom not with a flower but with a small, transient hole in space, a darkness from which whispered voices sometimes leak. These they call dead blooms, and they mark their locations with stacked stone to warn wanderers.

The quiet here is profound but populated. The wind whispers across the moss with the softness of a sleeper’s breath. Water drips from ice-encrusted leaves with a musical plink. The moss-hum ebbs and flows, felt in the chest. From the settlement come the occasional sharp notes of stone-on-stone, the melodic carrying calls, the low murmur of voices. But beneath all of it, the land’s own slow processes hum: the cycling fog, the creeping succulents, the settling of ancient rock. The plateau is active, a system in constant, unhurried motion, yet its voice is a murmur, not a roar.

The scale is planetary. From the highest stone tower—a squat, tilted structure on the settlement’s largest dome—one sees only more of the same: green rolling eternity hazed by mist, punctuated by the dark dots of isolated hermitages. The plateau generates its own weather; clouds form and die within its bounds, and the wind is a closed loop recycling moisture from ground to sky. There is no edge. It is said that to walk west long enough is to reach a cliff descending into a sea of clouds, but no one has returned. The density of human presence is heartbreakingly sparse, a filigree of stone and breath in a green desert. The people number in the low thousands, scattered across an area the size of an ocean, and one might wander for weeks to meet another soul.

The cold is a constant, a steady chill that seeps through moss-cloth and settles in bones. Even under the pale, high-clouded sun of midday, the air barely rises above freezing, yet the moisture remains liquid—some quirk of mineral content or reality-warp holding the water in a state of glacial damp. Puddles wear a skin of ice that can be broken, yielding cool, drinkable water. The people brew a bitter, warming tea from the moss, and they perform rites to hold the world together, their shamans tracing patterns in the air with carved stone to reinforce the fragile consensus of space and time. Without these small, daily rituals, they say, the plateau would dissolve into a dream, its ancient erosion finally complete.

The land is the end-state of a geography. Everything that could weather has worn to nubs; rocks are flaked with the kind of spalling that takes millennia. In some hollows, the ground is a coarse sand of ground-up granite mixed with calcified shells—not of animals, but of some earlier, stranger life now turned to dust. The people use it in their mortars, binding stone to stone, building their sparse archipelago against the void. And so the Rimeplate persists: ancient, cold, damp, lush, empty of animals but full of a slow, verdant life, a place where the world is both very old and very thin, where silence and mystery are woven into the fabric of every dripping leaf.
