There is a place that was once a marsh and is now something else.

You come to it through a tunnel of scrub-willow and stunted alder, the ground beneath you changing without announcement from damp earth to something harder, more regular. The path dips and you are suddenly among them: the condensers. They are steel towers, though "tower" suggests something taller than what stands here—they are perhaps four metres high, some leaning, most vertical, arranged without obvious pattern across an area no larger than a modest church. There might be forty of them. There might be sixty. They are close enough that you cannot walk between many without turning your shoulders.

Each condenser is a skeletal frame of rust-darkened iron, bolted and riveted in the heavy-handed style of an earlier century's industry, and within each frame hangs a web of copper tubing coiled around a central void. The copper is green-black with patina, weeping slow drips of water into catchment basins of riveted tin. The basins are full. They are always full. The water spills over their edges in thin, silent sheets and runs down channels pressed into the earth—channels that must have been dug once but are now simply *there*, as established as streambeds, lined with the same dark patina. The ground between the condensers is not mud but a kind of wet gravel, small stones locked together by rust and mineral deposits, and your boots make a sound like walking on damp toast.

The condensers are working. That is the first thing to understand. They are not ruins in the sense of having stopped. Each one hums at a frequency just below hearing, a pressure in the ears rather than a noise, and the copper coils are cold to the touch—colder than the air, which is cool and carries the faint mineral sharpness of recent rain. Water beads on every metal surface. It drips from every joint. The atmosphere here is supersaturated; you can feel it on your skin, a constant urge toward condensation. The machines are not pulling water from the air so much as *offering the air a place to become water*, and the air is eager to comply.

The place has a name, but no one agrees on what it is. A small sign on one of the condensers—aluminum, stamped, more recent than the iron—reads `STATION 4 / CONDENSER ARRAY / MUNICIPAL WATER CO.` but the municipal water company has been defunct for forty years and the town it served is four kilometres away and also defunct. Someone has scratched `THE DRIP GARDENS` into the patina of another condenser with a nail. A third has a brass plaque that says `MEJOR NO TOCAR` in an accent that doesn't belong to this country. The scatteredness of human intention is part of the place now. A coil of rubber hose lies half-buried in the gravel, leading nowhere. A wooden crate, soft with rot, holds a collection of glass insulators that someone gathered and then abandoned. There is a single rubber boot, upright, full of water, with a small fern growing from it—one of the very few plants that have managed to establish here. The vegetation parameter is sparse for a reason: the constant dripping and the mineralized ground and the something-in-the-water have made this place inhospitable to most things. Moss grows, but only on the north faces of the iron. Lichen crusts the copper in pale grey-green patches. The ferns are few and they are all growing *in* things—the boot, a rusted bucket, the cracked basin of a condenser that has fallen silent.

That last one draws your attention. Not all of them are working. Some have stopped, their copper coils dry and silent, their basins empty or filled with dead leaves. One has toppled entirely and lies half-submerged in a pool of its own making, the water around it stained orange with iron oxide. But most are active, and their activity is not steady—it is turbulent. This is the dynamic element, the thing that makes the place feel unstable even in its apparent stillness. The drips do not fall in rhythm. They come in bursts, in sudden flurries, responding to something in the air—humidity shifts too fine for you to feel, micro-eddies of temperature. A condenser near the centre will be silent for a minute and then suddenly release a cascade, dozens of drips at once, a brief percussion of water on tin that startles you every time. Then silence again. Then a different condenser, somewhere to your left, takes up the call. The effect is of a conversation you cannot quite hear, a logic you cannot parse. The machines are reacting to the world and to each other, and their reactions have the quality of a weather system—chaotic, self-organizing, full of false calms and sudden intensifications.

There is fauna here, but it is rare and furtive. You notice a spiderweb strung between two coils of a working condenser, its silk beaded with water droplets so fine they look like a diagram of a constellation. The spider itself is small and dark, huddled at the edge of the web, waiting. A single frog lives in one of the catchment basins—you see its eyes above the water, gold-rimmed, unblinking. It does not move. It may have been there for a very long time. Sometimes dragonflies come through, their wings catching the low grey light, but they do not stay. The place seems to tolerate life without encouraging it.

The light is the hardest thing to describe. It is normal light, overcast-daylight, diffuse and shadowless, but it behaves strangely here. The moisture in the air catches it and holds it. The copper and rust and wet stone do not reflect so much as absorb and re-emit, so that the whole space seems to glow faintly from within, a brown-green luminosity that makes you think of old photographs, of amber, of looking up through deep water. At certain angles, when the drips are active and the air is thick with spray, you can see tiny rainbows forming and dissolving in the spaces between condensers—not the full arcs of sky-rainbows but fragments, shards of spectrum that flicker at the edges of your vision.

There is a smell. Wet iron, yes, and the clean mineral scent of groundwater, but also something sharper underneath—the faint electrical tang of ozone, perhaps, or the chemical residue of whatever the condensers were originally designed to extract. The air makes your lungs feel slightly raw after you've been here a while. You notice that the spider, the frog, the ferns have all adapted to whatever it is. You are just visiting.

The place has boundaries. The condensers stop. The gravel gives way to mud, and the mud to marsh grass, and beyond that is the ordinary world of a low-lying landscape that has forgotten its own industry. But within the boundaries, the density is overwhelming. The towers press in. The drips fall from everywhere. The humidity wraps around you like a second skin. Every surface is active, layered with water and rust and the slow accrual of time. It is a small place, but it does not feel small when you are inside it. It feels like a world. It feels like something that has been here since before the marsh and will be here after, condensing itself out of the air forever.

The turbulent quiet continues. A condenser near the back begins to drip rapidly—drip-drip-drip-drip—and then stops as suddenly as it started. The frog blinks once. You realize you have been standing here for longer than you intended, that your coat is damp through, that the humming in your ears has begun to sound like a voice.

It is not a voice. It is the machines, doing what they have always done. You turn to leave and your boot knocks against something half-buried in the gravel: a small glass bottle, corked, with a rolled piece of paper inside. You do not open it. You place it back where you found it, upright, and it immediately begins to gather condensation on its inner surface, the paper darkening with moisture that comes from nowhere, from the air itself, from the endless gift of water.
