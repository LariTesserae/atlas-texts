The first thing you notice is the sound—or rather, its careful, breathing absence. Silence here is not empty but full, a held note, the air thickened by the slow exhalation of a billion leaves. Water beads and trails through it soundlessly, or nearly so: a faint hiss as droplets slide down fibrous trunks, a distant, rhythmic plink like a heartbeat swallowed by moss. The quiet is so deep you can hear your own blood moving, and it seems rude, a violation of some ancient compact.

You stand on a surface that is not ground. It is a woven thing, a mat of living root and hand-twisted cordage, wide as a road and springy underfoot. The weave is meticulous—each strand the thickness of your arm, some smooth and pale as tendon, others rough with bark, all of them alive. New leaves unfurl from nodes at your feet, tiny and translucent, tasting the air. This is the Floor of Lamentations, one of thousands of such pathways that span the Hollow. The Hollow itself is a space so vast it has its own weather: you can see clouds forming in the middle distance, soft grey bellies brushing the under-canopy. Far below, a different cloud layer drifts, silver and slow, hiding whatever drop exists. No one knows how deep it goes. The woven bridges and platforms form a three-dimensional net across an abyss that might be planetary in scale. Here, everything is made. There is no stone, no soil, no “earth” in the sense of untouched mineral. The substance of this world is ligneous, crafted: it is all one continuous artifact. And every inch of it is alive.

You look up. Above you, the vastness continues. Trunks—or are they pillars?—rise, spaced so far apart they seem like isolated towers in a misty realm. They are constructed, too: great spiraling bundles of cultivated wood, coaxed and trained over epochs by hands that knew only stone tools and patient devotion. The wood is dark, wet, glistening with condensation. Water weeps from every pore. It is warm here, a steady, enveloping warmth like a nursery, and the humidity is so high that the air feels like drinking. The light is dim, a perpetual twilight filtered through countless layers of canopy and cloud. There is no sun visible, only a directionless glow, a moth-grey luminescence that might be the sky remembering brightness or the phosphorescence of fungal mats high above.

Movement catches your eye. A thing like a ribbon, flat and jointed, pale as a rootlet, pours itself from one strand to another, silent, boneless. It is hunting. Across the gap, a cluster of small, many-legged animals—all soft angles and slow, deliberate steps—grazes on lichen that crusts the woven cables. They make no sound. The predator bridges the void in one fluid, looping motion, and the grazers scatter, their feet pattering like rain for just a moment before the quiet returns. This is a world of abundant fauna, but every creature is a creature of hush: padded feet, book-lungs that don't rasp, voices pitched below the threshold of hearing. Even their deaths are muffled.

You walk. The pathway splits, threads diverging around a node like a nocked spindle. At the junction, a structure emerges: not a building, but a denser weave, a cavity in the fabric. It is a dwelling, or was. Walls of twined wood rise in a seamless curve, opening into a chamber where platforms overlap. The floor here is smoother, polished by generations of feet. In the centre, a basin of woven fiber holds water, perpetually full from the ever-condensing air. There are tools left behind: needles of bone, an axe-head of chipped volcanic glass, coils of inner bark waiting to be spun. No metal, no metal anywhere. The makers of this world worked with what the living structure gave them: wood, leaf, sinew from animals, stone carried from who-knows-where. They pruned and guided, trained vines into archways, roots into ladders. This entire cosmos is their handiwork, a planet-scale basket grown and woven and tended since before memory.

And yet, no one is here. The dwellings are empty, the pathways untended but not decaying—for the plants are still alive, still growing slowly, held in their ancient forms by some inherited tropism. The emptiness is the third note of the quiet. You can walk for days inside this echoing, humid twilight and see no other bipedal thing. Only the furry, the scaled, the chitinous, moving in their silent economies. The emptiness makes the scale almost unbearable: each sound you make flies out and is swallowed by that infinite, woven distance. You begin to feel that you are inside a lung, or a mind, a space so thoroughly constructed for life that life itself became the architecture and then simply drifted off, leaving the building breathing.

The reality of the place is not quite stable. At the edge of vision, the weave sometimes pulses, as if the entire structure were drawing a slow, single breath. Perspectives shift: a bridge that appeared straight now curves gently to the left; a pillar that was round a moment ago shows a facet, a flatness that suggests deliberate shaping. Sometimes, in the fog, you see figures moving—not animals, but tall, weaving forms, hands of twig and leaf, the old tenders still at their work. When you call out, the sound dies at your lips, and the figures are only the patterns of moisture on your eye. The boundary between what is plant and what is made, what is living and what is tool, has dissolved. The moss on a step is the step; the step is the root. The wall is a membrane; the basin is a leaf. The entire world is a single, continuous organism that was, at some point, organized, given intent, and then left to run its course until the intent became indistinguishable from nature.

You sit at the empty dwelling’s edge, legs dangling over the abyss. Far across the Hollow, a weather system is brewing: a mass of vapour, thickening into rain. The rain will fall upward here sometimes, or sideways, carried by thermal whorls. You hear the first drops, a faint patting on the leaves, and then the sound is absorbed back into the hush. Warm water streaks your skin. The light dims further, a soft, wet darkness settling in. The active processes of the world continue: a bird—no, a leathery-winged thing—glides past, silent as a petal. A tendril near your hand curls, slowly, around the grip of the bone-axe, and you know that by morning it will have incorporated the tool into its flesh, another seam in the endless weaving.

You close your eyes, and the true sound emerges: a low, continuous murmur, felt rather than heard, the thrum of water rising through capillary action, of cells dividing in the darkness of the wood, of the entire immense, empty, total construction living its own quiet, purposeless life. When you open them again, the fog has swallowed the far pillars, and you are alone in a soft grey room that has no walls.
