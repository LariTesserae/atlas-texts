The Carapace City sprawls across a high plateau that seems to float above the world’s lower atmospheres. At this elevation, the air is thin and warm, perpetually damp as if the sky itself is a held breath. There are no clouds, yet moisture beads on every surface — weeping from the iron-rich cliffs, slicking the stone streets, condensing on the fluted stacks of factories. The water here does not fall; it seeps up from the plateau’s porous heart, pumped by some deep, slow thermochemical engine that has been running for geological epochs. Streams of warm, mineral-laden water braid through the city in open channels, steaming faintly in the normal light of a sun that seems always at noon.

There is no green. No leaf, no blade, no lichen. The landscape is a mineral tapestry: banded ochres, hematite sheens, salt-glitter crusts, and the dark luster of basalt worn to soft curves by ages of patient erosion. The terrain is old, settled into its own final form — every ridge and hollow a product of long equilibrium between uplift and dissolution. The city is built into and from this substrate, not so much constructed as excised: canyons of multi-tiered habitation blocks cut directly into the rock, their facades carved with the same weathered patina as the natural cliffs, as though the dwellings have been here as long as the plateau itself. Walkways and cableways span fissures, and everywhere there is the hum and clatter of industry — gear-driven conveyors, steam-pistoned pumps, the distant rhythmic stamp of drop-hammers. The tech level is recognizably industrial, full of brass, iron, and leaking steam, yet scaled to a civilization that has occupied this site for millennia beyond memory.

The city is vast. From any vantage, its structures repeat into a haze of distance, tier upon tier of factories, foundries, and stacked tenements descending into mist-filled gorges and climbing the domed backs of ancient volcanic plugs. It is dense, too: populations press cheek-by-jowl in the galleries, and the air is layered with sound — a constant loud chorale of machinery, rushing water, and the unceasing chitter and skree of the fauna.

And oh, the fauna. Overwhelming is the word. The Carapace City is named for its dominant lifeform: carapace-beasts, chitin-armored, multi-legged creatures ranging in size from thumb-knuckle to bison. They are everywhere, a moving pavement of arthropod bodies scuttling over every surface. Some are domesticated, harnessed to carts or used as living scaffolding for repairs; others swarm wild in the crevices, feeding on the algal-like bacterial mats that grow on warm seepstone, or on each other. The food chain here is built on chemosynthesis — the mineral-rich waters support thick biofilms of thermophilic prokaryotes, and the carapace-beasts graze these “rock-gardens” with specialized mouthparts. Larger predators, sleek and quick, hunt the grazers through the industrial alleys. The air teems with winged forms, their translucent wings catching the light in oily rainbows. Their chittering, rasping, and chirring forms a background roar that rivals the factories. The inhabitants of the city — humanoid, though with a chalky pallor and nictitating membranes — move through this living tide with practiced ease, their culture entirely shaped by coexistence with such massive animal life. They build their industrial processes around the beasts: using their discarded molts as raw material for a kind of chitin-steel composite, harvesting their metabolic heat for power, and routing their migrations through designed corridors in the city’s depths.

There is a slowness to this place that belies its noise and density. The dynamic is of gradual cycles. The carapace-beasts have a life-pulse tied to long-term fluctuations in the hydrothermal vents that feed the bacterial mats. Swarms might build over decades, then recede in a slow-motion bloom-and-bust that takes a century to complete. The city itself expands not by sudden construction but by incremental carving — a gallery widened over a century, a new shaft sunk so slowly that it is seen as a natural geological event. The erosion is established, but it is still ongoing, and the residents have learned to build with it, not against it, allowing their structures to weather into place.

The weirdness quotient is low but present. It is not an alien world, exactly, but something feels skewed. Perhaps it’s the way the carapace-beasts sometimes move in perfect synchrony, as if sharing a mind. Or the way certain industrial processes emit harmonies that seem to echo the creatures’ calls. Or the fact that at certain deep levels, the city’s original builders carved tunnels that align not with efficiency but with the migratory paths of an animal that may have been long extinct — a ghost architecture now inhabited by its successor species. The unusual sits atop the mundane industrial bones like a faint patina.

The light is normal, a steady pale gold, but it filters through steam vents to cast dancing shadows on the weeping rocks. The soundscape is a constant loud thrum: the industrial drone, the hiss of water, the skittering chitter of countless legs, and somewhere below, the deep, subsonic hum of the geothermal heart that pushes the waters up. It is a landscape that is simultaneously a city and a living reef, a warm, wet, high-altitude world where the distinction between organism and infrastructure has blurred over the patient aeons of its existence.
