The air is the first thing you notice—thick, warm, and carrying a weight that feels less like humidity and more like the breath of some vast, slow lung. It condenses on skin instantly, not as droplets but as a fine, pervasive slick, as though the atmosphere itself is halfway to becoming water. There is no transition; one moment you are somewhere else, and the next you are here, inside this pocket of the world that exists as a secret folded into a larger landscape.

The light is unremarkable at first glance—diffuse, shadowless, the colour of overcast midday—but it seeps from everywhere and nowhere, a general illumination that seems to radiate from the very surfaces around you. And those surfaces are all, every one of them, alive. Not merely covered in life, but constituted of it. The ground underfoot yields with the firm pliancy of a million tiny stems woven together, a continuous mat of liverwort and moss that is not soil topped with plants, but a deep, springy mattress of pure vegetation. It exhales warmth, as though metabolising the moisture in the air. When you lift your foot, the impression lingers for a moment, then slowly, silently, rebounds to its original contour—a motion that feels less like elasticity and more like memory.

You are standing in a hollow. Its scale is intimate, a room-sized depression cupped within a larger body you cannot see from here. The walls rise around you, not as earthen banks but as ribs of what might be roots, or trunks, or something in between—smooth, pale columns that ascend without branching until they arch inward high overhead, filtering the light into a soft lattice. They are widely spaced. That is the strange thing. Between these living pillars, there is not a dense tangle, but air and distance, a sense of openness that the word “sparse” only approximates. Each column stands alone, perhaps ten feet from the next, and the moss floor stretches unbroken into the shadows beyond, punctuated only by the columns themselves. It feels like the inside of a cathedral built not by hands but by an idea of a cathedral, translated into wood and fibre and the slow, inexorable growth of a mind that knows only plants.

This is the architecture the parameters demand: total vegetation, but sparse density. The space is defined by the plants, but it is not crowded. There is room to move, to breathe, even though every surface—floor, wall, vaulted ceiling high above—is living tissue. The columns are slick with a constant, weeping moisture that smells faintly sweet and faintly mineral, as if they sweat a dilute sap. They are warm to the touch, almost blood-warm, and if you press an ear to one you can feel a low, rhythmic thrumming, a pulse that doesn’t vary. It is that sound, multiplied by the dozens of columns, that fills the hollow with its signature noise: a steady, deep, mechanical hum, overlaid with a softer, wetter, more organic susurrus. It is loud, but not overpowering; it is the baseline sound of a factory floor where the machines are made of cellulose and chlorophyll instead of steel.

There are no human artifacts here. No bottle caps, no cut stumps, no rusted wire. The place is untouched, but it is not wild in the sense of chaotic. It is in a state of equilibrium so profound it might as well be frozen. Nothing grows, nothing decays. The moss floor is not expanding; the columns are not thickening; the faint mist that rises from hidden pores in the floor does not accumulate. It is a sealed system, a loop that has run so long it has worn a groove in time. The parameter says “never changing,” and it means it absolutely. You could wait a century and the configuration of the room would be identical: the same arc of each column, the same placement of each tiny, star-shaped flower that dots the floor in a scatter that never seeds or wilts. The animals, abundant as they are, move through the space without disturbing it. They are part of the equilibrium.

And there are so many animals. Not what you’d expect in a boggy hollow. They are small, mostly—no mammals, no birdsong—but the air is thick with their bodies. Beetles with iridescent carapaces crawl deliberately along the columns, their legs moving in timed lockstep. They produce a clicking that punctuates the hum like a factory’s timing chain. Tiny, translucent frogs cling to the moss, their throats pulsing but emitting a sound too high for human ears; you feel them more than hear them, a pressure in the sinuses. Dragonflies with wingspans like your hand hover statically, their wings a blur that somehow creates a constant, low drone. They are not flying from place to place; they are stationed, like drones on a production line, each occupying a fixed coordinate in the air. They face inward, toward some centre of the hollow you cannot see. And everywhere, on every surface, are pale, millipede-like creatures that move in a endless, orderly procession, tracing the same sinuous paths over and over, as if programmed. They are cleaning the columns, you realise. Or feeding them. In their wake, the wood’s surface gleams slightly, a fresh exudation of moisture.

The “industrial” parameter finds its expression here, in this strange biology. The hollow is a machine. Not a destroyed piece of machinery overgrown by nature, but a living system that has, over eons, self-organized into a state of production. What it produces is not obvious. Perhaps it is the air itself, which feels charged, ionised, as though the whole room is a battery. Perhaps it is the water, which collects in a central, perfectly circular pool you now notice—a lens of liquid so still it is invisible until you break the surface tension with a finger. The pool is ringed by a low rim of what looks like keratin, pale and smooth, and from it rise thin, hollow tubes that arch up to the ceiling and disappear into the moss above. The arrangement suggests a distillation apparatus, or a circulatory system. The water tastes faintly sweet, faintly electric, and when you withdraw your hand, a droplet beads on your fingertip and holds its shape for an impossible length of time before falling back with a distinct, discrete plink.

The sound of that droplet is the only event. Otherwise, the loud hum of the columns, the drone of the dragonflies, the click of the beetles, and the subsonic throb from the frogs form a continuous, unvarying chord. It is a resonant note that fills not just your ears but your chest, a sound so constant it becomes a quality of the space, like the light or the warmth. It is peaceful, in a deeply alien way. The weirdness is not overt; there are no glowing runes or impossible colours. It’s in the stillness, the perfect order, the sense that you have stepped into a diagram from a textbook on theoretical ecology, a model that should not exist outside a computer simulation.

The rules here are different. Time does not pass. Matter cycles, but the cycle is so tight, so immediate, that there is no accumulation or loss. The animals do not die; they are replaced without decay, one individual seamlessly taking the place of another in a ballet of maintenance. You feel, standing there, that you are a disruption, a foreign variable that the system is gently, aimlessly buffering. The moss under your feet is already beginning to forget you. The columns do not register your presence. The dragonflies hold their station. The only change is the droplet, and even that repeats at a frequency that is probably, if you cared to measure it, invariant to the millisecond.

Looking up, the vaulted ceiling of intertwined plant matter is festooned with what you first mistook for hanging moss, but which resolves into thousands of fine, hair-like filaments that tremble in the still air. They are roots, or fungal hyphae, or something else entirely—they are the same pale colour as the columns, and they each terminate in a tiny, wet nodule. They are absorbing the condensation, you guess. Or perhaps they are exuding it. The entire hollow is a system of flows: liquid moving upward through the columns, vapour condensing on the filaments, droplets falling back to the pool, the pool feeding the roots of the columns. A closed loop. A perpetual motion machine of water and life. No erosion here in the conventional sense of wearing away—the parameter says erosion is “established,” settled-form, and you understand now that it means the shape is weathered into its final, stable condition. All the sharp edges have been rounded over eons, but now there is nothing left to erode. The columns are polished by the millipedes to a smoothness that feels like bone left in a river for a thousand years, but no material is lost; it is just redistributed.

The temperature is a steady, enveloping warmth—the warmth of a compost heap, of metabolic activity. It doesn’t fluctuate. You feel no drafts. The air is heavy but not stifling; breathing it is like breathing syrup, full of oxygen and something else, a trace gas that makes you slightly lightheaded, slightly hyperaware. The smell is complex: damp moss, yes, but also a sharper note like ozone, and a richness like fresh-turned earth, and underneath it all a faint, almost imperceptible tang of something metallic, or maybe alkaline, as from a battery. It is the smell of a clean, living factory.

This is a hidden place. You might have walked past its entrance a hundred times without seeing it—a fissure between roots, a sinkhole in a bog, a space under a fallen log that opens into something larger. And what is outside? The parameters don’t say, but you imagine a vast, monotonous peatland, or a forest of such density that the sky is a rumour, and this hollow is an air pocket, an alveolus in the lung of the world. The scale is pocket-scale, a single room, a temple not built but grown. It is a place that exists for itself, not for any observer. And it will continue, humming and clicking and dripping and on, long after you leave, unchanged in every particular, a perfect, self-maintaining machine of leaf and water and chitin, buried in the warm, wet earth forever.
