High on the salt-rises of the Kettlah, where the air has the texture of chilled gauze and the sky holds no true color, there is a city that has forgotten its own name. The inhabitants—what few of them reveal themselves—call it simply Hark, or the Hark, a word that might mean “shelf” or might mean “patience” in a language that predates sound.

You notice the horizon first, because there is nothing else to notice between you and it. The salt-field extends in every lateral direction, a plain of crystalline mineral that has been weathered into countless shallow basins and low, sinuous ridges—an established topography, ancient and settled, the salt having flowed and dissolved and re-crystallized over geological spans until it reached a state of equilibrium with the thin, cool atmosphere. The surface is not white but a muted gray-violet, the color of a bruise long healed, with occasional veins of deeper mineral staining: copper-green, manganese-black, the faintest flush of selenium-pink. In the distance, the plain simply frays into haze, the salt-rises becoming less distinct, less present, until the distinction between land and sky becomes a matter of philosophical preference.

You are standing at extreme elevation, though the slope was so gradual you barely registered the ascent. The air is thin in a way that feels less like deprivation and more like clarity, as if some unnecessary medium has been stripped away and you are now perceiving things more directly. The temperature is cool—not cold, not biting, but a steady, even coolness that seems to emanate from the salt itself, the mineral body of the world breathing out its stored chill across the centuries.

Look down. The surface beneath you is not natural in its immediate geometry. You are walking on a thoroughfare—a constructed causeway of fitted salt-blocks, their edges softened by the slow attrition of wind-borne crystal dust, but still discernibly rectangular, still carrying the intent of order. This is the urban fabric of Hark, and it is the first paradox: a city of extreme density built in a landscape of near-void, a settlement-scale concentration of structure rising from an almost empty expanse.

The buildings of Hark are not buildings in any familiar sense. They are extrusions—forms that have been grown or precipitated rather than assembled, though the intelligence behind them is unmistakable. Salt-towers rise to varying heights, their walls composed of concentric layers of mineral that record, like tree rings, centuries of incremental deposition. The towers are hollow, their interiors vast and cellular, but from outside they appear as slender spires, some reaching fifty or sixty meters, their surfaces not smooth but intricately fluted, the flutes spiraling upward in patterns that might be decorative or might be structural or might be something else entirely—a notation, a memory-storage system, a prayer.

Between the towers stretch bridges and walkways of the same salt-substance, some as thin as a wrist, spanning gaps of ten, twenty, thirty meters with no visible means of support. These are not suspended by cables or propped by arches; they simply continue from one tower to the next, as if the intervening space had been redefined as solid. This is where the world begins to show its true nature—where the reality breakdown becomes palpable.

You notice it first in the bridges. Look at one for too long and the visual field begins to slip. The bridge is there—you can see the salt, the weathered surface, the faint crystalline glint—but the space around it feels wrong. There are moments when the bridge appears to be behind itself, or when two incompatible perspectives resolve simultaneously, and your mind, lacking the machinery to process the input, simply gives you a blank, a skip, a held breath. The inhabitants of Hark do not use the bridges; you realize this after some time. They move through the spaces between towers by other means—or perhaps they simply choose not to cross those gaps.

Who are the inhabitants? Rare. You might walk for an hour and see one, or think you see one, or remember having seen one earlier without having registered it at the time. They are tall and slow, their forms columnar, their bodies apparently composed of the same salt-mineral as the towers, which makes them difficult to distinguish from architecture. When they move, they do so with a peculiar fluid rigidity, as if each joint were a geological fault releasing in slow motion. They do not appear to have faces in any consistent sense; where a face might be, there is a shifting arrangement of planes and hollows, a topography of expression that resists interpretation. They make no sound that you can hear. The city is silent, or nearly so—a silence that is itself a substance, a presence, a medium through which other, quieter sounds occasionally drift: the faintest crystalline tick as a tower settles, the subsonic hum of wind moving across a salt-basin three kilometers away, the sound of your own blood moving in your ears.

And yet the city is active. This silence is not stillness. Processes are underway. You can feel them, if not hear them—cycles of mineral exchange, slow chemical conversations passing through the salt substrate, a metabolism paced in centuries. The towers are growing, or dissolving, or both in alternation. Some of the fluting on a nearby spire has changed since you last looked; a spiral that ran clockwise now runs counterclockwise, or always did, and your memory is the thing that shifted. The light does not help. It is dim, a perpetual twilight without visible source, as if the entire sky were a diffuser for some hidden radiance that never fully commits to presence. Shadows are soft, multiple, contradictory. Your own shadow sometimes points in two directions at once.

There is no vegetation here—not only in the sense of no plants, but in the sense that the category itself has no purchase. Nothing photosynthesizes. Nothing roots. The concept of “vegetation” feels like a rumor from a warmer, wetter world, a memory you brought with you and now find yourself unable to locate. Instead, there is the mineral ecology: crystalline growth-forms that might be fungal or might be geological, spreading in fine dendritic mats across the older surfaces, their branching patterns too purposeful to be random, too alien to be read. They are the color of tarnished silver, of lead, of absence.

And the rules. The rules of this place are not stable. The world here is not broken in a violent sense—there is no chaos, no dissolution. But the underlying agreements that hold reality coherent have become negotiable. Time behaves strangely; you might find yourself arriving at a tower before you saw it in the distance, or noticing that the salt-crust on your sleeve is weeks old though you have been walking for what felt like minutes. The sky, when you dare to look at it directly for more than a glance, shows no stars, no clouds, no gradient of light—but if you hold your gaze steady long enough, you see *through* it, into a deeper darkness where shapes move that are not shaped, where distances are not measured in meters but in severances.

And yet you are not afraid. The atmosphere of Hark is not hostile. It is indifferent in the way that geology is indifferent, in the way that deep time is indifferent—but it is not malevolent. The cool air, the mineral silence, the slow processional of the salt-beings, the towers that remember their own construction in spiral glyphs—all of it constitutes a kind of peace, if you can relax your grip on the world you came from. The city is empty, but not abandoned. It is dense with absence, thick with the space between things, and that density is, itself, a form of habitation.

Far out across the salt-field, a basin catches some fugitive light and glints—a momentary star, a flare of violet-white—and then subsides. The wind that you cannot feel moves a million salt-crystals in a migration that will take ten thousand years to shift a dune by one meter. Somewhere in the city, a tower exhales a thin stream of mineral dust that hangs in the still air like a question.

You keep walking. The causeway continues. The silence holds.
