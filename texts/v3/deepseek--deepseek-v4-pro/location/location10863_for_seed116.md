The chamber breathes around you—a wet, mechanical exhalation that never quite stops.

You’ve squeezed through a rent in the hull of something far larger than you can comprehend, a tear in the riveted iron skin of a structure that plunges into abyssal black. What you’ve found is a pocket: barely four meters across, a dimple nestled between colossal support struts and the outer pressure shell. The walls are not rock, have never been rock. They are layered steel plate, copper rivets the size of your fist, seams welded and re-welded into a palimpsest of repair. But age has smoothed it all. The edges are rounded, softened, as if the water has been licking at this place for millennia, eroding the hard industrial lines into gentle, organic curves. Mill scale and paint are long gone; the metal glistens with a patina of deep brown and gold, like old leather kept wet forever.

This is a fully made world, and it is dying of old age.

Water crowds every boundary. It leaks in through micro-fissures in slow, glistening trickles, cascades in a steady sheet down one bulging wall where a seam has failed. The space is submerged enough that the floor holds a permanent knee-deep pool, warm as a bath, heated by pipes you can feel thrumming through the soles of your boots. The temperature is a constant, enveloping 29 degrees—blood-warm, incubator-warm. Steam doesn't rise; the humidity is already total, hanging in air so thick it feels like breathing through wet wool. The light is normal daylight, strangely, impossibly, radiating from a single porthole high in the domed ceiling, a disc of grimy armored glass that glows with a steady, white industrial luminescence. No flicker, no hum—some ancient phosphor still burning after eons, filtered through a skin of green algae.

What makes this pocket a world is the lushness that has claimed it. Vegetation 2.2 is an understatement. Every surface that isn’t polished by rushing water is coated in a fur of living things. Not seaweeds, not quite—these are garden cultivars run wild, engineered for underwater oxygenation centuries ago, now evolved into a profusion of ruffled leaves and trailing vines that glow faintly bioluminescent violet at their edges. Fern-like fronds, thick as a man’s wrist, erupt from pipe flanges, their roots cracking through cast iron. Mosses that look like fine green velour blanket the ceiling. There are floating blossoms—pale, translucent cups that drift in the knee-deep pool, opening and closing with a languid, animal rhythm. The density is sparse because the chamber itself is small and open; the plants have colonized every surface but leave the central water volume clear, so there is a sense of generous space between the creeping walls and the few free-standing objects: a corroded pump housing, a ladder rung bent into a perfect spiral, a single massive valve wheel now serving as an anchor point for a cascade of flowering vines.

The fauna is abundant and strange. Things move in the water, a constant tick and rustle. Silver fingerlings, each no longer than your thumb, school around your legs when you stand still, nibbling at the film on your skin. Above the waterline, tiny translucent crabs with too many legs scuttle through the moss, each one carrying a single luminous blue dot on its carapace. There are larger presences too: a sinuous, eel-like creature, three feet long, its body segmented like a machine’s flexible ducting, homes in a hollow in the wall. It hunts the fingerlings with bursts of startling speed, its mouth a delicate ring of pumping cilia. And on the ceiling, a colony of things you can barely categorize—urchins? barnacles?—that periodically exhale a puff of bubbles in unison, a soft chorus of bloop-bloop-bloop that punctuates the steady hush.

That hush is the baseline: a throaty, constant roar of water forced through ancient plumbing, a low-frequency thrum from a pump that’s been running since before your species learned to smelt ore. Sound level 2.1 is true—it’s impossible to whisper here, everything is a shout above the ambient din. When the eel hunts, you hear its tail thwack the water; the crabs tap-tap-tap on metal; the bubbles pop and gurgle. Far away, through the iron bones of the structure, a rhythmic clanking starts and stops, some large and dying machine keeping a beat.

The weirdness seeps in through the creep of biomechanical fusion. The pump housing isn’t just overgrown; it’s incorporated. The moss has split its casing, and from the wound grows a stalk that terminates in a fleshy, eye-like bulb that pulses with the same rhythm as the clanking. Transparent shrimp pick at corrosion blooms and excrete a crystalline crumb that the fingerlings eat. It’s an ecosystem that has fully woven through the industrial substrate, so deeply that separating natural from made is meaningless. The ancient erosion, the worn edges, the settled-into-final-form quality—this place has been exactly this dynamic, loud, active, warm pocket garden for so long that its strangeness feels like the only sane reaction to eternity.

You are in a hidden, secret place. Scale 0.2: it’s tiny, intimate, a lung of the colossal drowned construct. Through the porthole, beyond the glass, you can just make out the true extreme of elevation—a vertical drop outside, a hull descending into black water that has no bottom. The pressure out there would crush you instantly. But here, in this one small pocket, the pressure is balanced, the temperature is constant, and the abundant life cycles endlessly. A sudden flurry as the eel strikes again, the pump thrum deepens for a moment, a cloud of bubbles from the ceiling urchins scatters the light. Everything is active, everything is moving. You could stay here forever, a flea in the fur of a slumbering god, and the globe of warm, salt-tinged water would never stop its slow, mechanical breathing.
