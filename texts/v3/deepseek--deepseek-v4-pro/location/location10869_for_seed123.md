The air here tastes of warm metal and wet stone—a faint, coppery tang that coats the tongue and lingers. It is not quite dark, but the light has no clear source; it seems to seep from the walls themselves, a steady amber glow that makes the space feel like the inside of a machineshop at dusk. The ceiling is low enough to touch, furrowed with condensation that never quite drips, only trembles. Every so often, a bead of water slides down a rib of rusted iron and vanishes into a seam with a soft hiss, like breath escaping.

This is not a cave, though it is underground. It is a narrow chamber—more a crevice, really—wedged between two vast concrete footings that rise into gloom above. The concrete is young, geologically speaking: its edges still sharp, corners unworn, the aggregate in its matrix glittering with unweathered flecks of quartz. Rebar stubs jut from one wall in a broken row, their cut ends bright orange with fresh rust, as if the formwork was stripped only decades ago. In places, the concrete bears the ghost of wooden shuttering, grain pressed into stone like a fossil of process. This is a place made, then left, and something else has moved in.

The floor is packed. Not with debris, but with life—or what passes for life here. A dense, low-growing mat of organisms carpets every surface, so thick that the original concrete and steel are visible only in patches. The dominant form is a lichen-like crust, liver-colored and pebbled, that radiates body warmth when you hold a hand above it. Interspersed among it are taller growths: fleshy stalks the height of a finger, translucent and veined with something that might be mycelium or might be wiring. They cluster most thickly around the weep holes in the concrete, where moisture seeps in a slow, rhythmic cycle—damp for three hours, dry for one—timed to some distant pumping machinery that has not shut off since the place was built.

The stalks sway, though there is no breeze. They sway toward heat. If you stand still long enough, they lean your way, and the ones nearest your boots begin to curl, gently, around the eyelets.

The sounds are few but persistent: a low-frequency hum that vibrates up through the soles of your feet, the occasional plink of a water droplet striking a hollow piece of metal, and a dry, papery rustle from the walls. The rustle comes from the beetles. They are the reason the floor is so densely inhabited, the reason the stalks grow in spirals rather than straight, the reason the lichen recolonizes the same patches in rotating cycles. The beetles are small—thumbnail-sized—and legion. Their carapaces are a dull, engine-oil black, each one etched with a fine grid pattern, as if machined. They move in waves, thousands of them, sweeping over the floor in slow currents, grazing the lichen down to the substrate, then moving on. When they encounter a stalk, they do not eat it; they pause, antennae testing the air, and then they follow it. The stalks seem to guide them, bending to indicate direction, creating transient highways across the chamber floor. A beetle that reaches the end of a stalk burrows briefly into the mat, deposits something small and pale—an egg, perhaps, or a borrowed piece of mineral—and then rejoins the current.

There are larger presences, too, though they are harder to see. In the narrowest part of the crevice, where the ceiling slopes down to meet the floor in a seam of dark iron, something hangs in a hammock of dried stalks. It is roughly the size of a cat, folded upon itself in an angular posture that suggests a creature with too many joints. Its skin is the color and texture of old leather, and it does not move while you watch, but you can see it breathing—a slow, irregular inflation of its sides that matches, beat for beat, the humming from below. It is wearing something on its face. A mask, or a remnant of one: a curved plate of perforated steel, like the guard from a factory fan, strapped on with twists of wire. Behind the perforations, something glints. Wet. Watching.

The beetles do not disturb it. The stalks grow around it, leaving a respectful gap. When a drop of water falls from the ceiling and strikes the steel mask, the creature makes a sound—a single, resonant chime, as if a tuning fork had been struck inside a bell—and then is silent again.

Tucked into a recess near the floor, half-buried under the lichen mat, is a small structure. It is unmistakably built: four walls of corbelled concrete fragments, a roof of rusted grating, a doorway no larger than a handspan. Inside, when you bend close enough to peer, is a miniature assembly of gears and cogs, all motionless, all crusted with copper salts. Below them, arranged in a careful grid, are nine beetle eggs. Beside them, a twist of wire holds a scrap of paper, marked in a script that is not human—though you can almost read it. It looks, in the low light, like an exploded diagram of a heart.

The temperature is warm enough that your jacket feels unnecessary. The warmth comes from below, radiating from the concrete itself, as if great engines once ran here and their heat has never fully dissipated. There is a smell, too, beneath the metal and wet stone: a yeasty, living scent, like proofing dough or the inside of a hive.

You are not the first person to find this place. Near the entrance—a narrow gap where the two concrete slabs part just enough to admit a sideways body—someone has left a pry bar leaning against the wall. It is bright yellow, fibreglass-handled, still clean. Beside it, scratched into the concrete with its tip, is a single word: NEST.

One of the beetles has climbed onto the handle. It sits there, antennae waving, oriented toward the hanging sleeper with the steel mask. The hum from the floor changes pitch, just slightly, and the stalks all lean at once in a new direction—toward the gap, toward the outside, toward you.

The world is only this room. It is packed full, dense with presence, and everything in it is busy living. The air is damp. The light is steady. The concrete has not yet begun to erode, but it is no longer in charge of what happens here.
