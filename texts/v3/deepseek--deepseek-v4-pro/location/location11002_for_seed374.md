The air hits you first, a solid wall of sound that presses against the ears until you stop noticing it as separate tones and it becomes the very texture of the world. It is a deep, continuous roar, woven from uncountable threads: the guttural thrum of steam vents, the hiss and pop of rising gas through mineral-thick water, the ceaseless high-pitched glissando of tiny lifeforms, and layered over it all, the basso chorus of things with larger lungs calling and answering in rhythms that never shift, never pause, never began and will never end.

When your eyes adjust, you are standing at the edge of a great basin, open as a weathered sea but cupped low between old ridges that rise as dark silhouettes through the steam. The light is a permanent twilight, a dim, directionless glow that seems to seep from the mist itself. There is no sun you can point to, no shadow, only a uniform grey-gold illumination that makes every surface appear to hold its own faint internal radiance. The air is warm, body-warm and wetter than any breath, so saturated that droplets condense on your skin as soon as you enter, beading on stone and leather and hair equally, running in rivulets down every surface that slopes.

This is a landscape of stone and water, of newly made ground. The basin floor and its terraced flanks are entirely built of travertine, the pale, porous rock that precipitates from hot springs. It lies in scalloped pools, stepped one into the next, each rim a delicate rounded edge unmarked by weather. The rock looks as fresh as if it were poured yesterday. There is no erosion here, no cracking from frost, no wearing by grit or storm. The mineral-laden water wells up endlessly from a thousand vents, a million seeps, flowing over the lips of basins in thin, perfectly smooth sheets, and yet the stone does not grow thicker. The pools do not fill or drain. The entire system is held in a perfect, motionless equilibrium, a fossil of a process still underway, a landscape that is perpetually emerging but never becomes.

There are no plants. No reeds, no moss, no trailing algae. The greening world has never touched this place. Instead, the rock itself seems alive. Every submerged surface, every permanently damp fissure, is coated with thick gelatinous mats in lurid oranges, rust-reds, chalky whites, and the electric blue-green of cyanobacteria. They paint the pools in concentric rings of color, mapping temperature gradients that never vary. These are the primary producers here, the base that supports everything else, and they glisten in the dim light like wet silk.

And everything else is abundant, dense beyond belief. The warm, mineral-rich water teems with life. Clouds of tiny flies, like a perpetual dark smoke, hover above every pool, their larvae grazing the bacterial mats in writhing mats of their own beneath the surface. Brine shrimp flick in pink nebulae through the warmer tiers. The stone itself, where it rises above water, is crusted with the living and the dead: tiny white tubes built by worms, the papery husks of moulted insects, the packed bodies of midges so thick they form a crackling, slippery layer underfoot.

This substrate supports the vertebrates. The noise, that overwhelming soundscape, is largely theirs. Frogs—thousands upon thousands of them—inhabit every niche. Some are no bigger than your thumbnail, some the size of a cupped hand, and they are all the same pale, semi-translucent grey, adapted to the dimness. Their throats pulse constantly, each one a distinct peeping or barking note that together weave a background wall of noise. Birds wheel through the steam: spindly-legged waders that pluck shrimp from the edges, and smaller, darting species that hawk the fly clouds. They nest directly on the rock, their guano adding to the mineral crust, and their own harsh cries knife through the lower frequencies. There are no silent gaps. The air itself is full, saturated with noise as much as it is with water vapor.

Yet this is not a wilderness. The density of life is matched and exceeded by the human density, because the basin is a city. The people here have built their lives into the very structure of the terraces, and they have done so without wood or cloth from any plant, for there are none. Their architecture is of stone and animal skin and bone. The travertine is easily worked with the soft copper chisels they forge in charcoal pits that burn rendered fat and dried skin, never wood. They have cut rooms into the solid rock behind the terraces, doorways framed with curving ribs of some large beast, roofs lashed from hide stretched over frameworks of antler or long bone. Stairs are carved into the stone itself, smoothed by the constant passage of bare feet, and ladders of knotted sinew drop from one level to the next.

The settlement climbs the walls of the basin in dense, near-touching clusters. Huts and cut-caves stack atop one another, their front walls open to the steam, platforms of hide and bone projecting out over the bubbling pools so that a person might lean from their doorway and scoop up a handful of brine shrimp without moving a step. Paths weave between structures, only wide enough for a single person to squeeze sideways; the inhabitants move through this vertical, saturated world with the casual grace of the frogs they largely hunt and husband. They farm the fly larvae in managed pools, tend the bacterial mats by scraping and re-seeding, and gather the constant fall of shrimp. Their technology is simple—hand-tools of beaten metal and chipped stone, pots of fire-hardened clay, fishhooks of bone, hibernation dens of piled rock—but it is applied with an exhaustive, repetitive skill that borders on ritual.

The people themselves are part of the ceaseless noise. Their voices are pitched to carry over the din, and so they shout in short, rhythmic bursts, a way of speaking that echoes the patterns of their world. They hammer copper on wet stone, beat hides, grind pigments from the colored mineral crusts, and their children chase each other along the narrow ledges, howling like the birds. There is no silence, not even at what might be night, for the light never changes intensity and the animals never rest.

Nothing here ever rests, and nothing ever changes. That is the deep truth of the place, the quiet paradox that underlies the deafening surface. The water that wells up from the earth brings dissolved stone at exactly the rate it is removed by the people and the creatures that use it. The animal populations, from the microbial mats to the frogs to the birds, are held at a perfect, stable abundance—never a plague, never a famine. The human population does not grow or shrink; births match deaths with the precision of a balanced equation. The terraces do not erode, do not crack, do not rise or sink. The steam never clears to reveal a sun. The roar never wavers in volume or pitch. It is a world caught in the instant of its own creation, a snapshot of potential that never resolves into past or future.

There are no seasons here. There are no trends. A child born in this basin will grow to old age hearing the same constant drone that greeted their birth, stepping on the same unweathered stone, dipping their hands into the same pools at the same temperature. The “emerging” geology, that raw, new-born quality of the rock, is not a phase that will pass; it is the permanent condition. It is a landscape forever arriving, forever young, forever still.

And it is, in its own contained way, entirely normal. The people here do not think of their home as uncanny or magical. They know the rules by heart, have built their society around the unvarying cycles, and find comfort in the very density and noise that would overwhelm an outsider. The abundant fauna, the primitive but intricate city, the wet and dim and deafening stability—these are simply the facts of existence. As you stand at the basin's edge, the steam beading on your clothes and the sound pressing into your skull, you realize that this place, so alien in its parameters, is for its inhabitants the most ordinary and complete of worlds: a home that has never been, and will never be, anywhere else.
