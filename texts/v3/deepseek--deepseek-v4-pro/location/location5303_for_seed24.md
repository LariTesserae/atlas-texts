The place is a shallow depression in the earth, no wider than a modest room, tucked into the base of an ancient, wind-scoured ridge. You would not see it from a distance; you stumble into it as a subtle concavity in the flat plain, a dimple where the ground sags as if exhaling after holding its breath for eons. The walls are not walls but a gentle grading of the same mineral crust that extends for miles—a crust of gypsum and anhydrite, pale grey in the dark, flaking in thin leaves like stale pastry. No loose rubble, no boulders, only this fine, flaky skin over a deeper mass of compacted salts and silts. The floor is a mosaic of desiccation polygons, each plate perhaps the size of a spread hand, fitting together with hairline cracks so thin they swallow what little light exists.

Light exists here only as a memory. The sky above is a permanent overcast, not of clouds but of a high, stationary dust layer, a gauze of fine pale particles suspended in the upper atmosphere that diffuses the sun into a directionless, deep umber gloom. Day and night are barely distinguishable; the darkness is a constant, warm charcoal grey that presses against the eyes. In the deepest recess of the hollow, under a slight overhang where the crust forms a shallow lip, the darkness thickens to almost palpable velvet. The air is warm—not the dry, stinging heat of a sunlit desert, but a steady, enclosed warmth, like the breath of an animal that is not there. It comes from the ground itself; the rock holds a low geothermal murmur, a deep earth fever that never fades. The temperature is unchanging, a constant 32 degrees Celsius, felt as a weight on the skin, a presence rather than a sensation.

This warmth, coupled with the absolute lack of water, has shaped everything. There is no moisture here—not as dew, not as vapour condensing on surfaces, not as a dampness in the cracks. The air is sterile, tasting of old dust and something faintly metallic, like the tang of a very still, very ancient battery. The gypsum crust is anhydrous; it does not weather by dissolution but by the slow, crystalline creep of dehydration over centuries, its surface a micro-terrain of sharp-edged shrivels and tiny, brittle curl-forms that crumble to powder if touched.

And yet, there is vegetation. Sparse, as prescribed: a scattering of individual organisms that are not quite plants in the familiar sense. They emerge from the polygon cracks, not rooted but growing horizontally, filamentous structures like grey, desiccated spiderwebs woven flat against the mineral floor. They are lichen-like, a symbiosis of fungal threads and a chemosynthetic bacteria that feeds not on sunlight but on the faint hydrogen sulphide seeping up through the deep faults. Their metabolism is achingly slow; they spread perhaps a centimetre in a century. To the eye, they appear as faint, dusty stains, like the shadow of a dead vine, but they are alive in the most minimal way—a metabolism so drawn-out that they live in near-perfect equilibrium with their environment. They do not grow or shrink. They have been here, in this exact arrangement, for tens of thousands of years. There is no animal life. No insects burrow, no reptiles bask in the dark warmth, no birds descend. The place is sterile of motility. The only sign of anything beyond the lichen-stains is a fine, velvety dust that clings to the overhang: a coat of dormant bacterial spores, powdery grey, that have settled over millennia and now remain motionless, neither dying nor germinating, held in a timeless suspension by the dryness.

Erosion has long ceased to be an active force. The hollow is established, settled into its final form. The wind that once carved the ridge above is gone now, the planet’s atmosphere too thin to lift grains. The only sound—and it is a sound you must strain to perceive—is a tiny, intermittent crepitation: the sound of salt crystals expanding and contracting in their micro-fractures, a crackle softer than the settling of a house, a sound like the earth dreaming. It is a sound that occurs perhaps once an hour, randomly, and it serves only to emphasize the silence. Otherwise, the silence is absolute. No wind, no water drip, no animal call. A silence so profound that you can hear your own blood moving, and so you quickly learn to hold still, to become part of the stasis.

There is no built structure, no artifact. And yet, near the centre of the hollow, there is a small, deliberate arrangement of stones—three flat shards of gypsum stood on their edges to form a tiny tripod no taller than a thumb. They have been there so long that their bases are fused to the crust by a microscopic cement of recrystallized salt. This is the only evidence of technology: the primitive, the early-tool-use. It is a marker, a sign, a message left by something that passed this way an incomprehensibly long time ago, a creature that also lived in a world without water, that perhaps saw this hollow in a slightly different light. The tripod is empty, holding nothing up. Its meaning is lost. It is not a building; it is not even a tool. It is a gesture, and it is the only punctuation in a world that otherwise has no narrative, no change, no dynamic.

This landscape is a photograph of a single moment that has been exposed for geological ages. It is a place where time has run out. The rules of the world here are simple: there is no water, so nothing dissolves; there is no light, so nothing grows with urgency; there is warmth, so the chemistry trembles on the edge of activity but never tips over; there is no life with a metabolism above a whisper, so nothing decays. Everything is in balance—a frozen equilibrium. The air, the rock, the thin grey lichens, the tripod, the spore-dust: all exist in a mutual stillness, a pact of non-interference. To enter this place is to become a disturbance, a droplet of heat and moisture that briefly threatens the immaculate aridity. But the hollow will outlast you. When you leave, your footprints will remain in the crust for a few decades, perhaps a century, before the slow crystalline creep of the salts erases them. Then the hollow will be exactly as it was, exactly as it is, exactly as it has been for the past two million years.
