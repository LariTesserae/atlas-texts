It is called the Thrumming Expanse. You stand at its edge — though “edge” is a courtesy, for the plain offers no boundary line, only a change in the angle of light. Behind you, the scree slope falls away into lesser elevations. Ahead, the world opens into a flatness so absolute that the horizon becomes a kind of joke: a perfect white-gold seam where land and sky bleed into one another. The sky is a washed-out turmeric, cloudless, and the sun is the color of hot brass. Heat presses down like a physical weight, even at this altitude — over three miles above whatever counts as sea level on a world that has never known water.

The ground is not sand, not salt. It is a mineral crust, brittle and crystalline, but it yields underfoot like cured leather. It is warm to the touch, and if you press your palm to it, you feel a faint vibration — a deep, subsonic thrumming that never stops. The crust is the color of old bone, but in some places it fractures into geometric plates and tilts upward, exposing cross-sections like the pages of a book: strata of pale amber, pearl, and a sickly lavender. There are no pebbles, no loose stones. The landscape has been weathered into its final form, sanded by wind and something else — something that leaves no grit, only a polished smoothness, as if the entire plateau were one solid object that has been patiently planed down. The erosion is ancient, complete, no longer active. This place has been in its own state for eons.

The air is utterly dry. You breathe in and your lungs feel lined with silk. There is no scent — no dust, no ozone, no organic decay. Only the thrumming, which up close resolves into a complex chord: a low drone, overlaid with higher, skirling harmonics that seem to come from no specific direction. The sound is loud enough that you have to raise your voice to speak, but it never changes; it is a steady-state noise, like standing inside the pipe of an enormous organ.

At first, you see nothing alive. The Expanse is empty — “empty” in the way of deep space. The density of matter here is almost an abstraction. You could walk for an hour and the view would remain identical: flat bone-colored ground, turmeric sky, hot brass sun. The scale is regional, perhaps the size of an inland sea, and its features are so vast and so widely spaced that your mind struggles to hold them in the same picture.

Then you see the first of them.

It begins as a ripple in the heat-haze, a dark line that wavers like a flaw in a windowpane. As you walk — or stand, or simply exist in its presence — it resolves into a form, but a form that is never quite fixed. It is a creature, if that word applies. It is immense, a walking terrain, maybe a third of a mile long. Its body is a series of articulated segments, each the size of a house, but the segments seem to be made of the same mineral crust as the plain, only darkened, as if the plain itself had been folded up into a living thing. It moves on dozens of legs, or perhaps they are pistons, or perhaps they are pillars of solidified vibration — your eyes cannot agree. The legs plant into the crust with a sound like a bell being struck underwater, a deep *tong* that joins the thrumming and then fades over many minutes. It moves slowly, so slowly that you can watch its progress only by noting that the heat-haze behind it has warped differently. A single step might take a quarter of an hour. The creature is a factory: along its flanks, openings dilate and contract, emitting puffs of something that is not steam, not smoke, but a kind of organized glow, a temporary radiance that scribbles itself out of existence. You catch the smell of hot metal, of electricity, of something that might be ozone if there were any water in the air to make it. The creature is an engine, a self-contained industrial process, and it is alive — overwhelmingly alive, in the way that a blast furnace is alive, or a city is alive. Yet it has no visible eyes, no mouth, no features you can name.

There are more of them. They are spaced so widely that from any one position you can see only two, perhaps three, at the limits of vision. They move in patient, crossing arcs, their paths dictated by gradients that are too slow to measure. Some are smaller, only the size of ships, and their movements are quicker — a step every few minutes. Some are so huge they seem to be part of the ground itself, and they do not move at all while you watch; they are merely there, humming louder than the rest, their backs crusted with the same pale strata as the Expanse, as if they have been stationary for geological ages. The fauna is overwhelming not in number but in presence. The sheer scale of life here, the sheer *occupied space* of it, dominates the senses. You are a mote on a table where titans crawl.

And there is the breakdown. The weirdness.

You notice it first at the edges of your vision. The crust, when you stare directly at it, is a uniform pale expanse, but in your peripheral sight it flickers with patterns — spirals, mandalas, circuit diagrams — that fade when you turn. The sky sometimes fractures into polygons, like a cracked plaster ceiling, and through the cracks is a darkness that is also a kind of light, a color that has no name. The thrumming occasionally skips, a beat dropped from the rhythm, and in that silence you hear something like a voice reciting numbers in a language you almost understand.

The creatures, too, are unstable. Their shapes are not memetic; they do not hold in memory. If you close your eyes, you cannot recall the exact arrangement of segments on the big walker. You remember it as a freight train, then a centipede, then a moving mountain of gears, then all of these at once. Reality is a suggestion here, a consensus that is fraying at the seams. Sometimes one of the smaller engines will pass close by — within a few hundred yards — and you will feel your own thoughts begin to skip, your sense of time stretch and compress. You might experience a memory that is not yours, of being an extrusion of hot rock, of pulling yourself along on limbs of crystallized force. The boundaries of self are as porous as the sky.

There is no water. There is no vegetation. The tech is all-pervasive yet unbuilt; the creatures are the technology, and the technology is the ecology. They maintain themselves, reproduce by budding off segments that grow into new walkers, and their slow migrations churn the crust, renew the mineral surface, keep the erosion in its final, balanced state. The sound they make is the sound of industrial metabolism — grinding, humming, the occasional shriek of metal fatigue that echoes for miles. But it is also a kind of music, if you listen long enough: a slow, patient symphony playing out over centuries.

This is a place that has forgotten the distinction between machine, life, and landscape. It is a desert of dry chemistry and heat, a high empty realm where the air is too thin for water to ever exist, where the only plants that ever grew were the mineral growths of an earlier, wilder epoch. Now only the fauna remains — overwhelming, loud, slow, and deeply, dreamishly wrong. It is a factory floor where the factories are animals, and the animals are ghosts, and the floor itself might be dreaming all of it. You stand on the Thrumming Expanse, and the thrumming never ends, and you understand that you are not a visitor. You are a note in the chord, a brief vibration on the crust, and the Expanse will weather you down too, in time.
