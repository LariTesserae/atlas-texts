It is not a cavern, though it lies below the surface. Caverns have walls. This place has none—not in any ordinary sense. It is a void, a hollow improbably suspended in the warm dark of the deep earth, and into this void has grown a world.

Call it a throat. A vertical shaft, roughly cylindrical, dropping into depths the light cannot reach. But the light does reach, because the light is not from above. The light is *in* the plants.

Every surface that exists here is plant. The walls—where walls can be said to exist—are not rock but a woven tapestry of living tissue, layer upon layer of roots and vines and the compressed, lithified corpses of ancient vegetation that have become a kind of breathing mortar. This is the architecture of the place: a continuous, self-supporting cylinder of vegetation that plunges downward into warmth, a tube of life suspended in an emptiness that should not be. Beyond the woven walls, there is only the vast hollow dark—the near-void of the shaft itself, stretching away above and below into obscurity.

You are inside the skin of the world, and the skin is alive.

The scale is intimate, hidden. You are not surveying this from a distance. You are *within* a single pocket of it—a small chamber where the woven plant-walls have parted into a room-sized cavity, a bubble in the vegetative mass. Roots as thick as thighs cross the space overhead and underfoot, braided into a floor that gives slightly with each step, resilient and warm, like walking on sleeping muscle. The air is thick with moisture. It condenses on every surface, beads up on the broad leaves that line the chamber, drips from the dangling tendrils overhead in a slow, irregular rhythm. It is not rain, but a constant weeping, a transpiration cycle that never quite dries. Water moves through this place in sheets and films, seeping along root channels, pooling in the cupped leaves of enormous epiphytes, then evaporating again into the saturated air. A drop hits your shoulder. Another, your upturned palm. The temperature is body-warm, a humid 28 or 29 degrees Celsius that makes the line between your skin and the air feel negotiable.

And the noise is incessant.

It is not a single sound but a chorus of small ones multiplied by millions. A constant low rumble of growth—the creak and groan of expanding roots, the wet pop of new shoots forcing their way through older tissue. The drip and trickle of water. A high, papery rustle as leaves adjust to some unseen air current, turning their faces toward the light that is not light. And beneath it all, a thrum, a deep sub-audible pulse that you feel more in your sternum than your ears, the combined respiration of a billion stomata opening and closing in slow waves, the great lung of the shaft breathing.

The light. It comes from the plants themselves, but it is a light that refuses to behave. It is a bioluminescence that does not simply glow but *leaks*. Veins in the leaves pulse with a pale green-white fluid that shines through the translucent tissues, and where the fluid runs, it leaves a faint afterimage in the air, a smear of light that hangs for a moment before dissolving. Some of the larger shelf fungi jutting from the walls emit a steady, rhythmic flashing, like a heartbeat made visible. Fruiting bodies dangle on hair-thin stems, each one a tiny globe of rose-gold luminescence that sways in currents of air too slight to feel. The overall illumination is about that of a well-lit room at twilight—you can see clearly, read by it, but the light has no source. It seems to be a property of the space itself, a glow that suffuses the warm wet air.

The colors are deep greens and bruise-purples, the pale white of roots never exposed to sun, the shocking orange of a parasitic vine that coils around a root-bundle. But the light is doing something to the colors. They shift, slightly, when you're not looking directly at them. A leaf that was green a moment ago now seems veined with a deep red that beats in time with your own pulse. The orange vine leaves a faint orange ghost of itself hanging in the air when you turn away. The reality of this place is thin, permeable. The plants are not just alive—they are *thinking* in some slow vegetative way, and their thoughts press against the boundaries of what you understand as real. The parameters of the world are off. The light has weight. The sound has color. A drip of water falls upward for a handspan before gravity remembers its duty. The weeping of the leaves sounds, for a moment, like distant voices speaking a language you almost knew in a dream.

Rarely, there are fauna. Small things. A beetle the size of a thumbnail, its carapace a perfect mirror, scuttles across a root and disappears into a crevice that closes after it like a healing wound. Once, you glimpse something larger moving in the tangle above—a sinuous shape, furless and pale, with too many limbs, that flows through the vegetative mesh as if the roots were parting for it. It makes a sound like a woman singing two notes at once, and then it is gone. The plants are the substrate, the architecture, the majority of the living mass. Animals are incidental, visitors passing through a world already complete.

This chamber is new, geologically speaking. The roots that form its walls are sharp-edged, not yet worn smooth by time or water. The edges of the leaves are serrated and fresh, unblemished. The whole pocket has the feel of something recently emerged—a bubble rising through the green matrix, a clearing that has just opened. The erosion here is not from without but from within, a biological process of growth and decay and rearrangement happening at visible speed. A tendril uncurls as you watch, reaches across the void of the chamber, and anchors itself in the opposite wall. The bridge it forms begins to thicken immediately, new leaves budding along its length. In a day, this chamber will be smaller. In a week, it may have closed entirely, crushed back into the vegetative mass, replaced by another pocket opening somewhere else in the endless green tube.

There are no buildings, no worked stone, no artifacts. The tech level is purely biological, the hand-tools of evolution. But there is craft here, of a kind. The way the roots have woven themselves into a floor that supports weight without tripping. The way the light-giving fungi are arranged in patterns that illuminate the space evenly. The way the water is channeled along specific root-grooves to pool in leaf-basins at convenient heights. This is a world built by plants, for a purpose that does not include humans but allows for them, provisionally, as long as they do no harm.

The shaft is the world. Above, the green tube continues, curving slightly, the light shifting through a spectrum of pale glows as different bioluminescent species dominate. Below, the warmth increases, and the sounds deepen, the thrum becoming a subsonic vibration that makes the water droplets shiver on their leaves. The entire system is in motion, an active, dynamic ecology of growth and competition and slow, vegetable warfare. Vines strangle vines. Fungi consume roots from within, only to be consumed in turn by bacteria that glow a toxic blue. The air cycles, heated by the earth's deep warmth, rising, cooling as it brushes against some unseen water source, sinking again, carrying spores and moisture and the strange, particulate light in a continuous convective loop.

You stand in the chamber, the only solid thing in a world of living flesh and empty space. A drop of sap, warm and faintly sweet-scented, falls from a broken root onto your arm. It glows for a moment, the light sinking into your skin, and for that moment you can hear the thoughts of the shaft—a slow, green pulse of contentment, a sense of endless unfolding, a wordless understanding that this is how the world was before the world was broken, and how it will be again after. Then the light fades, the sound returns to mere sound, and you are alone in the warm, wet, breathing dark, wrapped in a reality that does not quite know how to hold its shape.
