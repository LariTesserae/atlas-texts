You descend into the quiet of the Deep Reach—a name that itself has worn smooth over ages in the mouths of the lantern-keepers, though no one remembers who first spoke it. The stairs are not stairs but a sloping seam of rock, scalloped by ancient water into something like a road, its surface pocked with the faint grip-marks of hand-carved footholds. They are so old that the edges are rounded, almost soft, as if the stone itself has forgotten which shapes were made by hands and which were made by drip and dissolution. The air is cool and still, bearing the mineral weight of limestone and something sharper—a tang like old metal, rustless but alive. Your breath does not mist. The damp lives here without cold, an interstitial moisture that beads on walls and slicks the floor in patches that never quite gather into pools.

Light comes from everywhere and nowhere, a radiance that makes you blink after the surface gloom. It is not sunlight but a blue-white phosphorescence that emanates from the rock itself, from vast curtains of crystalline lattice that sheathe the cavern walls. These are not plants—nothing green grows here, no root or leaf or creeping stem. The crystal is a mineral flesh, a lattice grown in layers thinner than paper, and it translates some deep earth energy into this steady, sourceless glow. In places it has fractured into shards that lie scattered across the floor like frozen mirrors, and in others it coats the ceilings in ribbed vaults that imitate the architecture of living things but are pure geology. The light makes the space feel open, a volume so immense that you cannot see the far walls. You stand on a shore of sorts: the floor slopes away into an expanse of darkness that is not dark but a blue distance where the phosphor fails and the eye invents depth.

The city is scattered across this plain like a script eroded on a tablet. Once, a people built here—cliff-dwellers, chisel-wielders, makers of stone. Their structures rise now as low mounds, as broken vertebrae of wall, as occasional towers that still dare to stand, their windows empty sockets. The stone they used was the same as the surrounding rock, cut and stacked without mortar, each block shaped by patient pounding with harder stone, fitted so precisely that even now, after the erosion of aeons, a knife blade cannot find the seams. But the seams are there, widened by time, softened, the sharp angles blunted into organic curves. Entire facades have slumped into talus slopes of block and dust. The city was never dense; even when the builders walked these halls, they clustered their dwellings in small hamlets separated by vast carven plazas. Now those open spaces feel not empty but waiting, as if the city is a breathing lung between exhalations.

The erosion here is not violent but absolute, a process so old it has become a kind of stillness. The place has worn down to its final shape, a minimum of resistance, yet it is still wearing. You see it in the drip. Everywhere, from the high vaults—beyond sight, lost in the lambent haze—water condenses in slow, irregular falls. Single drops detach from stalactite tips and fall soundless for long seconds, striking stone with a plosive tick that is utterly swallowed by the space. This water is not plentiful; it comes in veils that shift with unseen air currents, pattering here and not there, leaving one pool deep as a fingernail and another pathway always dry. The damp is a presence more than a substance, a taste on the tongue, a glisten on the crystal walls. Over centuries, each drop carries away a grain of calcium, a molecule of memory. Columns have narrowed to threads, domes have thinned to eggshells. The city is being dissolved back into the substance from which it was carved. And yet it is also being built, because the same water deposits its load elsewhere, forming new crusts, new draperies that hang like frozen flesh from the remnants of walls. A slow, silent turbulence of creation and destruction, a flux on a geological clock.

This is where the fauna lives.

They are abundant, these creatures of the Reach, and they are its true citizens. You become aware of them not by noise—there is almost no noise—but by movement. Glimpses of soft, pale bodies slipping through the ruins. They have adapted to a world without plants, where energy comes from the crystal light itself. The base of everything is a fine, white fungal mat that grows on any surface where the condensation lingers, a mycelial lace that glistens like fresh frost. It digests the rock, releasing minerals, and in turn feeds smaller things. Flocks of winged things—not birds, but something like soft, featherless moths the size of your two hands—drift through the open space, their translucent bodies catching the blue light and scattering it. They make no sound; their flight is a passive floating, wings moving only to correct course. They feed on the fungal mats, grazing with rasping tongues that whisper at the edge of hearing.

Larger things hunt them. In the distance, you see a slow ripple of disturbance among the moths, and then a shape emerges from behind a crumbled arch. It is bipedal, long-limbed, with skin like polished alabaster and a head that is mostly a sensory fan of fine, vibrating cilia. It moves with a quiet deliberation, catching the moths not with hands but with a rapid extension of a sticky tendril that retracts into a facial pouch. It is one of the lantern-keepers, though they keep nothing; they are simply the largest of the native things. They live in the spaces between structures, never inside, and they watch with an intelligence that seems ancient and incurious. They do not speak. No sound comes from them at all, not even the pad of their feet on stone. They are as silent as the water’s slow work.

The turbulence of the Reach is not in sudden catastrophes but in the constant, cumulative churn of this silent ecosystem. The fungal mats wax and wane with the shifting moisture patterns, and the moth populations bloom and crash, and the keepers drift from one feeding ground to another, their passage wearing paths into the stone that look like natural water channels. The city rearranges itself at the pace of geology: a wall that stood for ten thousand years will suddenly—by the standards of this place, “suddenly” meaning over the course of a season—crumble because a critical drip has eaten through a key block. The sound of that collapse is a low, rubbery thud that echoes once and is gone, and for a while the moths avoid that place, and the keepers investigate with slow curiosity, and then the dust settles and the fungal mats creep in and the new ruin becomes part of the old.

You walk through it. The openness is deceptive; there are always structures near, but never so many that you feel crowded. The city has the feel of a memory palace abandoned, where each standing fragment holds a faint theme of its original purpose. One low mound is a labyrinth of knee-high walls, the remains of a communal dwelling whose roof has dissolved entirely; the chambers are exposed to the blue light, their floors crusted with crystal. Another place is a tower that, against all odds, still stands seven storeys high, its windows linked by external stairs that now end in empty air. You climb it anyway, and from the top you see the Reach as a whole: a plain of glowing rock and shadow, studded with these quiet shapes, stretching to walls you still cannot see. The ceiling is a false sky of glimmering points where the crystal growths are thickest, a constellation of unmoving stars.

And there is something else in the distance, at the edge of sight: a regular pattern, a grid of darker lines that might be roads or channels, but they are too straight, too precise. The builders left more than houses; they left a network that may have connected this place to others, a subterranean urbanism that now lies broken. You could follow it, if you wished, and walk for days through identical light and silent life, through this sparse and ancient metropolis that is only just barely dead—or perhaps never alive in the way we think, but always in this state of slow, waking change. The Weirdness is not in any single element but in the combination: a bright, open, underground city without plants, without metal, without sound, where abundant life moves to the rhythm of dripping water on stone, and everything you see is both a ruin and a growing thing. The scale is not human, and never was. The primitiveness of the tools that made this place matches the primitiveness of the life that inhabits it now: both are before complexity, or after it, in a world that has streamlined itself to the simplest possible cycle. Water, stone, light, flesh. No waste, no haste. A turbulence so slow it feels like peace.
