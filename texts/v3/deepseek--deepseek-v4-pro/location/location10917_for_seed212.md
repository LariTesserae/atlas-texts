The air itself seems to sweat. You stand at the rim of a vast, low bowl—a basin fresh as a wound in the earth’s crust, though no bare stone shows anywhere. The ground is not ground; it is a mat of living fiber, a woven floor of pale roots and creeping stems that pulses under your feet with a deep, vascular heat. Everything is warm, close, humid: the damp is not rain but a perpetual exhalation, a fog that combs through the layered surfaces and leaves a slick of condensation on every surface and every surface is plant. There are no animals. No insects tick in the crevices, no birds stitch the sky. The silence of absent fauna is a kind of negative space obliterated by noise.

The noise is immense—a cacophony that feels physical, a pressure in the ears. It is the sound of growth: a million tiny pops of cellular division amplified to a roar, the creak and groan of woody stems lengthening in real time, the slither of tendrils sliding over each other, the tear of old leaves surrendering to new. There are wet, percussive bursts—pods opening with the force of small detonations, scattering seeds that spiral down like shrapnel. The whole place reverberates with a bass hum, as if the root-mass is vibrating with some deep, anaerobic metabolism. You realize the city is breathing.

City: that is the only word for it, though no hand or tool shaped a single arch. The vegetation does not merely fill the space; it architects. Rising from the basin floor are towering, spire-like trunks that splay at their crowns into platforms and terraces, linked by bridges of braided liana thick as roads. Structures swell in bulbous domes of spongy moss, their skins punctured by precisely circular windows—holes grown, not carved, ringed with a darker tissue like scarred lips. There are avenues: long troughs where the underlayer has depressed into shallow channels, lined with a different species, a slick, water-holding moss that glistens with iridescence. In those channels, slow threads of liquid move—not pure water, but a greenish sap, streamlets of the city’s circulatory system.

This is a dense world: every cubic palm is filled. Surfaces layer upon surfaces; beneath the foot-mats, you sense a lattice of root-arteries, and above, the spires vanish into a canopy so thick it becomes a ceiling, then a second ground hanging inverted, where ferns dangle and pale, blind tendrils hunt the empty air. The scale is open enough to take in a full district—a valley’s breadth of this botanical metropolis—but the sheer crowding shortens perspective, turns every view into a near-cluttered tapestry of curve and coil and leaf-blade. It is warm, so warm that the sap sometimes steams where it pools.

And it is young. There is a sharpness to the forms that erosion would have long softened in an old landscape. The spire-edges are still rippled with the marks of their rapid extrusion; the arching bridges are tense, unscarred by decay, as if they sprung into place only hours ago. Yet simultaneously, everything is in furious flux. A dome swells visibly, its skin stretching until it tears, releasing a cloud of spores and a high, whistling shriek. A bridge snaps under its own growth-spurt, the severed ends whipping away to coil around new supports, already stitching a different route. The city is not stable—it is turbulent, a storm-state of creation and destruction. The patterns you are beginning to recognize shift even as you look: an avenue pinches closed, a spire leans and is absorbed by its neighbors, a new clearing opens where a mat of crawlers suddenly withdraws, revealing a dark, digestive pit that steams and bubbles, then seals over with a fresh skin of green.

This is where the world’s logic frays. The reality breakdown has seeped into the biology. Some of the plant-forms seem to contradict themselves: a single stem branches into three, yet each branch is also the same stem, overlapping in a way your eyes cannot track. Leaves occasionally turn sideways into colors that do not exist. There are arches that loop through themselves, a fibrous Möbius knot that pulses from some internal rhythm. The mist, too, behaves strangely, sometimes falling upward, beading on the undersides of surfaces with no regard for gravity. In places, the vegetation grows into shapes that are distinctly *remembered*—a half-finished spiral stair leading nowhere, a row of pillar-like trunks topped with flattened palettes that look too much like a colonnade, a perfect rosette window of cambium and phloem. The city is a memory of urbanity, rehearsed in the only medium this world allows: living tissue.

There are no builders, no mind that you can sense. The city builds itself, a chaotic, hyperactive anthill where the ants are also the galleries, the queen, the pheromone trails, and the food. The deafening sound is its voice—a constant, full-spectrum scream of vitality. You taste the damp on your lips, a faint sweetness, and feel the young, sharp edges of a world being born and devoured simultaneously. As you watch, a nearby tower of coiled vines unravels into a cascade of sap, a deliberate collapse that floods the adjacent channel and triggers a chain reaction: new shoots erupt instantly from the glutinous pool, spiraling upward, already hardening into a thicker, more ambitious structure, studded with thorns the length of arms. The thorns gleam, ceramic-smooth, and for a moment they catch the normal light—a warm, steady sun, invisible beyond the canopy ceiling but filtering through in blades—and the whole valley glints as if armored.

You understand, after a time, that the dampness is not occasional. It is the condensation of the city’s own sweat, the breath of its constant transpiration. It rivers through the channels, pockets in cupped leaves, drips from the high bridges in a fine, perpetual drizzle that never quite becomes rain but never quite stops. And the warmth is the fever heat of a metabolism that runs at an insane pace, burning through life cycles in hours, days at most. The young erosion is a bitter joke: the landforms never get old enough to erode. They are replaced before any wind or water can wear them down.

Step down into it. The mat accepts your weight, then adjusts, fibers tightening. A path forms—or rather, a path was already forming, and you are merely walking where it was about to be. The sound engulfs you, so loud it seems to reach through your skull and pluck at your thoughts. The smell is green and electric, the odor of crushed chlorophyll, of growth hormones and cell death, a composting edge beneath the freshness. The reality breakdown makes the distances play tricks: the far end of the valley sometimes seems near enough to touch, then recedes, as if space itself is pulsing with the same generative rhythm. You are in the open, yes—the valley is town-sized—but the dense, architecture vegetation encloses, so that you feel simultaneously in a vast arena and a cluttered room. The city is everything; it is the world entire, a pure-plant ecosystem that has swallowed even the idea of stone or sky.

And there is no escape from the dynamic. Turn around, and the archway you passed under is now a solid wall of tumbled fern-fronds. Retrace your step; the step is no longer yours, but a new boulevard curving away into a hypostyle hall of woody pillars. The turbulence has no malice. It is simply the state of things: a Solaris-like garden that dreams in forms, endlessly building and destroying a city that no one will ever inhabit—except perhaps the plants themselves, if they can be said to inhabit their own bodies. The silence of no animal life is not a lack but a concentration: all the language, all the agency, all the noise of the living world has been taken up by the green and given back as architecture, as deafening, as a warm and breathing urban wilderness.
