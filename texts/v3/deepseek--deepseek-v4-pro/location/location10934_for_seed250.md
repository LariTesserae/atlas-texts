The light arrives not as a beam but as a presence—cool white clarity that fills the water like a held breath. Above, the surface is a distant rippling lens, infinitely remote, yet the light column persists: undisturbed, unwavering, as if the ocean itself has been stripped of anything that might cast a shadow. There is no plankton cloud, no swirling detritus, no silt. The water is a lens of absolute transparency, a near-vacuum of matter, so empty that depth loses meaning. You can see for miles laterally, the view fading only because the eye eventually fails at the edge of an impossibly clean abyss.

What you see, across those miles, is a landscape in the act of becoming.

The seafloor here is not a floor; it is a skeleton rising. This is a ridge, a seam of planetary growth, elevated high into the photic zone by forces that have not yet finished their work. The terrain is a raw black architecture of basalt: pillowed lobes, glassy sheets, fresh talus slopes that have never known the scouring of sediment. There are no muds, no drifts of organic snow—the erosion index is so low that every surface is sharp, factory-new. The rock is cool to the touch (the temperature gauge would read a steady, bracing 8°C), its heat long since stolen by the vast water volume, except where new fissures split open in slow motion. Those fissures exhale a shimmer of temperature difference, a faint schliere that distorts the light for a moment before dissipating.

Sparse vegetation clings to the margins of this rawness. Not plants in any terrestrial sense—there is no soil—but thin, translucent films of chemosynthetic archaea that paint the older basalts with a faint patina of rust and pale green. They are more stain than lifeform, surviving on the hydrogen and hydrogen sulfide still seeping from the rock’s microscopic pores. They barely register on the scale of living things, a whisper of carbon in a mineral world. No animal moves among them. There is no grazing, no predation, no drift of jellyfish or pulse of fish school. The fauna score is an absolute zero. It makes the water feel even emptier, a sterile cathedral.

Yet the place is deafeningly loud.

Sound here travels with an eerie, bone-conducting fidelity. The water is an acoustic medium without clutter, so every event rings out across the entire vast scale of the ridge. The dominant note is a continuous basso profundo: the grinding, groaning, and tearing of lithosphere. Massive rock bodies torque past one another in ultraslow earthquakes that last for days, their energy translated into a subsonic hum that vibrates your sternum. Over that drone, staccato cracks ring out like gunshots—pillow basalts shattering as they cool too fast, their glassy crusts spalling under thermal stress. And at unpredictable intervals, a deeper, throatier roar: a fissure eruption. A curtain of incandescent magma blinks into existence along a new crack, its light briefly outshining the sun from below, an orange-black strobe that turns the whole water column to molten copper before the lava freezes to a new skin of rock. The quench is explosive on contact, a furious hiss that resolves into a million tiny pings as the contraction cracks race through the fresh stone.

This is not mere geology, though. Not exactly.

The basalt surfaces, when you drift close, are inscribed with patterns that are not random. They are not built structures—there is no metal, no jointing, no tool-mark—yet the crystalline alignments curve in fractals that repeat at every scale, from the centimeters of a chilled margin to the kilometers of a rift valley. The ridge itself is a spine of hypergeometry, self-similar and recursive. It is as if the planet’s crustal accretion is following a deep algorithmic template, a set of physical laws that no human lab has ever coaxed into existence. The technology, if that is the word for it, is inherent in the fabric of the matter: a nanoscale engineering of crystal nucleation, a tuning of melt viscosities, a feedback system that controls spreading rates with an accuracy beyond any dumb magma chamber. The entire ridge is a computer, a vast self-constructing machine built of olivine and pyroxene, processing planetary heat and chemistry into an ever-extending backbone of new world. There are no artifacts because the artifact is the landscape itself, a terraforming engine running an embryogenic program that has not yet reached its final state. The erosion score is near zero because this place rejects entropy; it is all construction, no decay.

The scale is such that the ridge generates its own weather. Not wind, but currents: gyres of chilled, dense water form along its flanks and cascade into the abyss, rivaling any atmospheric front in their momentum. The turbulence parameter manifests as these chaotic, shifting boundaries between water masses, their interfaces visible only as slight variations in refractive index—ghostly, shimmering walls that writhe and break like slow-motion storm fronts. The sound of these mixing layers is a constant tearing whisper, a static rush that fills the quiet gaps between the groans of tectonics.

High elevation and high water: the ridge crests are so shallow that the surface wave action—hundreds of meters above—sends down pressure pulses that visibly flex the thinnest crustal plates. The light, normal and full, reveals every detail: the branching networks of cooling cracks, the tiny gas vesicles frozen in basalt like trapped thoughts, the occasional rare bloom of a chemosynthetic mat that spreads a few centimeters before the next fracture swallows it.

The overall sensation is of being inside a birthing chamber the size of a continent. Everything is raw, immense, unfinished. The rules of this world are simple but alien: creation without entropy, sound without animal voice, light without biomass, complexity without a builder. It is an embryonic planet-ocean, held at the edge of form, running a software older and more advanced than any human engineering—and it is very, very loud.
