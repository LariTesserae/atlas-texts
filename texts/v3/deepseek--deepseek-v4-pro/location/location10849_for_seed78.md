The first thing you notice is the sound. Not a single noise but a continuous, multilayered chord—a hollow, resonant drone that vibrates through your bones and seems to come from everywhere at once. It’s the voice of wind moving through a billion breathing tubes, each note tuned by the diameter and length of the living pipes that form this world. There is no ground beneath you in any ordinary sense, only a lattice of tough, fibrous strands woven into a floor that sways with your weight, each step sending faint ripples for a hundred meters in every direction. You are suspended in the lower reaches of a structure so vast that its far side is never visible, only fading into a blue-gray gloom. You are inside a plant. Or rather, the plant is the place, and you are a tiny moving mote in its hollow interior.

Look up: above is not a sky but a cathedral of interconnected chambers, some wide enough to swallow cloud formations, others narrowing to throat-like passages that filter the constant, frigid wind into fluting harmonies. The walls are not solid but a filigree of cellulose and something like lignin, a skeleton grown into immense rigid hoops, cross-braced with diagonal stays, the whole thing rising kilometer after kilometer into the darkness. The darkness is nearly absolute, because the sun here is a distant, feeble star, and the plant itself does not photosynthesize in any visible-light way you understand. Instead, its outer skin absorbs trace heat from the planet’s core, funnels geothermal warmth up through capillary veins, and exhales a thin, ghostly mist from pores the size of houses. That mist is the only moisture here: intermittent, condensational. It forms suddenly as a freezing fog, a cloud bank that rolls through the lattice, leaving every surface slick with dew that tastes faintly of minerals and yeast. Sometimes the fog thickens to a drizzle, a vertical rain that falls upward or sideways depending on the pressure differentials, pooling in the cups of enormous, membrane-thin leaves before being sucked back into the circulatory system. These are the pockets of water in a larger dryness, the damp breath of a cold metabolism.

The temperature is a constant, sharp cold that creeps through your insulation—not a killing cold, but one that numbs exposed skin within minutes and makes every inhalation feel like a blade. At this elevation, the air is thin, and you are high on a planetary plateau, a geologically young uplift where jagged peaks of black basalt still poke through the botanical architecture in a few places. Those rock spires are strikingly fresh, their edges razor-sharp, unweathered by rain or time, because erosion here is not from wind or water but from the slow, relentless creep of tendrils and rootlets that crack the stone as easily as frost. The plants have only recently colonized this highland, and their architecture is still expanding, still spreading across the barren rock like a green-white tide of structural foam.

That emptiness—that near-void density—is perhaps the strangest part. Despite the total dominance of vegetation, solid matter is astonishingly sparse. The plant is a hollow-world organism, a three-dimensional net with a mass fraction so low it might as well be aerogel. The great chambers and connecting tubes are defined by walls only a few centimeters thick, yet strong enough to withstand the mammoth stresses of their own scale. Stretching away in all directions are galleries of empty space, sepia-dim and echoing, with the only visible “ground” being the intertwined catwalks of the plant’s own circulatory strands. Walking here means moving through a volumetric maze, climbing the sloping ribs, crossing swaying bridges of bundled fiber, stepping over gaps that plummet into untold depths. The scale is planetary: this single organism—or colony, or symbiosis—covers the entire highland and extends tendrils down the cliffsides into warmer, denser zones. The weather inside it is its own, with wind currents driven by differential heating, with small clouds forming and dissolving, with drifting spores that glow faintly in the dark.

Fauna is abundant. They are the source of much of the noise—the shrieks, clicks, and hoots that echo across the void. The inhabitants of this hanging ecosystem are adapted to three-dimensional living: gliding creatures with multiple sets of membranous wings that ride thermal gradients from one level to another; spindly, many-legged predators that pick their way along the catwalks with delicate precision; herds of balloon-like grazers that cling to the inner walls and filter the mist for nutritious microorganisms. Their biologies are slow, cold-blooded but insulated with hollow fur or feather-like scales, and their cries are designed to carry across vast distances, to locate mates or prey. The predator-prey cycle is visible in brief, violent bursts: a sudden scrambling chase, a flash of bioluminescent alarm, a splash of something hot and dark that freezes almost instantly into ruby crystals on the lattice below. The plant feeds on the carcasses, drawing nutrients from the bones that fall into its traps, a recycling loop that turns death into more architecture.

Scattered throughout this place are the marks of something else. Not built by the plant, nor by the current animal life. Sometimes you find a shelter woven of the same fibers as the catwalks but in patterns too regular to be accidental—a dome of interlocking triangles, a platform lashed with sinew. The scale is small, human-sized or slightly larger. The tools left behind are primitive: shards of knapped obsidian, drilled bones used as whistles, fire-hardened digging sticks. There are no metals, only stone, wood, and hide. Whoever they were, they lived here once, or still do, moving through the hollow country in small bands, leaving their faint marks. Perhaps they still exist, hiding in the remotest upper galleries where the air is thinnest and the darkness most profound, their eyes huge and black.

And the darkness. It is not complete. The plant itself emits light—or rather, the fungi that live in symbiosis with its inner skin radiate a dim, greenish luminescence in response to vibration and temperature change. As you move, your footsteps glow briefly underfoot, then fade. The animals have their own lights: signing and signaling in patterns of blue, orange, and white. The entire void is a starfield of tiny, cold fires, shifting with the activity of the place. But the light is not enough to see more than a few meters in any direction, so the impression is always of a infinite, faintly twinkling emptiness, a universe contained within a living shell.

The weirdness is not in any single thing but in the combination: a frozen, high-altitude, almost-geologically-new landscape where plants have become the entire world, but a world so thin-walled and voluminous that you live inside it like a bacterium in a sponge. The rules of physics seem normal, but the biology has escalated beyond any familiar scale. The dynamic processes never cease: the plant grows at the tips, extending new hoops and struts, while older sections slowly die and collapse, their substance reclaimed. The animals migrate in cycles, following the mist fronts. The wind never stops, funneled through fluted passages, and the sound of it—the constant, modulating drone—becomes a presence in itself, a voice of the living hollow earth.

Let your eyes adjust to the gloaming. Look down through the catwalk at your feet. Far below, a hundred meters or a thousand, a flash of movement: a herd of translucent grazers, their bodies silhouetted against the green glow of a fungal bloom. Look up: a ceiling of interlocked arches, each ridge a faint blue line against the black. Somewhere a predator screams, and the scream bounces twelve times before fading. The mist begins to gather again, beading on your face, chilling you further. You are in a forest that has become a geography, a architecture of living emptiness, cold and sharp and humming with life. You walk on, the web trembling, and the whole world breathes around you.
