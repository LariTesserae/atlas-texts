The sound comes first — a continuous, thrumming roar so total it seems to erase the idea of silence. It’s not the crash of waves or the rumble of thunder, but something finer: the hiss of uncountable raindrops hitting raw stone, multiplied into a static shriek that fills the skull. There is no echo here, no pause between impacts; it’s a single, churning pressure against the eardrums, a white flood of noise that vibrates through the ribcage. This is a place where water is not a substance but a condition, a medium you wade through even as you breathe. The air is thick with it — vertical rivers shredding into mist, rebounding from every surface, until the distinction between atmosphere and deluge blurs. You are not rained upon; you are inside the rain. Submerged.

You crouch in a hollow that barely qualifies as a shelter. It’s a pocket worn into the flank of a lava flow — a raw gas bubble that popped as the rock cooled, leaving an egg-shaped concavity just large enough for a body to curl into. The floor is gritty with fresh tephra, sharp-edged and unweathered. This is new stone. The volcanic ridge to which this fold belongs is still shrugging off its own creation, steaming faintly where the rain finds hot cracks. The whole landscape is embryonic, a geology just-born and already under assault. No gentle erosion here; the rain is a frantic sculptor, but it hasn’t had time to round the edges. Everything is jagged, broken, shattered into planes that glisten wetly. Dark basaltic fins jut from the slope like the half-formed vertebrae of a stone leviathan, their surfaces still showing the glassy sheen of quenched melt. The ground beneath them seethes with minute tremors — cooled skin over a restless interior.

Beyond the mouth of the hollow, the world is a shifting curtain of grey. Visibility comes and goes in pulses, driven by gusts that whip the rain into diagonal sheets. In the brief clearings, you can see how high you are: the lava ridge drops away precipitously into a gulf of swirling vapour, and beyond it, jagged peaks claw up through the storm, their summits lost. The elevation is extreme, the air thin and cool even through the moisture. Each breath tastes of cold iron and wet ash. The temperature hovers just above freezing; the rain is liquid but barely, occasionally thickening into slushy pellets that sting exposed skin. There is no comfort of warmth.

The vegetation is skeletal. In the hollow with you, a few pale lichens crust the stone, their fronds like frozen lace. Outside, along crevices where the water channels briefly, patches of moss struggle in olive-green cysts. Once, something taller tried to grow: a twisted, desiccated stem no thicker than a twig, stripped of leaves, juts from a crack ten feet away. It’s the only tree you can see, and it’s dead. In this relentless pounding, roots can’t take hold; the soil itself hasn’t had time to form. The ground is a scatter of grit and boulders, swept clean of anything loose by the runoff. What isn’t rock is pumice, too light to stay put, skipping downhill in the sheets of water.

And yet, this pocket is full of emptiness. The rain, for all its ferocity, is mostly open air; the droplets are dense but discrete, a swarm of liquid lenses zinging through a void. Between the streaks, there is a vast, grey nothing — an absence of body, structure, boundary. The landscape is a hollow cathedral of water and wind, with only the bare skeleton of the ridge interrupting the flow. It’s a place of negative space: the rock is the frame, the rain the negative image, the air a medium that carries more sound than substance. From the hollow, you look out into a volume that is remarkable for what it lacks — no soil, no thicket, no dapple of light, no animal movement. Just the hurtling dots of water, and the astonishing loudness of their passage.

Movement, when it comes, is furtive. A flicker of black against grey: a small bird, drenched and frantic, dives into the lee of a boulder. It shakes itself in a spray of droplets, then vanishes again into the murk. Once, you find the husk of an insect clinging to the rock inside the hollow — a stonefly nymph, far from any stream, its body hollowed out and dry despite the damp. Fauna here is a matter of rare, desperate visitations. The storm is too violent for permanent residence; even the lichens seem to be holding their breath.

The hollow itself holds faint marks of other presences. A half-ring of stones has been set against one wall, not mortared, just balanced — a windbreak, or perhaps the foundation of a long-vanished lean-to. Inside the ring, a flat slab of pumice has been placed, and on its surface, shallow grooves have been scratched: not quite writing, not quite a map, but a series of repetitive arcs that might be counting marks. A handful of charred twigs, long cold, suggests a fire that once burned very briefly. The craft is primitive, nothing more than hand-work, the stones gathered and placed without metal tool. Yet someone sheltered here, once, and left these scattered traces — a minor node in a network of such refuges across this unwelcoming terrain.

The light is unremarkable, and that itself is strange. It’s diffuse and directionless, a flat, grey illumination that seems to emanate from the cloud cover rather than a distinct sun. Shadows don’t fall; they’re washed out. The quality is liminal, a perpetual overcast afternoon that never deepens to twilight or brightens to full day. It’s normal, in that you can see texture and distance, but it’s a light without warmth, without glory, without variance. It simply is.

But the weirdness slides in at the edges. When the wind shifts, the rain doesn’t just fall — it hovers. For seconds at a time, the droplets slow and hang, as if the air has thickened to oil, each one suspended a foot from the ground, trembling. In these moments, the sound drops a tone, becomes a resonant hum, and the drops seem to bead with a faint inner light, just a phosphorescent glimmer, before crashing down all at once like a shattering chandelier. And sometimes, out in the grey gulf, you think you see shapes that aren’t rock: pillars, or arches, or the suggestion of a vast, occupied plaza that disintegrates when you focus on it. These are not reliable visions. They might be the rain itself, organizing briefly into geometries before dissolving. The ground, too, seems to be actively making itself — new rilles crack open as you watch, and out of them curls a thread of steam that smells of burnt sugar. The whole ridge is a process, a slow eruption of matter into form, simultaneously being torn apart by the water. Creation and destruction are locked in a turbulent, hissing stalemate.

In the hollow, you are hidden. The opening faces slightly downwards, sheltered by a brow of basalt. From outside, the pocket would be invisible in the mist. It’s a micro-environment, a single feature enclosed within the greater chaos. The storm screams past but doesn’t enter directly; the air inside eddies softly. You have the sense of being embedded within a mechanism, a tiny void that the world has temporarily forgotten to flood. The dynamic outside is hyperactive — a furiously exchanging bazaar of water, wind, and rock, particles trading places in constant acceleration — but inside, there is a precarious stillness. The boundary is the thin lip of stone that divides your breathable void from the liquid tumult.

As you wait, the elements continue their argument. The rain sharpens. The hiss becomes a roar, the roar a shriek, and then, impossibly, it quietens for a heartbeat, and you hear another sound beneath: a deep, resonant groaning, the voice of the mountain itself, still cooling, still settling, still emerging into a world that is simultaneously washing it away. Then the deluge resumes, and the pocket shrinks around you, intimate and raw and tremendously, destructively alive.
