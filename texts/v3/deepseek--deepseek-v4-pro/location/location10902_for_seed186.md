The place is called the Kongur-Gel Massif, though you have never heard it named, and neither has anyone else. It is not a place for names.

You stand not on ground but on a surface that was never soil. It is ice the colour of lead, old ice, pressed into lenses and terraces that hold their shape against the slow creep of gravity. The elevation here is punishing — well above the tree line, above the cloud line when there are clouds, above everything but the patient work of wind and the cold that lives here like a resident, not a visitor. The air is thin and hurts in a way that feels clarifying, as though each breath strips something unnecessary from the blood. The temperature has not risen above freezing in seven thousand years, and the ice beneath you has a memory that long. It remembers the shape of boulders now vanished, the weight of glaciers now retreated to the valleys below, the pressure of a sky that was once warmer and is no longer.

And yet the place is lush.

This is the first contradiction that makes the Massif strange, though it will not be the last. The vegetation does not grow from dirt — there is no dirt — but emerges directly from the ice itself, or rather from the narrow, weeping channels of liquid water that thread through the frozen mass like veins through marble. You walk. Or imagine walking.
