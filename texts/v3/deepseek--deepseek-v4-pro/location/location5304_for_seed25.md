The Basin of Teeth is not a place you enter so much as are absorbed by, a lowland sprawl beneath a sky that has never seen sun. What light exists is borrowed from the inhabitants themselves: a shifting, particulate glow—blue-white, cold-minded—that reveals more through its motion than its strength. It is a darkness made of layers, so that distance is a thing you feel rather than see, and the open scale of this place, its regional breadth of trough and ridge and long drainage run, is known only by the way sound travels, distorted and relentless.

The air is cool and saturated, a constant 10°C that clings like a wet cloth. Water does not fall so much as it condenses, a perpetual seep that slicks every surface. It drips from overhangs worn into great flowing forms by ages of patient dissolution. This is an established world, weathered into its own equilibrium: the limestone—if it is limestone—has been carved into basins, scalloped runnels, and smooth, parallel ribs that cup the darkness in their hollows. There are no stalactites here, no sharp young fractures; every corner has been rounded by the passage of innumerable bodies, the erosion of friction rather than chemistry. The terrain is a series of nested, open bowls, low elevation but never flat, dropping in lazy steps toward sumps that glint with a viscous, mineral-thick wetness.

No plants root in this stone. The vegetation index is a near-zero, not because the conditions are too harsh for photosynthesis, but because the very concept is irrelevant. Energy here does not filter down from above; it wells up. The primary producers, if the term can be twisted to fit, are the throngs of fauna themselves, their metabolic waste feeding a slime of chemoautotrophic biofilm that coats the rock in a transparent, protein-rich varnish. But this film is not plant, not fungus, not any kingdom you could name. It is a pre-botanical matrix, a living scum that traps minerals and smooths the stone further under a billion scraping feet.

And the feet are everywhere. The density is a physical pressure, a near-touching multitude per cubic metre. The overwhelming fauna moves not as individuals but as a fluid, a slow, seething tide of chitin and cilia. Imagine, if you must, a convergence of arthropod, annelid, and something wholly other—segmented bodies the length of your hand, flat and multi-legged, with soft underbellies that ripple against the wet rock. They are pale, bluish-white in the bioluminescent gleam, with heads that are mostly mouth, intricate and radially symmetrical, constantly worrying at the biofilm, at each other, at the water. They are blind, navigated by a dense fur of vibration-sensitive setae that bristles along their flanks. The deafening sound—2.7 on the scale, impossible to ignore—is the aggregate of their movement and feeding: a harsh, continuous hiss-stridulate-click-hum that resolves into something like a waterfall played through broken glass. It is so pervasive that silence becomes a fable; the creatures themselves are shaped by it, their signals carved out in frequency notches within the din.

This cacophony is the voice of the ecosystem’s active dynamic. The basin is a predator-prey theatre performed by a single broad class of organism. Larger morphs, slow but massive—armoured like trilobites but towering on multiple stilt-legs—plough through the carpet, their passage causing temporary clearings that seal over in seconds. Their mouths are grinding mills, and they take in the smaller types indiscriminately, along with the stone-scum and the mineral-hard sump water. The moisture content here (1.2) is deceptive: it is not a river, not a lake, but a dampness that pervades everything, carrying vibrations, softening edges. Pockets of deeper water lie in the sumps, and in these, a different morphology dominates—soft, pulsating sacs that filter the particulate fallout, their surfaces twitching with perpetual ingestion.

The weirdness, a solid 1.8, settles into you slowly. It is not that the biology is utterly unrecognisable—you can map functions onto forms—but that the rules are skewed. There is no birth or death as you understand it; individuals are modules in a continuous, regenerating sheet. When a creature is damaged, it does not die so much as dissolve, its components re-absorbed into the mobile mat. The larger ones are not separate species but emergent aggregations, temporary nodes in a networked biomass. The sound, from a distance, forms coherent pulses, as if the entire basin is vocalising, a single vast organism gurgling through a digestive cycle that spans days. You realise that the terraces are not just eroded rock; they are shaped to channel this flow, the ribs acting as baffles to direct the traffic of bodies, the sumps as stomach-ponds. The landscape is both terrain and anatomy, built by the fauna over deep time, until the distinction between skeleton and scape is meaningless.

No artifact, no structure, no technology exists here. The score of 0.1 for built and 0.4 for tech is absolute. Yet the place is not untouched—it is touched relentlessly by itself, a closed loop of sensation and reaction. The darkness is total except for the glimmers, and the glimmers are part of the signal, a synesthetic addition to the sound. Light, sound, and pressure are one conversation, a constant negotiation of space and appetite in a world where the very concept of an individual self is blurred into the density of the crowd. The scale is open enough that if you could perceive the whole, you would see this basin extend, connecting to other basins via saddles smoothed by the same endless migration, a regional system in thick, damp, cool equilibrium, never silent, never still.
