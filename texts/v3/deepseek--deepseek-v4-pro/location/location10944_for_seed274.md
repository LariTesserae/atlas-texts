The air itself is wet. Not just humid—wet in the way breath becomes visible at the edge of condensation, a constant film of moisture settling on every surface, slicking stone and skin alike. The temperature hovers somewhere in that ambiguous band of warmth that feels neither generated nor seasonal, simply *present*, a body-heat warmth that has always been here and will always be. No breeze moves through the space; the wetness doesn't arrive on wind, it exudes.

It's a hollow. A single chamber, roughly vaulted, though the ceiling is not architecture—it's the underside of something, a domed concavity worn into dark, close-grained stone. The scale is intimate: perhaps thirty feet at its widest, twelve at its highest point, a room-sized pocket in the world. The boundaries are self-evident. Curving walls of wet rock sweep down to a floor that is not flat but gently dished, and everything—walls, floor, ceiling—is covered.

Covered, not with life, not with the green or brown of growing things, but with a mineral fur. A dense, soft-looking crust of pale grey and off-white accretions, saturated and dripping. This is the packing, the density: there is no bare stone visible, no gap in the crust. Every inch of surface has been claimed by the slow precipitation of minerals from the constant water. The growths have their own morphology—rounded lobes, knobs the size of fists, fluted curtains where the wall drops vertical—but they are not organisms. They are deposited, not grown, layer laid upon layer in a stillness so profound that the distinction almost ceases to matter.

The water moves, but barely. It weeps from the ceiling in a hundred places, not in drips but in continuous, hair-thin threads that slide down the mineral lobes and add their own microscopic skins of calcite. The floor holds shallow, utterly still pools, their surfaces unbroken, reflecting the dimness back on itself. The sound is a near-silence populated by the faintest possible clicks and ticks—each drop's arrival too quiet to hear individually, but together creating a texture of sound like the softest imaginable rain heard through stone. Sometimes a larger drop forms, hangs, falls; the *plink* is swallowed instantly by the wet air.

There are artifacts here. Marks of presence. Scattered, primitive—the parameters said scattered, and they are. In the center of the hollow, a flat slab of stone has been propped level on three smaller stones, and on it sits a shallow bowl, crudely pecked from the same grey rock. It overflows, perpetually replenished by the ceiling's weeping. Around the slab, pressed into the mineral crust of the floor, are faint depressions—knee-prints, perhaps, or the repeated settling of bodies in the same positions over a span of time that has no meaning here. One wall bears scratches, not writing, not imagery, just marks: tally lines too numerous to count, some softened almost to invisibility by the relentless mineral deposition. The only metal is a single object half-sunk in the crust near the slab: a copper pin, green with patina, no longer than a finger, its purpose unreadable.

The light comes from the mineral itself. A property of the stone, or of the water, or of some symbiotic relationship between the two—a faint phosphorescence, blue-white and steady, just enough to see by. It casts no shadows because it comes from everywhere at once, from the walls themselves. The air, thick with micro-droplets, catches the light and makes of the whole chamber a soft glow, a diffuse box of luminescence.

Nothing moves but the water. No fauna: no blind fish in the pools, no skimming insects on the water's surface-skin, no microbial mats coloring the stone. The sterility is absolute, but it doesn't feel sterile—it feels *chosen*, or perhaps pre-biotic, a world on the cusp of deciding whether life is a worthwhile experiment. The warmth and the wet seem to promise life, and the promise is perpetually extended, never fulfilled.

This is a place in equilibrium. The rate of mineral deposition exactly matches the rate of mineral supply; the pools never deepen, the crust never thickens beyond its current state, the knee-prints in the floor never fade because they are maintained, regularly, by something that comes here and kneels. The tally marks increase, but so slowly that the change might be geological rather than biographical. The temperature and humidity are constant, locked, time-invariant. The landscape does not change because it has reached its final form, a closed system cycling water from liquid to vapor and back again, minerals dissolving and precipitating in a loop with no losses.

It is a room that feels like a shrine, but the shrine is to itself. The scattered human marks—the bowl, the tally wall, the knee-hollows—are the only sign that anything outside this hollow has ever noticed it exists. They are primitive, but not ancient; simple, but not crude. They suggest a relationship of pure maintenance, the presence of a caretaker whose entire purpose is to tend the bowl, to kneel, to make a mark, to leave, to return. Nothing in the chamber asks for more.

The air tastes of wet stone and a faint, clean mineral sharpness, like the smell of rain on hot rock, but cooler, older. It is the taste of deep time, of patience without object. The hollow is not waiting for anything. It is complete, packed to its limits with silent, dripping presence, and it will outlast whatever kneels here.
