The city is a raw wound in the earth, a hollowing-out still underway. Every surface is new, unweathered—the walls show chisel marks as fresh as if they were made an hour ago, sharp facets catching the light with a matte, unpolished glint. There is no dust, no patina of age; the place is still being born, its edges angular and jagged, the floors only roughly leveled. The air is cool and carries the constant, low-grade dampness of deep stone—not dripping, but a pervasive moisture that slicks the walls with condensation and leaves a metallic taste on the tongue. Somewhere out of sight, a subterranean watercourse murmurs, its voice threading through the packed honeycomb of dwellings like a quiet, steady breath.

The light is puzzling in its normalcy—a pervasive, even illumination that fills the larger galleries and trickles into the narrowest warrens. It has no single source and no flame-flicker, as if the very walls exhale a soft radiance, or as if generations of hand-polished mirror tunnels have brought the sun down into this underworld and diffused it until it feels like a cloudy afternoon. Whatever its origin, it renders the city in a palette of grey, ochre, and bone. The sparse vegetation thrives on this diluted light: pale, tendriled mosses clump along the edges of seepage; ghostly lichens speckle the buttresses of carved stone; and in the occasional wider chamber, a sole, etiolated tree—some hybrid of willow and fungus—drapes long, feeding strands into a communal cistern.

The city is packed with an almost suffocating intensity. Every cubit of space has been claimed, carved into a dwelling, a workshop, a storehole, a passageway. Structures grow from the rock itself—walls are integrated with the native geology, rooms are excavated rather than built, and the divisions between one family’s niche and another’s are often just sculpted columns or hanging screens of woven fiber. Stairways cut into the stone wind upward and downward, barely a shoulder’s width apart, linking levels that multiply like the chambers of a termite mound. There is no concept of an empty room; even the ceilings are studded with storage baskets and sleeping lofts reached by notched poles. The density is such that when you stand in the main concourse—a thoroughfare just wide enough for three people to pass—you feel the bulk of a thousand lives pressing in around you, their warmth imprinting on the damp stone.

The inhabitants are here, though they move with an almost silent deliberation that belies the population count. Primitive tools dictate the rhythm: the hiss of a stone saw biting through limestone, the soft drumming of wooden mallets on raw chippings, the whisper of woven sandal on grit. There is no clang of metal beyond a few hammered copper fittings; technology is bone-hafted chisels, knapped flint scrapers, and cordage twisted from cave moss. Every sound is muffled by the moisture-laden air and the close-set walls—a murmur of voices, a patter of water into a catch-basin, the subtle creak of a slab door pivoting on leather hinges. It is a city of quiet industry, a hive that hums just below the threshold of hearing.

What open space exists is functional and fleeting. A larger cavern serves as a market, its ceiling lost in darkness above the reach of the light, where harvested fungi and pale, blind fish from the river are bartered in near-silence. Temporary structures of hide draped over bone frames cluster along the edges, but they are folded away when not in use—space is too precious for permanence. Deeper still, the excavation fronts are active: crews with stone tools extend the city, biting into the seam of softer rock, their progress marked by freshly opened hollows that immediately fill with scaffolding and the first tentative walls. The city is consuming itself, the rock becoming void, void becoming dwelling, in a cycle that feels as organic as breathing.

Rare fauna flit through this landscape—a tiny, translucent gecko on a damp ledge, a moth with wings like ash drifting from one lichen patch to another, the occasional tethered goat-like creature feeding on pulverized stone-dust and moss mash in a narrow pen. They are the only animal presences, and they seem to have adapted to the silence, barely making a sound. The atmosphere is not oppressive, though; the coolness and the quiet give it a cloistered calm, a sense of embryonic suspension. This is a place still shaping itself, raw and unfinished, its rules still being carved into its very bones.
