You are not underwater, but you cannot shake the certainty that you are drowning. The air is a liquid weight against your skin, a saturated, near-freezing slurry that beads on every surface and drips upward as often as down. This is the Sunken Heights—a broad, wind-scoured plateau held high in the throat of the world, where the clouds have collapsed under their own weight and now lie permanently pooled across the topography like a ghost ocean. There is no surface to this sea, no boundary between substance and void; it is a continuous, slow-moving wall of supercooled mist, heavy as a sodden wool blanket and just as lightless. Even at what must be midday, the world is a deep indigo murk, lit only by the things that light themselves.

The first thing you notice is the sound. It is impossible not to. A steady, low-frequency drone hums through the stone itself, a vibration that rises through the soles of your feet and fizzes in the sinuses. It is joined by a million percussive drips, the plinks and splats of condensation moving across rock, and beneath that, a deeper, rhythmic sloshing as if some immense bladder empties and fills miles away. The whole landscape resonates like the interior of a shell held to the ear, but multiplied into an open regional vastness. The noise is not chaotic; it has the quality of a sustained chord, a machinery of moisture that never powers down. This is not a quiet, frozen place—it is a loud, active engine of phase change, cycling from vapor to droplet to seep with relentless metabolic rhythm.

The terrain underfoot is ancient and settled, smoothed into scalloped whalebacks of dark schist and basalt, the erosional memory of a time when water was only a visitor, not a resident. Now those same low domes and sinuous gullies are glazed with a living pelt of bryophytes so profoundly cold-adapted they feel like frozen fur. Every crack holds a lens of clear ice that never melts, though it is not winter; the cold is a permanent attribute, a 0.5-score chill that sits just above freezing but steals heat with the efficiency of a thousand wet tongues. The ground is open, extending in all directions in a sparse scatter of low rises and shallow bowls, the kind of mid-scale topography that lets you feel you are in a distinct region, not a single room. Each feature stands alone, separated by wide intervals of smooth, dark rock and standing puddles of fog so dense they reflect nothing.

What rises from this bare framework is the vegetation, and it is lush in that alien way of things that have never known competition from animal mouths. There are no grazers here, no insects, no birdsong, no hidden mammals. Fauna is an absolute zero—a fact that leaves a ringing absence. The plants have filled that ecological vacuum with a slow, botanical fury. They are huge but widely spaced, each specimen an island of complexity in the murk. Imagine trees that are not trees: trunkless pillars of suede-textured tissue, mottled liver-grey and deep lichen-green, swelling at the top into a burst of strap-like fronds that dangle and writhe in the shifting density currents. Each frond is fringed with bioluminescent nodes, a constellation of cold blue-green pinpricks that pulse in a lazy syncopation, illuminating the immediate fog in a ghostly halo. Because they are sparse—a single pillar here, another a hundred yards distant, a third glimpsed as a smudge of light across a shallow valley—you navigate between them like a ship between lighthouses in a sea of cloud. Some of the pillars have a secondary growth: shelf-like extrusions that catch the wetness and direct it into internal channels, and when the pressure builds, they release a gout of stored water with a resonant, wooden *thump* that contributes to the omnipresent noise. That is the sloshing you hear: the collective hydraulic pulse of the groves.

And they are groves, after a fashion, though never dense. Between the pillars, smaller ground growths creep: things like translucent cabbages the size of sheep, their leaves overlapping in geometric spirals that focus the dim light onto a central core of warm pink. These are the ephemerals; they shrivel and regrow over cycles measured not in seasons but in the passage of fog-banks, their life histories tied to the movement of invisible moisture fronts. The dynamic is palpable—the whole place is in a slow churn. Fog thickens and thins in waves, driven by temperature differentials you can’t feel until your skin prickles. The pillars sway incrementally, tracking some unseen flow. Puddles form and drain into sinkholes with a sucking gulp. This is an ecosystem in motion, but one where the predator-prey dynamic has been replaced entirely by the interplay of condensation and evaporation, pressure and release.

Scattered traces of human presence do exist, but they are so primitive they read at first as natural formations. A ring of standing stones, each hip-high and capped with a slab, the whole assembly tilted at an angle by millennia of frost-heave and slow dissolution. The rock is not local; it is a granite erratic, dragged here on some earlier, more solid phase of the world. Inside the circle, you might find a scatter of worked chert—simple blades, an awl of sharpened bone (from what? no animals have been here for an incomprehensible duration), the rusted stain of iron so crudely smelted it still holds the shape of the bog-ore it came from. Elsewhere, a single drystone shelter leans into a protected hollow, its doorway a dark mouth exhaling an even colder draft. The walls are thick with the same bryophyte fur, but inside, if you crouch low, your hands might find a smoothed wooden bowl, a woven net of plant fiber stiff as wire, a charcoal smear on the ceiling that still smells faintly of burnt fat. The tech is hand-tool, manual-craft, early-tool-use with perhaps a glimmer of simple metal—a knife of hammered native copper, green and sweating. Whomever once inhabited these heights either left or faded, leaving only these sparse, faint marks. Their structures are not ruins in the dramatic sense; they are weathered down to near-invisibility, part of the established, settled-form erosion of the landscape itself.

The weirdness suffuses all of it. There is the absence of animals, for one—a silence of anything that moves with intent. There is the way the bioluminescent fronds sometimes synchronize across vast distances, ripples of light passing from pillar to pillar in a wave that has no apparent function, unless the landscape is communicating. There is the fact that the fog, at the edges of your perception, sometimes seems to congeal into upright shapes that hold momentarily before dissolving, a trick of the shifting densities that your brain insists on reading as figures. There is the cold that never deepens into killing frost, the sound that never stops, the sense of being simultaneously high in the mountains and under miles of sea—a cognitive dissonance that becomes a kind of pressure on the mind. This is a place where water has overwritten the rules of altitude, where vegetation has replaced fauna, where technology never escaped the grip of hand and stone, and where darkness and noise are the baseline reality. It is a strange, active, open, sparse, lush, cold, submerged highland, long-established and humming its own loud and ancient tune.
