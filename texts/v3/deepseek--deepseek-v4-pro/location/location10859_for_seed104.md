The world is a suspended ocean, a vast atmospheric sea that fills the space between what was once land and what was once sky. You drift through it, though “drift” is a word for lighter mediums; here, movement is a slow, viscous glide through water that is not quite water, thick with something almost gelatinous but clear as glass. The temperature is warm, like a shallow tropical lagoon, but the pressure hints at depth—this is a deep place, even though its “surface” might be miles above you, where the light first fractures and begins its long fall into the abyss.

Light arrives as a permanent twilight, a dim, diffuse glow that seems to emanate from everywhere and nowhere, as if the water itself is faintly phosphorescent. There is no direct sunbeam, only a soft radiance that gradients from a pale teal overhead to a bruised indigo further down. The color spectrum is muted: greens and blues and occasional flashes of muted violet. The dimness feels ancient, as though the sun, too, has been worn down by eons of filtering through this planetary fluid.

Sound is a rarity. The world is quiet, but not silent. There is a constant low hum, a vibration that might be the planet’s own deep breath, a sound felt in the bones rather than heard. Occasionally, you catch a distant, muffled click or a whispery rustle, but these sounds fade before you can locate their source, leaving only the profound hush of a place that has long since settled into its final, stable state.

The land below—if “below” still holds meaning—is a terrain of ancient, rounded forms. Hills worn to nubs, plateaus smoothed into gentle undulations, all sculpted by a slow erosion that has been at work since the world’s infancy. This is the end result of a billion years of patient wearing-away, a topography so ancient that it has almost forgotten its original shape. In places, the ground rises into high ridges and submerged mountains, their peaks still far above the unseen depths, but even these are gentle, softened, as if the world is an old face where every line has been blurred by time.

Yet, despite the high elevation of these ridges—some towering thousands of feet above the lowest trenches—the water is everywhere, a continuous liquid atmosphere. There is no shoreline, no transition. The “high” elevation here is measured not from a sea surface but from the deepest rifts, so you might be floating over a summit that is still submerged under a mile of water, the topography inverted: peaks are just higher parts of the deep, valleys are even deeper. The water column itself is structured in layers, each with a slightly different density, creating invisible boundaries where the rules of buoyancy shift unpredictably. Sometimes you find yourself rising without effort; other times, the water thickens and grips like a gentle hand, urging you downward.

Vegetation is lush beyond reason. It grows not from soil but from the water itself, or from the ancient stone, or from nothing at all. Great, translucent fronds unroll in slow motion, kelp-like but with leaves that fracture light into rainbows. There are forests of floating bladders and spiraling vines that drift in the current, tangling together into vast, three-dimensional mats. Some plants are anchored to the worn rocks, their holdfasts embedded in stone so polished it looks like bone. Others are entirely free-floating, forming dense, near-touching clouds of life. In some places, the density is so high that you cannot see more than an arm’s length ahead; a seemingly solid wall of vegetation blooms and sways, each individual plant just inches from its neighbor. The density is not just superficial: the water itself is saturated with microscopic life, a living broth that thickens the medium.

Despite this lushness, fauna is rare. You might travel for hours and see no creature larger than a seed. Then, a shape appears—a translucent ribbon with dozens of unblinking eyes, or a drifting disc of jelly that pulses once and vanishes into the green. They are few, and they move with a slow, deliberate economy, as if every calorie must be conserved. There are no swarms, no schools, no sudden strikes. The ecosystem is active, but its pace is geological: nutrient cycles that take centuries to complete, predator and prey interacting in a slow dance where both might starve before the chase is done.

And yet, the place is dynamic. The water moves in massive, cyclic currents, whole oceans of fluid rolling in gentle, planet-sized gyres. The vegetation responds, its growth patterns following the flows, creating seasonal blooms that last decades. The light dims and brightens in a cycle too long to be a day, perhaps the world’s own slow wobble. There is weather here: not wind, but shifts in temperature and density that cause upwellings and downwellings, great vertical migrations of water that carry nutrients from the deep. Sometimes, you witness a “rain” of particles falling from above—not water through air, but a denser saline drizzle descending from a higher layer, catching the light like silver dust.

Scattered throughout this vast, damp world are remnants of something else. Not ruins, but marks: a curved arch of stone too perfect to be natural, standing alone on a smoothed hill; a line of pillars, their edges softened by millennia of gentle dissolution; a single, tilted slab etched with patterns that might have been writing, now grown over with a crust of tiny glowing polyps. These structures are small, faint, and far between. They speak of a time before the world became this, when the water was not so high, or not water at all. Now they are just notes, occasional artifacts that the vegetation embraces and slowly dismantles.

And then there is the weirdness. The reality breakdown is not obvious at first—it creeps in at the edges of perception. Distances are untrustworthy: a nearby frond might suddenly seem miles long, or a distant ridge might snap close enough to touch. Gravity is a suggestion, and sometimes the suggestion changes: you might be drifting laterally, then find that “down” has become tilted by ninety degrees, the ancient ground now a wall. Bubbles rise in squares. Water sometimes flows like a liquid, other times displays facets like a crystal, and in some pockets it is nearly solid but you pass through it like a ghost. The light can carry flavor. Sounds from your own movement might arrive before you make them. It is as if the laws of physics, after an eternity of wear, have become threadbare and are now wearing through, patched with nothing.

This is a place that has aged past the end of life, yet life persists, a dense and quiet tapestry of plant matter in a warm, deep, planetary ocean without a sky. The silence, the dimness, the slow motion, the occasional, unnameable fracture in reality—all of it feels not broken, but as if this is the oldest stable state, the form the cosmos relaxes into when all other possibilities have eroded away. You hang suspended in the high, submerged, ancient, lush, dense, vast, quiet, barely-living heart of a world that has forgotten the difference between sea and sky, and you realize that “landscape” here is not a place, but a process—an active, cycling, breathing dissolution that has been happening for so long it has become a place of its own.
