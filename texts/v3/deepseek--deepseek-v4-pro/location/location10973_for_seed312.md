High on the world’s shoulder, where the sky presses thin and pale as a vein of quartz, the city clings to the young mountains like a second, deliberate geology. It is a place so vast that storms are born within its sprawl—clouds pool in the deeper amphitheaters of its carved basins, and on the highest terraces, the air is so sharp it seems to ring. The settlement is not a single core but a dense, layered embroidery of stone structures following the ridge-lines and crevasses, as though the rock itself had been coaxed into human purpose without ever breaking its essential coldness. Towers of stacked, dry-fitted schist rise from the fog, their edges still razor-sharp, unsoftened by time; this is a landscape newly born from the earth’s most recent upheaval—sheer, angular, uneroded—and its inhabitants have built quickly, urgently, as if the mountains might settle further under their feet.

Everything is close here. The density is not just of dwellings but of presences: stairways thread between homes built so tightly they share walls of split stone barely thicker than a handspan. In the crepuscular half-light that filters through the perpetual overcast—normal, grey, diffused by altitude—the air smokes with breath, both human and animal. Fauna are everywhere, a second populace. Shaggy, barrel-chested *pakka*—beasts of burden with spiraling horns and oil-rich wool—stand flank to flank in terraced byres, their lowing a constant, felt vibration more than a sound. Flocks of greasy-feathered *skirr* (like small, flightless birds with probing beaks) pick through the narrow alleys for scraps, their movements patient and deliberate. The animals outnumber the people, and the people move among them as among furniture, their relationship one of ancient, slow mutualism.

The dampness comes not from rivers or rain but from what the locals call *the seep*: a pervasive, interstitial moisture that condenses every morning on every vertical face. The young rock, still riddled with micro-fractures, sweats. At dawn, the entire city glistens with a film of water that drips from eaves carved of mountain-slate, runs in trickles down the fluted pillars of communal halls, and pools in the hollows of the paving stones. The vegetation—sparse, opportunistic—has claimed these seams of wetness. It is a flora of patience: crustose lichens that spread in slow ink-stains over the walls, cushion mosses that fatten in the joints of the masonry, and the occasional, startling *wire-root* shrub whose leaves are reduced to thorns and whose stem, when crushed, yields a single bead of turpentine-scented sap. The cold (a true zero on the scale of life’s comfort, barely above killing frost even at high summer) keeps growth rates geologic. A moss tuft may take a human lifetime to cover a hand’s breadth of wall.

The city’s technology is of the hand. Iron exists—won from shallow, high-altitude veins—but it is rare and mostly hammered into simple spikes, hooks, and the curved blades of the *snathe*, a short haft-tool used for everything from butchering to carving masonry. Construction relies on precise stone-chipping, animal sinew lashings, and a deep knowledge of how schist cleaves along its natural planes. Structures are assembled without mortar, their stability a negotiation of mass and friction. There are no great wheels here, no complex gears. Loads are carried on backs, or on the pallets of the *pakka*. Water is fetched in cured bladders from communal condensation-catchments that look like immense stone leaves tilted to the sky.

Sound is sparse, economized by the thin air that carries poorly and by a cultural disposition toward quiet. Voices, when they occur, are low and often sub-vocalized; the city’s primary language involves many gestures made within the folds of oiled-wool cloaks. The animals are louder: the *pakka* groan in their sleep, the *skirr* chatter over finds of bone. But even these are muffled by the close-packed stone and the hovering mist. What noise exists is the scuff of leather on rock, the distant, crystalline crack of a shifting pebble, and the very occasional, fluting whistle of a herdsman calling *pakka* from the high pastures that fringe the city’s upper edge. No bells, no hammers. Just the immense, slow silence of altitude, textured by small living sounds.

Dynamics here are stretched across such long durations that a stranger might think the place frozen. Seasons are barely distinct; the temperature wobbles only a few degrees over a year, and the vegetation’s growth is invisible day to day. The city itself has been “under construction” for generations—the same family might spend a lifetime shaping a single chamber from the mountain’s face, their children picking away at the next lintel. The only perceptible change is the slow migration of the *pakka* herds, which trace a circuit around the mountain’s waist over the course of a decade, following a vegetative cycle so sluggish it is almost abstract. Even the mist seems patient, lifting for a few hours each afternoon to reveal the staggering scale of the place: the city’s outermost terraces vanishing into blue-grey distance until they become indistinguishable from the raw, unwalled crags, the whole arrangement suggesting a continent-sized labyrinth that has weathered nothing because it is still in the process of being made.

The atmosphere is one of contained urgency, a density of life held in check by the cold. In the lower gullies, where the seep is strongest and the air warms by a fraction, the density intensifies to something almost uncomfortable: bodies and stone in constant, damp proximity, the smell of wool, animal musk, and the metallic tang of wet schist. Higher up, on the exposed parapets where the wind shears away the mist, the city feels more like a skeleton emerging from the cloud, its sharp-angled towers and uneroded arêtes catching the normal, grey light with a kind of severe gleam. It is a place that could exist nowhere else, a primitive urbanism woven into the very fabric of a young, cold, and restless mountain range, where the fauna are as numerous as the people and the only constant is the slow, ambient drip of water condensing from the thin air, returning to the stone it just left.
