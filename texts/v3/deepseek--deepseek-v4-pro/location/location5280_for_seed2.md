This is the World-Machine’s Coolwater Reach. You drift in it, suspended, and the first thing that registers is the sound: a ceaseless, layered thrum that enters your bones before your ears can make sense of it. It is the bass of thousands of distant engines, the clink and hiss of moving metal, the churn of artificial currents, and woven through it all, a living silk of clicks, grunts, and high-frequency cries—the voice of abundant, invisible life.

Light falls from everywhere and nowhere. It is a cool, even luminescence, mimicking a slight overcast noon, though you are three kilometers below any sky. There are no shadows here, only a gradient of brightness that fades with distance into a blue-grey haze, the water itself turned into a lens of infinite depth. This is the first of the vastnesses: the light-space, which has no wall, no floor, no ceiling that you can perceive, only a slow shift in the quality of illumination that tells you the nearest sun-panels are a few hundred meters behind, and the next array is somewhere ahead, lost in the blue.

The water is cool against your skin—not the biting cold of the deep ocean, but a steady, industrial chill, the temperature of coolant cycling through heat exchangers. It carries a faint metallic taste, clean but not barren. And in this cool, bright nowhere, you begin to perceive the structures.

They arrive as a darkening in the haze, a sharpness. First one, then three, then a forest of them, receding into the blue as far as your eyes can push. They are completely, aggressively new. Every edge is a knife. Every plane is unmarred by growth or corrosion. The material is a composite of steel-grey and pale concrete, shot through with copper cabling that glints an angry orange where the light catches it. There are no organic curves here: it is a world of right angles, truss-work, and the deliberate, modular repetition of industrial logic. This sector—and it is only one sector, one cell in a body that stretches beyond all swimming—is an array of vertical cylinders, each a hundred meters across, their bases lost in the deeper dark, their tops open to the blue above. They are cooling towers, evaporators, or something that serves those functions without air. No two are exactly alike in detail, but they follow a rhythm, a sharp, perpendicular music that speaks of scaled production, of a built order that has not yet learned to weather.

Between the cylinders, the emptiness is its own presence. You can swim for minutes in a straight line without coming close to any surface. The water is clear enough that you see the next structure long before you reach it, and in that transit you are a speck, a mote in a fluid cathedral whose walls are made of distance. This is the second vastness: the volume. It is not a void—it is a deliberate, engineered emptiness, a working space for the movement of fluids and creatures and machines. And within it, the fauna moves.

They are everywhere, and they are normal—or as normal as anything can be in a wholly made world. Silver shoals of herring-like fish, each no longer than your hand, bunch and scatter in shimmering sheets. They react to some invisible signal, a pressure wave perhaps, and the whole school wheels, a solid silver object that disintegrates and reforms. They are so dense that their collective movement creates a low-frequency pulse you feel in your chest. Below them, larger shapes cruise with the slow patience of predators: a species of grouper, heavily built, with the characteristic mottling of a fish that has evolved to blend into natural rock. Here, against the flat grey of a tower base, the camouflage is a patch of organic nonsense, a moving graffiti on the machine’s skin. They don’t mind the dissonance. They hunt.

The machinery is alive too, but in a different rhythm. Maintenance drones—submersible crawlers on magnetic tracks—inch along the cylinders, their welding arms folded, their sensors flickering. A massive conveyor gantry, a bridge of moving belts and sorting arms, spans the gap between two towers a kilometer distant, its silhouette a fine black line against the glowing blue. It emits a regular, percussive clatter that arrives a half-second after the visual, the sound lagging in dense water. Something is being moved: perhaps harvested nodules from a mineral-rich seep, or processed biological slurry from the fish farms. The whole system breathes production.

There is weather here. The artificial currents are the winds of this world. A shift in the thrum, a change in the direction of your own gentle drift, and a plume of slightly warmer, slightly more turbid water rolls past. It carries a scent of ozone and organic rot—a vent from a processing plant releasing treated effluent. The plume teases out a new behavior: the herring move towards it, mouths working, sieving something nutritious from the waste stream. The grouper follow. For a few moments, the empty space becomes a frenzy, a localized storm of predation and feeding, the scatter of scales like sleet, the water thick with motion. Then the plume passes, the integrated turbulence dampens out, and the vast cool quiet, shot through with its persistent mechanical hum, settles back into place.

Sparse vegetation is exactly that: sparse. In the angles of certain structural brackets, where a nutrient film accumulates, there are patches of engineered algae. They are a deep, unnatural emerald, growing in perfect geometric mats, and they glow faintly with their own bioluminescence when the main light dips. They are not wild; they are a component, a filter layer. Elsewhere, there is nothing green at all. The base of the towers disappears downward into a darkness that the overhead light cannot touch, a darkness that is not empty but simply unvisited. Far below, in the trench between foundations, there might be a different kind of ecosystem—one that thrives in total darkness on the slow rain of industrial detritus. But here, in the working layer, the visual field is a clean, bright, enormous, and almost unnervingly young expanse.

The total construction is most obvious when you look for a horizon. There is none. The cylinder array does not meet a seafloor composed of sediment and rock; instead, the towers plunge into a grid of interlocking metal plates, a manufactured abyssal plain that extends seamlessly to the limit of sight. Even the particulate matter suspended in the water is controlled—a faint scattering of inert silicates for light diffusion, nothing like the organic snow of a natural ocean. Every element has a part number, every surface a fabrication date stamped in a corner. You are inside a machine so large that its components can be mistaken for geography, yet it has not been here long enough for that mistake to become truth. The edges are too sharp. The silence between the sounds is too clean.

Standing on a maintenance platform—a barely visible spiderweb of grating suspended in the blue—you can feel the whole system vibrate. It is not the vibration of decay or struggle; it is the steady, powerful pulse of a new engine running at designed capacity. The empty spaces are there for access and for flow. The abundant fish are there as part of the processing chain or as tolerated passengers. The cool water is the working fluid. The light is an operational requirement. Everything is in motion, everything is loud, and everything, from the smallest copepod to the most distant tower, is an element of a landscape that knows itself to be brand new.
