The world here is a slow, breathing dark. You drift—though there is no real drifting, only a perpetual, muscular stillness held at bay by the constant, gentle stroke of your own cilia. The water is warm, an almost body-temperature caress that wraps around you from all sides, carrying the faint, metallic tang of dissolved minerals and the softer, organic perfume of decay. Light is a memory; the sun has been forgotten here, pressed out of existence by the weight of an ocean that goes on and on above, a miles-thick lid of black. But it is not utterly lightless. The darkness is laced with a dust-fine glitter, a perpetual snowfall of marine snow that drifts down from invisible heights, and it catches the faintest phosphorescence: a mesh of blue-green pinpricks, an aurora of bacterial sheen on every surface, the occasional slow pulse of a brighter gleam from somewhere deeper in the labyrinth.

The terrain is a city. But it is a city that has never known a tool, a mind, or a flame. What rises from the silty floor—which is itself a composite of eons of diatom shells and compressed, gelatinous ooze—is architecture born of biology alone. For miles and miles, in every horizontal direction, the seabed is armored with a dense, intricate thicket of towers, tubes, pinnacles, and bridges, all built by worms. Not the soft, burrowing kind, but serpulid colonies that have, over tens of thousands of years, fused their calcareous secretions into a single, sprawling megastructure. Each tube is the diameter of an ancient tree trunk, some straight as organ pipes, others spiraling or twining together like the ornamentation of a baroque cathedral. They rise six, eight, ten meters from the floor, their openings flared like trumpets or wrinkled like pursed lips, and from them the worms extend feathery, blood-red crowns, combing the current for invisible food. The crowns are retracted at the slightest disturbance, so the city seems to breathe: a forest of trembling, quick-flinching sensors.

The layout is not random. Over so much time, the tubes have accreted in patterns that mimic the logic of a dense, pre-modern settlement. There are broad “avenues” where the tubes grow sparser, their walls encrusted with bivalves that have, in turn, died and become new mineral substrate, paving the route with a mosaic of shell. There are “plazas” where the tubes form a ring, their crowns facing inward like a council of elders, and within these rings the water swirls a little warmer, a little richer in hydrogen sulfide from a diffuse vent below. There are “canals,” actually narrow, meandering trenches carved by persistent bottom currents, flanked by ranks of tubes that lean over them, their hard structures arching and sometimes kissing to form roofs. The whole thing is undercut by a secondary network: the tunnels of larger, soft-bodied creatures—crustaceans, echiurans—that have excavated dwellings in the accumulated silt at the base of the towers, linking them together into a catacomb of burrows.

Vegetation, in the strict sense, is a ghost. No sea grass, no kelp, no green photosynthesizers can survive here. What you might mistake for flora are instead slender, glassy ropes of deep-sea sponge, white and translucent as spun sugar, that drape from the higher tubes like old telephone wire or weeping willow fronds. They filter the water, adding to the clarity near the ground, and occasionally fluoresce in soft blues when disturbed. Their presence is sparse, an accent rather than a covering, marking the boundaries between one “district” and another, or outlining the entrances to especially old burrows.

The fauna gives the city its real life. Schools of blind shrimp, each one a fleck of moving amethyst, pulse through the avenues in clouds so dense they momentarily eclipse the background glow. They are prey, and their predators are everywhere: squat, many-armed octopods with skin the texture of ancient stone that lie motionless in doorways of tube-form, waiting; elongated fish with toothy maws that move like living needles, trailing long sensory whiskers; giant isopods, each the size of a loaf, trundling along the shell-paved lanes like municipal cleaners, their antennae whisking up every corpse and shred. The soundscape is mostly a vast, soft hiss—the collective murmur of water filtering through feathery crowns, the occasional click-and-scrape of carapace on calcium, the plaintive, sub-bass thrum of a distant vent. Sometimes a loud, sharp pop echoes as a worm retracts its crown instantaneously, a tiny sonic boom of life avoiding death.

This is a place of deep-established erosion, where nothing is sharp. Every edge has been softened by the ceaseless, patient lick of the current, by the slow accretion of manganese oxide that paints the oldest tubes a dark, purplish black, by the dissolution and reprecipitation of carbonates that blur the delicate original ridges into knobs and bumps. The landscape is in its own state, weathered and in balance, a climax ecosystem that has been building and crumbling and rebuilding for millennia without changing its essential nature. It is warm because of the vents—not boiling, but a steady, diffuse heat that seeps through the seabed, making the entire region a kind of tropical lagoon in the abyss. The elevation is low, an undersea plain that dips and rises gently, never forming true hills, just a shallow bowl that cups the city and holds the slightly warmer water close.

The weirdness here is not alien; it is only a slight shift from known things. The tube-worm colonies are real, but here they have exceeded any natural scale, forming a true three-dimensional fabric. The density is startling: in some districts, you cannot move a hand’s breadth without touching living calcite armature or the mucous veil of a sponge. The openness comes from the sheer extent: peer down an avenue, and the perspective stretches until the tubes merge into a pixelated blur of phosphorescent dots, hinting at other neighborhoods, other rings, other burrow-catacombs, all extending past the horizon of your perception. It is an urban density writ in biology, an infrastructure of pure, pre-tool life that creates a world within a world, as complex and layered as any human metropolis, but quieter, darker, and entirely innocent of intention.

Above it all, the infinite ocean lid presses down, and the snow falls without end.
