The place has no name because no one has ever spoken here. But if it did, the name would be something like the Archival Deeps, or the Rift, or whatever sound a machine makes when it freezes solid and keeps running anyway.

You are standing—no, you are suspended, buoyed, if buoyancy still means something here—inside a liquid that is not water. It is clear, and it fills everything from the fractured floor to the invisible ceiling, a vertical column of it so tall that pressure changes the way light bends long before you find a surface. The liquid is cold, a fraction of a degree above its own freezing point, and it carries the cold not as a shock but as a steady, patient leakage of heat from any body unlucky enough to enter. This liquid is empty. Not empty of substance, but empty of contents. A cubic kilometer of it holds perhaps a handful of drifting particulates, a few strange ions, and the memory of something dissolved long ago. The rest is volume. Pure, waiting volume.

You are on a ridge. It is one of many, a sharp-edged spine of raw basalt that runs in a mostly straight line for a distance best measured in time zones. The ridge is young in geological terms—freshly fractured, its edges still sharp as broken glass, unsoftened by the gentle chemistry of the fluid around it. This whole formation cracked open only a few thousand years ago, a seam ripped through a cold planetary crust by forces that have not yet settled. The elevation here is high compared to the abyssal plains below, a mountain range inverted, a chain of peaks drowning in slow motion. The ridge crest is perhaps three kilometers from the nearest trench floor, and above you the column of liquid extends for dozens more, fading into a region where the faint illumination from below gives out entirely.

That illumination is industrial. It does not flicker, because nothing here changes. Along the ridge, spaced at intervals that suggest a grid planned by minds with different intuitions about distance, there are structures. Each is a lattice of steel and ceramic, a skeletal rig about the size of a small building, anchored to the basalt with leg-thick bolts. The rigs bristle with pipes, conduits, and the frozen bell-shapes of pumps. They were built to extract something—minerals, heat, chemical gradients—from the fresh rock, but they are silent now. Their engines have not moved in uncounted years. Wires trail from their bases into the void, broken ends drifting slightly in the current that does not exist. These scattered machines are the only architecture for a thousand kilometers in any lateral direction. From a distance, they look like a network of waystations for a metro line that forgot its trains.

And yet there is light. Normal light, the kind a human eye would call white with a hint of blue, emanates from the apex of each rig. Floodlights, powered by something still stable in their cores, throw cones of illumination into the liquid. The light does not travel far—a few hundred meters before the clarity betrays it, scattering into a diffuse glow that outlines the topography in soft gradients. The beams overlap in places, creating corridors of visibility along the ridge. Beyond them, the plains are the dark of a light year.

The sound is the first thing that makes you understand the scale. The rigs are not silent after all. Each one emits a tone, a low and constant note around thirty hertz, just at the edge of what you can feel in your ribs. The tone is a product of their cooling systems, a standing wave in their metal frames that couples to the liquid and propagates outward with terrifying efficiency. The rigs are tuned. All of them. Hundreds of them along the ridge, spaced precisely so that their notes overlap and reinforce, and the liquid, dense and homogenous, carries the sound without attenuation. The result is a drone that fills the entire volume of the Deeps, a single note sustained and flattened into a vibrating plane of pressure. The sound is so loud that it stops being heard and becomes an environment. It shakes microscopic crystals in the basalt. It sets up harmonic nodes where the pressure doubles and then cancels out a meter away. Swim into one of those nodes and your lungs would simply stop knowing what to do. The drone never changes. The machines do not degrade; the liquid temperature does not fluctuate. The note will be the same a million years from now.

The fauna do not mind the sound. They are built for it.

They are abundant. Slowly, in the lit corridors and at the edges of the dark, you begin to see them. They are not fish. They are not anything Linnaeus would have recognized. They are translucent ribbons, some no wider than a hand, others long as buses, undulating with slow rows of cilia that catch no current—there is none—but instead flex against the standing wave of the drone. They navigate by it, orienting their bodies along the pressure gradients, rising and falling through the fluid column like iron filings aligning to a magnetic field. There are spheres too, clusters of gel around a central node of geometric calcium, and they spin. Constant, patient, centrifugal, using the rotation to sift the rare particles from the liquid. Some fauna have bioluminescence, a cold chemical light that flashes in patterns you cannot decode. They blink in the spaces between the floodlights, constellations of brief living stars.

Things eat things here. A ribbon will drift too close to a sphere and be caught in its micro-eddies, pulled in, dissolved by enzymes that work just above the freezing point. Larger forms—triangle-shaped sheets of fiber, almost architectural—coast above the ridge and descend on clusters of spheres, engulfing them. The food web is slow, minimalist, a closed loop of nutrients that never leave the system. There are no plants. The base of the chain is chemosynthesis: microbes embedded in the basalt walls crack hydrogen sulfide from still-cooling minerals, and the spheres harvest them. The ecosystem has reached an equilibrium that excludes growth. Populations are stable, exact, death and reproduction balanced in a thermodynamic truce.

A few of the fauna have begun to colonize the rigs. Thick mats of biofilm, shimmering faintly, coat the lower struts. A species of ribbon has learned to press itself against the vibrating housings of the pumps, using the machine's frequency as a parasite uses a heartbeat. They do not damage the rigs. They have become part of the unchanging equation.

This is a young world geologically, a realm of sharp edges and recent fractures. Nothing is rounded. The basalt is a mosaic of conchoidal chips. The metal of the rigs is unscratched. The liquid, so perfectly stable, has no capacity to erode. It is a preservative. A cold, clear, sound-filled preservative.

You float, feeling the drone in your teeth, watching the slow spin of a calcium sphere as it processes an invisible stream of heat-eating bacteria. The scale becomes apparent only when you notice that the next rig in the line, whose light you can see as a distinct point, is an hour away at the speed of sound. The ridge continues beyond it, and beyond the next, into a haze of illuminated liquid that eventually fades to black. Somewhere out there, at a distance where even the drone begins to phase-cancel into something like quiet, the ridge ends in a sheer cliff, a two-kilometer drop to an abyssal plain that no light has ever touched. There, things live without eyes, navigating only by the reflected echoes of the endless note.

Nothing here has ever changed. The drone is the clock, and the clock has no hands. This is a place that will wear out the universe by simply continuing. The last thing to fail will be the lights, and by then, the life will have long since stopped needing them.
