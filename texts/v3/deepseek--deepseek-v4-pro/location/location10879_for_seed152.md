Let me tell you about an old cistern that became a forest.

You would miss it from above. Someone long ago — before the city memory ran out — hollowed a chamber into the limestone beneath what is now a tangle of narrow streets. The roof caved in centuries past, and the collapse opened a rough oval mouth to the sky, maybe twenty feet across at its widest. Looking down from the crumbling lip, you see only green: a dense clog of ferns and broad-leaved things that drink the half-light. The city has forgotten this place, or never knew it, and so it has been allowed to become something else entirely.

Climb down.

The old stones are slick with a permanent seep of moisture, the walls weeping a thin mineral film that never quite dries. Even when the city above bakes in afternoon heat, the cistern holds a close, warm humidity — the air thick and still and tasting of wet limestone and the sweet rot of old leaves. The temperature here stays steady, a constant living warmth that rises from the composting floor and the slow respiration of the walls themselves. It is not hot. It is enfolding.

The light comes down grey and diffuse, filtered through the canopy of broad leaves that has sealed the opening like a lid. Below this ceiling, the space opens unexpectedly — the cistern is larger than the collapse suggests, maybe forty feet across, the ancient dome still partly intact, cupping the whole pocket in curved stone. Beams of stronger light find gaps in the vegetation only in late afternoon, and then they move across the ferns like slow searchlights, turning the fronds translucent for an hour before fading.

And the sound. Or rather, the near-absence of it. The city noise above — footsteps, voices, the distant clatter of some cart — reaches down only as a muffled suggestion, so attenuated by depth and stone and leaf that it becomes texture rather than information. What you hear instead is close and quiet: the constant small drip of condensation falling from the domed ceiling onto broad leaves, a sound so steady it becomes a kind of silence. The shifting rustle of fronds settling against each other. And, everywhere, the tiny percussive clicks of insects — beetles navigating the wet litter, some cricket-like thing with a slow, patient rhythm. No birdsong. The birds cannot get down here, or choose not to; the opening is too small, the flight path too vertical. The absence of their voices makes the space feel interior, like a room in the earth.

The ecology here has made its own rules. Without direct sun, without wind, without seasons in the usual sense, the cistern has become a terrarium of its own logic. Giant ferns — their ancestors perhaps spores that drifted down on a single rain — have grown into a layered understory, their fronds arching two, three feet long. Mosses coat every stone surface, thick as felt, holding the damp in their bodies. Something pale and fungal threads through the leaf litter, a white mycelial network visible in patches when you push the litter aside. The plants have adapted to dimness; their greens are deep, almost blue in the shadows, the chlorophyll concentrated to catch every photon that falls.

And there are animals. This is the part that surprises — how much life has found its way down. Tree frogs, small and bright as wet jade, cling to the vertical stone walls during the day, their throats pulsing. At dusk, they begin a slow, spaced-out chorus — not a cacophony but individual calls, each one a single clear note, and long silences between them. Geckos hunt the walls, pale and almost translucent, generations of them adapted to this sunless world. Millipedes curl in the damp crevices. A colony of something — rats, perhaps, or a species that has diverged from rats — has made a long-term home in the deeper darkness where the old cistern floor slopes and a pool of permanent water sits, black and still and warm, fed by the seepage from above. You don't see them often, but you hear them: soft skitterings in the dark, the occasional small splash, and then the ripple spreading in rings that catch the faintest light before disappearing.

The place is not finished forming. The walls are still dissolving, slowly — the constant moisture working the limestone grain by grain, finding the softer veins and hollowing them into small pockets and channels. Water moves through the stone in a capillary crawl, and where it emerges it leaves behind a thin deposit of calcite, so that in some places new formations are growing: tiny rippling curtains of stone, no wider than your hand, fresh and white and fragile as eggshell. The cistern is simultaneously falling apart and building itself anew, a geology in miniature, a process that would be invisible if you didn't stop to look.

Ten people could stand here, at most, and only if they stood close. The space is intimate in the way a chapel is intimate, or a womb. The curved walls hold the warmth of all the slow biological burning happening within — the composting, the respiration, the mycelial digestion. Everything here eats and is eaten, quietly, without urgency. There is no hunger in the cistern, only metabolism. The temperature and the moisture and the steady supply of falling leaves from the world above keep the system balanced, a closed loop that has stabilized into something like a creature.

And at the center of it all, directly under the opening, the light falls on a single feature: a small fig tree, somehow rooted in a crack in the ancient stone floor, its trunk no thicker than your arm, its crown just brushing the lowest ferns of the canopy. It will never fruit in this light. It will never grow taller than this. But it has been here for decades, existing in a state of patient suspension, its leaves broad and dark and perfect, catching the droplets that fall from the world above, sending them down its stem in slow rivulets to the moss at its base.

The cistern is a pocket, a parenthesis, a place the city forgot to fill in. It is damp and warm and breathing. It is not wilderness — it is too shaped by the old human walls for that — but it is no longer human either. It has become something self-governing, a small sovereign state of ferns and frogs and stone that dissolves and reforms.

Come at dusk, if you can find it. The frogs will be beginning their spaced-out call, and the last grey light will be filtering through the leaves, and the air will be full of the smell of wet stone and living things, and you will understand that you are standing inside something that is still becoming itself.
