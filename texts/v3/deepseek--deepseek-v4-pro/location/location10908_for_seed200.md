It is called the Basin of the First Hour, though no one has ever spoken that name aloud within its bounds. You descend into it without noticing a boundary—the air simply thickens, the light curdles, and the ground beneath your feet turns from something ancestral to something unborn.

The light is a dim, bronze-green exhalation, as if the sun is permanently eclipsed by a lid of cloud that never shifts. That haze condenses on your skin before you feel the heat. The temperature is a steady, drenching 33 degrees Celsius, the exact warmth of blood, and the humidity is so absolute that the distinction between air and water loses meaning. You breathe through a wet filter; your lungs grow heavy. Every surface weeps. Water doesn't fall as rain here—it simply materializes, beading on every edge, running in slow sheets down vertical planes, percolating upward through the ground. The ground itself is barely solid: a world of interstices, moss-throated clefts, pools that form and never drain because there is nowhere for them to drain to. This is a low place, a vast sunken bowl—something between a collapsed caldera and a seabed that never learned it was exposed. It stretches for kilometers in every direction, ringed by faint ramparts lost in the mist, an open amphitheater of wet.

The sound hits before the place resolves visually. It is loud—alarmingly, suffocatingly loud—but it is not chaos. It is a single, unchanging chord. A drone that layers croaks and ticks and wet percussive slaps into a steady-state roar. Once you hear it, you realize it has no pulse, no rhythm, no swell or fade. The same individual frog—or thing like a frog—has been striking the same note from the same throat for longer than you have been alive. The same insectile click repeats with clockwork fidelity. The sound is a standing wave, a sonic topography as fixed as the rock beneath the life. If you wait an hour, a day, a year, it will not vary by a decibel. This is the first rule you learn: nothing here changes.

The second rule you learn by touch. The terrain is, paradoxically, raw. Erosion has not happened here. The rock is olivine-black basalt, fractured into fins and blades and needle-towers that would shred uncareful feet, except that every surface is padded in a dense, lush integument of life. Vegetation rates 2.2 on whatever scale you carry: a wet, tangled, deep-pile carpet of ferns, liverworts, mosses so thick they have their own topography, their own succession. But there is no succession. The ferns are unfurling permanently; the mosses are in a state of perfect hydration, not growing, not browning at the tips. This is not a young landscape so much as a landscape caught at the moment of youth, an embryonic geology whose newborn sharpness has been wrapped in a swaddling of organisms that never age.

And the fauna. Overwhelming. Density 2.3—near-touching, layered, high-saturation—is an understatement. Every cubic meter of this place is packed with bodies. Tree frogs, the size of your thumbnail, form a contiguous living skin on the moss. They are not moving. They are breathing, their flanks pulsing in unison, but they do not shift position. Beneath them, a stratum of salamanders with gills like feather-dusters lie in the soupy pools, permanently neotenic, permanently still. Above, on the few vertical rocks that rise above the mat, bats or bat-analogues cling in a fur-mosaic so tight that the stone itself seems to be furred. Their eyes are open, glistening, but they do not blink. Insects—glossy beetles, slow-walking phasmids—stand among the leaves as if taxidermied, yet their antennae twitch in a rhythm synchronous with the drone. Nothing preys on anything else. Nothing mates. Nothing dies. The ecological pyramid has become a flat plane, a living photograph in which the living part has been left on, idling, forever.

The weirdness, a 0.9, rests in the why. There is a scent, slightly metallic, that hints at some emanation from the rock—a gas, a mineral exudate—that arrested time for biology the moment the first spore landed. Or perhaps the basin is simply too new. The erosion score of 0.1, "emerging," means the cracks in the basalt are fresh, the glassy surfaces unetched by chemistry, the very minerals in a pre-weathering state. This ground was birthed hot from the earth and then, instantly, ambushed by a lush, prolific life that somehow never began the slow process of wearing it down. No root pries open the joints; no acidic bog-water dissolves the feldspar. The life and the stone uneasily coexist without interacting, two timelines that touched and then jammed.

There are no built structures (0.0), not so much as a bent twig that might be a marker. No tools (0.4), unless you count the way a particular beetle's carapace mirrors the exact curve of a water-drop, a perfect natural technology that no one uses. The scale is open—you can walk for an hour, a day, and the basin remains the same: the same drone, the same density of motionless frogs, the same dim light that never becomes day or night. The hot, wet darkness is perpetual; maybe the sun swings far to the north, or maybe the mist itself generates a faint bio-luminescence from the omnipresent spores, enough to see by but not enough to cast shadows.

At the center of the basin, if center means anything in a place with no gradients, there is a weeping rock taller than the rest, finned like a gothic cathedral made of raw obsidian. From every pore of it, water oozes—not flowing, but simply appearing as globules that hold their shape for a century before deciding to merge with the saturated air. The water is pure, tasteless, sterile. It does not carve. The rock beneath is the same tomorrow as it was at the moment the first heat of creation left it. And the frogs on it, and the bats above, and the ferns within the cracks, are still at the same moment. The first hour of a world's existence, extended to infinity.

If you stay long enough, you might notice your own breathing start to match the drone, your own blood adjust to the ambient heat. The place is a perfect equilibrium. It asks nothing of you and offers nothing. It is the sound of a trumpet that never decayed, a chord struck at genesis and held.

You climb back out through the humid seam between the basin and the world, and the sound breaks, and air dries, and time restarts its engines. Behind you, the First Hour continues, uneroded, unaltered, a lush and overwhelming and forever-loud never-was.
