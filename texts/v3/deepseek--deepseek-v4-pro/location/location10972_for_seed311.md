You descend.

The pressure doesn’t crush you—it’s been calibrated, repurposed, woven into the place’s own logic. Here, the deep is less a wilderness and more a crowded room you’ve been expected in. The first thing you notice is the sound: a constant, layered thrum that isn’t simply noise but architecture. It’s the growl of pumps cycling hypersaline coolant through capillary mazes, the distant *whump-whump* of a pressure-exchange lung that breathes for the entire crevice, the hiss of micro-jets keeping every surface scoured clean of settlement. No silence. No empty space. The sound is the shape of the water here—and the water is everywhere.

This pocket is sunk into a rift you’d miss if you weren’t its intended occupant. The walls are raw basalt, sharp-edged, glassy in places, the cuts still fresh enough that light skitters off cleavage planes without a single rounding of time. It’s a geology of yesterday, or maybe this morning—the whole hollow might have been chewed out by something that left no tool-marks, only sheer, planar facets, as if a cube of reality had been deleted and the sea had rushed in after. That radical newness is the first strangeness: no sediment, no pelagic snow, no slime. Just hard, unweathered rock and, crammed into every cubic metre of it, the city.

You hesitate to call it a city—it’s too intimate, too scaled to the reach of an outstretched arm. A pocket-scale urban saturation. The structures don’t sit *on* the rift floor; they are threaded, stacked, cantilevered, fused into a three-dimensional lattice so dense that “between” has lost its meaning. Habitation units the size of closets, data-blister modules, spires of flickering logic that extend needle-fine into the fluid—all of them a matte, non-reflective grey that drinks the light, except where seams of active circuitry pulse a faint, warm amber. There’s no zoning, no gradual suburbs-to-centre logic. It’s all centre. It’s all infrastructure. A fully-occupied volume where every surface performs work.

The tech is beyond you, not because it glows or hums with incomprehensible force, but because it violates the physics of *stuff*. You watch a conduit bridge two platforms—it’s a loop of what looks like mercury, flowing in a möbius strip that never touches itself, tracing a path through air that water cannot enter. A pocket of dry gas is somehow held open inside this submerged tangle, a lung-within-a-lung. Elsewhere, a cluster of hexagonal cells rotates slowly, each facet a display showing a temperature differential, a pressure gradient, a flow rate—but the cells share no axes, no bearings; they spin in a mutual orbit that shouldn’t be stable. You sense that “materials science” has moved somewhere past the periodic table, into a domain where matter is persuaded rather than assembled. The sharp edges of the rift walls are mirrored by the crisp geometries of the built stuff—both share that unnerving newness, as if the whole ensemble had been printed into being only hours ago.

Water is not just the medium; it’s an active character. The parameter reads submerged but not simply flooded—this is a dynamic submersion. Rivers flow along paths that ignore gravity: a spiralling column of dense, cold brine coils upward around a thermal exchange pillar, so salty it makes the surrounding seawater look like a separate phase. In other corridors, the water is aerated to a milky fizz, its sound a constant churning; elsewhere, it’s electro-purified to a clarity that makes distance impossible to gauge, so you reach for a handhold only to find it a metre beyond your fingertips. The whole pocket is threaded with weather—microcyclones of temperature and salinity, little fronts that form and dissolve. All of it is loud. The hiss of phase-change, the roar of laminar jets keeping apertures clear, the basso thrum of a thousand hidden mechanisms.

Vegetation: sparse. Nearly none. But there’s something organic in intent if not in origin. In a few recessed niches, you spot sheets of a deep green biofilm, cultivated in exact rectangles, glowing faintly under narrow-spectrum LEDs. It’s not plant, quite—more a photosynthetic gel, perspiring oxygen bubbles that pearl up and are whisked away by the current. A crop of something. That’s the 0.7. The 0.1 of fauna: no motion but the water’s own. No fish, no crustaceans, not even a micro-shrimp in the water column. The absence is intentional. The filters that hum at every intake are pore-sized to exclude everything larger than a molecule. This place is animal-free by design.

Yet it’s dynamic. The multiple processes are the life here. The ecosystem is one of pressures and voltages, of thermal loops switching between regimes, of data-packets moving through the fluid medium as modulated ultrasound that you can feel in your teeth if you drift too close to a relay node. If there is a predator-prey cycle, it’s in the symbiotic tension between the heat-rejection network and the cold extraction sinks—a constant, oscillating negotiation that sends shudders through the lattice.

The light is normal: a steady, full-spectrum white that radiates not from points but from the surfaces themselves, as if the walls and pipes and struts are faintly luminescent. It’s not dramatic; it’s the administrative light of a place that operates round-the-clock, a perpetual noon that flattens shadow and reveals every packed detail. There is no sky, but the upper boundary of the pocket—if you can call it that—is a crisscross of structural members so dense it forms a ceiling, beyond which the larger ocean presses. You are inside a hidden chamber, a single-contained feature within the larger blankness of the midwater. The scale is intimate-room yet impossibly rich: you could spend a lifetime here and never map every conduit, every diagnostics rune etched into a valve housing.

Coolness pervades. Temperature 1.2—a persistent, clammy chill that steals body heat but never rises to a shiver. It’s the cool of a server room, of deep water, of surfaces that are actively maintained a few degrees above freezing to optimize thermal gradients. You notice frost forming on a junction where cold brine leaks a little too fast from its pipe, a white fuzz that then gets sliced off by a maintenance jet as if the place were embarrassed by its own imperfection.

And it is urban—1.5, a settlement-scale. There is a logic of streets, but they are vertical, diagonal, mapped in an octagonal grid that you have to learn to swim through. Junctions are signaled by subtle shifts in the thrum. A cluster of modules that might be dwellings, though too small for a human body, suggests a population of presences you don’t see: maybe the inhabitants are embedded, downloaded, or are themselves the pulsing patterns in the data-spires. The place is packed with occupation, but not organic bodies. The density of *something* is absolute.

Hidden, intimate, packed—this is a world that feels like a secret organ of some larger entity. Staring into one of the rotating hexagonal cells, you catch a reflection of the whole rift: a blueprint of itself, folded into infinite regress. The loudness, the sharpness, the liquid logic—it doesn’t welcome you. It doesn’t reject you either. It is simply too occupied, too fully in motion, to notice. You are a soft anomaly adrift in a sharp, cold, and unendingly active machine. And the water, the ever-present water, carries on cycling through its micro-seasons, keeping every edge new.
