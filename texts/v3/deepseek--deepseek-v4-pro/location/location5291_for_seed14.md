It begins not with a horizon, but with a pressure.

Here, the world is a single, continuous substance, a medium that is neither truly liquid nor fully mineral. It is a slurry, a suspension of fine glacial silt and dissolved ancient stone so thick that the word “water” fails. It has the temperature of melting ice, just above freezing, a cold that doesn’t bite but seeps, a thermal inertia that has not flickered in millennia. Light is a memory the molecules have forgotten. This is the Benthic Expanse, a landscape drowned in a darkness so absolute it feels less like an absence and more like a velvet weight pressed against the eyes.

The terrain is a paradox: utterly flat and unimaginably chaotic. This is the result of an erosion so ancient it has become a final, stable state. For eons, gentle, inexorable currents have planed the underlying basalt into a plain of absolute geometric precision, a level of flatness that human engineers could only dream of. This is the “oldest-stable-state,” a place that has been worn down to its very essence and can be sculpted no further. Yet across this perfect floor, the true landscape is in constant, turbulent creation. It is built not of rock, but of density.

The density is the key. It is a 2.9, a packed, saturated fullness. You do not *look* at this landscape; you *feel* it as a panorama of differential pressures. Every cubic metre is a bazaar of chemical ghosts and mineral whispers. The primary structures are columns, but not static ones. They are thermohaline plumes, chaotic, chimney-like draperies of super-saline brine, weeping from the seafloor. Heavier than the already-heavy surrounding cold, they cascade downwards in slow-motion waterfalls, creating inverted rivers of sinking salt that meander across the plain. To drift into one is to fall, suddenly, through a liquid floor.

The “sparse vegetation” and “rare fauna” are here, but they operate on a geological timescale and a chemical logic. There are no plants. Instead, the sparse life is lithoautotrophic. Vast, flat mats of chemosynthetic archaea, a single cell thick, plaster the silt. They are not green but a spectral, non-colour born of iron and sulfur metabolism—a sheen of tarnished silver that briefly ignites into a dull, phosphorescent rust-orange at the edges where two competing mats meet in a slow-motion chemical war. This is the only light, a fleeting bioluminescence like heat lightning in a sky made of stone.

Fauna is rare and primitive, operating at the level of hand-tool technology on a microscopic scale. Imagine a foraminiferan, a single-celled giant the size of a lentil, its shell not secreted but constructed. It moves on pseudopodal treads, collecting grains of the rarest minerals—micro-meteoritic olivine that has drifted down over millennia from the surface world. Using a primitive, biologically-secreted cement, it brick-lays these grains into a flawless, multi-chambered spiral shell, a tiny, hand-crafted cathedral moving through the dark. These are the architects of this world, their built structures (a 0.2, but not a zero) the only exception, a primitive tech of the organic.

The turbulent dynamic is a constant, chaotic rearrangement of these presences. A micro-tremor, the last dying echo of a continental birth pang a hemisphere away, will cause the silt plain to liquefy. In an instant, the perfect flatness dissolves into a turbulent cloud of suspended sediment, a blinding (if there were any light) maelstrom of particles that repaints the terrain, burying the slow silver wars of the archaea and sending the shell-builders tumbling. This is the Solaris-like creation/destruction cycle: a landscape not of forms, but of events, a place that is constantly being erased and redrawn by the very substances that comprise it. Silence returns not as peace, but as a held breath before the next inevitable, whisper-quiet cataclysm. The only sound is a sub-sonic, infrasonic groan, the sound of a planet cooling, transmitted through water and bone.
