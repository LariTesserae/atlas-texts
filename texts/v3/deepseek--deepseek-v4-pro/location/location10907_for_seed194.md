The place reveals itself only in fragments, received against the palms and the soles of the feet and the fine hairs of the inner arm—not through the eyes, for there is no light here, none, not the faintest photon from fungus or mineral fluorescence, not the distant memory of a star. The darkness is absolute and has been absolute for longer than the thing that built this place has had a name.

You are inside a hollow. The hollow is modest—a room, a chamber, a pocket carved from the world by hands or by time or by the patient negotiation between the two. The floor is level but not smooth; it yields slightly underfoot with a texture that is neither soil nor stone but something fibrous, densely woven, a mat of compressed filaments that have the dry springiness of ancient felt. This is the floor, and the walls, and the ceiling. They are all the same material, continuous, curving into one another without seam or corner. The space is intimate, perhaps three body-lengths across, its ceiling low enough that a raised hand meets it with a brush of fingertips.

The material. You come to know it through touch. It is plant, or was, or is in some final state that the word "plant" only approximates. The walls are composed of stems—thousands, millions of them, each no thicker than a cord, running parallel and pressed together with the tightness of geological strata. They are smooth under the finger, cool and faintly waxy, and they give off no heat of their own. When you press, the wall compresses slightly and then resists, firm but not hard, more like dense cork than timber. The stems run horizontally along the floor and vertically up the curved walls, merging into the ceiling in a radial convergence, a slow vegetal architecture that has forgotten it was ever alive.

This is all of it. There is no water here. The air is dry, so dry it draws moisture from your lip if you breathe through your mouth. It tastes empty, faintly of dust and tannin and something metallic, something like old copper left in a sealed room. No damp, no seep, no drip. The dryness is ancient and structural, baked into the fibers over spans of time that have erased the memory of rain.

The temperature is cool, not cold, not the biting chill of a cave or a root cellar, but a steady coolness that sits just below the comfort of skin, the kind that makes the body aware of its own edges. There is no wind, no draft, no movement of air at all. The stillness is complete.

And yet it is loud.

The sound is the first thing that disorients. It comes from the walls, from the floor, from the ceiling—a low, continuous, multi-voiced groaning that never stops. It is the sound of thousands of fibers shifting against each other in imperceptible motion, a slow creak of cellulose settling through gradients of tension established before memory. The sound is rhythmic but arrhythmic, like the breathing of a creature that inhales over decades and exhales over centuries. Sometimes a sharper crack, muffled but distinct, punctuates the drone—a single stem releasing some accumulated strain, breaking not in failure but in adjustment. The sound fills the chamber. It is loud enough that you must raise your voice to be heard, if there were anyone to hear. It is the voice of the place itself.

This chamber, this hollow room, is not natural. It was made. The walls are too regular, the curve too deliberate, the floor too level. Someone shaped this space, or something. The stems around the entrance—a low arch, now sealed with a plug of the same fibrous material—show cut ends, not torn or chewed but sliced cleanly and then worn smooth by the same vast stretch of time that has softened every edge. There are other signs. Along one wall, set into the stems themselves, a series of niches have been hollowed out, each the size of a cupped hand. They are empty now, but their interior surfaces are burnished darker than the surrounding fiber, as if from the repeated friction of objects placed and removed over generations. A ledge, a simple horizontal protrusion of bundled stems, runs along the curve opposite the arch. It is too low to be a bench, too wide to be a shelf; perhaps it was an altar, a bed, a workspace. Its surface is grooved with long parallel depressions, the wear of tools or bodies.

The people who built this—and they were people, not spirits, not gods, people with hands and needs—they worked with what the world gave them. The world gave them stems. The fibrous stems are the substrate of everything, the sole material of construction, of craft, of life. There is no stone here, no metal, no clay. The tech is primitive, but sophisticated within its narrow parameters: stems cut with stem-blades hardened in dry heat, stem-fibers twisted into cordage, stem-pulp chewed into paste and dried into vessels, stem-bundles bent and bound into every shape a settlement requires. The niche-carvers and ledge-builders lived here, in this room, in this settlement—for outside this chamber there are others, a honeycomb of rooms, a town of hollows carved into the endless fiber mass. The arch leads to a corridor, and the corridor branches, and each branch ends in another chamber like this one, some larger, some smaller, all of them shaped from the same material by the same patient hands. The settlement is silent now. The people are gone, and have been gone so long that the stems have begun to close the hollows, creeping inward by microns each year, the walls swelling slowly back toward the center, reclaiming the space.

And outside the settlement? Outside is more of the same. The fibrous mass extends in all directions. No sky. No surface. The vegetation is total, is the world. The stems are the geology, the geography, the ecosystem entire. There is no soil beneath them, no bedrock, only stems compressed into stems, the oldest layers so dense and fused that they behave like stone, while the newer growth—newer by millennia—remains springy and distinct. The plant-as-substrate is the planet, or the continent, or the land, and it has been this way for so long that the concept of "ground" as anything else is meaningless.

The emptiness of the place is a quality of the spaces between things. Between stem and stem, between the fibers that twist together, there are gaps—microscopic, air-filled, dry. Multiply these gaps by the trillions and you have a world that is mostly void, mostly hollow, a vast foam of cellulose and air. The density is so low that the whole structure seems almost to float in its own emptiness, a sponge the size of a mountain range. A footstep compresses a hundred thousand air pockets. The walls breathe out ancient atmosphere when you press against them.

It is a world without animals. The fibers hold no burrowers, no insects, no worms. The niches are empty of food-stores, the ledge bears no bones, no feathers, no fur. Whatever the people ate—and they ate something—it was not flesh. Perhaps they ate the stems, the pith, the inner layers softened by chewing or fermentation. Perhaps they grew something in the niches, a pale fungus that fed on the fibers and provided in return. Perhaps the ecosystem was pure plant, plant eating plant, plant digesting plant, the slow circulation of cellulose and lignin through generations of hands and mouths and back into the walls.

The weirdness of the place is subtle but insistent. It is the weirdness of total darkness and total sound, the disjunction between the low cool stillness and the groaning voice of the walls. It is the weirdness of a world made of a single thing, a world that has achieved its final form and settled into it, accepting it, continuing it without change. The erosion is ancient—not the erosion of water or wind, but the erosion of time itself, the slow grinding of fibers against fibers, the rounding of every edge, the darkening of every surface, the wear of hands repeated over so many generations that the niche walls are polished like old wood. This place is old not in the way a mountain is old, but in the way a house is old, a house that has been lived in so long that every threshold is worn into a curve, every doorframe smoothed by shoulders brushing past. It has been shaped, and that shaping has itself been shaped by the passage of bodies through it, and that passage has continued for so long that the shaping and the wearing are indistinguishable.

The low elevation is the deepness of the chamber within the greater mass. There is no sun, no sky, no external reference, but there is a sense of being below, of being embedded. Above, if one could travel upward through the stems, the density would lessen, the fibers would grow looser and more springy, the air might move, and eventually—after what distance?—there might be a surface, a boundary, an outside where light exists. But that is theoretical. The people who lived here never went there, or if they did, they did not return, or they returned and did not speak of it, or they spoke and their speaking was lost in the groan of the walls.

The slow dynamic of the place is the breathing. The stems are still alive, in some minimal patient sense. They shift. They creep. They adjust. Over centuries, a chamber narrows by the width of a finger. Over millennia, a corridor pinches shut. The settlement is being digested back into the world-body, as slowly as continents drift. The loudness of the walls is the sound of this digestion, always present, so constant that the people who lived here must have stopped hearing it, the way surface-dwellers stop hearing wind. But it shaped them. It entered their speech, their rhythms, their dreams. They built in cycles, cutting new hollows as old ones closed, migrating through the fiber-mass in a slow generational current, a town that was never in the same place twice, a city that flowed through the dark like a bubble moving through slow amber.

And yet here is this chamber, still open, still shaped, still bearing the marks of hands. Perhaps the people are not gone. Perhaps they have simply moved on, deeper or higher or sideways, and will return in some far cycle when the stems have regrown and are ready to be cut again. Perhaps they are somewhere in the dark even now, hollowing new rooms, their voices lost under the groan of the fibers. Or perhaps the chamber was abandoned in a time of crisis—a shift in the slow metabolism of the world, a pulse of heat or coolness or dryness that drove them out. The niches are empty, but not swept. A faint dust of desiccated fiber-sap coats the ledge, undisturbed. Whatever happened, it ended quietly, without violence, without disruption to the slow pace of the world.

You stand in the center of the chamber, in the absolute darkness, with the sound of a billion stems singing their slow song around you. The air tastes of tannin and age. The floor holds your weight with the slight give of a living thing that is not quite alive but not quite dead. The space is small, hidden, intimate, a pocket carved by hands in a world made entirely of plant, entirely of fiber, entirely of one continuous material that has been shaped and re-shaped across spans of time that render the concept of "ancient" inadequate. There is no water, no fire, no light, no animals, no metal, no stone. There is only the dark, the cool, the dry, the loud slow voice of the walls, and the patient emptiness that waits for hands that have not yet returned.
