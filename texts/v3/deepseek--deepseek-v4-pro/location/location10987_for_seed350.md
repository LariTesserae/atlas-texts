The descent is less a drop through depth than a slide across a boundary no instrument marks. Above, the fluid was something like water—clear, saline, moving in predictable flows. Here, it thickens, becoming warm and faintly adhesive against the hull, carrying a fine suspended glitter that turns the beams to bronze. The light is a perpetual dusk, a dim translucence stirred by wide, slow convection. At first, the quiet is absolute, the muffled hush of a cocoon. Then the sound arrives, not as something heard but as something entered: a full-body thrum that rattles teeth and shivers through the thickest plating. It is a place of deafening noise, a constant, layered roar like a thousand engine rooms run into one, punctuated by deep sub-sonic booms that churn the fluid in visible rings.

The terrain below is young, raw as a fresh wound. Sharp-featured, it thrusts upward in splintered ridges and knife-edged fins of what might be basalt, but which gleam with a wet, oily iridescence. There is no erosion worth the name—no rounded edges, no settled sediment, as if the whole floor was molten only decades ago and flash-frozen in place. The elevation is low, a vast abyssal plain dotted with these abrupt, jagged upthrusts, their tips just touching a warmer, higher stratum. Scale is open, a regional sweep that stretches beyond sensor range: valleys, hillocks, chasms all crisp and new, a geology in violent infancy.

Yet this harsh mineral landscape is choked with life of a kind. Lush is too tame a word; it is dense beyond anything surface-born. Every spine, every crevice, every vertical face is swaddled in growth. It is not plant, not animal, but something fluid in category. Great ribbony fronds like kelp—but kelp that glows faintly from within, its veins pulsing with a slow, industrial luminescence—rise in tangled forests. Their blades are thick and rubbery, warm to the touch as living flesh, and they sway in the currents with a muscular, deliberate motion that suggests volition. Between them, a low, carpet-like nap spreads, a moss of cobbled metallic plates that itch with static discharge. Bubbles the size of rooms wobble upward from buried orifices, their surfaces iridescent with oils, and when they burst, they release a sigh like a steel door opening. The density is such that the growths overlap, compete, merge, forming a multi-layered rind that quivers ceaselessly. There is no animal life—no fish, no crustaceans, no jellies. The only movements are the plants and the machines.

For machines there are. Scattered across this welter, half-sunk into the razor rocks and strangled by the vegetation, lie the remains of an industrial age. They are not ruins; many still function. A row of massive turbines, their blades spinning slow but steady, generates a low-frequency grinding that sets the fluid to standing waves. Pipework, some as wide as tunnels, coils across the plain, bleeding streams of warmer, chemically-rich liquid. Gauges and panels, barnacled with that crawling metallic moss, still blink with idiot sequences of colored light. There is a deep, ongoing purpose here, a scaled production that no longer has a maker, only the ongoing churn of production itself. The built mark is small against the landscape—a single pump station here, a half-collapsed refinery there, a lattice of dangling cables—but its presence pervades, a ghost in the machine-vegetal weave.

The temperature is warm, a blood-heat that intensifies near the vents. Oddly, it sometimes spikes without source, or patches of cold bloom like bruises in the fluid. This is the first hint of reality's frayed edge. Movement is turbulent in the extreme; currents shift direction in an instant, forming gyres that spin off into vortex streets, then collapse. The fluid itself has no stable phase—here liquid, there a gel, further off a fog of suspended droplets where the “water” becomes aerial, a wall of rain moving up and down. Gravity tilts. Once, a whole section of kelp-forest rotated ninety degrees relative to everything else, its fronds waving cheerfully sideways to the current. Down deep, a chasm opens not into seafloor but into a colorless, roaring elsewhere—a seam of pure noise that has visual form, a tear in the water through which light leaks without source. The weirdness is constant, a low-grade reality breakdown, as if the rules of this world are being drafted and discarded moment by moment.

The vegetation is not immune. It grows into the wounds, knitting them with frantic, fibrous tissue, or it recoils, forming barricades of interlocked stalks. In one shallow valley, the entire mass of flora pulses in synchronous seizures, responding to a rhythm that has no source. The sound, always deafening, here becomes a pressure that can buckle metal, a physical assault of overlapping harmonics—the metallic scream of shearing rock, the thump of huge pistons, the wet roar of fluid cavitating and collapsing, all layered with a high, thin whistle that never quite stops, as if the planet itself were breathing through broken bellows.

And so the landscape exists in a state of perpetual, turbulent creation and destruction: a sea-floor factory-garden where the water is not water, the plants are not plants, and the line between geology, biology, and engine is lost in the dim, warm, roaring dark. It is a place that makes no concessions to the stable, a cradle of constant becoming where the only certainty is that nothing will hold its shape for long. The last thing you notice, before the noise and the heat and the tremble of dissolving physics erase everything, is the smell—a sharp, clean tang of ozone and hot copper, filtering in through a hull that suddenly feels paper-thin. And then the current takes you, and the landscape rearranges itself once more.
