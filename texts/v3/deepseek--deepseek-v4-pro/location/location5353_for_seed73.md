The sky is a heavy, bruised-gray lid sealed over the world, leaking a perpetual fine drizzle that never quite becomes rain. You are standing at the edge of a vertical landscape, where the ground falls away in terraces of black and green—a plateau ripped open by time, dropping thousands of meters into cloud-filled gorges whose bottoms are only rumor. This is the Throat of the Mender, a region named in fragments of a language no one speaks anymore, and it is in the slow, patient throes of being reclaimed by its own strange life.

**Terrain and water:** The rock is ancient basalt, columnar in places, fractured into hexagonal pillars that jut like the pipes of a drowned organ. Water is the landscape’s constant motion. It weeps from every seam, sliding in oily sheets down the rock faces, fattening into rivulets that braid through cushions of moss. The air itself is a saturated sponge; you inhale spore-laden moisture and taste metal. In the shallow basins that pocket the plateau, water lies still, skin-warm, filmed with iridescent slicks of bacteria. Every edge drips. The rocks are never dry—their surfaces are black with a patina of wetness, slick underfoot, giving the whole elevated world a lacquered sheen. This is no arid peak: it's a hot, seeping highland, a marsh suspended in the sky.

**Vegetation:** Lushness here isn’t pastoral; it’s aggressive, layered to the point of suffocation. Every pillar and ledge is a tiered garden. Lowest stratum: a liverwort carpet so dense it feels like walking on tongues. Above that, ferns with fronds the size of canoe paddles, translucent and water-beaded. Shrubs with waxy red leaves grow gnarled and horizontal, twisted by the slow push of wind. Then the trees—they are not like trees from the lower world. Their trunks are masses of aerial roots, burrowing into their own crumbling bark, forming cages of living wood. Canopies interlock in a roof of dark, dripping green, and from every branch hangs a curtain of epiphytes: orchids with blooms that exhale visible vapor, bromeliads holding miniature ponds in their leaf-bases, and a beard-like lichen that shimmers with a faint, algorithmic glow when touched. The air is thick with the smell of rot and nectar. This vegetation is not just alive; it is *crowded*, each niche packed, stems and leaves nudging one another in a slow-motion struggle for light that never resolves.

**Temperature and elevation:** The heat is oppressive and wet, a greenhouse heat that shouldn’t exist at this altitude. You sense the elevation in the thinness of the oxygen—your breath comes shallow—but the warmth is pervasive. It rises from below, perhaps from geothermal vents hidden in the gorges, or from the dark, metabolizing earth itself. Vapor curls from fissures, and the rock is warm to the touch. This is an extreme elevation, a place where the mountaintop should be tundra but is instead a tropical hothouse, its vertical walls creating their own microclimate, a chimney that traps heat and moisture and life.

**Erosion and scale:** The landscape has the settled appearance of something that finished its dramatic changes eons ago. The basalt columns are rounded, soft-edged, etched by centuries of water and root. No fresh rockfalls here; the talus slopes are blanketed in soil and furry with growth. What’s being carved now is subtle: a pothole deepening by a grain each year, a crack widening at the pace of lichen. Across the open scale—you can see for kilometers along this plateau rim, across a basin of similar spires and gorges that recede into misty blue—the forms repeat, a fractal of plunge-pools and overhangs. It’s a regional pattern, not a single feature, and it breathes with a slow, unified rhythm.

**Density and fauna:** Density isn’t just in the vegetation. The air is a soup of winged things. Insects drone in multi-layered chords: the thrum of beetle wings, the whine of mosquitoes in shifting columns, the clicking choruses of something like cicadas but with a metallic timbre. Birds—or things bird-shaped—flit and dive. They are abundant to the point of distraction. Some have too many wings; some have scales instead of feathers, iridescent with that same faint luminescence. Higher in the canopy, slender, silk-furred quadrupeds with elongated fingers move slowly, browsing on the lichen that glows. Their calls are low-frequency rumbles, felt as much as heard. Even the rock pools teem: thread-thin eels coil in the warm water, and when they surface, their skin refracts light in impossible angles, as if coated in a film of tiny prisms.

**Built and tech:** The signs of former residents are scattered and subtle—until you learn to see them. A slab of stone might have a seam too straight, a surface too smooth, which when you press your palm against it thrums with an undetectable current. Halfway down a cliff, a delicate suspension of what looks like carbon-filament cables still holds up a platform that now serves as a nesting site for a colony of wheeling creatures. The tech is beyond modern: it embeds itself in the natural, mimicking it, or perhaps the natural has learned to grow over and through it. You find a staircase carved into a waterfall—each step a perfect cantilever that seems to repel moss—leading to a doorway that is just a shimmer in the air, a lens of bent light reflecting a world slightly more orderly than this one. The scattered remains are less ruins and more like dormant organs. Some structures hum, a subsonic note that tugs at your teeth. Others weep a clear, viscous fluid that hardens into crystalline spheres—self-repairing, slowly, over decades. Nothing here looks fully operational, but nothing is fully dead either. The technology is a patient presence, a faint blueprint still asserting itself.

**Light and weirdness:** The light is normal in hue—a diffuse, overcast daylight, the kind that bleaches color from some things and makes others more vivid—but what it falls on is strange. The strangeness is in the edges. At night (which comes slowly here, a long twilight), the landscape does not go dark: the lichen glows with a faint script-like pattern, repeating the same sequence of dots and dashes across every colony. The bioluminescence ripples in waves when the wind blows, as if stirred by a signal. Some of the remnants pulse in response, brief shafts of coherent light flickering on and off in a pattern too complex for human minds to trace. The fauna exhibits behaviors that hint at embedded intelligence: a bird will land on a specific mossy knob, and the knob will rotate, presenting a different side. They are part of a system, a slow-turning machine of ecology and leftover design.

**Sound and dynamic:** It is loud, in a steady-state way. The drip and trickle of water creates a fluid base drone. The insect chorus never stops—it merely changes register, from the mid-day hum to a deeper, rattling evening rhythm. Wind moans through the columnar gaps, producing tones like a badly-tuned pipe organ. And beneath it all, the thrum of the ancient tech: a constant, barely audible sub-bass, like the heartbeat of the plateau itself. Yet all this sound is part of a dynamic that is glacial. The water paths have been the same for millennia. The trees die standing, held upright by a cage of their own roots, decaying over centuries into veined spires that then host new saplings. The tech repairs itself at a pace almost imperceptible: a cracked dome will seal with a smooth new skin, but it takes a human lifetime. This is not a place of sudden storms or flash floods; it is a place of ongoingness, steeped in itself, where the loudness is just the breathing of a very old, very slow entity.

To walk here is to feel you’ve entered a dreaming machine—a high, hot, wet world so densely packed with life and abandoned wonders that it hums with a sense of purpose you can’t quite decipher. Every stone is etched, every pool alive, every remnant a quiet testament to something beyond the current physics. The landscape doesn’t change; it merely persists, in a state of loud, lush, saturated equilibrium.
