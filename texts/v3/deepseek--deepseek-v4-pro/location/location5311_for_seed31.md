The plain lifts itself into the clouds and stops, a slab of black basalt bitten from the earth’s cooling skin and turned upward, so fresh the edges still carry the glint of congealed fire. This is a high land, a plateau shoved into the sky before the rains found it, and the rains have never left. Warmth rises from somewhere—the rock’s own memory of formation, or the slow vent of unseen depths—and meets the thin, cool air of altitude, wringing moisture from every breath. It is always wet here, but there is no storm, no driving sheets; instead a patient, universal exhalation of mist that clings to the stone, beads on every vertical face, gathers into rivulets that trace the cleavage lines like tears along newly opened cracks. The rock weeps endlessly, and the sound is a quiet language of drips and trickles, a susurrus so constant it becomes silence.

The terrain is a forest of prismatic shapes—dykes, spines, shattered plates—the raw forms of a land that has not yet learned to slump or soften. No erosion rounds these edges. The water flows, but it does not cut; perhaps the basalt is too hard, too newly born, or the water carries dissolved stone back out of solution and coats each channel in a thin skin of travertine, filling the incipient scars. The slopes are sharp, the fissures narrow and exact, the basins cradles of unrippled water. It is a young topography, as if the earth here has just finished its act of creation and paused, holding its breath.

Scattered across this fresh canvas, vegetation erupts in isolated, lush profusions. Where a seam widens or a slab tilts to hold a pocket of mineral soil, something emerald and extravagant spills out—a congregation of broad-leaved ferns, their fronds furred with droplets; cushions of moss thick enough to sink a hand into, their surfaces a constellation of tiny wet stars; and, here and there, stunted trees whose roots wrap the rock like fingers, their canopies flattened into dark platforms against the pale mist. But between these islands, the basalt runs bare and wet, a slick blackness reflecting the diffuse light. The density is sparse: each pocket of green has its own breathing room, separated by expanses of shining stone that give the eye a long, uninterrupted reach across the plateau.

Within this openness, a human geometry emerges, ancient and absorbed. They built with the rock, prying it loose and stacking it in low, massive walls, shaping dwellings and causeways from the same black basalt, using fire and patient percussion. No metal holds these stones together; they are fitted by weight and precision, or sealed with a paste of mineral silt. The settlement—no one here would call it a city, though by the scale of organized construction it is one—spreads in a loose web across a slightly raised bench, a terraced cluster of flat-roofed structures, some still occupied by descending wisps of steam from thermal vents tapped below. There are channels, painstakingly hand-chiseled, that run between the houses, coaxing the constant flow into cisterns and baths, a quiet plumbing that never fails because the water never ceases. The build is primitive, but the labor behind it is monumental, a stone-age urbanism that has endured in this changeless wet. The streets are empty zones of reflective rock; the roofs are shallow basins capturing the sky’s exhalation. And nothing moves that is not water or human.

For there are no animals. Not a skittering insect across the water’s skin, not a bird’s silhouette against the fog, not the rasp of a frog from the moss. The silence is absolute except for the water’s voice. The air is still, no gradient of temperature draws a breeze, and the mist drifts only with the slowest, almost imperceptible migration, as though the world’s clock has been stilled. It is a landscape in equilibrium, where time has become a property of the rock, not a process. The sharp ridges will not round, the channels will not deepen, the ferns will not spread beyond their chosen crevices, and the stone city will not crumble. It is a place that was made, and then completed, and now simply persists—a wet, breathing terrarium sealed at altitude, warm and quiet and eternally new.

From the edge of the settlement, the view pushes out across a valley-sized sweep of the plateau proper, where the same pattern repeats to the limits of vision: dark, angular prominences fading into the white veil, the scattered punctuation of plant-life vivid against the wet rock, the dark mirrors of still pools, and the faint, regular lines of other built terraces receding, like echoes of this one. It is an open, mid-range scale, a region rather than a scene, unified by the pervasive damp and the hush. No change marches across it; no season turns. The light itself is a constant, a diffuse midday glow filtered through the cloud deck, neither bright nor dim, casting no harsh shadows on the angled rock. It is a normal light, but frozen in a single hour.

This is a world newly lifted from the hot depths, wrapped in cloud, embraced by water, colonized by green emissaries that dare not spread too far, and settled by people who learned to build with what creation handed them. It exists now in a long, unchanging moment, as though listening to its own quiet breath—the ceaseless condensation, the tiny dripping—a place without animals, without noise, without erosion, without time.
