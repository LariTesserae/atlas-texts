The first thing that strikes you is the noise. It comes not from any single source but from everywhere at once, a tapestry of sound so dense and layered that it ceases to be a collection of separate calls and becomes a single, throbbing presence. It presses against the eardrums like deep water, a constant thrum punctuated by high-pitched shrieks, the percussive rattle of chitin on stone, the liquid gurgle of unseen throats. To call it deafening is insufficient; it is a physical force, a vibration that rattles the teeth and makes the air feel solid. You cannot speak over it, cannot think over it. You simply exist inside it.

Your eyes search for the source, but the darkness swallows everything beyond arm’s reach. It is not the true dark of the void—there is light here, but it comes in a thousand tiny points, a scattered constellation of fungal blues and greens, of animal lamps that wink and drift, of phosphorescent slimes that coat the rocks and drip from overhead. The canopy above must be thick beyond imagination, for no true daylight penetrates, only a faint, diffusive glow that might be the memory of a sun. This is a world lit by decay and appetite, a bioluminescent twilight where shadows are not absent but merely blur and shift with every blink.

The ground underfoot is treacherous, a maze of jagged rock so sharp that even the thickest leather would be sliced. The formations are young, geologically speaking—limestone needles and blade-thin fins thrust up from the damp soil in chaotic clusters, their edges raw and unweathered. There is no soil in the conventional sense; it is a spongy mat of living and dead vegetation, knotted roots, and mycelial threads that coat the stone like a living plaster. Moisture beads on every leaf and stem, a constant condensation that never quite falls as rain but gathers in droplets that slide and plink into hidden pools. Here and there, shallow depressions hold black water, perfectly still, their surfaces unbroken until some unseen shape rises to gulp air and vanishes again with a sound like a wet kiss.

The vegetation is a riot of green—but not the gentle green of a temperate forest. It is a feverish, congested green, the color of things that grow too fast. Every vertical surface is furred with mosses, lichens, and curling ferns. Vines as thick as a man’s arm spiral up the rock spires, dripping curtains of aerial roots that sway without wind. Broad-leaved plants with waxy, dark foliage cluster so tightly that you cannot see between them; to move forward is to push through a yielding, fleshy resistance. The density is obscene. You are surrounded by layers: the canopy far above, a middle realm of twisted branches and epiphytes, a shrub layer of sprawling cycads and thorny rattans, and the ground layer of slime and thallus. And through it all, the fauna.

There is no moment of stillness. Eyes, countless eyes, gleam from every crevice. Armored things scuttle over the rocks in incessant motion, their legs ticking like clockwork. In the darkness, you cannot distinguish individual creatures; they merge into a single, writhing current of life. A sudden flurry erupts as a mass of sinuous, multi-legged forms cascades down a rock face, each one perhaps the size of a cat, their bodies glowing with a sickly yellow light. They move with a collective purpose, streaming into a pool and churning the water to foam. The noise redoubles—a screaming, chittering crescendo that has no discernible cause.

This is a country in perpetual turmoil, a landscape that refuses to settle. The sharp rocks are the bones of a world that tore itself open not long ago. Perhaps a rift, a basin that dropped as the earth’s crust stretched, creating a mesh of faults and sinkholes. The water table rises and falls with an almost tidal rhythm, though no moon governs it; instead, subterranean conduits gulp and regurgitate the moisture, so that a dry hollow might become a steaming lake in minutes, and then drain away just as quickly, leaving flopping creatures in its wake. The vegetation responds with horrifying speed—some plants visibly grow in the span of a heartbeat, their tendrils snaking out to fill any new gap, while others collapse into slime overnight, only to sprout again from the same root. The very ground seems to curdle with decay and rebirth.

And there is something more. Amid the chaos, you begin to notice patterns that are not purely natural. A pile of stones, each one carefully selected and balanced, forms a dam across a rivulet, redirecting the flow into a mossy basin. The dam is crude, but unmistakably constructed. A vine has been stripped of its thorns and woven into a simple loop hanging from a branch—a snare, perhaps, or a signal. On a flat rock, several quartz-like pebbles are arranged in a nearly geometric spiral; beside them lies a chipped piece of flint, its edge sharp enough to cut. These are not the works of a permanent civilization. There are no huts, no walls, no hearths. The artefacts are ephemeral, abandoned as soon as they are made, scattered like toys after a game. But they speak of intelligence—of a mind that grasps the concept of tool and technique, if only in a fleeting, instinctual way.

The makers are elusive. You catch glimpses: a creature that walks upright on two legs for a dozen steps before dropping to all fours, its forelimbs ending in nimble, many-fingered paws. It is covered in iridescent scales that shimmer blue-green in the fungal light. It carries a stick, which it uses to probe a crevice, extracting a wriggling grub. It eats, discards the stick, and vanishes into the throng. A troop of similar beings rustles in the canopy, chattering a complex rhythm that might be language, or might be just another strand in the wall of sound. They use leaves to cup water, stones to crack nuts, and perhaps even elemental fire—you think you catch the scent of smoke, but it might be the smouldering of a lightning-struck tree. The tech is there, but it has not given rise to architecture, to settlement, to accumulation. It is a tool-use that lives only in the moment, as dynamic and unstable as the terrain itself.

The air is never still. Warm, of course—a steady heat that hovers at the edge of discomfort, the kind of warmth that breeds fungi and quickens the blood. It carries a thousand smells: the sweetness of rotting fruit, the acrid musk of animal glands, the mineral bite of wet limestone, and above all, the thick, living reek of chlorophyll and chitin. The dampness is less a presence than a state of being; you are never dry, but you are also not drenched. It is the moisture of exhalation, of transpiration, of the earth sweating. Every leaf drips, every stone secretes, and the air itself leaves a film on your tongue.

In the infrequent open patches—where a rock spire has collapsed or a tree has tumbled—you can sense the scale of the place. It is not a mere grove or a swamp, but a basin that stretches for miles, ringed by broken hills that leak steam. The jagged topography rises and falls in a series of steps, each one a micro-habitat: fern-choked benches, pools of hot mud, crevices where pale, blind things sing to each other. The sky, when you can catch a glimpse through a rent in the canopy, is a deep, bruised purple, thick with clouds that never release. Perhaps this entire region lies under a permanent volcanic haze, or within the influence of some atmospheric anomaly. The light, what little there is, has a twilight quality, as if the sun were permanently in eclipse.

The fauna is not merely abundant; it is overwhelming in its diversity and its interactions. Every niche is crammed with competitors. The sharp rock faces are scaled by fungal gardens tended by ant-like creatures that march in orderly columns, their bodies touching end-to-end. Flying things—some feathered, some membraned—erupt in swarms from the pools only to be snatched by larger fliers that swoop without seeing. Predation is constant, and death is as thick as life; the ground squelches with the bodies of the fallen, already being processed by a shimmering crust of decomposers. The noise is the sound of this furnace: mating calls that must pierce the din, territorial screams, the crack and snap of battles fought in the mycelial slime. It is a carnival of violence and procreation, a festival that never ends.

But the most unsettling aspect is the turbulence. The landscape is not a passive stage; it is a player. The water that rushes from hidden springs can carve a new channel in an hour, toppling trees that then become substrates for a fresh explosion of growth. The very rock sometimes cracks with a sound like thunder, and a new fissure vomits a plume of steam or a gush of mineral-saturated water. There is a ceaseless shifting, a refusal to form a stable equilibrium. Perhaps the whole basin is alive in some sense—the mycelial network that threads through the rocks might possess a distributed intelligence, orchestrating the flow of nutrients and the movement of water to maximize its own growth. Or perhaps the tectonic forces beneath are still in violent flux, with daily microquakes that rearrange the topography. The sharpness of the features is evidence of this youth: every edge looks freshly-broken, every surface unweathered by time or rain. Erosion has barely begun its smoothing work; the land is raw, newborn, and angry.

In this tumult, the primitive tool-use takes on a new significance. The creatures that wield sticks and stones are not taming the environment; they are surfing its chaos. Their constructions are immediately destroyed or repurposed by the next flood or tremor, and they will build again, remaking their tiny dams and snares as the ground shifts under them. There is no accumulation of cultural memory, because the memory is in the landscape itself—the spores that survive, the roots that reroute, the instinct that tells them how to shape a flint. It is a technology of the now, perfectly adapted to a world where tomorrow might bring a new chasm or a new sea.

You stand in the middle of it, and you realize that you are the only static thing, and your stillness is an affront. The sounds batter you, the darkness disorients, the damp seeps into your core. And yet, there is a terrible beauty here—a beauty born of extremes, of life pushed to its most rampant, most inventive, most dissonant expression. This is a landscape that is still deciding what it will be, and for now, it is everything, all at once, in a roaring, luminous, sharp-edged dream.
