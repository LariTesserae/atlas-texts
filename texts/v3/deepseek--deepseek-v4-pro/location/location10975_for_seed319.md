High above the sea, where the air thins but never chills, the plateau lifts its broad, weathered back into a permanent haze. It is a place of unending sound, a roar that has grown so old it has become a kind of silence—settled into the rock, expected as the dripping of water.

**Terrain and the State of Things**

The ground is a dark, finely pitted stone, slick in places and crumbling in others—erosion so advanced it is no longer a process but a home. This is a landscape that finished changing long ago, and now merely wears itself gently down, grain by grain, in a patient equilibrium with the forces that shape it. The elevation is high enough that the horizon feels pulled taut in every direction, an open, regional sweep of stone and scattered structure, but the air is dense with moisture. Warmth pools in hollows among the rock, where shallow water stands, never growing cold, never quite drying. It is a post-rain world without the memory of the storm: water seeps from every fissure, beads on vertical faces, condenses on metal and stone alike, runs in rivulets down ancient channels carved by nothing but time and the gentle persistence of wet breath.

**Water and Sparse Life**

Water is everywhere but deep. It lies in sheets across flat tables of bedrock, fills the hollows between cobbles, drips from every overhang with the regularity of a slow pulse. Marsh-edge fringes appear where sediment has gathered—dark, peaty muck dotted with stunted, wiry reeds so sparse they seem like afterthoughts. The vegetation holds on in isolated, desaturated clumps: pale lichens crusting the north faces of rocks, tufts of coarse grass growing from cracks, here and there a low, twisted shrub with leaves like hammered copper. The scarcity of plants is not a sign of harshness—the warmth and wetness would suggest a riot of growth—but of something else, something kept at bay. Fauna is rarer still. There are no birds crying out, no insects buzzing near the puddles. Occasionally, a thin, translucent lizard might be seen pressed against a sun-warmed stone, or a millipede the color of rust winding slowly through a patch of moss, but these encounters are separated by hours, days.

**The Built Form and the Sound**

And then, the city. Not a city in the sense of dense streets and stacked dwellings, but a dispersed, sprawling settlement of industrial purpose, its elements placed with the sparse logic of the plateau itself. Brick smokestacks rise from the stone like monuments to some patient, mechanical faith, their bases wrapped in coils of pipe that sweat and drip. Factory halls built of the same dark rock, roofs of corrugated iron gone soft with rust, stand in low clusters, spaced far enough apart that the noise from one does not quite mask the noise from another, but blends into a continuous, overlapping roar.

Sound here is not an event; it is the atmosphere. It is the ground note of existence. The air thrums with engines—great, slow-turning diesel generators perhaps, or steam turbines running on geothermal heat—that never cycle down. Hissing vents release clouds of vapor that join the general humidity. Clanking, rhythmic and irregular, echoes from within buildings where machines of indeterminate purpose stamp, crush, or turn. Conveyor belts rumble, carrying unseen loads from one squat structure to another. The overall effect is a deafening wash, so constant and so layered that individual sources become impossible to pinpoint. You do not hear the city; you feel it in your teeth, in the hollows of your skull. It has been this way for so long that the stone itself seems tuned to the vibration.

**Light, Atmosphere, and the Strange**

The light is normal—a diffuse, gray-white, a light that could be noon or late afternoon, a light that erases shadows but does not brighten. It comes from a sky that is often a uniform blanket of low cloud, the ceiling of the plateau. The warm, wet air holds everything in a close, muffled embrace, and yet the scale remains open: from any point, you can see across stretches of wet stone and scattered industry to the next cluster of stacks, the next flat-roofed workshed, and on into a hazy distance where more of the same presumably continues. The density is sparse, but the urban character is undeniable; it is a city stretched thin, its built elements separated by acres of wet, empty rock. Paths exist: iron walkways suspended just above the water, roads of packed gravel that squelch underfoot, and narrow-gauge railways with rails eaten at the flange-edge by rust, along which a lone, slow-moving wagon might be pulled by a trundling engine once an hour.

The weirdness seeps in at the edges. The slow dynamic means that nothing seems to change—the same puddles are always there, reforming after each rainless drizzle; the same machines always run; the same clouds always hang. And yet, there are signs of a deep, patient process at work. The rock is not merely wet; it is weeping a mineral-rich fluid that, upon contact with iron and brick, forms a pale crust, so that every structure appears to be fossilizing in place, growing a stony skin. The sparse vegetation, when you look closely, has a dull, metallic sheen to its leaves, as if it has taken up the fine industrial dust into itself over generations. The few animals are unnervingly silent, their movements slow, their colors blending perfectly with the wet, rust-stained stone. It is a place that feels both ancient and stillborn, where industry has become geology, and the boundary between the built and the eroded has long since disappeared.

**Rules of This World**

This landscape operates on a logic of equilibrium and immense patience. The water cycle is a closed, fogbound loop: evaporation rises, condenses immediately, falls as a pervasive mist that never becomes rain, seeps back through the porous rock to feed the industrial boilers in a cycle that has run unbroken for centuries. The machines, perhaps, are not powered by fuel brought in from outside, but by the slow heat of the earth, tapped through deep-bored shafts that also release the moisture. The city was not built, it seems, but grown—an emergent property of the plateau, its bricks fired from local clay, its metal cast from ore that once lay in veins now emptied, its design a patient accumulation of accretions. The deafening sound is the voice of this balance, a steady-state signal that the system is in health. Sparse density is a requirement: the machines need space to breathe the wet air, to let the moisture settle, to prevent their own heat from building. The strange quietude of the fauna, the metallic tint to the plants, the slow, fossilizing growth on every surface—all speak to a place where human industry has become another form of weathering, another chapter in the long erosion of a high, warm, and weeping world.
