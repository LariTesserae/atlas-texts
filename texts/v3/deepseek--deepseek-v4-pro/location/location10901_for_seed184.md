The Ceiling breathes. That's the first thing you'd notice, if you could notice anything down here—the slow, tidal flex of the membrane overhead, a continent of translucent skin stretched across iron ribs, exhaling a fine, warm mist that never quite becomes rain. The mist hangs in the space between Ceiling and water, a permanent fog-layer that diffuses the dim orange glow of the sodium lamps into something almost liquid itself, a light you could drown in.

Below the fog: the Lattice.

This is a submerged city, or what became of one. The water is everywhere, warm as blood, clear but deeply stained—tea-colored from a thousand years of leaching through the peat-and-root tangle of the world above, which no one has seen in living memory. The depth here is not vertical but horizontal; you don't sink, you drift, through corridors and chambers and collapsed domes, all of it packed tight, room against room, structure against structure, until the water itself seems to have architecture. Buildings weren't built here so much as deposited, accreted, generation upon generation of industrial settlement layered like coral. Brick tenements slump against iron foundry shells. Copper pipes the width of your body weave through everything, some still warm to the touch, carrying something that thrums.

The temperature is steady: blood-heat, 37 degrees exactly, maintained by the geothermal exchange coils that spiral down through every level. It makes the boundary between your skin and the water feel theoretical. You're a body in a larger body, and the larger body is old.

Sound doesn't travel right here. It's not silent—nothing this dense with life-support machinery could be silent—but the sounds are attenuated, muffled by the water's weight, transformed. The groan of a settling floorplate becomes a whale-call, a low-frequency pressure change rather than a noise. The distant, rhythmic *thump-pause-thump* of a piston pump somewhere deeper in the Lattice arrives as a felt pulse in the sternum, not quite heard. Bubbles rise from somewhere—always, somewhere, a slow leak—and they sound like glass bells when they hit the Ceiling membrane. Conversations, if there are any, would be pressure-signals, tapped on pipes, carried through the water as vibration-language. No one has spoken aloud here in a very long time.

The strangeness is the garden.

You see it before you understand it: a green glow, softer than the sodium lamps, emanating from the hollowed-out shell of what was once a pumping station. The walls are furred with vegetation. Not seaweed—something terrestrial, adapted, gone aquatic. Ferns with fronds that wave in the convection currents. Mosses that have learned to filter-feed, their tiny leaves opening and closing in a rhythm that matches the distant piston's pulse. Vines thick as your wrist twist through rusted pipe-flanges, their leaves a deep, bruised purple on one side and phosphorescent blue-green on the other. The light they give off is biological, chemical, a slow oxidation that produces no heat, only this dreamlike glow. This is the Engine-Heart Garden, and it is the only place in the Lattice where something unbuilt thrives.

Everything else is packed, saturated, *full*.

The buildings are occupied not by people but by process. Each room, each corridor, each repurposed factory floor houses a function: a bank of ceramic filters, slowly clogging; a forest of copper condensing coils, each one beaded with distilled water that tastes of metal; a rack of brass gears the size of dinner plates, turning so slowly you can watch the corrosion-dust accumulate and fall. The city is an organism of maintenance, maintaining itself. Machines tend machines. The density is not of bodies but of *purpose*—every cubic meter of warm, dim water has a role in the larger metabolism.

Sometimes you find bones. They're always small, always disarticulated, always clean. The fauna here is rare and cryptic—you'll never see the things that cleaned these bones. Occasionally, if you stay very still, you might catch a flicker of movement at the edge of the garden-glow: a pallid, eel-like shape with too many fins, or a creature that looks like a disembodied hand crawling along a pipe. They don't approach. They have no interest in you. They have the same relationship to the Lattice that mites have to a mattress—indigenous, invisible, inevitable.

The Ceiling breathes. The garden glows. The water holds its warmth. And somewhere, in the deepest coil of the Engine-Heart, the main pump completes another cycle, sending its subsonic message through the submerged rooms: *still here, still here, still here.*
