There is a high place where the world is still being made. It is a plateau stitched into the sky, the air thin and damp as breath against a cold mirror. The elevation is such that horizons seem to fracture—distances that should be far are pulled close, peaks rising across the basin like unfinished thoughts. This is not an old landscape. It is embryonic, raw, its bones only half-set. Rock faces show no weathering; they are clean shear planes, obsidian-smooth or pockmarked with the crisp voids where gas once bubbled through cooling stone. The ground is a mosaic of young basalt, crumbled tuff, and seams of metallic ore that glitter dully in the permanent twilight. Erosion is a rumour here, whispered by a few trickles of water that do not yet know their way. The courses are intermittent, more like the sweating of stone than true streams: seepages that gather in shallow lapis-blue pools between ridges of newly thrust land, then vanish into fissures hours later. This is a place of condensation environments—fog is the great hydrologist. It rolls in twice a day from the void beyond the plateau’s edge, a dense marine layer that never broke over an ocean, instead settling as beaded moisture on every surface. The vegetation makes the most of this. It is lush in a way that surprises: not the riotous green of a jungle but a deep, close-cropped exuberance of mosses, liverworts, and coiled ferns that glow emerald-black in the dimness. These plants have no interest in the sky; they grow into the fog, their fronds etching the air for molecules. Entire swaths of the plateau are carpeted in a thick, sponge-like mat that squelches underfoot, releasing a scent of cold earth and ozone. Where the water lingers in pockets, strange succulents with translucent leaves swell like glass beads, and thin-skinned fungi push up overnight, their caps rimmed with symbiont creatures that look like minute, furry starfish.

The light barely qualifies as such. It is dim—a perpetual dusk that shifts only from deep gunmetal grey to a bruised amber when the heavy blanket of vapour thins for a few hours. No direct sun reaches here; the plateau sits beneath a lenticular cap of cloud that never dissipates, and below that, the industrial exhalations of the settlement add their own veiling. Yet there is light enough to see by, a diffuse, sourceless glow that seems to cling to the damp air itself, making every spire and chimney a silhouette of inky black against a slightly lighter black. The sound is loud, but not in a way that assaults. It is a layered, omnipresent thrum: the chuff of massive ventilation systems built into the cliffsides, the deep, slow pulse of engine houses that rattle the fillings in your teeth, and the constant, percussive breathing of factories that never fully sleep. This is a settlement-scale urbanity laid out with an industrial logic, but it is sparse, stretched thin across the open basin. Between the districts—clusters of cylindrical iron reactors, gantries, and windowless worker barracks built from the same black rock they stand upon—lie great expanses of that embryonic terrain. Railway lines, narrow-gauge and glistening with condensation, cut across the moss plains on raised embankments of cinder, connecting the production zones. You can walk for an hour through the dripping quiet of a fog field and suddenly encounter a ten-story stamping mill, its walls sweating lubricant, its flywheels turning with a patience that matches the landscape’s own slow clock.

The dynamic here is gradual. Processes are long. The engines run at a constant, low tempo, their rhythms aligned to some cycle measured in months, not minutes. A single piston might complete a stroke over the course of a day, driving a geared system that feeds powdered ore into arc furnaces with the slowness of sedimentation. This is production scaled not to human urgency but to the pace of the emerging geology itself, as if the city is in dialogue with the raw forces still shaping the rock beneath it. The built environment is layered: older generations of machinery are entombed in newer rock, because the plateau is still rising. The “emerging” erosion parameter means that the land is actively forming, and the city has had to adapt. In some districts, buildings are constructed on giant jacks that level themselves as the ground swells beneath them. In others, entire factories have been built inside massive geodes that grew around the machinery like a calcite shell, the industry continuing inside while the world accretes a new skin.

This reality breakdown—the weirdness parameter pegged at 2.6—is not an intermittent glitch but a baseline condition. The rules here are in flux. The most visible manifestation is the fauna, which is abundant to the point of being a constant presence, yet none of it is entirely stable. Creatures exist in an almost liquid state of possibility. The moss plains host herds of what look like six-legged elk, their antlers branching into fractal patterns that shift if you stare too long. Their calls are part of the soundscape: a sonorous, metallic honking that harmonizes with the machinery. But these animals, like many things here, sometimes flicker. A grazing individual might for a few seconds show through its body the ghost of another form—a smaller, quilled creature; a swarm of separate wingless birds; a knot of light. Then it snaps back. The workers pay little mind. The industrial processes seem to rely on this instability; the ore itself is a substance called “gelidite,” which exists as a mineral only when cooled to the ambient temperature of the plateau. If it warms by a few degrees, it begins to flow into other phases—sometimes a volatile gas, sometimes a living sludge that must be re-contained. The abundant fauna includes the “whistle-rats,” knee-high rodents that nest in the pipework and whose bodies can phase through solid metal when startled, but only in one direction, making them a nuisance for electrical systems. There are flocks of flying creatures like manta rays that glide along the thermal updrafts from the smelters, their wingspans easily twenty feet, silent until they pass through a pocket of stable air and suddenly sound off like foghorns.

The urban layout respects this porousness of reality. Streets are not always streets; some are designated merely by painted lines on the rock, because pavement tends to morph into a viscous tar if the fog thickens. Buildings have no permanent doors, only openings that can be sealed with a curtain of heavy gauge chain, and signage is often auditory—a series of recorded bells or machine noises that tell you where you are better than any map. Maps themselves are provisional, sketched on slate tablets that update each dawn as surveyors mark the latest movements. The density is sparse by design: you need empty space between presences, both for safety and to allow the landscape’s own slow breathing. A factory might sit half a mile from its workers’ dormitory, connected by a tube-tunnel that hangs from cable stays, swaying gently, its interior lit by bio-luminescent fungi that the inhabitants cultivate. These fungi are part of the lushness, a deliberate symbiosis. They feed on the condensation and give a faint, steady light, turning the tunnel into a greenish artery.

The temperature is cool, barely above a chill. Even inside the factories, the heat of the processes is localized, absorbed by the omnipresent damp. Workers wear thick wool-lined coats that smell of machine oil and mold, and their breaths plume. The water, that 1.1, is just enough: occasional rain falls as a fine, almost granular mist that seems to hover rather than fall. It collects on every horizontal surface, drips from the eaves of corrugated iron roofs in a syncopated rhythm that becomes part of the music of the place. There are riverine systems of a sort, but they are suspended—aqueducts of bamboo-like polymers that channel condensation from the high ridges down to mill wheels, which turn with funereal slowness, grinding not grain but mineral samples.

The atmosphere is a constant low-level tension, not of threat, but of a world holding its breath. The slow dynamic means that sunrise and sunset, if that’s what the shifting gloom can be called, are events that take days. The light dims to almost nothing, then gradually brightens over a sixty-hour cycle. The city adjusts: work shifts are based on these slow pulses, and the soundscape alters as well, with the engine houses cycling up a minor key when the light is at its lowest, as if humming the dark into place.

It’s an open landscape, regional in feel. From the edge of the main settlement—a place called Geode Central on the old, unchanging sign engraved in stainless steel—you can look across the basin and see the next industrial cluster as a constellation of dull orange glows, cranes like skeletal fingers, and the occasional flare of a gas vent burning off excess reality. Between here and there, the moss valleys stretch, dotted with tussocks of black ferns and the occasional herd of those phase-shifting elk. A road, more a set of deeply rutted tracks, winds across it. But most transport is by the cable trams that run silently overhead, their cars looking like iron lunglets. The scale is mid-range: the entire plateau is perhaps thirty miles across, rimmed by mountains that are still sharp as knives, having never suffered the wearing of wind and water. Those peaks are so sheer and uneroded that they reflect the factory lights in weird, upside-down mirages when the fog is thin. The reality breakdown makes the peaks seem closer or farther on different days; sometimes they appear as a painted backdrop, flat and unconvincing, and you can walk toward them for hours without getting any nearer. That’s just the boundary condition.

The ecology is, against all reason, rich. The fauna abundance is not an accident; many species have adapted to the instability, and some may be products of it. The whistle-rats, for instance, are definitely native, but there are also “smog-bats” that roost in the warm exhaust of the smelters and feed by filtering particulates from the air with membranes that shimmer like oil slicks. Their droppings accumulate in great mounds that heat up and grow a unique form of heat-loving mycelium, harvested for insulation. There are large, slow-moving beetles with carapaces of fused metal dust, so heavy they can only move across the flattest ground. Their migrations circle the basin once every geological epoch—they’re currently about a third through, and their ancestors passed this same way before the factories were built. The industrial humans have learned to simply station their equipment elsewhere.

The “urban” score of 2.5 speaks to a deep infrastructure, not necessarily a teeming population. The settlement is a city by any measure—thousands of workers, barracks, a central council hub built in a repurposed cathedral-sized geode, pipe networks, electrical cabling, schools for the engineers’ children, a market that sells both food and oddities from the deep faults. But because it is sparse, it doesn’t feel crowded. There is always a view of the open terrain between buildings, and the city walls are not continuous—they are more like breakwaters, segments placed to redirect the creeping flows of temporary liquifaction or the occasional reality storm. The tech is industrial in an almost anachronistic way: massive cast-iron machinery, boilers, steam-driven computers that use punch cards made of mica, great Tesla-coil-like generators that produce a field stabilizing the gelidite. It’s the 19th century as dreamed by someone who knew about quantum mechanics but only via metaphor. Electrified, yes—the trams run on overhead lines that crackle with blue static—but the electricity is generated by tapping the temperature differential between the cold air and the warm body of the plateau, using banks of Seebeck plates half a mile long. It’s a scaled production, industrial in the sense of a whole society organized around extraction and processing, but the product is not simply matter; it’s stasis, the maintenance of a thin thread of consistent reality in a place where things tend to dissolve.

The rules of the world: they are local and provisional. Nothing here is taken for granted. A foreman’s daily check includes testing the solidity of his boots by stomping three times; if the sound is a clean clang, it’s a good day. If it’s a wet splat, he goes back inside and waits. The slow dynamic means that disasters are usually gradual—a building might lean an inch too far over a month, giving plenty of time for evacuation and the deployment of reality anchors, which are just large steel spikes driven into the ground and connected to a humming capacitor. The weirdness is treated with a kind of weary pragmatism. Children learn to read the signs: a sudden sparkle in the air means a probability flare, stand still until it passes. If a patch of ground starts to look like a drawing, don’t step on it. The fauna knows this too. The elk will detour a mile to avoid an area where colours seem to bleed, and the whistle-rats phase more often when the fog thickens, which makes their nests in the machinery all the harder to evict.

Sound is loud, but it’s a spacious loudness. The deep hum of the engines is the bass note. Over that, the higher-pitched whirrs of the cable trams, the periodic hiss of a pressure release from a distant vent, the elk calls, the rat squeaks, and the constant drip of water from a thousand eaves. In the gaps between industrial zones, the soundscape is dominated by the moss plains’ own music: the soft, crinkling sound of the lichen growing, if you press your ear to the ground—a sound like cellophane being stretched. It’s a place where the boundary between the built and the emerging is never settled. The city is always being partially reclaimed, not by wild nature in the old sense, but by the world’s own process of becoming. Every few decades, a new vent opens in a factory floor, and a crop of the glass-bead succulents pushes through, and the workers just build a planter around it and adjust the pulleys.

In the deepest, dimmest hours of the long twilight, when the fog presses in and the reality flickers grow more frequent, you might see the plateau’s edge peel up like a piece of film, revealing a darkness underneath that is not sky but the raw sub-structure, gridlines of possibility not yet solidified. The city’s lights, scattered across the basin, hang in that void like lanterns on an invisible scaffold. And the slow engine of the world keeps turning, one piston-stroke per day, forging a landscape that may someday know erosion, sunlight, a proper rain. But for now, it’s exactly this: a damp, cool, half-made, industrial city clinging to a fragment of emergent reality, surrounded by abundant, impossible life, and loud with the sound of its own patient construction.
