The place knows itself as **The Gullet**, a chamber wrung from the interior of a single, continent-spanning organism. Its walls are not walls but the inward curve of a root-bole so immense that the space it encloses—cathedral-scale to human senses—is merely a pocket of air within a living body.

---

**Light** arrives from nowhere and everywhere. No sun penetrates the meat of the world, yet the air itself glows with a steady, pearlescent luminescence, as if the atmosphere has been breathed luminous by the sheer mass of metabolism around it. It is the light of a permanently overcast noon, diffuse and shadowless, revealing every surface.

And every surface is **vegetation**. The parameter says *total*, and here that means the distinction between plant and ground, plant and structure, plant and atmosphere, has long collapsed. The floor is a mat of aerial roots, some as thick as torsos, woven into a springy, faintly warm carpet that exhales moisture with every compression. The walls are the parent flesh: a deeply folded, liver-colored substance that is neither bark nor skin, creased with sphincter-like valves and weeping a thin, mucoid sap that smells of turned earth and clove. Over this the secondary growth riots—epiphytes crusted upon epiphytes, ferns that grow on the backs of liverworts that grow on bracket fungi that digest the primary tissue and are themselves digested, a slow-motion war of total occupancy. The ceiling, forty feet above, is a canopy of pendant leaves the size of awnings, overlapping like shingles, dripping intermittently into the hollow below.

That dripping is one thread in a **deafening** tapestry. Sound here is not a backdrop; it is the medium. The score is written in a billion tiny percussions: the constant, percussive *tock* of water drops falling from leaf-tip to root-bole, each drop its own note, together a roaring static like a rainstorm on a tin roof. Through this cuts the vocalization of **abundant fauna**—a density of animal life that seems impossible given the emptiness of the central space. They are creatures of the walls, the crevices, the dangling lianas. There are troupes of slender, six-limbed mammals with faces like wet fruit, who howl in cascading, overlapped phrases, each call triggering a response from a dozen in different vibrational keys. There are flocks of membranous fliers—not birds but something older, with wing-skin stretched between elongated ribs—that course through the hollow in tight, murmuring clouds, their collective wingbeats a subsonic thrumming felt in the sternum. And there are the **Gulpers**, the ambush predators of this inverted forest: lumpen, fungal-pale things that adhere to the walls and inflate themselves to near-invisibility, then collapse into hollows with a sound like a wet slap when something passes. Every predation, every mating shriek, every territorial drumming on the resonant plant-flesh adds a layer. The total volume is a physical presence, a pressure that makes the eardrums flex continuously, a wall of noise so saturated that silence would feel like a vacuum.

But the space feels **empty** because all this life crowds the envelope. The central void—the cathedral nave—is a volume of almost nothing but humid air and glowing mist. To walk across the root-floor is to move through an open room, the walls a good twenty paces distant. The emptiness is architectural. The density parameter’s near-zero is literal: there is more life per cubic inch in the walls than in the hollow’s heart. The air here is thick with scent and spore and sound, but of solid matter, there is only the swinging lianas and the occasional fluttering transit.

This emptiness has been shaped. The **built** parameter says *urban*, and here that means the hollow is not a natural void. It has been cultivated over centuries by a settlement. **The Hinderers**, they call themselves, a population of primates-without-tools who live within the flesh of the world-organism. Their dwellings are grown, not built: pear-shaped bladders that extrude from the parent tissue when a Hinderer pupates, attached to the walls by umbilicus-stems. Each bladder is a single room of translucent membrane stretched over a cartilaginous frame, furnished with a palpitating floor that absorbs waste and exudes fresh water. The Hinderers’ urban organization is visible in the spiral arrangement of these bladders around the central void, in the traffic patterns of their ceaseless climbing, in the communal dung-gardens where favored fungal strains are cultivated. It is a town without a single artifact, a city running on biology alone. The **tech** is none, not even a shaped stick; the Hinderers use their own bodies and the directed growth of the superorganism to meet every need.

The place is **warm**—a steady, enveloping warmth like a drawn bath, coming up from the living floor and radiating from the walls. It is **damp** enough that the air beads on skin, that all surfaces are slick, that moisture condenses on every hair and drips from the tip of every leaf. It is **low** elevation, sunk into the world’s great depression, where air pressure is higher and the heat gets trapped under the endless canopy above. And it is **established**—the Gullet has looked this way for epochs, the wear-patterns of Hinderer hands polished into root-struts, the floor worn into a shallow bowl by generations of footsteps and the slow peristaltic settling of the organism.

The **weirdness** is the breakdown: reality here is not fully shackled. Sound does not merely travel; it has a weight, a slow viscosity that makes the air ripple in visible shock-fronts when the Gulpers clap shut. The Hinderers do not die but molt, shedding their outer form to fuse with the parent tissue, becoming new bladders, new lianas, new sensory nodes that feed the organism’s vast, dreaming mind. The creatures adorning the walls are not separate species but phases of a single life-cycle, the fliers the larval form of the wall-stuck predators, the howlers the adolescent stage of the Hinderers themselves. Time loops: a drop of water falling from the ceiling might take an hour to reach the floor, hanging in mid-air, swelling with light, while the heartbeat of the world-skin pulses around it at a rhythm of geological days.

The whole chamber is **active**, a single continuous process. Weather is not external but internal: every few hours the lung-like walls contract, the valve-orifices gape, and a warm, gale-force wind scours the hollow clean of spores, then dies as swiftly as it came. The Hinderers migrate in slow, vertical tides, ascending to the ceiling to feed on leaf-exudates, descending to sleep in their bladders. The fauna cycle from predator to prey to scavenger, a churn so rapid it looks like the walls are crawling. And through it all, the sound—a sound of such complexity that it begins to convey meaning, a language the Hinderers are born understanding, the voice of the world telling itself its own shape.

Step into The Gullet, and you step into a lung, a throat, a town square, a womb, all at once. The walls breathe. The floor eats. The air sings until your bones rattle in sympathy. And somewhere in the luminous mist of the central emptiness, a Hinderer hangs by its tail, draws breath, and adds its own howl to the chorus, perfectly in tune with the universe’s one, deafening note.
