The air does not merely hold water; it collaborates with it. Each breath is a sponge, thick and warm against the skin, carrying the weight of an ocean that has decided to live overhead, condensing on every surface and then re-evaporating on the way to the ground. A perpetual, tepid drizzle coats the flesh of this place. It is not rain so much as exhalation, a landscape weeping from every pore at a temperature just below body-heat, so that the boundary between self and world becomes a matter of wetness alone. The light is daylight, but diffused through a ceiling of vapour so uniform that shadows are soft suggestions, grey on grey-green, no sun-disk ever visible. Sound arrives first as a low, omnidirectional roar, a basal rumble that seeps into the bones before the ear can parse it. It is the sound of vast volumes of water moving—not in falls, but in capillary creep, in drip-chains that link leaf to root over hundreds of metres, in the gentle collapse of saturated tissues, in the hiss of mist dragged sideways by a wind that never stops reshaping the clouds. Beneath that, a sharper note: a creaking, crackling, tearing noise, organic and continuous, like a forest floor scaled up to tectonic dimensions.

When the mist parts—it does not lift, but rather thins in channels, sculpted by the gusts—the terrain resolves not as land but as architecture. There is no soil, no exposed rock, no geological truth. Underfoot, the surface is a sprung mat, resilient and slightly warm, woven from millions of rhizomes as thick as cables, their outer bark polished to a kind of leathery smoothness by the perpetual damp. This is the floor, the foundational substrate, and it extends in all directions, forming broad, shallow basins that hold sheets of water stained amber by dissolved tannins, and low swells that serve as causeways. But it is not flat; it is a worked surface, a constructed plain. The rhizome-mat is stitched together with seams that look intentional—linear depressions where two strands meet and have fused, forming gutters that direct the flow of the condensate. Every few hundred metres, the mat humps upward into a deliberate berm, and from these berms rise the structures.

They are trees only by the loosest inheritance. Each is a solitary spire, utterly alone on the plain, so that the spaces between them are truly open—vistas of wet air and shimmering water-sheets stretching for kilometres before the next tower rises. This sparse placement, this deliberate isolation, makes each one an event, a landmark that draws the eye and then holds it. The spires are columnar, their bases swelling into buttresses that resemble the roots of a banyan but fused into a continuous, windowless wall. The material of their trunks is not wood but a compressed fibre, like paper pressed under heat and time, yet alive—damp, warm to the touch, and sometimes rippling with slow, muscular contractions as sap the consistency of honey is pumped up from the mat below. The surfaces are never smooth. They are covered in a lattice of ridges and furrows, sharp-edged and precise, as if carved by a tool that left its mark but was never removed. These are the features of a young landscape, unsoftened by decay: the edges are still raw, the junctions between structural elements crisp, the whole assembly looking as though it emerged fully formed from the hot mist only days ago. And perhaps it did.

There is no natural succession here. The spires do not grow slowly from seeds; they are extruded. As you watch—and if you stand long enough, you will see it—the base of a distant tower suddenly softens, its fibres rehydrating into a paste, and from that paste a new buttress pushes outward, thickening and hardening in the space of an hour, its surface drying into the same sharp-ridged texture. The process is violent, accompanied by a sound like bones snapping underwater and the release of a steam-heat that shimmers the air. The entire structure writhes during this growth, a temporary softening of its rigid form, and then it sets, harder than before, until the next pulse of transformation. The ground mat responds in kind: its woven cables unweave and reweave, opening a cistern here, closing a channel there, always in response to the shifting architecture above. This is a world entirely built, a total construction, and yet there is no sign of a maker, no tool marks beyond the fibres' own grain, no metal, no stone, no fire. The technology is the biology itself, a pure expression of plant as machine, plant as city, plant as the entire made world.

The spires are not monolithic. As you traverse the plain on the springy rhizome-bridges, you begin to see that each tower is hollow, or rather, filled with a vertical succession of chambers separated by diaphragms of perforated tissue. These are catchments. The condensation that forms on the tower's outer walls is guided by the ridge-channels into its interior, where it pools in tiered basins. From the outside, you see this water weeping perpetually from thousands of small spouts—modified lenticels—at different heights, each one dribbling a thin stream that is caught by the lower chambers or falls to the ground mat, where it is instantly absorbed. The sound of this endless dripping, multiplied by the thousands of spires scattered across the open basin, creates a spatial chorus: near and far, high and low, a polyphony of percussive notes that meld into the general roar. The air temperature keeps the water from cooling; it is always tepid, steaming slightly as it hits cooler pockets of mist. At the very top of each spire, hidden in the cloud, there is a broad, flattened crown of respiratory fronds—not leaves for photosynthesis, but gill-like structures that capture the mist directly, extracting moisture with a motion like slow breathing. They undulate, and that undulation sends waves down the spire's body, keeping the sap flowing.

Because this is a young system—erosionally young, dynamically adolescent—the landscape is in a state of constant, turbulent rearrangement. The spires do not simply grow; they also collapse. The process is sudden and spectacular. The sharp-edged fibres that make up their walls are under enormous turgor pressure, and occasionally a stress-concentration at a growing tip will trigger a chain reaction. A crack propagates down the length of a spire with a sound like a tree snapping in a hurricane, but sustained and multiplied. Then the entire structure softens from the top down, its upper third deliquescing into a slurry of plant matter and stored water that sloughs off in a steaming avalanche. The collapse scatters fresh, sharp-edged fragments across the plain—chunks of the compressed-fibre “bark” as jagged as any freshly broken stone. These fragments are not weathered; they are still hot, still wet, still alive, and within hours they begin to fuse with the ground mat, healing over the wound. Meanwhile, from the broken stump, a new extrusion begins, often in a slightly different form, as if learning from the failure. The landscape thus cycles through creation and destruction on a timescale that feels breathless, hyperactive, a bazaar of form where every stall is being rebuilt and torn down simultaneously. The loudness of it all is not merely ambient; it is the sound of continuous construction, a permanent state of storm without weather, the noise of a Solaris-like ocean of plant-matter thinking itself into shape and then doubting its thoughts.

There are no animals, not an insect, not a bird, not a microbe visible to the eye. The ecology is a closed loop of one kingdom. The plants respire their own moisture, feed on their own decay, and the energy to drive this system comes from a deep-seated geothermal flux that keeps the plain hot and saturated. The high elevation—perhaps a plateau ringed by unseen peaks—traps the moist air, creating a lid of inversion. The strange weirdness lies in the pervasive sense that this is not accidental. The sharpness of the features, the intentional-looking channels, the modular spacing of the spires, the way the ground mat routes water exactly where it is needed—all hint at a design that pre-dates any conceivable designer, a biological algorithm that has been running so long it has become indistinguishable from a will. But it is a plant-will, slow and green and wet, utterly alien to animal cognition. To walk here is to walk through a mind made of leaves and water and pressure, a mind that is at this very moment deciding to tear down a tower to your left and raise a bridge to your right, for reasons you will never perceive.

The openness of the scale means that from the top of a low rhizome-ridge, you can see the pattern repeated for tens of kilometres: a scatter of spires, each one solitary, each one different in height and girth but sharing the same architectural language of sharp ridges and dripping spouts. Between them, the vast, reflective sheets of standing water are interrupted only by low, floating mats of a different plant substance—a scum of bright green that coalesces into temporary islands, then dissipates. These are not separate species; they are the same biological material in a different phase, the liquid state of the construction, harvesting the diffuse light. The light itself is steady, neutral, as if the sky had forgotten how to change from day to night, though there is a diurnal dimming. In that perpetual twilight, the spires gleam with wetness, their surfaces catching what little shine exists and scattering it into halos. The temperature never dips, the water never stops, the growth never ceases. And the sound, the roar and the drip and the creak, is so constant that it becomes a kind of silence, the baseline of a world that has built itself entirely from the one impulse: to make, and unmake, and make again, forever wet and sharp and hot.
