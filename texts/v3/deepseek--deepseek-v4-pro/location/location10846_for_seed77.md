The plain begins before you fully understand it is there—so vast that for a long while it reads as sky beneath sky, a low haze of heat that smudges the horizon line into something softer than earth. The ground is not ground, not in the old sense. It is a made thing, a continuous carpet of pale, knobbled substance that stretches in all directions, unbroken but for the occasional rise of a low mound, the gape of a shallow basin, or the sparse dark tuft of a plant clinging to the surface. You walk on it, and it gives slightly beneath your weight, a faint resilience that speaks of life not yet hardened fully into rock.

The light is ordinary daylight, a clear, unremarkable yellow-white that flattens the distance into shimmer. Heat rises from the crust in visible waves—temperature here a constant presence, a weight that settles onto your shoulders and into the hollows of your lungs. It is hot, but not the killing heat of a desert; this heat is humid, enveloping, a hot breath that carries the smell of wet limestone and something organic, yeasty, like bread dough left too long in the sun. The dampness comes and goes. You might walk for an hour under a bleached sky and feel only the weight of vapor in the air, the cling of it on your skin, and then the light will shift almost imperceptibly and a fine drizzle begins to fall, not so much rain as a settling of droplets out of saturated air. It lasts minutes, sometimes seconds, and then the heat returns to suck the moisture back upward in curls of steam that ghost over the highest points.

This is a landscape just coming into being, and everywhere that rawness shows. Erosion has not yet had its way; every edge is sharp, every contour fresh. The substance underfoot—call it bio-stone—is laid down in thin, concentric ridges like the growth rings of a boulder, each layer a pale grey or cream or faintly blushing pink, depending on the mineral content of the last accretion. The ridges form scalloped patterns, whorls, radial bursts that speak of some underlying biological rhythm, a pulse of deposition that spreads outward from scattered nodes across the plain. These nodes are the mounds, some no taller than a knee, a few rising to the height of a person. They are dome-shaped, pitted with tiny pores, and very slightly warm to the touch. From a few, a clear, watery fluid oozes slowly and trickles down the flanks to pool in the miniature basins, where it evaporates by afternoon and leaves behind a glistening trace of new mineral, a glaze just atoms thick. The whole plain is a factory floor the size of a nation, but the factories are alive and the product is the earth itself.

Vegetation is sparse, but not absent. What grows here has adapted to a rootless existence on a substrate that is constantly, almost imperceptibly, alive. The plants are dark, stringy things—long, flattened blades of a rubbery texture that sprout directly from cracks in the bio-stone, anchoring themselves with sucker-like pads. They are a muted olive-black, almost as if they have been scorched, and they twist slowly toward the light with a motion too slow to see but unmistakable when you return after an absence. Each plant stands alone, separated from its neighbors by wide expanses of bare crust, so that the eye traverses great distances of pale, ornamented ground before finding the next dark accent. The spacing is so regular it feels deliberate, as if some unseen gardener had planted them for a sparse aesthetic, but it is only competition for the seep-water that weeps from the mounds. Their blades catch the drizzle, funnel it down to their pads, and in the silence you can hear, if you are still enough, the faint, papery rustle of their twisting.

There are no animals. None. No insects hum in the hot air, no bird calls puncture the stillness, no scuttle of small life in the crust's crevices. The quiet is not an absence so much as a presence—a thick, attentive silence that fills the ears with the soft sound of your own pulse. But the place is not still. It is active in other ways. The biological pace here is slow, geological in tempo, yet continuous. The crust grows. You can perceive it if you watch: at the lip of a seep pool, a thin white line of new deposition hardens from gel to solid over the course of an hour. The mounds breathe, a cyclic swelling and subsidence that follows the heat of the day, and with each breath the pores exhale a faint mist of spores or perhaps something finer, a living dust that settles and adds a microscopic layer to the topography. Weather cycles move across the vastness—small clouds form above the basins where evaporation is strongest, and every few days a real rain will fall, a sudden, drenching downpour that fills the pores to overflowing and sends thin, braided streams of water down the low gradients, carving nothing, but feeding the slow accretion with new dissolved minerals. The streams are short-lived, absorbed back into the crust within hours, leaving only a tracery of damp that darkens the pale stone for a while.

This is a total environment, entirely built, down to the bedrock—if bedrock there is, it lies so deep beneath the accumulated strata of living construction that it might as well be another world. The bio-stone is not a veneer; it is the landscape, hundreds of meters thick in places, its lower layers dead and compressed into a dense, marble-like matrix that still holds the ghost-patterns of ancient ridges. The whole thing is a single entity, or a multiplicity so closely intertwined that the distinction loses meaning: a symbiosis of microbial life, fungal mycelia, and mineral-precipitating archaea that together form a continent-scale organism in the shape of a plain. It is, in its entirety, a vast, low, hot, damp, living machine without moving parts, constructing itself out of air and water and the dissolved stone of whatever world-fragment it first anchored to so long ago.

And it is beautiful, in a way that requires you to slow your perceptions to its pace. The play of light across the patterned ridges as the sun wheels over; the sudden diamond flash of a thousand droplet-lenses after a rain; the deep, resonant quiet that makes your own breathing sound like a storm. This is a place at its beginning, still raw, still under construction, but already so complete in its own logic that it seems to have always been here, making itself into tomorrow.
