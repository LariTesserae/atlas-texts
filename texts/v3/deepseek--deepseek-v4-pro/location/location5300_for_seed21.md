You come to it through a fault in the rock, a narrow cleft that funnels a breath of steam-saturated air so hot it feels like a second skin. The cleft widens, and suddenly you are inside a place that seems to have been cupped from the surrounding high-altitude desert by a half-forgotten hand—a roughly circular depression no more than sixty paces across, walled by cliffs of freshly fractured obsidian that glisten with perpetual condensation. All around, the high plateau stretches away in a scrub-and-scree monotony, but here, in this small, sealed bowl, the world flips into something that does not belong to any elevation or latitude you can name.

The first thing you notice is the humidity. At three thousand metres, the air should be thin and desiccating; instead, it is dense and gluey with moisture, clinging to your throat, beading on your skin. The source is a ring of vents that pucker the base of the cliffs—cracks and fissures exhaling steady columns of vapour that rise and curl like inverted waterfalls. The heat is geothermal, volcanic in origin, but it carries a strange, flat mineral tang that tastes of ozone and something else, something faintly organic, like the breath of an enormous animal. The vents themselves are not natural in shape: they are too regularly spaced, their mouths lipped with what looks like pearlescent ceramic, worn and chipped as though they have been here for millennia. These are scattered, like afterthoughts, a few of them collapsed and others half-choked with ferny growth.

The terrain is a study in sharpness and youth. The floor of the bowl is a jumble of angular boulders and shards of glassy basalt, the edges so crisp you could cut yourself just by looking at them. No gentle rounding from centuries of water—the place feels as though it had been shattered into existence only recently, perhaps by a seismic event that tore the peak apart and then was immediately frozen in time by the aggressive heat and moisture that now attack it from beneath. Between the boulders, the ground is a thin, black, mineral soil, perpetually wet, almost a slurry in places where steam condenses and drips back down the obsidian walls. Small pools, perfectly circular and suspiciously identical in diameter, dot the floor—each one a lens of scalding-clear water fed by a gentle upwelling, and each ringed by a thick, gelatinous mat of algae that seems to pulse with a low, internal bioluminescence, a soft blue-green that waxes and wanes in no pattern you can discern.

Vegetation is lush but, as the parameters suggest, sparse—there is a great deal of space between the living things. It thrives in isolated, fiercely competitive clumps. Thick, rattan-like vines, studded with thorns that weep a dark, oily sap, loop across the boulders in lazy arcs, their leaves broad and heavily waxed, a deep crimson verging on black. Here and there, a single, tall stalk—like a yucca but scaled up monstrously—sprouts from a crevice, its crown a burst of fleshy, translucent fronds that snap shut like a startled moth when a droplet of water falls on them from above. Over by the largest pool, a stand of what might be fungus communicates in slow motion: pale, shelf-like bodies layered one over the other, each exhaling a fine mist of spores that shimmer with an iridescent, metallic glitter. The spore mist is one of the only things that moves with any sense of purpose: it drifts in low, flat clouds that spiral slowly inward toward the centre of the bowl, then rise suddenly, as if caught in an invisible updraft, and disperse over the cliff tops. There’s a rhythm to it, a breath-cycle that runs on a fifteen-minute loop, and it’s oddly synchronised with subtle shifts in the temperature of the pools. The whole place is dynamic, a collection of processes ticking away in a quiet, watchful manner.

And it is mostly quiet. The pervasive sound is a low, liquid mutter—the drip of condensation, the sigh of steam from the vents, the faint, glassy clink of cooling rock. But beneath it, almost below the threshold of hearing, you can detect a steady, regular pulse, like the tolling of a distant bell filtered through miles of water. The sound does not travel so much as it arrives already in your skull, a throb that you feel in your molars. Occasionally, there is a sharper note: a vine, heated too quickly by a stray gust of steam, snaps like a whip-crack against the rock; the fungus releases its spore burst with a soft, wet exhalation, almost a moan. Then silence reasserts itself, heavy and patient.

Fauna is so rare as to be more like a rumour. You might glimpse a tail-like tendril slipping into a pool as you turn your head, or see a scatter of tiny, three-toed prints in the mineral slurry that vanish at the edge of a boulder, as if their maker simply sublimated. Once, you are certain you see something akin to a dragonfly—but its wings are a tessellated lattice of fibrous crystal, and it makes no sound as it hovers, perfectly still for a full minute, before blinking out of existence. Nothing here seems to eat anything else in a recognizable predator-prey way; rather, the ecology seems to run on a direct conversion of geothermal heat and the strange mineral brew in the water. The organisms appear to be partly silicon-based, or perhaps something more exotic: the vines leave patterns of charged particles on your skin when you brush past them, and the algae mats shift colour in response to your presence, as if reading your bioelectric field.

The scattered marks of artifice are the key to the weirdness. Pockets of the far-future tech are half-embedded in the walls and floor. Protruding from a mass of obsidian near the northern cliff is a perfectly smooth, seemingly metallic rod, no thicker than your thumb, that vibrates with a frequency too high to hear but strong enough to make the air shimmer around it. It casts no shadow. Nearby, partially buried in the slurry, is a flat panel of a substance that is neither metal nor glass, covered in what looks like a shifting, liquid display of geometrical equations that dissolve and reform as you watch. The panel is still functioning, if that’s the word for it; it feeds off the ambient heat and moisture, or maybe off something less tangible, and its purpose seems to be a quiet, ongoing conversation with something far away and indifferent. Once, you find a small, round object—like a white pebble, but impossibly light—that hovers at knee-height when you set it down, remaining exactly where you place it in the air, spinning slowly. These artifacts are not ruins; they feel like discarded tools, or perhaps monitoring stations, left behind by intelligences that have moved on to scales of engineering we can’t fathom—nanoscale or cosmic, a tech that has woven itself into the local physics and now just hums along, barely noticing our presence.

The light in the bowl is a normal, bright mountain daylight, filtered through a thin layer of high cloud that is slowly being pulled apart by the rising steam. It gives the obsidian a wet, shark-skin gleam and catches on the metallic spores so that they sparkle like mica. As the afternoon wears on, the shadows sharpen, and the temperature climbs further—now approaching conflagratory—and the whole place feels like a small, contained argument between the planet’s deep heat and the cold, thin sky. A tiny, cathedral-scale ecosystem with its own logic, its own time, and its own strange, inscrutable sentience in the way it waits.
