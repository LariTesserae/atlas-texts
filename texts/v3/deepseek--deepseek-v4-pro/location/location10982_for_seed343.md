The place is not so much a landscape as a slow exhalation of green, draped across a body of stone that has forgotten its own youth. You stand at the edge of a chasm that is more a memory of a canyon, its walls worn to gentle undulations by time beyond measure. The drop below is immense—a thousand feet at least, maybe more—yet the air is so thick with drifting spores and mist that the bottom is a suggestion, a rumor of darker green. The temperature is warm, a steady, enveloping warmth that feels like breath on your skin, and everything glistens faintly with condensation. There is no rain, not now, but the air itself is a wet kiss, and in the hollows of the stone, pockets of collected dew wink like scattered mirrors.

The stone is not bare. It hasn’t been bare since the world was new. A continuous pelt of vegetation—mosses, liverworts, crustose lichens, and stranger things—clings to every surface, so thick and integrated that the rock beneath is only a rumor. The entire chasm wall is a single living tapestry, a vertical meadow that shimmers in gradients of jade, olive, and emerald. This is the vegetation: not trees, not bushes, but a primordial mat that has become the substrate itself. It is feet thick in places, a spongy, breathing rind that gives slightly underfoot, as if the whole cliff face were a sleeping beast. Roots, if they can be called that, weave through the moss like veins, anchoring the mass to stone that no longer sees light.

And yet, the density is a paradox. The space is nearly empty. The chasm is so wide, the sky so vast above, that the living wall on the far side is a hazy stripe of color. Between you and it, the air is a cathedral of nothingness. A few immense forms punctuate the void—not trees in the conventional sense, but colossal columns of compacted vegetation, like ancient termite mounds sculpted from moss and time, rising from the depths to pierce the mist. They are spaced hundreds of yards apart, their surfaces pocked with hollows and ledges where ferns cascade in slow-motion waterfalls. These towers are the architecture of the place, and they might be plants, or they might be the detritus of a million years of growth compacted into pillars. All living mass here has become one thing.

The sound is overwhelming, a constant pressure against the eardrums. It comes not from one source but from everywhere: a shrill, piping chorus that rises and falls in waves, punctuated by deep, booming calls that vibrate in your chest. The fauna is the ruling force. The air is thick with flying things—not birds, but something more like winged frogs, with membranous skin stretched between elongated digits, their iridescent bodies catching the light as they wheel and dive. They are the size of small dogs, and they gather in swirling clouds around the vegetal towers, their collective chirping a needle-sharp treble. Below them, clinging to the moss walls, are creatures like flattened geckos with far too many legs, each one singing a percussive, clicking rhythm. In the deeper shadows, something much larger moves: a slow, pendulous shape that swings from anchor points of thick vine, its body a shaggy mass of symbiotic moss, and its call is a low, resonant hoot that echoes for miles. The ecosystem is in constant, frantic motion—predator and prey, mating display and territorial fight—all enacted in the empty air with the chasm walls as stadium seating.

Scattered across the broader region, on the rare ledges and in the entrances to shallow caves, are signs of a different presence. Small, low shelters made of packed moss and bent sticks lean against the cliff, their openings so narrow that only a child could easily enter. They are old but not ancient; the moss has grown over their edges, softening their outlines. Inside, you might find a flaked stone knife, a coil of twisted fiber, the charred remains of a fire pit dug into the living floor. The builders are gone—or perhaps they are still here, smaller than you’d expect, watching from the shadows with eyes that reflect the faint bioluminescent glow that pulses from the deeper crevices at twilight.

The elevation is dizzying. You are on a high plateau, a tableland that ends abruptly in this chasm, and behind you the terrain rises into smooth, bald domes of stone that were once mountains. They are so ancient they have been eroded to nubbins, their flanks polished by wind and time, now covered in the same unbroken moss. The sky is a pale, warm gray, the light normal and diffuse, as if through a permanent softbox of high cloud. The air smells of wet earth and sweet decay, with a faint animal musk underneath.

This is a place that has reached its final form, a steady-state ecology that has persisted for eons. The active cycles of weather—the daily misting, the occasional thicker rain that seeps through the moss and drips into hidden aquifers—keep it damp but never flooded. The warmth is constant, the seasons barely a flicker. It is an open, regional expanse, a valley system of immense scale, where a traveler could walk for days across the springy plateau and encounter only the same endless vista of moss, the same distant towers, the same deafening chorus of life. The weirdness is subtle: the way the moss pulses with a slow bioluminescence when disturbed, the way the flying creatures occasionally form patterns in the air that seem too deliberate to be random, the ancient, flattened faces carved into a single boulder by some forgotten hand, now half-erased by lichen. It is unusual but not alien, a place where life has simply taken a slightly different path, and then held still for millions of years.
