The city breathes. Not the breath of life—there is no green thing here, no leaf, no blade of grass—but a slow, mechanical respiration from vents in the pavement and the flanks of factory husks, pushing streams of warm, wet air into the already saturated atmosphere. The light is a perpetual twilight, though it is midday: a low ceiling of ochre cloud seals the basin, trapping the heat and the moisture, so the air gleams faintly, as if the whole world were a tarnished mirror. Everything is damp. Not with standing water—though there are pools—but a pervasive cling of condensation. The metal railings sweat. The concrete walls weep a fine, oily sheen. The ground underfoot is a patchwork of cracked asphalt and exposed aggregate, slick with a film of water that never quite evaporates, only redistributes itself in the sluggish convection of the hot, heavy air.

This is a city of vast, open emptiness. The density parameter is near zero: you could walk for a kilometre down the centre of what must once have been a major thoroughfare and encounter nothing but your own reflection in the puddles. The buildings rise on either side, brick and steel and glass—most of the glass long since fallen into the street as granular pebbles—but their scale is monumental, their spacing generous. A grid of districts spreads across the low-lying plain, a valley nudged between old, worn hills that are themselves bare, scoured to their mineral bones. The erosion here is old; the edges have been round for centuries. The city has settled into its own decay like an animal curling to sleep, its bones poking through but arranged in a pattern that suggests a long-accepted equilibrium.

Yet it is overwhelmingly inhabited. The fauna score is high, and the empty spaces are not empty at all when you shift your gaze to the smaller scale. The ground moves. A constant, murmuring flux of grey and brown bodies: rats, yes, but not the lean, nervous rats of other cities. These are sleek and fat, waddling in the gutters, their fur clumped with moisture, their movements unhurried. They are the keystone. Above them, roaches pour in slow streams over every surface, a living varnish that parts and reforms around the larger scavengers. The air is thick with insects too—not buzzing, for the soundscape is oddly muted—but clouds of midges and small, silent moths that dance in the eddies of heat from the vents. On the higher ledges, where the dim light is just enough to see by, pigeons huddle in dense, breathing mats, their coos a soft undertone you only notice when you stop and listen. There are cats, feral and ghostly, slinking with that peculiar, low-bellied patience, their hunting mostly unseen. And sometimes, in the wider plazas where the broken pavement has subsided into shallow, permanent marshland, you’ll hear the plop of an amphibian—frogs, pale and sluggish in the heat, or something like newts that dwell in the culverts. The fauna is not just present; it is the city’s true population. It is the dynamic element, the ecosystem in motion. The rats eat the roaches; the cats eat the rats; the insects feed on the organic slurry that oozes from the old drainage systems, a rich soup of industrial byproduct and animal decay. The cycle is perpetual, and it is quiet.

Quiet, because the animals have little call to be loud. The ambient noise is a low, industrial hum: somewhere in the city’s heart, engines still turn, turbines still spin on geothermal or residual fuel, producing a subsonic throb you feel more in your chest than hear. The factories—great windowless blocks of corrugated metal and pitted concrete—are not dead; they run on automatic cycles, stamping out products no one collects, or regulating flows of steam and brine through pipes that vein the earth. The sound is a deep, mechanical purr, overlaid by the whisper of condensing steam, the occasional drip of water from a broken gutter, the soft scuffle of a rat’s claws on a metal stair. There are no bird songs, no rustling leaves, no human voices. It is a hush that fills the open space.

The hot, wet, dim conditions have prevented plant life. There is no soil to speak of—only mineral deposits, flaking rust, and the alkaline crust that forms where puddles repeatedly dry and wet again. The built environment is total, a sealed mineral landscape. Where bare earth shows through cracked foundations, it is baked clay, hard as terracotta, colonized not by roots but by pale fungal threads that trace delicate, branching patterns in the dark. That’s the closest to vegetation: a thin, almost invisible network of mycelia that lives on the waste, breaking down what the animals leave. It smells faintly of yeast and wet iron, a scent that hangs in the warm air.

Walk deeper, and the scale opens further. The city is built on a gentle slope that trends toward a central basin of old industry: smokestacks that no longer smoke but radiate a visible heat-shimmer, cooling towers that exhale perpetual plumes of vapour into the choking sky. Here the wetness intensifies. The streets become canals, shallow and stagnant, reflecting the dim ochre glow in oily rainbows. The water is warm to the touch, fed by upwelling springs from the geothermal gradient beneath the valley. It laps against the foundations, wearing them smooth. The erosion is mature: every edge is blunted, every iron beam is swollen with rust-scale thick as bark, every brick has the soft, rounded look of river stone. The city has been here a long time, long enough for the weather, the water, and the animals to reshape it into something almost organic, a reef of steel and concrete.

And yet, it is normal. There is no magic here, no alien strangeness. This is a place that could exist, a forgotten industrial settlement on some hot, humid Earth, where the people left and the machines kept running, and the animals came to fill the hollows. It is dynamic: the clouds above condense and release a fine, warm drizzle that lasts for hours, then pauses, then begins again. The water level in the canals rises and falls imperceptibly with some distant tide or subterranean rhythm. The rats have their territories, their breeding seasons, their sudden die-offs that replenish the soup. The cats hunt the sick and the slow. The pigeons stir from the ledges in silent flocks, wheeling in the dimness when a cat leaps, then resettle. It is a landscape in motion, but a slow motion, the pace of hot, wet, heavy air moving across an open, empty city slung low between old hills. The place is itself: a settled, weathered, mineral-and-animal town that breathes steam and whispers with the many feet of the unseen.
