The first thing you notice is the sound: a chittering, hissing, splashing chorus that fills every inch of the vast dark, so constant and layered that it becomes almost a silence of its own—a heavy, breathing pressure against the eardrums. It never stops, never shifts in pitch or volume, a steady roar of life held in perfect, frozen equilibrium.

You are deep below the surface, in a wound of the world that opened recently. The ceiling above is lost in shadow, but here and there a faint, cold light seeps from the walls—patches of phosphorescent minerals glowing a sickly blue-green, enough to trace the sharp angles of the place. This is a young chasm, a collapse cavern still raw from its birth. The rock faces are jagged, freshly shattered, unweathered by time or water. Shards of basalt and obsidian lie in angular fans across the floor, edges so keen they might cut. There are no round pebbles, no soft banks of sediment, only fracture planes and abrupt drops, as if the earth cracked open only moments ago—and then stopped, frozen.

The space is immense. You could lose a town in here. What passes for a floor slopes away into a series of broad terraces, vast and mostly empty, the stone between features smooth and bare. The density is sparse: great, echoing emptiness separates the scattered landmarks—a cluster of house-sized boulders here, a deep pool of still water there, a lone rib of rock rising like a knife blade. There is no clutter, no debris save the original fall. Everything has space to breathe, and the dim light pools in that emptiness, tapering into gloom.

Water is present but elusive. The air is damp, cool on the skin, a perpetual clamminess that clings to stone and flesh alike. Condensation beads on every surface, dripping in slow, fat drops that fall with a plink lost amidst the animal din. There are no streams, no flowing rivers, but scattered across the terraces lie pockets of water—deep, clear pools that gather in natural basins, their surfaces glassy and unrippled except where the creatures disturb them. Some are mere puddles, others large enough to swallow a house, all fed by the unending drip from above. The temperature is a steady cool, not quite cold, like the chill of a root cellar, unchanging and damp.

Vegetation is sparse, clinging to life on the margins. Pale, translucent ferns and colorless mosses grow in isolated tufts near the pools, their fronds thin as tissue, barely moving in the still air. Fungal growths—puffballs and shelf fungi, grey and white—stud the dampest walls, glowing faintly with their own soft luminescence. But they are few, scattered, wide gaps of bare rock between each colony. The plants do not define this place; they are incidental.

What defines this place is the fauna. Overwhelming, teeming, crawling, flapping, splashing life. The cavern is a cauldron of animals, all of it pale, eyeless, and loud. The pools are thick with pale, translucent fish and blind salamanders, their movements a constant, swirling dance. On the rocks, colonies of pale crabs scuttle sideways, their shells clicking, and insects with long, thread-like antennae crawl over the stone in search of fungal spores. In the air, leather-winged creatures—not quite bats, something older, with needle-toothed snouts and chirping calls—wheel in the dimness, navigating by sound and the faintest glow of bioluminescent patches on their chests. The ground is alive with soft, slug-like things that leave glistening trails, and centipede forms yard-long that weave between the boulders. And everywhere, the noise: the high-pitched chittering of the flyers, the deep croaks of unseen amphibians, the splash and plop of bodies hitting water, the scrape of chitin on stone. It is a constant, unceasing roar, so pervasive that it feels like the voice of the stone itself.

Nothing here changes. The ecosystem has reached a perfect, timeless balance. The same number of flyers are born as die each moment; the crabs molt and grow old and pass into the next shell with no net growth in population. The pools are never more or less full, the drip constant, the temperature unwavering. There are no seasons, no day or night—only the eternal dim twilight of the minerals. The noise never fades, never builds. It is a frozen equilibrium, a snapshot of a world that will never age.

For all its strangeness to surface eyes, there is little weirdness here in the context of this world. This is a normal cave ecosystem, just pushed to an extreme of density and noise. The creatures are simple, primitive. Some, the larger ones perhaps, show signs of rudimentary tool use: near the pools, you might find the cracked shells of large mollusks, smashed open with a specific stone that is carried from elsewhere and left with an almost ritualistic precision. A stick stripped of bark might be wedged into a crevice to fish out grubs. But no structure rises, no artifact stays. The tech level is just a whisper, a hint of manipulation, as primitive as the place itself.

Walk through this dim, loud hall. The ground is sharp, the air cool and wet on your face. You are a trespasser in a world that has never seen the sun, never known a changing wind, and never will. The life around you does not react to your presence except to flow around your feet, a river of chitin and slime and pale flesh that closes again after you pass, leaving no trace. The equilibrium holds. The sounds swallow you. And the vast, sparse, sharp young cavern stretches on, unchanging, into the endless, echoing dark.
