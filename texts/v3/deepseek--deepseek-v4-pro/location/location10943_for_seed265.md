You come to know the place first by the soft, constant pressure of water against your skin. Not falling, exactly—the sky is a low, featureless void without source or end—but condensing out of the black air as a fine warm mist that beads on every surface and trickles down the sharp planes of the world. The moisture moves with a patience that feels geological, each droplet following a path etched narrow and fresh through dark stone. The breath you draw feels heavy, full, as if the atmosphere itself were only half gas, half liquid. The temperature is a steady embrace, the kind of warmth that blurs the boundary between body and environment until you forget where you end and this place begins.

As your eyes adjust—though adjust implies a change, and here the darkness is absolute, your sight relying on something other than light—the terrain resolves into an endless mosaic of interlocking basins and low ridges, all crafted from a mineral too smooth and too dark to name. It resembles basalt but carries a faint metallic sheen, like cooled slag from some impossible furnace. Every surface is fresh, sharp-edged, unscoured by time. The ridges rise no more than a few meters, their crests knife-like, and they divide the land into a labyrinth of shallow catchments, each cupping a lens of black water so clear and still it replicates the void above. There is no true horizon, just a gradual fade where the geometry of the near field dissolves into a more distant hum of presence—the sense that this fractured plain repeats for miles, a regional-scale tessellation of wet, new-formed land.

Your footsteps, when you take them, make a sound that is swallowed almost instantly: a thin, metallic *click* of contact, then nothing. The quiet here is a layered thing. It is not the absence of noise but a baseline so low and so dense that it feels like a substance, a weight in the ears. Into it, at irregular intervals, smaller sounds intrude—a muted clank, a hiss of steam, a low rhythmic thudding that travels up through the stone and into your bones. These are the voices of the scattered remnants. You encounter one soon enough: a single squat cylinder of riveted iron, as tall as your waist, weeping a slow pulse of lubricating oil into the water at its base. It hums to itself, a frequency just below hearing, and its surface is warm. There is no sign of what it powered, no other structure near. Just this patient machine, persisting in the dark, obeying a logic that has outlived its context.

The engine, like the others you will find, is half-swallowed by the dense life of the place. For all the water, the vegetation is scarce—a scattering of wiry forms that erupt from cracks in the fresh rock. They are not plants in any comfortable sense. Some are rigid, jointed like iron reeds, their tips glowing with a faint bioluminescence that comes and goes too slowly to use as light. Others are tangles of fine copper wire, curling and uncurling in a movement so gradual you can only perceive it by looking away and looking back. They do not photosynthesize; they seem instead to pull something from the moisture itself, or from the ambient heat, or from the low hum of the engines. But if the flora is sparse, the fauna is a saturated, layered, near-touching inverse. The basins and ridges are teeming with life that occupies every niche: the water, the air just above it, the crevices, the very interstices of existence.

Most of it is invertebrate, or built on body plans that mock the categories of biology. Translucent polyps cluster on the submerged edges of the ridges, their tentacles rippling in synchrony with a slow, internal contractile rhythm. They are packed so densely that they form a living skin over the rock, each individual touching its neighbors, and beneath them you can see the polished stone still gleaming, unchanged by any erosion. Hard-shelled things—shaped like crabs but with too many legs, or like beetles big as your hand but with shells of what appears to be machined aluminum—crawl over one another in layers, their movements deliberate, unhurried. Their friction produces a fine, constant rustle that blends into the quiet until you almost mistake it for the sound of your own blood. In the water, something large and segmented hangs suspended, its body wrapped around a core of soft blue light. It breathes through gills that are unmistakably radiator fins, and as it exhales, a tiny puff of steam rises and is immediately absorbed by the mist.

This is the abundance: a saturated density of organisms that seem to have evolved or been assembled to exploit the very breakdown of standard physical law. The weirdness here is not an occasional glitch but a foundational condition. You notice it in the way the iron reeds sometimes flicker, existing simultaneously several inches from their prior position without any intervening motion. In the way your own shadow, though there is no light source, sometimes stretches briefly at an angle that does not match the contours of the ground. In the long, slow loops of time: a droplet of water might hang in the air for ten full heartbeats before deciding to fall; a distant ridge might, over the course of an hour, subtly rearrange its geometry, the sharp edges softening and re-forming in a different configuration, as if the land itself were breathing through a sequence of slow, semi-solid states. This is a young landscape not because it is recently born, but because it is perpetually re-emerging, reality failing to settle on a final form. The erosion is stalled at the initial sharp stage; the world has never aged.

Human influence, or whatever intelligence resembles it, is scattered across this impossibility as a series of faint, melancholic artifacts. Beyond the oil-pumping cylinder, you find a spool of copper cable as thick as your arm, uncoiled for a hundred yards across a basin, its endpoint plugged into a socket of fused sand. A little further, a structure: not a building, but a single wall of corrugated steel, standing unsupported in a shallow pool, its edges sharp as the day it was cut. Rivulets of condensation slide down its fluted surface and drip onto a cluster of the polyp-things, which have grown over what might once have been a floor. There is a switch on the wall, a heavy industrial toggle, and when you test it, somewhere in the dark a solenoid clacks once and a brief, unbearably bright arc of electricity jumps between two unseen points, casting the whole basin in a momentary, shocking tableau of gleaming chitin and steam and eyes that reflect nothing familiar. Then the dark returns, deeper for the contrast.

The sense of scale is oceanic. Not of vertical vastness—the land is flat, the sky is low and close—but of horizontal unendingness, a territory that could swallow a town, a valley, and still keep going. The dense life, the scattered machines, the sharp wet rock, all repeat in diminishing perceptual clusters, variations on a theme, until they merge into a hum of presence at the edge of awareness. There is no day/night cycle, no seasonal shift. The dynamic is an almost imperceptible loop: the mist condenses, the water slowly migrates through the lattice of basins, the engines pump and hum, the life pulses and consumes and decays and re-forms, all in a rhythm whose basic unit might be the decade, or the century. The ground under your feet is warm, and if you stand still long enough, you can feel a faint, deep vibration, like a factory heartbeat, or a continent slowly thinking.

You stop by a larger pool, one that stretches far enough to lose its far shore in the dark. The surface is a perfect mirror. When you look down, you see not your reflection but a dense constellation of those soft blue lights—the radiator gills of the suspended swimmers, hanging just below the skin of the water, thousands of them, packed so densely they form a kind of galactic fog. One of the crab-things taps against your boot with a sound like a spanner on a pipe, then turns and ambles away into a crack. The quiet reasserts itself, the hiss of steam from a distant vent, the slow drip of reality resettling. The place goes on, patient and strange, a wet, warm, dark, dense, industrial, sharp-edged, slowly mutating ecology of the broken real. It is always just beginning. It is always full.
