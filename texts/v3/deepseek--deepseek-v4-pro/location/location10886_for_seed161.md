The settlement clings to the flat cap of a lone mesa, a thumb of ancient rock thrust thirteen thousand feet above the salt-pan desert below. There is no water here—no stream, no seep, no morning dew pooling in hollows. The ground is dry mineral dust, fine as talc, and the air smells of hot stone and something faintly resinous. Yet the mesa is choked with life, a dense, rustling, dripping wilderness of flesh-leaved plants that have never tasted liquid water.

The vegetation is a layered delirium of succulent forms: tree-like euphorbias with candelabra arms and waxy, photosynthetic bark rise forty feet, their crowns a tangle of leafless, milk-sapped fingers. Beneath them, plump rosettes of agave thrust blade-edges outward, and a waist-high mat of iceplant and creeping stonecrop sprawls over every inch of ground, their tiny, beadlike leaves packed so tightly that the soil is invisible. Lichens crust the rocks in ochre and bone-white, slow-breathing things that crack micron by micron over centuries. Everything is swollen with stored moisture—a botanical reservoir—and if a stem is cut, it bleeds a thick, clear ichor that tastes faintly of salt and minerals. The forest drinks from the clouds alone. Each afternoon, upwellings of warm air from the badlands condense into a thick fog that smothers the plateau. The plants comb the vapor with microscopic leaf-pores, the droplets beading on their smooth surfaces and rolling down to the root-crowns. There is no rain, no drainage, only this daily breath of mist, and the steady, patient gorging on atmospheric ghost-water.

The mesa top is small—barely four acres—and the world beyond its edge is a void. Sheer cliffs plummet into a haze of heat and distance, where the salt-fields glitter like a broken mirror. Erosion has had aeons to work. Once this was the core of a volcano, a plug of hard phonolite. The softer outer cone has long since vanished, swept away into the basin, and even the resistant rock has been scoured by wind and grit into smooth swells and hollows. The surface has a polished, skin-like texture, and everywhere are shallow basins—ancient potholes—where dust collects. Nothing is sharp; the landscape has been rubbed to a drowsy, settled state, a final form that will endure another million years with barely a change. This is a place that has forgotten how to be young.

In this cramped, sky-high jungle, the human presence is equally dense. The settlement—if it can be called a town—is a vertical maze of dwellings stacked and wedged among the euphorbias. Over generations, the inhabitants have carved chambers directly into the thick, corky trunks of the giant succulents without killing them, the living wood sealing around doorways as if embracing the intrusion. Between these, they have raised walls of stacked stone, dry-fitted, and roofed them with overlapping agave leaves, their fibers woven into a watertight thatch. Walkways of braided sinew and twisted fiber suspend from one level to the next; ladders of lashed cane lean against every surface. The architecture is purely additive, cellular, an organic growth of rooms and terraces that follows the logic of the forest. There are perhaps two hundred people here, living in a three-dimensional tangle where a neighbor’s hearth might be directly beneath your feet and a child’s sleeping loft directly overhead. The scale is that of a cathedral, but one whose naves are living corridors of green flesh and whose spires weep toxic latex.

Technology is of the simplest earth-and-hand sort. The people use stone adzes to shape the euphorbia wood, bone needles for sewing fiber-cloth, and fire-hardened sticks to scratch patterns into the living rock. They have no metal except the occasional nugget of native copper hammered into a blade, and they build no wheels, no pulleys. Their crafts are patient: basketry, cordage, the slow carving of a bowl that takes a lifetime. The only energy beyond muscle is the sun, diffused and dim.

For the light is perpetually filtered. The fog endures for hours, and even when it thins, the dense canopy casts everything below into a green-tinged twilight. The sky is a rumor. The sun appears as a white smear, then vanishes. Combined with the warm, steady air—the temperature rarely dips or spikes, hovering in a humid, 20-degree range—it creates a kind of sensuous, hushed grotto-atmosphere, though far from quiet.

Sound is the one thing that cannot be dampened. The mesa is cacophonous with fauna. Life here is small and multitudinous, adapted to the three-dimensional labyrinth. Finger-sized geckos chirp from every crevice, their calls overlapping into a creaky, metallic chorus. Flocks of nectar-feeding birds—dun-colored, needle-beaked—dart between inflorescences, their wing-beats a perpetual whir. The dominant noise is a throaty, sawing buzz: a species of flightless cricket the size of a child’s thumb that lives in the agave crowns, the males vibrating their abdomens to lure mates. The sound rises and falls in slow, tidal waves, a drone that can be felt through the soles of your feet. Mixed with the constant rustle of the plants themselves—the slight, grinding shift of their water-swollen tissues—and the moan of wind over the cliff edge, the ambient volume is that of a busy workshop. And yet the dynamic is glacial. The crickets have buzzed exactly this frequency for uncounted generations. The birds have nested in the same euphorbias since before the village was founded. The fog rolls in at the same hour, the plants grow imperceptibly, and the stone softens grain by grain. Nothing is sudden; everything is a slow, slow turning.

In the deepest part of the mesa, where the wind-carved hollows converge, there is a natural amphitheater. The rock walls rise, smooth and stained dark by lichens, and the crickets’ drone concentrates into a vibrating point. The villagers gather there at dusk, not for ritual exactly, but from some immemorial habit, and they sit while the fog pours over the rim and the light gilds the mist to a dirty amber. They sit and they listen, as their ancestors listened, to the sound of a world that has forgotten water but has never learned to thirst.
