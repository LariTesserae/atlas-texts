The hamlet clung to the inside of the bowl like a cluster of shelf fungi on a damp trunk. Above it, the rock walls rose nearly sheer, their planes so fresh they still held the glint of fracture—no rounded shoulders here, just edges keen as split slate, the mountain’s bones barely weathered. Mist wove continually through the upper crags, condensing into beads that dripped slow from every cranny, so that despite the cool air, everything held a slick of moisture. It wasn’t rain, exactly; more a perpetual exudation, as if the stone itself sweated.

In that high pocket, the vegetation had gone riot on the wet. Mosses padded every surface—trunks, rooftops, the gaps between stones—thick as pelts, in olive and bruise-purple and a startling lime that seemed to glow in the dimness. Ferns unrolled from fissures, their fronds spanning wider than a person’s outstretched arms. There were flowering things too, pale and waxy, their petals translucent as onion skin, cupping tiny reservoirs of water. The few spindly trees that had found root angled outward over the drop, their bark cloaked in ruffled lichen, their canopies interlaced so densely that daylight arrived only as a sifted grey-green, like light sunk through lake water. The whole hollow felt submerged.

Dwelling-places were sparse among the growth—a dozen at most, scattered around the bowl’s curve with generous swathes of wet greenery between them. They were raised on stilts of unbarked timber, lashed with strips of hide, roofed in overlapping plates of wood that had gone silver and bloomed with moss. Ladders leaned up to doorways, and from the platforms, walkways of split logs spanned gap to gap, suspended over a floor of ferns. Nothing was nailed; everything was bound, pegged, or balanced in a logic of pressure and fit. The technology of a people who had no metal hotter than a campfire, but who understood the give of wood and the grip of wet rope.

And everywhere—everywhere—the others were moving.

The fauna of the hollow came in an overwhelming tide, a relentless simmer of bodies. Small furred things with too many eyes skittered along the walkway rails, their paws making no sound on the damp wood. They moved in synchronized pulses, whole populations flowing from one side of the settlement to the other like liquid shadow, their tiny mouths perpetually testing the air. Winged things, not quite birds, clung upsidedown to the underside of the canopy, their membrane wings folded, their sonar—if it was sonar—emitting at frequencies that registered only as a faint prickling behind the human ear. Serpentine forms slid through the aerial roots and dangling moss, limbless and slow, their scales a perfect match for the wet bark. Insects clouded the air in silent, drifting veils: midge-like, but larger, their abdomens pulsing with bioluminescent chemistry, cold green and blue glows that winked in the perpetual twilight. The strangeness lay in their silence. With so much life, the air should have been a riot of calls, rustles, chirps. But the quiet was nearly absolute—a thick, listening quiet, broken only by the faint plink of condensation drops hitting pooled water, and a low, almost subsonic hum that might have been the collective breath of the place. When a moth the size of a hand passed by, its wings beat without a whisper, as if sound itself had been absorbed into the moss.

The settlement’s inhabitants moved through this thrumming quiet with careful, practiced economy. They had skin the color of damp wood, and their tools were blades of knapped obsidian and woven baskets sealed with tree resin. They knew which insects to harvest by the color of their glow, and where the serpentine things nested, and when the tide of furred shadows would shift. Watchers sat on the highest platforms, not looking outward for enemies, but inward, reading the choreography of the fauna as one reads weather. Because here, the animals were the weather. Their migrations through the hollow cycled with a clockwork complexity: every few hours, the winged things would drop from the canopy in a silent cascade, spiral through the mist, and then rise again, and the humans would move their cookfires indoors because the drift of wing-scale could poison a broth. Predation happened in plain sight, but softly—a furred thing would be plucked from a rail by a pale, spindly arm that emerged from a fern, and the victim never cried out, only stiffened and was gone, while the rest of the tide continued unruffled.

At the bowl’s lowest point, a shallow basin of rock collected the seep, forming a pool no wider than a hut. It was the one place the fauna did not crowd. The water lay perfectly still, dark as polished obsidian, reflecting the faint glimmers from above. The people drew from it with gourds carved with spiral patterns, and they never, ever made a sound at the water’s edge. It was said—or not said, but shown—that the pool was the mouth of something, and if you dropped a stone, the entire hollow would hear it, and the quiet would break in ways that could not be mended.

So the silence held. The mist drifted. The sheer walls seeped. And the life, overwhelming and numinous, moved in its endless, muted dance around the scattered platforms of a hamlet that had become just another organism in the wet, breathing web.
