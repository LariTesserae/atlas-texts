The first thing you notice is the coolness—not a biting cold, but a steady, gentle leaching of warmth that reminds you the sun has never touched this place. The air is so dry it tastes of salt and metal, of old lightning and grinding stone. It’s dim, a perpetual twilight cast by rows of amber luminescent globes that hang on braided cables, their hum a near-subconscious frequency, like a held breath.

You are in a shaft, but that word is too simple. It’s a wound in the world, a vertical tear through crystalline geology that glitters with unweathered edges. Every surface is sharp, freshly fractured, the planes intersecting at harsh angles still dusted with a fine, sparkling powder. No water runs here, no rain falls; there isn’t even humidity to soften the contours. This place is newborn, a scar still healing, and in its extreme elevation—a pocket hidden within the highest needle of a salt-plain spire—it feels like standing on the edge of creation.

The shaft is not empty. It is dense, almost claustrophobically layered. Structures cluster on the walls like barnacles on a ship’s hull: pods, spiraling domes, multi-faceted cubes, all fused from the same crystalline minerals but laced with rust-pocked iron, brass gears, and windows of thick, oil-smeared glass. They are stacked, suspended, cantilevered in a vertical labyrinth of walkways, rope bridges, and rattling cable-car tracks that skim the sheer faces. This is a settlement, an urban pocket built into the void, its architecture an archaeology of continuous, chaotic renewal—older structures swallowed by newer ones, each generation of industry layered on the last without erasing it.

The hum beneath your feet is not the earth. It’s engines. Deep in the shaft’s throat, massive turbines turn, driven by pressure differentials rather than steam, their working fluids a non-aqueous gas that hisses from valves in sudden, whispering jets. Pistons clank in distant rhythm; electric motors whine from the darkness overhead, powering conveyor belts that transport something you can’t quite see. The air smells of ozone, hot grease, and a faint, sweet-sour tang—the exhalation of a chemistry that doesn’t involve water. This is industrial, mechanized, electrified; a factory-city built in a place where day and night are meaningless.

And yet it is mostly quiet. The sounds are isolated, absorbed by the crystalline walls and the web-like growths that coat them. Those growths are the closest thing to vegetation: a pale, knotted lattice that pulses with a slow, internal rhythm, neither plant nor fungus but something simpler, a biological machine. It’s warm to the touch, and it edges every crevice, spreading into new cracks as they form. It feeds on the mineral dust and the faint electrical fields, and it, in turn, is tended.

The fauna are abundant. They move everywhere in the dim light: scuttling things with chitinous bodies polished like black glass, ranging from the size of a thumbnail to the heft of a small dog. They navigate the vertical world with ease, their too-many legs finding purchase on the sheer crystal planes. They are silent except for the click of claws, the occasional rustle of antennae, and a collective, soft sigh that seems to breathe through the entire shaft when the machines idle. They swarm the fungal webs, grooming them, perhaps eating, perhaps communicating. Some of them carry tiny, gleaming components—gears or circuits—and weave them into the lattice. The boundary between animal, machine, and ecosystem is blurry here.

The atmosphere is turbulent, hyperactive in slow motion. This is a place of instability: the shaft is still settling, still cracking. Without warning, a sharp report echoes up the shaft as a new fracture splits the wall, and instantly the web begins to ooze into the wound, the black-shelled fauna rush to the site, and a swarm of small, drone-like things—or are they winged animals?—descend from the gloom with a whir of membrane wings. They lay down a track of recycled composite material, bridging the gap even as the dust still hangs in the air. The city adapts, constantly, chaotically, a bazaar of metabolic and mechanical energy.

There is a subtle weirdness, an uncanny quality that sit uneasily. The walls shimmer with iridescence at the edge of vision. The air sometimes distorts, not from heat but from something else—a pressure trick, a chemical mirage. The inhabitants, if there are any besides the fauna, are never seen; perhaps they are the fauna, perhaps they are the city itself, a hive mind of chitin, crystal, and engine. Or maybe they are too small to perceive, a microbial civilization whose works are these vast structures. The scale is hidden: from outside, this entire world would fit in a palm’s breadth, a single dark flaw on a salt pinnacle under a starless sky. But inside it is everything—a pocket universe of dim light, cool breath, sharp edges, and the never-ending hum of a dry, abundant, turbulent existence.
