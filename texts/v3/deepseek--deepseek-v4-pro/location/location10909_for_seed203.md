The world opens downward.

You are standing on what your body still wants to call ground—a woven mat of roots so wide and dense it has its own topography, its own shallow hills and sudden drop-offs where the weave parts. The mat is not soil. It is living, or partially living, a composite of pale fibers the color of bone marrow, some dry and brittle, some turgid with a cold sap that beads on the surface in the damp. Every surface, in fact, gathers moisture. Not rain; there is no sky to rain from. Condensation. The air is cold, just above freezing, a chill that creeps through the weave underfoot and wraps around your ankles, and the moisture comes out of the air itself, fogging on every strand, collecting into pendulous drops that fall only when they grow heavy enough to lose their grip. The sound of a drop falling is a small, bright tick, and then a long silence before the next.

The light is pervasive and sourceless, a white-green luminosity that seems to emanate from the root mat, from the distant walls, from the far-flung webworks overhead. It is *bright*—painfully bright until your eyes adjust, as if noon were trapped underground and had nowhere to go. There are no shadows unless you make them yourself with your own body, and then the shadow is sharp-edged, crisp, the mark of a world that has not yet learned to soften its angles. Everything here is young. The root-braids have splintered ends, fresh breaks oozing a clear, slow fluid that freezes into glassy beads in the cold. The walls—when you can glimpse them through the lattice—are not rock in any ordinary sense. They are a compressed tangle of stems, leaves, fruiting bodies, all crushed into a solid mass but still visibly plant, with the flat, sharp fracture planes of something recently snapped. This world has not been worn down by time. It is in the state of first formation, a place barely born and already dying in pieces, and the death feeds the next surge of growth.

The scale is the first thing that breaks your sense of proportion. The cavern—if it can be called a cavern—is not a room. It is a space large enough to hold weather. A slow river of air moves through it, a cold and steady breeze that smells of green things and wet stone, though there is no stone. The breeze travels a mile or more as it crosses from one side of your perch to another, and it carries with it shreds of vapor, localized fogs that wander like translucent creatures. You can see for what must be leagues, and the view is not of a floor and ceiling but of a three-dimensional forest turned inside out. The true majority of the world is empty volume. Roots and vines and fern-like fronds span across it, bridging from one wall to another in catenary curves, but the spaces between them are vast—chasms of air in which whole cities could drift. That is the strange quality of this place: it is at once entirely made of vegetation and nearly void. The plants *are* the architecture, but they are architecture drawn in the sparsest possible lines, a filamentous skeleton holding up the emptiness. The density is so low that you feel a kind of vertigo; there is almost nothing solid per cubic mile, and yet what is solid is all plant, and what is plant covers everything that exists.

And it is *cold*. The cold seeps into the sap, slows it to a viscous crawl, so that the plants grow in a kind of suspended urgency. Here and there, pockets of liquid water gather in the cups of enormous waxy leaves, and these pools have a thin skin of ice that cracks at the lightest touch. The damp is not enough to be wet; it is a perpetual clinging humidity that beads on skin, on tool handles, on the scattered remnants of something not plant.

For there are built things here. Scattered, small, and utterly abandoned. On the broader root platforms, where the weave is tight enough to bear weight without sagging, you find them: circles of stacked stone, hearths that hold no ash but only the tiny fossilized fronds that fell into them and were never cleaned. Tools of a sort—a hand-axe chipped from a knot of dense rootwood so hard it rings when struck, fibers twisted into cordage, the remains of a shelter woven from still-living vines that have now outgrown their intended shape and spiraled upward into a wild funnel. The technology is primitive, early-tool-use, pre-metal, but it is not crude. There is a sense of careful craft, of people who understood the plants and made their lives in the interstices of the green. And then they left, or died, or something took them, because now the structures are being consumed. New growth pierces the stone circles, gently prying them apart. The tools lie half-embedded, slowly sinking into the mat. The marks are faint, and growing fainter, and there is no one here to read them.

The only sound is the tick of water, the creak of a stretching fiber, and—if you listen carefully—a faint, whispery rustle that never stops. That rustle is the turbulence. The dynamic of this place is bizarre: it is a storm that never breaks. The vegetation is in constant, chaotic motion, but at a scale that makes it seem almost geological. A vine as thick as an ancient oak will be inching across a gap, and you can see it move if you watch long enough. New shoots lance out from nodes, growing visibly in the space of minutes, then brown and wither and snap. The snaps you never hear because the pieces fall into the void and the sound is swallowed by distance. The growth is Solaris-like, mindless creation and destruction, a churn of living matter that follows no seasonal rhythm but pulses in fits and starts. Sometimes a whole section of root mat will tremble and fracture along fresh lines, sending a rain of fibrous debris into the abyss, and you realize that what you stand on is not stable, not permanent, but a momentary coagulation in a liquid process. The cold keeps it from being frantic; it is a slow storm, a turbulence of glaciers in green.

The rare fauna appear only at the edges of vision. Small things, pale and many-legged, that travel the root highways in search of sap or carrion. They make no vocalizations. A larger shape, perhaps the size of a dog, slides along a hanging liana, its body flattened into a ribbon that exactly mimics the texture of bark. It pauses, senses you, and vanishes into a knot-hole that seals behind it. There are no flocks, no herds. The biomass is almost entirely plant; the animal presence is a thin, opportunistic film on the surface of the vegetable world.

And yet it is a world—a whole, living, breathing strangeness. The air tastes green and mineral. The light has no warmth but immense clarity. You can walk for a day along a single root and see no rock, no soil, no watercourse larger than a seep, only the ceaseless, cold, bright weave. The landscape is a paradox: total vegetation and near-void; below surface and brilliantly lit; young and already dying; quiet and in perpetual storm. It does not care that you are there. It grows over the places that were made for human hands, erasing them without malice, and the sharp edges of its new growth catch the light like knives.
