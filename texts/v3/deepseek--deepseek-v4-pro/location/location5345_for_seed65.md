The first thing you notice — the thing that makes noticing possible, that scrapes the mind clean of any other thought — is the sound. It is not a single noise but a pressure, a continuous breadth of frequencies so total that it becomes a form of silence, the way a blaze of white light can blind. It is the roar of air in motion, but not the gusting, arrhythmic wind of lower places. Here, the wind is a constant, laminar flow, a river of atmosphere pouring over the edge of the world without beginning or end. It has no pauses, no surges, no lulls. It is a drone that vibrates in the chest and teeth, and within it, other sounds flicker: a high, metallic chittering; a distant, piping cry that bends and fractures in the Doppler shift of the gale; a percussive clatter like pebbles dropped onto a drumhead, repeated endlessly. After a time, you realize these are voices of living things — abundant, everywhere, but invisible against the glare.

The light is the next thing. At this extreme elevation, the sky is a thin membrane stretched too tight. The sun is a white hole, and the illumination is not golden but a hard, ionized silver that bleaches the rocks to the bone. Shadows are few and razor-sharp, for the land itself offers little to cast them. This is a place of horizontal brilliance, where the eyes must learn to squint not just upward but across, where the very air seems to have shed its softness and become a lens for radiation.

You stand on a surface that is not soil but the exposed nerve of the planet. The terrain is a vast, open tableland tilted slightly toward a distant rim, a plain of rock that stretches farther than the clarity suggests, its true scale deceptive in the thin air. It is an erosional landscape in its old age, worn down not by the violent fracture of youth but by an eternity of patient, incremental subtraction. There are no jagged peaks here, no fresh talus slopes. Instead, the bedrock emerges in long, whale-back undulations, smoothed and grooved by millennia of wind-driven grit, fluted and polished into flowing forms that seem halfway to liquid. The erosion is "established" — a state of equilibrium so deep it feels geological, a kind of exhausted perfection. Nothing is falling down; everything that could be carried away has been carried away, leaving only the resilient skeleton, which itself is now sculpted into aerodynamic shapes aligned with the ceaseless air-stream.

It is cool. Not the bitter, lung-shrinking cold of high altitudes elsewhere, but a steady, pervasive chill that hovers just above the point of freezing, never rising or falling. The temperature is 1.0 — cool, unchanging as everything else here. The dampness (0.6) comes not from rain but from the frequent passage of cloud-bellies dragging across the plateau. They are not rain clouds; they are fog, thin and fast, that condense on every surface, beading the rock with a slick of moisture that the wind instantly tries to strip away. The result is a dynamic tension — a constant deposition and evaporation, leaving the stone perpetually damp but never wet, as if it sweats. In the hollows of the whale-back rocks, small basins catch this condensation, forming ephemeral pockets of water that shimmer but never grow. These are the only visible water sources, and they are surrounded by the only visible life: thin, crustose lichens of a pale sage-green and rust-orange (the sparse vegetation, 0.9) that mottle the rock in patchy mosaics, taking a century to spread a hand's width.

And yet, the fauna is abundant (2.2). You cannot immediately see it, because the density of the place (0.5) is so low — an overwhelming emptiness stretches between presences. The landscape is a near-void, a colossal arena where every object is an isolate. A single boulder the size of a house stands a kilometer from any other feature, its flanking ground as bare as a swept floor. In that cavernous space, life has become acoustically hyper-present. The creatures here are small and airborne. They are birds, or things very like birds, that have adapted to the perpetual gale. They are masters of dynamic soaring, capable of locking themselves into the wind gradient, stationary or carving precise arcs while expending almost no energy. Their bodies are compact, their wings stiff, and they fledge from nests glued with saliva to the underside of isolated boulders. There are hundreds of thousands of them, but spread across the open scale of the plateau (1.8 — a regional theater, you sense, a single coherent valley-or-plain of stone bounded by the true peaks far on the horizon) they become a constant, distant presence, their cries and the rustle of their movements woven into the roar. Their guano stains the rocks around the condensation pools, a chalky white accretion that feeds the lichens in a slow, closed loop.

The weirdness (0.8) is subtle, an "unusual" quality that stems not from any one overtly alien element but from the uncanny perfection of the equilibrium. This place is "never changing" (dynamic 0.3). It is time-invariant. The fog, the wind, the damp, the chittering birds, the never-rising, never-falling temperature — all are locked into a frozen cycle, a tableau vivant of a single, eternal afternoon. There are no seasons. No day and night, it seems, in the quality of light, just a perpetual, bright overcast glare when the fog is thickest, or a blazing, direct sun when it thins. The birds do not migrate; they only wheel. The water does not flow; it only beads. The landscape has achieved a kind of deathless stability, a climax beyond which no succession can occur. It is a place that has outlasted its own dynamism.

There are no built structures, no artifacts (0.3). No technology of any kind, not even the primitive (0.8) hand-tool you might imagine; that parameter is irrelevant, a spectral presence that only highlights the absolute absence. This is an untouched, undeveloped world, raw and self-contained. The ears, after hours, learn to separate the layers of the deafening sound into individual streams: the fundamental roar of the planetary jet, the high piping of the nearest flock, the percussive tap of beaks probing damp rock, the silvery hiss of evaporation. And in that separation, you realize the place is not hostile but indifferent, a finished product of physics and biology that has sealed itself into a final, immutable form. It does not startle; it does not welcome. It simply persists, forever, in a state of bright, empty, clamorous repose.
