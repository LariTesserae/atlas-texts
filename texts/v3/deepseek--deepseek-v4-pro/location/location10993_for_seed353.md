The first thing you notice is the heat—a dry, blanketing weight that wraps around you like kiln-fired wool. It rises not from a single source but from everywhere: the floor, the walls, the very air, all radiating a steady, oppressive warmth that hovers just shy of unbearable. You feel it first on your skin, then in your lungs, a desiccating presence that pulls the moisture from your breath almost before you exhale. Here, water is a memory, a ghost of chemistry long since replaced. The atmosphere is a thin, sterile gas, perhaps nitrogen laced with volatile organics, carrying a faint metallic tang and the musty-sweet scent of slow decomposition.

The darkness is nearly absolute. Not the soft darkness of a moonless night, where stars or distant glow give shape, but a thick, light-eating murk that seems to press against your eyes. Only occasionally does a glimmer pierce it: a sullen orange wink from a heat-vent high above, the crawl of blue sparks along what might be a cable, or the pale, rhythmic phosphorescence of some fleshy growth pulsing in the depths. These pinpricks serve less to illuminate than to make the darkness feel vast—an open, airy volume you sense rather than see, a cathedral of shadow extending horizontally and vertically into silence.

That silence is the second thing. It’s a mostly-quiet, broken not by voices or animal cries but by a low, constant thrum—the heartbeat of engines buried deep in the structure—and the occasional creak or groan of immense masses shifting. There’s a rustle, too, a dry, papery sound like a billion tiny hands brushing against one another, though no breeze moves the dead air. It’s the sound of growth, of expansion, of cellular matter pushing outward inch by inch in the dark. When the turbulence spikes—as it frequently does—the quiet tears open: a sudden hiss of escaping pressure, the grind of metal against something fibrous, a low, shuddering boom that travels up through your bones. Then, almost immediately, the muffled quiet returns, as if the entire place absorbs the noise into its dense, padded flesh.

For everything here is flesh: a total, saturated vegetation that doesn’t simply cover the ground but is the ground. What you stand on is not soil or rock but a thick, springy mat of interwoven plant matter, its surface a tightly packed mosaic of tiny, brittle leaves and waxy tubers. No stone remains exposed; the entire landscape, for as far as the open scale suggests—a town’s worth, a valley’s breadth—is constructed entirely from this living substrate. You are inside a built world, a fully-made environment that has erased any trace of natural geology. Walls rise not as cliffs but as vertical gardens of immense, intertwined stems, each as thick as a man, curving upward into the gloom and branching into elaborate, vaulted architecture. Pillars that are trunks, archways that are trained vines, floors that are matted roots: everything is plant, and yet everything is unmistakably a product of design. The organic forms repeat with industrial precision, mile after mile of factory-grown tissue shaped into ducts, conduits, chambers, and frames.

This is a landscape of pure vegetation, but not the soft green of a terrestrial forest. In the absence of light, chlorophyll is useless. These plants are pale, ranging from a sickly ivory to a deep, bruised purple, their surfaces waxy and reticulated, designed to retain every molecule of moisture. Some segments are ribbed like radiators, dissipating the relentless heat. Others are studded with nodules that crackle with blue static when touched—bioelectric nodes feeding power to the ancient machinery nestled within the living walls. The tech here is industrial, early electric, a clattering, hissing, mechanized presence of iron and copper that has merged seamlessly with the botanical. Pistons push against pulpy sapwood; turbines spin inside ventricles of flexible vine; conveyor belts of braided grass haul loads of crumbling, dried-out plant mass toward hoppers where it is reabsorbed or burned. The whole place hums with scaled production, a factory that is also an ecosystem, churning out—what? Perhaps only itself.

It is ancient and endlessly turbulent. The parameters of deep erosion are visible in the smoothed, near-flattened contours of the main arteries: massive tubular tunnels bored through the vegetation, their walls polished over eons by the constant slide of growth and decay. Yet the surface is never still. New shoots erupt from every seam, only to be scoured away by mechanical claws or wilt in a sudden, localized blast of superheated air. The whole landscape is a chaotic, anthill-energy bazaar of creation and destruction, a storm-state that never calms. You might stand on a platform of dense, compressed fiber that shudders as a section below it dissolves into a slurry of enzymes, only to be immediately regrown from feeding tendrils that curl upward from the darkness. The density is claustrophobic: there is no vacant space, no clearing, no gap between one presence and the next. Every cubic meter is packed—with machinery, with vegetation, with the two intertwined so completely you cannot tell where one ends and the other begins.

Elevation gives you a vantage, but not a view. You are high, perhaps on a terrace or a catwalk of woven stems that spans a vertiginous drop, but the darkness and the density swallow any distance. The open scale is felt as a pressure of empty air above and below, a sense that the space extends far beyond your immediate reach, even if it is filled with the bristling, living architecture. You hear the distant groan of another factory-heart miles away, and the sound travels through the plant matter like a nervous impulse. There is no fauna—no skittering insect, no nesting bird, no mammal’s footfall. The only movements are the slow, grasping contractions of tendrils, the pistoning of machine parts, and the occasional cascade of dry detritus as an ancient section, finally too worn, crumbles into dust that sifts down through the layers. The silence is absolute in its lack of voice; this is a world that has forgotten animals entirely.

And it is weird. The longer you stay, the more the strangeness permeates. The plants exhale not oxygen but something faintly acrid, and their touch leaves a residue like cold ash. Some of the industrial processes seem to run on loops that make no physical sense—a gear train that moves but never turns a shaft, a vat of bubbling resin that replenishes without input. The darkness is so complete you begin to sense shapes with your skin, with your ears, mapping the hot, humming, shifting labyrinth in a bodily way. It is a place that feels simultaneously at the end of its life—settled into a final, cyclically-repeating form—and violently, aggressively alive, as if the very substance of the world is a Solari-like dream of matter, perpetually building and un-building itself in the hot, dry dark.

This landscape is a dry, hot, dark, and densely packed industrial-vegetal megastructure: an ancient, turbulent, and utterly constructed realm where plant life is the architecture, the machine, and the ground, leaving no room for water, light, or animal existence.
