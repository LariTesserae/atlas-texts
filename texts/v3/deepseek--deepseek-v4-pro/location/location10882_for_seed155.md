The Needleleaf Plateau unrolls beneath a pewter sky—high, cool, the air thin enough to feel each breath as a small theft. It is damp here, not with standing water but with a perpetual seep: condensation pearls on every surface, mist curls along the ground each morning, and a fine, drifting drizzle materializes at odd hours, as if the atmosphere itself is indecisive. This moisture has worked the landscape for geological ages, smoothing, rounding, settling everything into a final form. There are no sharp edges left. The world here has been worn down to its essential shapes, ancient and patient.

And those shapes are all plant.

Vegetation is not a feature of this plateau; it is the plateau. Every solid thing is living tissue, or was. The ground underfoot is a spongy mat of compressed fibers, a thousand generations of growth flattened into a springy, resilient floor. From that floor rise the structures—and they are structures, organized, purposeful—that form the city. This is an urban place, a settlement of industrial scale, but its materials are stem, root, and bark. The buildings are not built; they are grown, or were grown so long ago that the distinction has blurred into irrelevance.

Tower-factories of spiraled cambium reach upward and outward, their flanks studded with rounded knobs and smoothed ridges where limbs were pruned or fused over centuries. They stand widely spaced, each a discrete presence in the open air, so that the city feels less like a dense grid and more like a archipelago of colossal organisms. Between them stretch broad avenues of bare, polished root-mat, damp and resilient. There is much space between presences—a sparse scattering of massive things, each the size of a town in its own right, yet yielding to the overall sense of a single, interconnected settlement stretching to the misty horizons. This is a regional capital of its kind, open and breathing.

The atmosphere is a symphony of noise: creaking, groaning, a rhythmic thumping like vast heartbeats, the hiss of steam venting from valves that are not metal but lipped apertures in living wood. Industrial sounds, but organic. The plant-machines are in motion. Their internal processes are not silent biology; they are mechanized. Sap is pumped through translucent tubes with the regular chunk-chunk of pistons, driven by the expansion and contraction of gases in sealed chambers. Leaves modified into broad, fibrous belts run across pulleys of hardened lignin. Somewhere, a deep, sub-audible thrum resonates through the ground—a generator of some kind, rooted far below, its energy harvested from the thermal gradient of the plateau’s bedrock. The technology is industrial, but it is not built upon nature; it is nature, turned into a factory ecosystem eons ago and now running on its own ancient programming.

This is a place at the end of life, yet intensely active. The erosion of time has not stopped the processes; it has only worn them smooth. The plant-factories still perform their functions, producing—what? Not fruit, but refined substances: resinous cakes that harden into amber-like ingots, powdery spores collected by frond-paddles and funneled into sacs, thick syrups that drip into hollowed trunks. There are multiple cycles at once: a weather cycle, as mist condenses, runs down sculpted channels, and evaporates when occasional sun breaks through; a biological cycle, as growth tips emerge, mature, and are subsumed back into the whole; a mechanical cycle, as energy is converted, stored, and spent. It is a predator-prey dynamic without predators or prey, simply the rhythm of production and consumption within a single, sprawling, living complex.

Fauna, in any recognizable sense, is almost absent. Rarely, a tiny, translucent mite may drift on a filament of silk, a fleck of motion in the vast stillness of the open spaces. But nothing with a backbone, nothing with a voice, nothing that grazes or hunts. The plants need no pollinators, no dispersers; they have replaced all those roles with their own internal machinery. The scarcity of animal life makes the city feel even more strange, as if it has swallowed every other kingdom and repurposed their bodies into its own extensions.

The light is normal—a diffuse, gray-white illumination that shifts gradually through the day, sometimes brightening to a pale silver when the high overcast thins. There is no dramatic sunrise or sunset, just a dimming and brightening that governs the pace of the plant-factory rhythms. Shadows are soft, edges always a little blurred by the ever-present moisture.

And yet, for all its normalcy of light, reality here is not fully stable. The weirdness creeps in at the corners. A tower’s profile might ripple, as if seen through heat haze, though the air is cool. A vent might open and emit not steam but a brief, soundless flash of color that leaves an afterimage. The laws that separate plant from machine, living from constructed, seem to have worn thin as well. A stem might grow directly into a gear; a leaf might have the tensile strength and shimmer of steel. There are places where the root-mat ground does not feel solid but instead gives a sensation of walking on a membrane, with something vast and empty beneath. It is a reality breakdown, not catastrophic but pervasive—a steady-state anomaly, as if this landscape has been running its own physics for so long that the rest of the universe’s rules have begun to forget it exists.

The plateau continues, open and expansive, for miles in all directions, until it slices off into sheer drops that descend into cloud. The city has no outskirts; it simply stops where the ground gives way, the last twisted columns of living architecture poised on the rim, their roots dangling into the abyss. The sound is loudest near the center, where the largest structures cluster, their industrial heartbeat overlaying a constant, shushing wind that never ceases. This is a place fully itself, settled into its final, active, improbable form.
