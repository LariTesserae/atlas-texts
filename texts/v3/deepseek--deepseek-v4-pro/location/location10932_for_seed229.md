The place is called the Silt-Groves of the Keening Wall.

You stand on a leaning lip of stone—a bone-coloured shelf of crumbly pumice that juts out from a cliff face lost in the dimness above. The ground underfoot is not soil but a dense mat of dried, hollow fibres, like a carpet of old paper wasp-nests. It crackles with each step, and from those cracks rise tiny puffs of bitter dust. The air is warm, thick as breath, and carries a faint tang of ozone and baked clay. Light comes from no visible sun; the sky is a seamless, bruised-purple vault that glows with a low, steady phosphorescence, as if the world is inside a giant sealed eyelid. Shadows are soft but deep, pooling in hollows and beneath the growths.

And the growths. They are why “lush” is the word scribbled in some long-gone traveller’s log, though they are nothing like a forest. They stand scattered—each one a solitary sovereign, separated by generous sweeps of open, fibre-crusted ground. They are columns, or perhaps towers, ten to fifteen feet high, swelling from narrow waists at the base into broad, bulbous heads. Their substance is not wood or flesh but a porous, mineral secretion, like pumice soaked in resin, veined with glassy threads that catch the dim light and throw it back in fleeting glints of turquoise. The surfaces are riotously alive with texture: frills and flanges, honeycombed recesses, curling paper-thin edges that tremble constantly. From a distance, each one looks like a silent green-grey geyser frozen mid-erupt, draped in lichenous ruffles of deep russet and burnt orange. This is the “vegetation”—not green, not soft, but undeniably lush in its mad proliferation of form, a baroque exuberance of dry life.

There is no water. Not a drop, not a mist. The word for water is a fossil here. Life runs on a different sweat: the columns feed on a faint, oily exudate that seeps up through the pumice floor from the mountain’s deep viscera—a sluggish, non-aqueous broth of siloxanes and metallic salts. You can smell it, a low note like hot flint, and sometimes see its sheen in the earth’s fissures. The columns absorb it through their roots (fibrous, shallow, spreading wide below the mat) and transpire a fine, dry silicate dust that glitters in the grove’s slow currents of air.

The air itself is never still. The place is a cathedral of small turbulences. Eddies spin in the spaces between the columns, set in motion by temperature differences too subtle to feel but strong enough to keep the frills and flanges ever-quivering. This is the source of the sound—the Keening. Each column is an instrument. The papery edges rasp against each other; the hollow chambers within the bulbous heads whistle and moan as the gusts pass through their labyrinthine pores; the glassy veins vibrate at their own high, metallic frequencies. The combined noise is a continuous, roaring whisper, like a thousand voices caught just on the edge of screaming, threaded through with percussive rattles and deep, bone-shaking hums. It is loud. It fills your skull until you cannot think, until the sound becomes the world’s own breathing.

And the fauna. They are abundant—maddeningly so. Tiny creatures the size of thumbs, with bodies like jointed seedpods and six legs each ending in a tuft of sensory cilia. They pour over the columns in ceaseless waves, a pattering, clambering tide of them. They gnaw at the frills, consuming the silica-rich tissue and leaving behind perfectly geometric little holes, like lace. Their droppings are minute glass spheres that pile up around the column bases in glinting dunes. The creatures communicate in a language of high-frequency clicks and sudden chorused shrieks that punctuate the keening, a frantic pointillist counterpoint. They are not alone. Hovering in the spaces between pillars are spindrift creatures—translucent membranes stretched over a basket of whiskers, riding the eddies like strange jellyfish of the air. They hum a lower note, and when they collide, they ring like struck crystal.

Evidence of other minds: scattered, faint, primitive. On the lee side of some columns, you find loose rings of piled volcanic stones, no higher than your ankle—windbreaks, or perhaps the foundations of vanished shelters. Inside these, caches of hand-sized scrapers chipped from obsidian, still razor-sharp, their edges worn with use. The scrapers are sized for hands smaller and more dexterous than a human’s. Sometimes, carved into the bases of the columns, there are pictograms: spirals, dot patterns, stylized shapes of the hovering air-jellies. The craft is simple, almost feral, but deliberate. No one is here now. Or no one you can see.

And yet, the place is in turbulent flux. The columns grow and die over cycles you can almost witness. A fresh one pushes up through the mat as a pale, glistening knoll, its surface covered in a glaucous bloom that hardens within hours. By nightfall—though night never truly comes, the light merely dims to a deep aubergine—it might be knee-high. By the next waking, it has sprouted its first flanges. Meanwhile, older columns crumble. You hear, beneath the keening, the deep, groaning crack of a head splitting, then a cascade of glassy rubble crashing to the mat. The ground is littered with these ruins, slowly being reabsorbed into the fibrous carpet. The air jellies die when they grow too large; their membranes burst in a puff of sparkling dust, and the dust settles on the columns, seeding new growth. A kolony of the seedpod-things will strip a column to a skeleton in a few days, then swarm off to another, leaving the bare spire to be weathered into a flute that adds a new, mournful voice to the choir.

This is a small place—a grove, a chamber, a cavity in the mountain’s high shoulder. You sense its boundaries: to one side, the grotto is walled by a sheer face of black, glassy basalt that sweats the oily life-fluid; to the other, a vertiginous drop into a layer of motionless, dim-purple cloud. The entire habitable ledge is perhaps fifty paces across. A hamlet for the unseen carvers, a single-feature world defined by the columns and the keening.

The weirdness is not a side-note but the deep rule. There is a pulse here, a slow, geological heartbeat that syncopates with the wind-eddies. Sometimes, you feel the stone ledge flex underfoot, just slightly, as if it were the hide of a vast, dormant animal. The symbols on the columns seem to shift position when you look away. And above all, there is the sense that the whole grove is a conversation, a dense weave of information passed between the vibrating columns, the clicking fauna, the drifting jellies, and the gasses seeping from the deep rock. The sound is not just noise; it is a complex, self-modifying signal, a bazaar of non-human meaning that is constantly building and destroying itself. Somewhere in that storm of sonic data, the stone-scraper users once listened and joined in with their simple patterns.

Now, in the warm, dim, turbulent now, you stand at the edge of the small lush dry grove, ears ringing with its loud chorale, and watch a new column crack the surface near your feet, reaching up with blind, mineral haste toward the glowing purple sky, as the old ones sing their abrasive hymns and the tiny gnawers skitter on with their ceaseless, urgent work. The place is settled in its strangeness, as if it has always been this way, a small point of chaotic, loud, dry, dense life in the high quiet of a dying world.
