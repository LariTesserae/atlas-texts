The air comes in layers here—cold and wet and thick with something that isn’t quite mist, more a perpetual seeping, as if the atmosphere itself sweats. You are high up, though the horizon is an unreliable thing, and the light never really arrives; it seeps too, a dim gray-green that pools in hollows and runs thin across the open spaces. You stand on what might be called ground, but it gives underfoot with a slow, fibrous rebound, like walking on a mattress of cold-saturated sponge. This is the vegetation, and it is everything—there is no soil visible, no bare rock, no clear water surface despite the wetness. The plants have become the substrate, the architecture, the terrain. And they are old. Erosion here has been working on a timescale so vast it has rounded off not just peaks but entire geological eras, leaving a landscape that feels flattened not by collapse but by having settled into its final, irreducible form: a vast undulating mat of intertwined life, so ancient it has forgotten the taste of minerals.

The vegetation is a single continuous thing, but sparse in a way that’s hard to parse until you start moving. The mass is total—every cubic meter is plant matter—yet it is riddled with channels, air pockets, hollow veins that allow passage. The "trees" (though that word fails) are gargantuan, bloated columns of spongy tissue, each one spaced far from its neighbor, so that you walk through a colonnade of pale, waterlogged trunks rising thirty meters before splaying into a canopy that blocks the sky entirely. Between them, the ground cover is a dense lace of feathery fronds and bladderworts the size of sheep, all of it motionless except for a constant, slow dripping. The spacing feels deliberate, like the negative spaces are part of the organism’s architecture—necessary voids for gas exchange, for the movement of the abundant fauna that rustles and screeches unseen.

That fauna is abundant, yes, but it’s not like animals you’d know. They are extensions of the plant system, or perhaps parasites on it, or perhaps both in alternation. You catch glimpses: segmented bodies sliding through the fibrous matrix, leaving glistening trails that heal over in minutes. Things with too many legs scuttle up the trunks, their chitin translucent, and their internal organs visible as glowing motes—bioluminescence that pulses in the dim, never enough to light the way but enough to confuse depth perception. The sound is loud and constant: a low-frequency thrum that you feel in your teeth, overlaid with sharp, metallic clicks and the occasional tearing noise, like fabric ripping on an industrial scale. It’s a factory floor noise, but the factory is alive and furious. The dynamic of this place is turbulent indeed—a ceaseless, chaotic creation and destruction. As you watch, a whole section of the ground ahead heaves upward, a blister of plant tissue swelling and bursting, spewing a cloud of spores and something that might be steam, and in the aftermath a new structure is already knitting itself together from the wound, a spiral ramp of green flesh that hardens into a corkscrew tower. Nothing is stable. The landscape reshapes itself hourly, driven by forces that feel less like biology and more like a storm-state of matter.

The industrial parameter is there, but not as you’d expect. There are no human structures, no machines of metal. Instead, the vegetation itself exhibits industrial logic: it processes, it refines, it produces. You see vast hanging bladders that contract rhythmically, pumping fluid through transparent tubes; they generate a rhythmic chuff-chuff like steam engines. There are structures that resemble cooling towers made of chitin and cellulose, exhaling plumes of condensation that smell sharply of ammonia and wet earth. The ground in some areas is hard and hot under the damp, as if internal fermentation or some kind of biological electricity generates waste heat, and you come across patches where the plant matter is calcified into a brittle ceramic, the remains of some ancient metabolic kiln. The tech parameter integrates seamlessly: it’s a living factory, a biomechanical plant processing something—maybe water, maybe atmospheric gases, maybe the very fabric of reality, because that’s where the weirdness breaks through.

The reality breakdown is not a subtle thing here. The rules are inconsistent. Gravity will sometimes tilt, not physically but perceptually—you find yourself walking perpendicular to a trunk, then realize your feet are still on the ground and it was the tree that bent itself sideways in your vision, or perhaps you did. Time stutters. A drip will pause midair, a droplet holding its shape for three heartbeats too long before splashing up instead of down. There are zones where your shadow detaches and moves ahead of you, or where the sound arrives before the event—you hear a crash, and then a massive frond slowly collapses ten seconds later in utter silence. The fauna, too, plays with non-Euclidean spaces: a creature that appears to be a tangle of wet ropes will vanish by folding into itself, leaving a brief afterimage of geometric pain. Color sometimes bleeds out of objects, leaving them gray and flat, then reinfuses from the edges in a different hue. You keep seeing what looks like a distant horizon with a city skyline—industrial smokestacks and gantries—but it’s never any closer when you walk toward it, and the silhouette glitches, repeats, stutters.

The cold is a wet cold, a 0.5 that seeps through insulation and clings to bone, but it’s not a freezing cold; the water never freezes, just stays at a temperature that feels like a clammy hand on the back of your neck. The elevation adds a thinness to the air, a slight hypoxia that makes the weirdness more hallucinatory, more persuasive. Water is everywhere, not as standing pools but as the very medium of life: the plants are turgid, weeping from every pore, and when you press a hand against a trunk, moisture beads instantly on your skin. The atmosphere is so saturated you feel you’re breathing through a wet cloth. Sometimes a fine, cold drizzle falls not from the sky but from the underside of the canopy, a condensation cycle driven by the metabolic heat and the cold air above, so it feels like the forest is sweating on you.

The erosion parameter, 3.0, means this place has been weathering since before complex life should exist, yet here it is, a stable climax state—stable in its instability. The ground has been worn down to a rolling plain despite the high elevation, because the living mass has replaced any mineral topography. It’s an eternal, deep-time surface where nothing is permanent but the pattern of perpetual renewal. You sense that the very molecules have been recycled so often they’ve lost their original identities; the carbon in that frond might once have been a mountain, then a million generations of creatures, then a cloud, then a star. And now it’s this loud, breathing, sparse-not-sparse factory of green dimness.

You walk—or wade—through a place where the boundary between organism and environment, between machine and life, between past and future, is completely dissolved. The only constant is the noise: the thrum, click, tear, hiss of an industrial ecology that has been running without maintenance since the world was young, tearing down and rebuilding the same carbon over and over, under a sky that never brightens, in a cold that never deepens, with animals that are more like moving symptoms of the vegetation’s metabolism. And you realize that the sounds you took for wind and structural settling are actually communication—a dense infrasonic language vibrating through the wet fibers—and that all of this, the whole open valley of it, is one thing thinking long, slow thoughts about erosion and heat and the strange warm-blooded intruder with a shadow that sometimes walks in the wrong direction.
