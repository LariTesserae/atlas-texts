The Clouded Steps do not end.

They rise in tiers that the eye cannot hold all at once, each vast limestone shelf a world of its own, stepping back and back into the mist until the highest ledges vanish into the grey. The limestone is ancient and water-worked, its surface running with solution flutes and sharpened into blades where the acid rain has been at it longest, but softened elsewhere into undulating pavements pocked with circular pans that brim with clear water. Every ledge, every terrace, every tilted plateau between one scarp and the next holds water in some form — shallow meres that reflect the cloud-cover in perfect silver, dripping lips where groundwater seeps from the porous rock face, channels worn into the stone by millennia of flow, and everywhere the sound of it: not loud, not splashing, but the steady, close-at-hand percussion of drops falling from moss, the whisper of sheet-flow over tilted stone, the wet exhalation of saturated air moving against rock.

The steps climb into the cloud deck, and the cloud deck is permanent. It sits at this elevation as a fact of planetary physics — the warm wet breath of the lowland forests rising against the escarpment, condensing at this precise altitude, forming a horizontal ceiling that the highest terraces inhabit. Up there, the light comes diffuse and directionless, a soft grey glow that makes shadows impossible. Down here, on the mid-steps, the cloud breaks occasionally, and sunlight spears through in columns that move across the landscape like slow searchlights, igniting the moss to an impossible green before moving on.

And the moss is everywhere. It is the vegetation and it is the density. It grows not as a covering but as a substance in its own right, a saturated sponge-city of sphagnum and liverwort and things that have no names in any language of the lowlands. It reaches depths of three metres in the hollows, a trembling, water-logged mass that exhales moisture with every compression. Walking on it — not that anyone does — would be like walking on the skin of a living thing, each footfall sending ripples of water through the surrounding growth. It mantles the limestone, rounds the sharp edges, fills the grykes, spills over the terrace edges in green waterfalls of itself. The moss is the architect now, not the water. The water shaped the stone; the moss has claimed it, softened it, built its own topography on top.

The moss-world is dense with small life. The fauna is abundant but not showy. You have to look. Under the moss-cap, in the permanently dark, permanently wet zone where the living moss gives way to brown peat, there are entire civilizations of springtails and mites and nematodes, packed so densely that a handspan of substrate contains more individual animals than there are stars visible from the planet's surface. On the surface of the moss, amongst its tiny leaves, grazers move in slow herds — tardigrades in countless thousands, their transparent bodies navigating the water film that coats every surface, feeding on algae and bacteria and each other. Larger things — "larger" meaning visible to the naked eye — move through the moss forest: pale spiders that build webs between the stalks, their silk beaded with condensation; small amphibians that breathe through their skins and never leave the saturated zone; flatworms that glide on their own mucus trails, hunting with a slow, liquid patience.

And through it all, silent, the machines work.

There are no structures here, no buildings, no walls. But the industrial presence is unmistakable once you know what to look for. The machines have been here for a very long time — long enough that the moss has grown over most of them, long enough that the limestone has begun to reclaim them, long enough that they have become geology. Their forms are rounded now, softened by corrosion and overgrowth, but the basic shapes persist: cylinders, domes, the straight lines of rails and conduits that cut across the natural drainage patterns. They are factories, or were, or still are — it is difficult to tell whether they operate or merely persist. In some places, a low vibration runs through the rock, a steady hum that is felt in the feet rather than heard. In other places, small vents open in the moss, exhaling steam that smells faintly of hydrocarbons and hot metal. The steam condenses immediately in the cool air, joining the permanent moisture, indistinguishable from the natural mist within seconds.

Their purpose is unclear. Some of the cylinder-sets pump water, cycling it from the lower meres to the higher terraces in a slow mechanical heartbeat, and the moss has adapted to this rhythm, growing denser around the artificial seeps. Other machines seem to be digesting the limestone itself, extruding some processed form of calcium through ceramic pipes that run for kilometres across a single terrace before vanishing into sinkholes. The waste heat from these operations creates microclimates — patches of warmth where the vegetation grows differently, where the amphibians cluster in greater numbers, where the tardigrades reproduce faster. The ecosystem has woven the machines into itself. They are not an intrusion; they are a nutrient cycle.

The silence is not total. The water drips. The distant vibration hums through the stone. Occasionally a block of moss slumps, releasing a soft wet sigh. Very rarely, something calls — a thin, high note that carries surprisingly far in the moisture-heavy air, the territorial song of one of the amphibians. But there is no wind at this elevation; the cloud ceiling presses down too heavily. No birds. No insect buzz. The sounds that exist are small sounds, close sounds, the intimate acoustics of water moving through a saturated system.

Look up from any terrace and the view is the same: the next terrace rising, its face a wall of grey and green, waterfalls that are more seep than fall, the mist coiling at its lip. Look further and the perspective compresses, the tiers stacking like a staircase built for something unimaginably large, each step its own horizon, until the mist takes everything. The scale is impossible to grasp from within. This is not a mountain. It is a continental feature, a great upthrust of the planet's crust that has been eroding for epochs, its retreating edges forming these steps, each one a former plateau that collapsed and was reclaimed by the hydrological cycle. The moss is the living memory of this process, holding the water, slowing the erosion, building up instead of wearing down.

The highest terraces, the ones inside the cloud, are different. No one goes there, but the machines report — or would report, if there were anyone to receive — that the moss gives way to something else. In the permanent fog, where the temperature drops further and the light is a constant twilight grey, the vegetation changes. The moss becomes crystalline, its cell walls impregnated with silicates, forming structures that are half-plant and half-mineral. The machines up there are different too, older, barely functioning, their metal so deeply intergrown with the crystalline moss that it is impossible to tell where engineering ends and biology begins. Water there is not liquid but vapour-saturated, every surface slick with a condensate that never quite forms droplets. The sound is different too — a faint crackling, the sound of silica structures growing, a sound almost too high to hear.

But that is the cloud-zone, and that is a different world. Down here, on the mid-steps, in the place where the moss reaches its maximum thickness and the water runs in visible channels and the sunlight sometimes finds its way through, the landscape has reached a kind of equilibrium. The industrial machinery hums at a frequency the amphibians can feel through their skin. The water cycles through stone and moss and machine. The terraces step back into the mist, each one holding its own small sea, its own forests-in-miniature, its own weather of drips and seeps and condensing breath.

Nothing is still. Everything moves — the water, the animals through the moss, the moss itself growing and dying and becoming peat, the machines cycling their incomprehensible substrates. But the motion is slow, and it is quiet, and it has been happening for so long that it has the quality of stillness. The Clouded Steps are a place that has been becoming itself for geological time, and it is still becoming, and it will be becoming long after the last machine finally seizes and the moss reclaims the last trace of processed metal.

For now, the machines hum. The amphibians call. The water drips. The moss grows. The steps rise. And the mist holds everything.
