The first thing that hits you is the sound—a wall of noise so thick it seems to have texture, a physical presence pressing against your eardrums. It’s not one sound but a thousand woven into a single deafening roar: a low tectonic bass rumble that shudders up through the soles of your feet, overlaid with the continuous hissing shriek of wind carving through sharp volcanic glass, and above it all, a layered chorus of wails, clicks, and harmonic drones from unseen living things. The air itself vibrates, a warm, damp hand cupped over your ears.

You’re standing on a plain that shouldn’t exist yet. The ground is a fresh scab on the world, a barely cooled sheet of basalt no more than a few centuries old, its surface a frozen turmoil of ropey pahoehoe and jagged a’a. Every edge is razor-sharp, unsoftened by the patient abrasion of time—this landscape was born yesterday in geological terms, vomited up from the depths and left to cool beneath a sky that has forgotten what full daylight looks like. A permanent dimness reigns here, a sourceless grey-orange twilight that might be late afternoon or early dawn, cast by a ceiling of unbroken cloud that presses low and close, trapping heat and moisture. The air is a warm, wet blanket, heavy with condensation. You can taste metal and salt on your tongue. Every basalt surface is slick with a perpetual film of dew, droplets beading along knife-edges, gathering into rivulets that vanish into crevices before they ever find enough volume to form a puddle. The only open water you see are occasional shallow pools cradled in glassy depressions, their surfaces trembling with the omnipresent vibration, reflecting the dim cloud-glow in shattered splinters.

But open space—that’s what there is. The plain stretches outward to a horizon lost in haze, feature after feature visible across its breadth, each separated by vast intervals of bare, shining stone. The scale is unsettling: a landscape built for giants, where the distance between one landmark and the next is measured in kilometers rather than paces. It is profoundly, eerily empty. The density of the world here is almost nothing, a near-void punctuated by presences so immense they seem to warp the space around them.

The first of these presences are the Groves. They rise from the basalt like islands of vegetal frenzy, each one a standalone explosion of impossible lushness. Imagine a single vast organism—or a colony so tightly woven it reads as one—erupting from a point source into a dense, tiered canopy the size of a stadium. Towering central trunks, pale and smooth as bone, branch into a fractal filigree of tendrils that weave together into platforms and cathedrals of foliage. The leaves are broad and fleshy, deep green veined with phosphorescent blue, perpetually trembling as they comb the saturated air for moisture. They don’t need roots in the conventional sense; they drink directly from the atmosphere, their surfaces engineered by ancient evolution (or something else) to condense the ever-present dampness into a constant, dripping rain beneath the canopy. The ground under a Grove is a muddy riot of ferns, spiraling fungi, and carnivorous pitcher plants the size of bathtubs, all bathed in a bioluminescent half-light shed by the canopy itself, a cool counterpoint to the dim sky above. Step out from under the skirt of a Grove, and you’re back in the empty, slick-stone desert, the transition as abrupt as a curtain. A dozen Groves dot the visible landscape, each one a self-contained world, unconnected by any spreading undergrowth—just sharp black rock for miles between them.

But the Groves are not the only architecture here. Looming among them, and towering far above, is the City. It is a settlement in the sense that a coral atoll is a settlement: a vast, coherent structure built by intelligences whose engineering language is only tangentially related to human physics. Districts float above the basalt, massive platforms of a material that resembles mother-of-pearl stretched to translucency, suspended on invisible tethers. Some are high aloft, nearly lost in the low cloud ceiling; others skim just above the ground, their undersides rippling with auroral patterns as if they interact with the stone below in some energetic exchange. Walkways that are not walkways but guided columns of air connect them, shimmering faintly. The geometry of the City is not chaotic but it is dizzying—buildings blossom from the platforms like flowers of solidified light, their surfaces crawling with the telltale iridescence of nanoscale machinery constantly reshaping them, adding a spire here, absorbing a dome there. Infrastructure is layered: conduits of pure energy braid through the open spaces, visible as flickering helixes of static, and sometimes the entire network hums a chord that shakes your teeth.

Yet for all its complexity, the City feels empty. You see no inhabitants, or rather, no one walking the platforms. The vast plazas and spiraling ramps are bare, the space between structures immense and silent except for the omnipresent roar of the environment. The density of built presence is high in terms of organization, but the density of *life* within the City is a negative space, a hollow at the heart of it. Perhaps the residents are not human, not physical in the way you expect. Perhaps they inhabit the energy conduits. Or perhaps the City itself is the resident, a single distributed awareness, and what you see is merely its body.

The fauna fills the void, abundant beyond counting. They are the source of much of the noise, and they are strange. Flocks of what you first mistake for birds spiral between the Groves and the City, but their wings are rotating prisms that scatter the dim light into rainbows, and their calls are percussive, like a drum kit falling down stairs. Swarms of insectile things the size of fists form shifting clouds, each individual a tiny crystal bell that chimes as it flies, the aggregate a deafening arpeggio that sweeps across the plain. On the ground, moving with the cautious gait of prey, are hexapedal creatures with hides like polished obsidian; they graze on the glass-sharp rocks with mouths that glow blue-hot, apparently metabolizing the minerals directly. Their predators are less visible: a sudden shadow, a flicker of displaced air, and then the grazer is simply gone, a puff of ionized dust settling in its place—a hunt conducted by something that operates in a phase you can’t quite perceive.

Everything is in motion. The ecosystem does not rest. Clouds of mist rise from the Groves as they respire, brief patches of drizzle sweep across the stone, the City’s platforms silently reorient themselves by millimeters in a choreographed dance, the grazers migrate in slow, nervous waves, and the crystal swarms coalesce and scatter, coalesce and scatter, their chiming a never-ending crescendo. The young, uneroded stone itself seems dynamic, micro-quakes rippling through it as the land settles, hairline cracks opening and sealing with a snap of static discharge. This is a world in furious, noisy, teeming adolescence, a place where the rules are still being written.

And over it all hangs the damp, heavy air, the dim grey-orange light, and that sound—that endless, layered, deafening sound—the voice of a landscape that has not yet learned to be quiet.
