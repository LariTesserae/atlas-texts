The high plateau spreads outward in every direction, a breath-held expanse that defies the eye’s demand for a horizon. There is no true ground here, no mineral skin of the world. Instead, the terrain is a continuous, layered thickness of vegetation—a living, breathing substrate that rises and falls in gentle swells, as if the earth beneath were only a rumor. Every surface is plant. The footing is a springy mat of interwoven rhizomes and moss-like growths, dense and waterlogged, spongy underfoot. Step, and it depresses with a slow, silent exhale; lift your foot, and it swells back, erasing all trace. Above this under-layer, stems rise in a crowded verticality: fibrous columns, wide as bodies, coated in a rime of condensation that never quite freezes. They twist upward without straightness, branching into a mid-canopy of broad, cupped leaves shaped like dark leathery hands, their edges fringed with droplets. Higher still, a third tier arches into a ceiling of flat, overlapping fronds that knit together, blotting out the sky save for veins of pale light that filter through in a perpetual gray noon.

The cold is a constant, a thin blade laid against any exposed skin. At this elevation, the air is sharp and spare, yet it carries a peculiar dampness that never resolves into rain. Instead, moisture gathers and drips in slow, soundless runnels from leaf-tip to leaf-tip. Pockets of water pool in the hollows of the vegetation: in the bowls of giant bromeliad-like structures, in the tight-furled funnels of spiral stalks. There are no streams, no ponds, only these held aliquots of liquid, always just at the point of brimming, shared through a capillary network that threads the entire landscape. The temperature hovers just above freezing—a thermal plateau sustained by the sheer metabolic mass of the vegetation, which hums with a low, distant warmth, as if a great engine worked somewhere deep within.

This is a place of profound quiet. Sound, here, is a swallowed thing. The vegetation absorbs it, the air is too thin to carry it far. The rustle you expect—the gossip of leaves, the creak of fibrous stems—never comes. Even the wind, a near-constant flow at this height, moves without voice; it slips through the canopy in smoothed laminar sheets, never eddying into turbulence that might coax a whisper from the fronds. What sound does exist is a near-subaudible bass note, a frequency felt in the sternum rather than heard: the deep, rhythmic pulse of the entire plant-mass, a systolic-diastolic cycle as the superorganism draws water from its roots and vents vapor into the sky.

And it is a superorganism, though that word falls short. The vegetation here is total—a pure-plant ecosystem where individuals have dissolved into a single, chaotic wholeness. It is also in a state of perpetual, violent becoming. *Turbulent* is the parameter, and here turbulence takes the form of a landscape that refuses to hold its shape. Across the plateau, sections of the canopy heave and collapse continuously, as if caught in a slow-motion storm. A wall of leaves, fifty feet high and half a mile long, will suddenly sag, its support structures softening into a brown sludge that drips noiselessly into the underlayers. Even as it sinks, fresh shoots spear upward from the decay, spiraling with a speed just shy of visible. The whole surface seethes with this cycle of creation and destruction: domes of vegetation bubble up, spread, and are reabsorbed; pillars of hollow stalk rise in rows, then twist and fall without a sound. It is a landscape that builds and unbuilds itself hourly, a bazaar of form where nothing is permanent but the pattern of impermanence.

There are no animals. Fauna is a rumor from another world. No birds glide between the fronds, no insects drone in the cool air. The only movement is that of the plants themselves—a blind, ceaseless kineticism. Yet the place does not feel dead. It feels eerily awake, a machinery of flesh that has replaced the entire logic of an ecosystem with something else.

That “something else” is where the weirdness seeps in. The vegetation is not merely alive; it is *industrial*. The tall stalks are not simple stems but segmented tubes of a chitin-like polymer, ringed with metallic-tinged bands that glint dully in the filtered light. Many are hollow, and from their open crowns, they periodically emit a fine mist that smells faintly of ozone and wet earth—a breath measured and timed, as if regulated by valves deep in the root-system. Scattered across the plateau are what can only be called vents: broad, chimney-like structures of overlapping scales that release visible heat-shimmers, though they make no sound. The leaf-canopy overhead is veined not just with sap but with a faint, web-like tracery that glows with a low amber bioluminescence, just bright enough to be seen in the day, suggesting a network of electrical or chemical signals propagating across miles. The entire landscape feels like a factory floor designed by a mind that never discovered straight lines, a scaled production of biomass and transmuted minerals, running its cycles without a single engineered artifact. No built structure exists—nothing is constructed by hands or minds, yet the entire plateau is an engine.

The rules of this world are not our rules. The constant turnover, the Solaris-like creation and destruction, suggests that the vegetation is not growing toward any climax, but simply processing. It takes something from the atmosphere—perhaps thin airborne minerals, perhaps the energy of the cold wind—and converts it into architecture, then just as quickly disassembles it. The moisture cycle is a closed-loop: evaporation from the vents, condensation on the leaves, reabsorption through the sponge-floor. The silence is a byproduct of perfect acoustic impedance; every surface is tuned to swallow vibration. Even the rate of decay and regrowth seems calibrated to maintain a steady-state topography, an established erosion that has been in balance for millennia, the landscape as a whole neither advancing nor retreating, only churning in place.

Standing in it, you become aware of the density. This is not a place you walk through; it is a place you press into. The stems and leaves crowd near, some warm, some cold, all slick with damp. The air is thick with oxygen—perhaps too thick, a product of the unceasing photosynthesis that runs on some other light than the sun’s. The ground presses back against your soles, as if testing your weight. And though the silence is absolute, you might catch, at the edges of perception, a patterned flow in the movement of the vents and the collapse of the domes—a rhythm that almost resolves into a signal, a message broadcast in the only language this world has left: growth and dissolution, without end.
