The suns were wrong.

There were three of them today—cold white discs stacked in a triangle across the bright, bruised-silver sky—and their light fell without warmth, sharpening every edge of the Thrumming Steppe until the world looked etched on glass. By noon the discs would merge, or one would blink out, and the light would change direction without a single shadow moving. No one remembered how many suns were normal, or even if there had ever been a normal. The steppe accepted the light the way old bone accepts a chill: completely, silently, as something that had been happening forever.

You stand at the lip of a basin that shouldn’t exist at this altitude—a plateau gouged into the summit of a mountain range that has no name because names don’t stick here. The elevation is a physical weight on the lungs, the air thin and damp, and your breath plumes in bright mist that doesn’t rise so much as peel sideways into the general wetness. Water is everywhere. It weeps from the pale stone in steady rivulets, seeps up between pebbles underfoot, condenses on your skin faster than you can wipe it away. The ground is a saturated sponge of shattered rock and fine grit, and every step presses a footprint that fills immediately with clear, cold liquid welling from below. There are no streams, exactly—more a continuous, directionless movement of water across surfaces, as if the mountain itself is sweating out a fever. In the deeper crevices, pools lie mirror-still, and sometimes their surfaces tremble with no visible cause, sending concentric ripples that don’t obey the angle of the light.

There are no plants. The parameter said *no plant life*, and it is absolute: not a blade of grass, not a lichen, not a smear of algal green. But the steppe is not empty. In place of vegetation, mineral formations erupted from the wet stone in dense, vertical forests—thousands upon thousands of thin spires, pillars, and blades of rock, some no wider than your arm, others thick as ancient towers, all rising ten, twenty, thirty feet into the bright air. They are packed so closely in places you could not walk between them without turning sideways, their surfaces crawling with a filigree of crystal growth that looks like frozen frost but is warm to the touch. This is the “density” the parameters promised: a saturated, near-touching presence, a landscape of countless individual forms all competing for light that doesn’t nourish them. The rock is a pale, pearlescent grey, shot through with veins of something opalescent that shifts color when you look away—from blue to gold to a hesitant lavender. In the crystal patches, tiny bubbles of trapped water glint, and sometimes they pop audibly, releasing a chime-like sound that joins the great sound.

Ah, the sound. You’ve been hearing it since you crested the final ridge: a constant, resonant hum that thrums through the soles of your boots and the cage of your ribs. It is loud, not in a way that hurts but in a way that erases silence entirely. The wind—always present, always cool and wet—moves through the spires and pillars, and the spires sing. Each one is hollow or fluted or tuned by millennia of erosion to some specific note, and the collective result is a shifting, dissonant chord that never repeats but never resolves. Sometimes a deeper tone resonates from the pools, a liquid gong beat when a spire’s tremor hits the water just so. The hum is the voice of the place, and it has a physical presence, stirring the perpetual mists that wreathe the spires’ upper reaches into slow spirals that defy the wind’s direction.

Erosion here has reached an established, settled state—the parameters called it *in-its-own-state*, and you feel that. Nothing is raw; every edge is rounded or fluted or carved into runneled forms by water that has been working for eons. The spires are not crumbling actively; they are stable, finished, as if the mountain has accepted their shape as the correct one. Boulders lie embedded in the wet grit, their surfaces polished to a mirror sheen, and in them you see distorted reflections of the bright sky wheeling. The whole basin has the feel of a place that stopped changing a long time ago, but which is now held in an active, dynamic balance. Activity flickers everywhere: water moving, mist shifting, the hum modulating, crystal popping, the light changing. It’s not a dead landscape; it’s a landscape where the processes that elsewhere take geological ages have been compressed into a visible, breathing tempo.

Scattered across the steppe, half-hidden among the denser clusters of spires, are remnants of something built. Primitive structures—low walls of stacked, unworked stones, the occasional leaning dolmen or cairn—dot the basin in no discernible pattern. They aren’t ruins exactly; they’re too deliberate, too small, as if each was the work of a single person, and they were made with hand tools or no tools at all, the stones simply chosen to fit together. Some of the structures seem to be shelters, a few slabs tilted to form a lean-to. Others are markers or altars: a flat stone atop three uprights, its surface cupped to hold a pool of water that never overflows even as mist condenses on it. There are handprints in some places, pressed into a clay-like deposit that has since turned to stone, and they are human-shaped but wrong—too many fingers, or knuckles that bend backward. Whoever built these things did so long ago, and they are gone now, or they are hiding, or they have become something else. There is no animal life here; the parameter was clear. The fauna count is zero. The air is empty of insects, the pools empty of anything swimming. But sometimes, from the corner of your eye, you see movement between the spires—a shadow that isn’t cast by any sun, a flicker that resolves into nothing when you turn. The weirdness meter reads 2.7, reality breakdown, and that means the line between presence and absence has thinned to a smear.

The weirdness is most evident in the rules of the world. Water here does not always flow down; you watch a rivulet slide sideways along a spire’s flank, horizontal and patient, before dripping upward into a tiny overhang. A mist bank rolls through the pillars at knee height, and when it passes, the rocks it touched are a slightly different color, as if the mist erased a layer of age. Distance is unreliable: a spire that seemed twenty feet away is suddenly within arm’s reach, yet when you look back, the gap you crossed is as wide as ever. The three suns, when they still numbered three, cast shadows that point in different directions for the same object, and one of those shadows is darker than the others and faintly leached of color, like a hole in the visual spectrum. The temperature stays cool, a steady nip that never quite threatens freezing, but your skin prickles with a warmth that comes from inside the rocks—not geothermal heat but something more like stored light radiating back, a mineral fever.

Scale here is open; the basin stretches for miles, ringed by the shattered peaks of the massif, their ridges sharp as broken teeth. The spires, for all their density, are not an endless forest—they cluster in groves and thickets, separated by open flats of wet gravel and mirror pools, so you can see across half a mile of glinting, humming terrain before the next grove begins. It feels like a region, a self-contained world on top of the world, its boundaries defined by the encircling mountains whose lower slopes are black and sheer and wet, weeping sheets of water that never reach the basin floor but vaporize before they hit. The whole place is a condensation chamber, a cloud factory; above, the sky is a bright silver-white glare, but occasionally a rift in that glare shows a deeper darkness studded with not-stars, points of light that move too purposefully and blink out when you try to count them.

You walk deeper, and the sound changes with proximity to different spire clusters. One grove hums at a lower register that makes your teeth ache; another produces a fluted, almost-melody that feels like a half-remembered song. The ground is firm enough in places, but elsewhere the gravel gives way to patches of a jelly-like substance that vibrates underfoot—mineral pulp, maybe, or water held in a semi-solid state by the weird physics. Your boot prints last only moments before the jelly closes, the surface returning to a mirror that reflects not you but the sky as it was a few seconds ago. You kneel by a pool and see your face ripple, age, grow young, and then the reflection winks and you are staring at a different person entirely, one with eyes that know this place.

In the densest thicket of spires, you find the largest built structure yet: a ring of standing stones, each one carved—not with tools, but with what looks like fingers and water and immense patience—into a rough column, and in the ring’s center, a platform of polished obsidian that is dry, completely dry, the only dry surface in the basin. On it rests a single object: a bowl carved from the opalescent stone, filled with liquid that glows faintly blue and does not evaporate. Around the bowl, handprints in soot. This is technology at 1.1, primitive, handcrafted, perhaps pre-metal or simple metal, though you see no metal anywhere—only stone and water and light. The bowl hums when you approach, adding a new note to the chorus, and for a moment all the spires around you shift their pitch to harmonize with it. The light bends, the ground trembles, and a wave of vertigo passes as the basin’s reality briefly aligns into something almost comprehensible.

Then it passes. The suns—however many there are now—continue their erratic path. The water continues to weep and flow sideways and up. The spires continue to sing their endless, loud, active song. And you understand that this place is not a ruin; it is an ongoing event, a process that has reached its settled form but is still fully alive, just not with life as you define it. The parameters all hold: wet, cool, extreme elevation, bright light, no plants or animals, dense with non-living presences, scattered with primitive marks, loud with the sound of its own being, and thoroughly, irrevocably broken from whatever reality you came from. You find a lean-to among the spires, its slabs still dry inside, as if waiting, and you sit, and you listen, and you watch the water climb.
