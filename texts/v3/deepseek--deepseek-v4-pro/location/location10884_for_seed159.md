The way down comes not as a tunnel but as a throat—a long, spiraling fissure that exhales warm, wet air in slow, rhythmic gusts. The walls are slick with condensation, beaded like the skin of something alive, and in the failing light of the surface, they gleam with the unmistakable sheen of raw, untarnished mineral. This is new rock, geologically speaking: basalt that cooled mere centuries ago, sharp-edged and unweathered, its surfaces still crisp with the contraction cracks of its birth. No erosion has smoothed these passages. They are embryonic, a world still deciding its final form.

Then the light changes. Not abruptly, but as a gradual suffusion, a silver-gold glow that seeps into the air itself—normal light, daylight-bright, welling up from below. The throat opens, and you step onto a ledge overlooking a space so vast it takes the mind a moment to accept it: an open cavern that could swallow a town, its far walls lost in a haze of drifting vapor. The ceiling arches a thousand feet overhead, studded with the source of the light. It is not a single sun but a dense, fractal constellation of luminous nodules, each one a mineralized mound the size of a cartwheel, pulsing with a steady, sourceless radiance. They are alive, or were, or exist in some category that predates that distinction—a reef of light-producing crystals that grows in clustered, colonizing drifts across the stone. Together they cast a shadowless glow, warm-toned, that gives everything a slightly amber cast.

The air is loud. Not with echo, but with a continuous, multi-layered shriek—a dense frequency of animal noise so complex and so omnipresent that individual sounds can barely be parsed. There are shrill piping calls, gravelly chitters, the leathery whirr of thousands of wings, and beneath it all a droning hum, like the sound of a city heard from miles away, except it comes from all directions and from right beside you. The cave is alive with motion.

You notice the fauna gradually, because at first it reads as the environment itself moving. The rock floor across the cavern basin, a half-mile below, appears to ripple. It is not rock. It is a carpet of bodies—small six-limbed creatures, dog-sized, packed so densely they walk over one another without complaint, their flat, chitinous backs forming a living pavement that shifts in waves as pressure builds and releases. They are grazers of something invisible: their heads, each a tiny scoop-like mandible, rasp at the stone itself, scraping up a film of greyish algae? fungus? No, a mineral-eating bacterial mat that coats every stationary surface in a thin, glistening sludge. That is the only vegetation here, if the word applies: a biofilm of extreme simplicity, a pre-plant kingdom of single-celled lithovores that sweats up from the warm, damp rock. The grazers churn across it, and in their wake the mat re-grows visibly, a sheen spreading like oil on water.

Above this crawling sea, the air is packed with flyers. They are not birds but something like dragonfly-moths, their wingspans a foot across, their bodies long and segmented and bearing too many legs. They swarm in helical towers and avalanche down in sudden, synchronized plunges, chasing smaller fliers—gnat-things that spin in dense, spherical clouds. The clouds change shape constantly, bazaar-like in their chaotic reorganization, now stretching into ribbons, now contracting into tight, defensive balls. The predators slice through them with open, needle-toothed mouths, and the sound of their passage is a hiss like rain. There are larger shapes too, cruising above the melee: leather-skinned tetrapods with long, keeled snouts and no eyes, navigating by the echoes of their own bellowing calls. Each bellow is a low, chest-thumping note that you feel as much as hear.

Water is not absent but it is furtive. A river cuts across the basin floor, a broad, sluggish ribbon of almost-still water, its surface broken only by the movements of the creatures drinking or hunting along its edge. Elsewhere, moisture condenses on every overhang and drips in constant, tinny notes, collecting in shallow pools that wink with light. Steam vents pock the basin, issuing wet heat from some deeper geothermal source, and around them the fauna is thickest, a pulsing, writhing mass of competing bodies. The warmth feeds the mat, the mat feeds the grazers, the grazers feed the flyers, and the flyers fall and die and feed the mat again. It is an economy of sheer density, near-touching, layered, every niche crammed to over-spill.

Then there are the structures. Scattered across the higher ground on this side of the river, where the ledge broadens into terraces, small shelters have been built. They are not the work of the crawling grazers; these are deliberate constructions. Dome-shaped huts of stacked stone, their walls chinked with a thick, dark substance that might be dried biofilm or something less savory. Entryways gape open, low and narrow. They number maybe a dozen, scattered but within sight of one another, and from one a thread of smoke rises, carrying the smell of charred meat. Tools lie about: hand-axes of knapped flint and crude hammer-stones, some with hafts of long bone bound by sinew. A few show the dull glint of native copper, cold-hammered into blades. This is a settlement, or a camp, or the beginning of one—the faint marks of a people with hand-tools and the ability to shape metal, though just barely.

You catch a glimpse of one of them, a figure moving between two huts. It walks upright, but with a gait that is not quite human—the legs too long, the torso hinged forward, the arms hanging to the knees. Its skin is the same grey-brown as the rock, and it carries a woven basket, collecting something from the carcass of a dead flyer. It does not notice you. Its attention is on the sky, the swarming life, the constant, turbulent change that demands ceaseless vigilance. For this is not a static world. The dynamic here is the parameter that dominates all others: the cavern is in a state of storm-like creation and destruction. The light-nodules wax and wane, some nights dimming to near-darkness while others flare. The river changes course without warning, spilling into new channels carved in a single hour by flash floods from some unseen source. The steam vents erupt occasionally in gouts of scalding spray that kill everything in a wide radius, leaving fresh, raw rock for the mat to colonize first. And the fauna reacts—flees, swarms, dies, reproduces—with a manic, anthill energy that never rests. Birth and death are simultaneous, overlapping, a city of animals in perpetual, loud crisis.

This is a place just coming into being. The geology is raw, undecided. The ecosystem is a clenched fist of over-abundance. The light is inexplicably daylike underground. The fauna is overwhelming, the sound a physical force, and somewhere in the midst of it, a small, scattered tribe of tool-makers huddles and hunts and grubs a living, their structures barely a whisper against the roar of a world in the throes of its own embryonic, churning genesis. The air is warm, damp, alive, and utterly, beautifully deafening.
