You descend a spiral stair sliced into raw limestone, the air thickening with every step. By the time the stairs bottom out into the chamber, heat wraps you like a wet blanket. It’s a breathless, geothermal heat, the kind that lives in your lungs and dew-soaks every surface. The station is a single vast room—cathedral-scaled but tightly enclosed, perhaps fifty meters across and thirty high, its ceiling lost in a permanent gloom the bulbs can’t reach. A few yellow industrial lights droop from the blackness, their globes sweating, casting dim pools on platforms and water below.

The floor is mostly an open grid of metal grating suspended over churning, milky-blue pools. Those pools are the heart of the place: fissures in the cavern floor that bleed water superheated by something deep, something volcanic. The water never stops. It wells up, spills over tufa rims, cascades into lower basins with a sound like a distant dishwasher run amok. Steam rises in lazy, constant veils; droplets condense on every pipe, every beam, your skin, the lenses of your goggles. Every ten seconds, a larger drip splats into a pool from a stalactite of mineral fuzz that has begun, patiently, to wrap a steam pipe in fresh rock.

The infrastructure is the old, heavy kind: riveted iron, copper pipes gone green at the seams, a single Parsons turbine humming at the center on a concrete island. Catwalks span the void like bridgework, but there’s huge emptiness between them—the architect respected the cavern’s original hollows. You can stand on a platform and see no handrail for ten meters, only darkness and drifting steam. The place is, technically, a settlement: a hamlet of one giant machine and its attending walkways, a maintenance locker, a cable-elevator cage. It’s organized, but thinly arranged. Sparse.

What it has instead of furniture is life. Overwhelming, skittering, chittering life.

The crickets own this station. They are pale as rice noodles, long-antennated, with the blind, wandering gait of creatures that have never needed eyes. They don’t so much swarm as drift in permanent occupation—a carpet that shifts when you step near, a living grout that fills every crack in the concrete and every flange of the turbine housing. Their shed exoskeletons crunch underfoot in papery mats. They climb the walls in slow, continuous motion, and when one falls it lands with a soft *pik* on your shoulder, then scuttles off. The air is a steady rustle made of tiny legs. At rest, they chirp: a metallic, two-toned chorus that answers the turbine’s thrum, so constant it becomes a texture rather than a sound. Together, the machine and the insects make the place loud—not painfully so, but you’d have to raise your voice to be heard.

Lizards work the edges, fat house geckos gone pale as everything else, whose slow-flicking tongues are the only visible reaction to the heat. They cluster on the warmest pipe-joints, upside-down, motionless for hours. Now and then a cricket strays too close and a gecko pulses forward like a drop of spilled mercury; the rest of the time they barely acknowledge the world.

There is almost no vegetation. In the bluish halo of the steam, a scatter of moss glows faintly on the wettest rocks—*Schistostega*, perhaps, or some heat-tolerant cousin—forming a few pale green stars. That’s it. No ferns, no grass. The mineral deposition outruns anything botanical. This is the “emerging” the geologists talk about: the cavern isn’t eroding away, it’s being built, layer by slow layer. Every pipe drip swells the tufa lips around the pools; the steam etches a microscopic skin of calcite on every iron rail. Workers come through twice a year with hammers and acid sprays to hold back the geology, to stop the station from sealing itself into a solid block of travertine. It’s a battle fought at a geological pace—imperceptible minute to minute, relentless across seasons.

There’s nothing fantastical here. Just a hot, wet box of stone and industry, given over to small tenant-creatures that multiply in the dark. If you stand still long enough, the crickets forget you, and you can watch the steam curl around the bulbs, listen to the *drip, hiss, chirr*, and feel the whole place breathing its slow breath, building itself grain by grain while the turbine spins on.
