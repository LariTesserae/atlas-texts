The place is a plain, but it was never a natural one. Under the cold weight of the ocean, it stretches in all directions with the memory of geometry: a city worn down to its bones, then drowned so long ago that the sea has forgotten the surface.

The water is a black lens. At this depth—shallow for an ocean, but deep enough that the surface is a rumor—the light arrives only as a teal-gray ghost, a residual bleed from some far-off open sky that could be a lie. It never truly illuminates; it just makes the darkness less absolute, a charcoal murk where shape and void become negotiable. The cold is a constant presence, a dense, slow-fingered chill that hovers a breath above freezing, heavy with salts and the faint burn of minerals. It wraps every surface in a sluggish envelope, muffling even the act of sight.

Everything underfoot is made. There is no bedrock, no natural sand, no primordial silt. The "ground" is an interlocking puzzle of fitted stone blocks, each one hand-squared by some ancient, patient labor. But time—eons of it, quiet and relentless—has almost erased the craft. The blocks are rounded now, softened like old soap, their edges blunted into one another so thoroughly that they feel like a single undulating pavement. In places they’ve settled into shallow basins where the water pools stiller than elsewhere; in others they’ve tilted, forming long, low ridges that vanish into the gloom. The scale of the work is incomprehensible: this floor of made stone goes on for hundreds of miles, a continent of artifice where every inch was once placed by intent.

Rising from this floor are the densest remains of what stood above. Pillars, walls, the stubs of towers—all reduced to their foundations, their highest points no taller than a person’s reach. They are packed so closely that swimming between them means constant navigation, threading gaps just wide enough for a body, skimming over toppled lintels and collapsed vaults. The material is varied: some are the same grey-black basalt-like stone as the floor, others a paler, pitted limestone that crumbles if you look too hard. Every surface is textured by erosion’s fingerprints—not sharp fractures but deep, pocked wear, as if the water has been rubbing its thumbs over them since the world was young.

This artificial skeleton hosts a forest. In the cold dark, vegetation flourishes in an almost obscene abundance. Giant kelp anchors itself in the cracks between stones, sending up serpentine stipes as thick as a wrist, climbing toward a light that barely exists. Their fronds are broad and leathery, undulating in a current so slow it takes minutes to see them shift. They’re a deep olive-brown, almost black in this dimness, and their surfaces are grizzled with epiphytes: finer, hair-like algae that drape in curtains, and patches of a bluish encrusting life that might be a kind of bacterial mat. The kelp forms a canopy overhead, a swaying, interlocked roof that swallows the faint light and returns nothing but deeper shadow. Between the pillars, a dense understory of shorter algae—rust-colored, feathery, and fat, succulent-looking fronds that cluster like submerged ferns—covers every horizontal surface, softening the stone geometries into baroque, organic contours. There is no bare rock anywhere; even the vertical faces carry a thick fur of diatoms and pale cyanobacteria, giving them a patina of sickly gold or silver depending on a light that isn’t there.

The vegetation is the only life. There is no animal movement, no fish flickering in the corners of the eye, no scuttling crustaceans, no worms in the silt that isn’t. The water is empty of everything but plants. This absence is a rule of the place, as fundamental as the cold. Fronds wave, but nothing comes to graze them. Spores drift in lazy clouds, fall, and germinate in a silent, closed cycle of growth and decay that feeds only itself. The ecology is a single kingdom’s soliloquy.

Sound is sparse but not absent. The cold, thick water transmits low frequencies with a kind of visceral boom. Very occasionally, a deep groan rolls through—a stone block, under centuries of stress, finally shifting a hair’s breadth. The kelp, when it moves in a rare pulse of current, makes a sound like the rubbing of wet canvas, a slow, deliberate whisper. There’s a constant, almost sub-audible crackle that might be the combined noise of countless tiny gas vesicles in the algae popping, or the minute exhalations of photosynthesis in the dark. Otherwise, silence: a heavy, insulating quiet that makes the few sounds feel like intrusions from another age.

The air would be water, but there is no air. The rules here are liquid and ancient. Everything happens at a pace that resists human perception. The currents are tidal, but the tide is a half-month pulse, not a daily one; the water shifts laterally in a deliberate seepage, carrying a barely perceptible load of dissolved stone and detritus from one gently inclining plain to another. The process of erosion is so mature it is almost stable—a din of slow dissolution that has reached its quietest phase. The city, the forest, the sea: they have settled into a shared final form, a negotiation of mutual surrender. The built world has been taken apart and worn down to its essence, then given back by the vegetation as a scaffold for more building, a weaving of stone and flesh so complete that the original distinction is philosophical.

There is a faint, pervasive glow that is not light in any conventional sense. It’s a side-effect of the cyanobacteria that coat the oldest northern faces of the pillars; they phosphoresce a dim, steady blue-green when the darkness is purest. It outlines the pillars in ghostly rims, a contour of cold fire that reveals the density of the place—pillar after pillar, wall after wall, fading into a haze of glowing edges until the world seems to be a boundless honeycomb of flooded rooms, each holding its own quiet forest. The glow pulses, too, but with a rhythm tied to the temperature of the water, growing imperceptibly brighter as the chill deepens through the long winter of the deep, then dimming over centuries in a cycle no living eye could map.

This is a place made by hands that knew only stone-on-stone, by beings who shaped their entire world and then, for reasons untold, departed or perished or simply stopped. What remains is total: no wilderness, no untouched ground to return to. The made thing has become the environment, and the environment has embraced it back into a kind of wildness. It is a vast, dark, cold, dense, slow garden of sunken architecture where the only breathing is green.
