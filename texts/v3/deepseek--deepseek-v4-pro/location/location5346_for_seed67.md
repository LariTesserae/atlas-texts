The floor is warm iron, ribbed in endless repeating lines that run straight to the vanishing point in every direction — a grid pressed into metal plates the size of city blocks, their seams sealed with something dark and slightly yielding that gives off a faint mineral smell when you step on it. The iron itself is never dry. Condensation beads on every surface, a slick of moisture that reflects the orange glow of the lights in long smeared streaks. The humidity is total, a weight in the lungs, a second skin. It gathers in the seams and trickles somewhere below, a quiet sound you can't quite locate, as if the whole place is slowly weeping.

Above, the ceiling is lost to distance. There are lights up there, hanging from gantries you can barely make out — a diffuse amber haze that thickens and thins in slow, patient cycles, never quite brightening or dimming but shifting in some way that suggests respiration. The lights are probably electric, or something like it. There's a hum, a frequency felt more than heard, a vibration that lives in the sternum and the soles of the feet. It never changes pitch. It has been the same pitch for a very long time.

Across the iron plain, things move. They are everywhere — not swarming, not chaos, just presence, a density of bodies that fills the space from the floor to as high as you can see. They are not animals in any way you recognize, but the parameters say fauna and so they are fauna: vast herds of something that might once have been mechanical, their bodies built of the same iron as the floor, their limbs jointed with pistons and cables that flex with a wet, organic sound. They have no heads you can identify, no faces, but they are covered in what might be fur — fine black filaments that ripple as they move, each strand beaded with water. They brush against each other constantly. The space is too full for them not to. There is nowhere they are not touching.

When they touch, a sound emerges: a low, soft chime, like a tuning fork struck underwater. It rings for a moment and then fades into the hum. Multiply this by the thousand, the hundred thousand, and the silence is not silence but a fabric of overlapping tones, a quiet so dense it becomes a kind of pressure. No single sound rises above the rest. The whole population moves in a flow that is utterly constant, a circulation that has no beginning or end, no source or destination. They are just moving, together, around the iron world, and they have been moving forever.

The terrain rises in places — not hills but housings, rectangular extrusions of riveted metal that vent steam in long, patient exhales. The steam is warm, almost body temperature, and it joins the general wetness without disturbing it. These structures are factories, or were, or are continuously becoming factories. Inside them, through gaps in the metal, you can see motion: gears the size of houses turning at speeds too slow to perceive directly, their teeth perpetually meshing, their motion visible only in the way the condensation on their surfaces shifts over hours. They produce nothing. They simply turn. The hum comes from them, or they come from the hum.

There is no natural substrate anywhere. Even the air tastes constructed — flat, metallic, carrying a faint tang of oil and ozone and something else, something organic that might be the exhalations of the fauna or might be the fauna themselves slowly breaking down into their component parts. The floor below the floor, if there is one, is unreachable. Digging would reveal more iron, more seams, more of the same warm weeping metal, down and down until the concept of "ground" becomes meaningless.

The scale of it swallows you. From any point on the floor, the iron plain extends in all directions beyond the limits of sight, curving faintly if you watch long enough — a suggestion that this is not a flat world but the interior of something spherical, something built entirely around a core of warm, humming industry. Weather happens here, though there is no sky: slow migrations of thicker mist that move across the herds, condensation that falls in sheets from the upper darkness, a kind of rain that is just the accumulated moisture of a million exhaling vents reaching critical mass and releasing all at once. The fauna don't react to it. Nothing reacts to anything. The equilibrium is perfect.

Strange, the parameters said, and it is strange — not in any shocking or alien way but in the quiet, persistent wrongness of a place where every surface is intentional and nothing has ever grown. Where warmth and water and motion suggest life but the life is made of iron and oil and has no biology to speak of. Where the only ecology is a closed loop of bodies brushing bodies, sounding their underwater chimes, following paths laid down in the metal by their own unvarying passage. The weirdness is in the completeness of it, the totality of the construction, the sense that this place was not built so much as it decided to exist, fully formed, and has been maintaining itself in perfect stasis ever since.

In the distance — though distance here is a slippery thing — the herds thicken into a mass so dense it becomes a wall, a surface of moving black filaments and gleaming metal joints that rises up into the haze. There is no horizon. There is only that wall of bodies, shifting imperceptibly, and the sound of them, and the warmth, and the wet iron underfoot, and the hum that has no source and no end. Somewhere in that wall, if you watched for long enough — hours, days, years — you might see a single component complete its cycle, a piston reach the end of its stroke and begin its return. But you would have to be very patient. The place is not going anywhere. It has all the time it needs.
