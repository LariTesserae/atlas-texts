The place is a pocket — a single, vast, stepped cavity sunk into a greater world of baked salt and ferrous stone. You could walk across the crust above for a day and see only heat-shimmer and the white flats stretching away, and then you would find the lip: a crumbling overhang where the ground simply falls away into a great hidden hollow, like a bubble that cooled in the planet's skin. The cavity is perhaps two hundred paces across and forty deep, a single contained feature, and it holds an entire settlement within its walls.

The atmosphere inside is dim. The sun above is a knife of actinic white that cuts down only at noon, and for the rest of the cycle the light comes from elsewhere — from the muted glow of hot mineral seams in the walls, from the slow ember-pulse of charcoal beds, from the faint blue luminescence of certain growths. The air itself is thick with heat, a steady 2.8 that never relents, pressing on the skin like a second body. It is a dry heat, moistureless to the point of cracking stone, for there is no water here at all, not a drop, not a bead of condensation. The chemistry of this place has found another path.

And yet the cavity is lush.

The vegetation is not green. It does not use water. It grows in great profusion across the terraced walls of the hollow — tangled, ropey, fleshy things that draw their substance from the mineral-laden vapors seeping from deep vents. The growths are the color of old bronze and tarnished copper, with occasional blooms of sulfur-yellow and a deep, bruise-purple. They spread in thick mats that hang like pelts from every ledge, and they rise in spiraling towers that mimic the forms of coral or antler, built grain by grain from precipitated salts and metallic oxides. In the dim light they glisten faintly, their surfaces slick with oils rather than water, and they give off a dense, spicy, almost animal smell — hot metal and fermentation, a living reek. The word "lush" applies not because the growths are soft or watery, but because they are abundant, layered, intricate, overgrown. Every surface below a certain angle of repose is thick with them, a continuous pelt of living chemistry that pulses with an internal rhythm, slow and peristaltic.

There is no animal life. No fauna. Not a skittering leg, not a wing, not a burrow. The movement in this place is all on other scales: the slow creep of the bronze vines as they adjust their positions toward new vent-cracks over the course of days; the shudder of a spore-case opening; the human and non-human hands that tend the growths.

For this is an urban place. The hollow holds a settlement — a town, a city even, built into the terraced walls and the floor of the cavity. The people here are not many, but their construction is dense, layered, organized. The architecture climbs the stepped walls in tiers of stacked stone and packed mineral-fiber bricks, each tier anchored to the living growths or woven through them. Rope bridges and notched-log ladders connect the levels, spanning gaps where the salt crusts are unreliable. Dwellings are small, low-ceilinged, their walls often curving to match the natural contour of the cavity, their roofs sometimes the floor of the terrace above. Windows are narrow, unglazed, and many rooms are carved directly into the harder ferrous stone, their inner walls worn smooth by generations of rubbing shoulders and hands.

The technology is primitive — hand-tools, simple metalworking, manual craft. The people work iron smelted from the same ferrous stone that forms the cavity walls, drawing it from small bloomery furnaces dug into the terrace lips, where the heat of the vents can be channeled. Their hammers are stone-headed; their saws are toothed iron blades worked with sweat. They cultivate the bronze vines with hooked knives and bare hands, scraping the oily films into clay pots, cutting away the oldest towers to clear space for new growth. The entire settlement is a machine of living heat and human labor, and it is loud.

The sound is loud in this pocket, because every act reverberates in the enclosed space. The thud of a hammer on iron rebounds from wall to wall, layered over the lower, constant sound of the vents — a deep, thrumming chuff-chuff that never stops, the breath of the earth itself. Voices carry. The rasp of saws, the chime of dropped tools on stone, the crack of splitting salt-crust, the faint high ringing of stressed metal cooling — it all mixes into a constant wash of noise that fills the hollow like the heat does. Even at rest, the place hums with the creak of the growths, the falling of dry debris, the mutter of charcoal settling. There is no quiet here.

And everything is active. The vents shift their flow over weeks and months; new cracks open, old ones seal with mineral precipitate. The bronze vines sense this and begin their slow migration, sending out exploratory tendrils that harden into permanent trunks, abandoning old branches to dry and crumble. The people follow the growths, dismantling and rebuilding terraces, re-laying ladderways, shifting the bloomeries. The cycle is not predator-prey — there is no predation at all — but it has the same dynamic restlessness, a biological pace governed by heat and mineral gradients instead of hunger. Weather, of a kind, occurs: when the vent-pressure builds to a certain point, a great gout of hot, sulfurous gas can burst forth and fill the bottom of the hollow, forcing everyone to the upper terraces for a day until the atmosphere clears. The people track these rhythms with notches on tally-sticks and with an intimate, bodily knowledge of the sounds and smells that precede an event.

The scale is hidden, intimate despite the urban density. From above, the hollow is a crack, a pinprick, invisible until you stand at the very lip. From within, the world is the walled sky of the cavity, the dim glow of the seams, the tangle of bronze vegetation, the stepped geometry of roofs and terraces rising all around. A single feature contained within itself.

The density is near-empty, not because the settlement is sparse, but because the space above it is vast. The hollow arches upward forty paces to the crust, and in that upper region there is almost nothing — only the occasional bridge-rope stretching between the highest terraces, the faint upward reach of the tallest vine-towers, and the slow drift of hot gases. The emptiness above is palpable; it gives every sound a long reverberation and makes the sky of the place feel like a held breath. The people live in the lower and middle tiers, packed into the rich zone where the growths are thickest and the heat is most constant, and the great void above them is a presence as real as the walls.

There are no animals. No birds cross that empty upper space. No insects whine in the growths. The only motion besides the people and the vegetation is the movement of heat-shimmer and vapor, and the slow geological settling of the salt-crust that occasionally sends a fine white dust raining down from the rim. The ecology is a closed loop of mineral exhalation, chemical deposition, and the human-mediated harvest. The people eat the oily paste they scrape from the bronze vines — a substance dense with sulfurous amino compounds and inorganic nutrients that their bodies, over who knows how many generations, have adapted to metabolize. They build with the dried stalks. They burn the crystals that form in the abandoned tunnels.

It is a world without water, but it is not a desert. It is an intimate, enclosed, loud, hot, dim, mineral-lush city secreted in a pocket beneath an empty salt plain, kept alive by the breath of the earth and the labor of human hands, in a cycle that has settled into a weathered, long-existing state, still active, still changing, but in balance with itself.
