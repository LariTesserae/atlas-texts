The plateau holds the city like a memory of a fever dream — a monument to industry stretched across a continent’s uplifted spine, so high that the sky feels thin and jaundiced, a permanent amber noon. It is warm here, a dry, enveloping warmth that never peaks into heat, just a steady exhalation from the ground, from the metal, from the slow oxidation of things that haven't moved in millennia. The air is perfectly still most days, carrying no moisture, no scent of life except the faint mineral tang of rust and the ozone whisper of sleeping circuits. This is a world without water, where chemistry runs on metal salts and silicon gels, where the only liquid is the sluggish seep of lubricant oozing from ancient seams, black and viscous, pooling in gutters etched by wind-blown grit across ceramic pavements.

There are no plants. In their place, a filigree of mycelial growths spreads through the infrastructure — pale, branching tubes thinner than a hair, coating the undersides of gantries and threading through cracked conduits. They are not quite alive in the traditional sense, more like a nervous system of precipitated minerals, a crystalline lattice that grows so slowly it takes a century to bridge a metre. They do not photosynthesise; they feed on thermal gradients and the faint piezoelectric pulses of the city’s dying machines. They are the closest thing to vegetation, and they are woven into every surface, a fragile lace that crumbles to dust at a touch.

The city itself is vast — scale-of-its-own-physics vast. From any vantage, the built environment recedes into a blue-grey haze of distance, towers and bridges and ziggurat-like factories stepping down the endless slopes of the plateau. It is urban, undeniably: a planned, layered construction of colossal proportions, designed for millions but now holding only a sparse population of things that skitter and perch. The density is low, immense empty plazas separating clusters of functional hulks, each cluster perhaps a former district, now an island of low hums and flickering lights in a sea of silent debris. The buildings are extreme in elevation, too — spires that seem to pierce the stratosphere, their tops lost in the perpetual amber overcast, their bases rooted in canyons where the plateau fractures, dropping away into depths that breathe their own microclimates of warm, stale air.

Erosion has had its way, but patiently, over geological spans. The edges of the structures are not sharp; they are rounded, bulbous, like melted wax. Wind has sculpted the metal into flowing forms, great billowing ripples frozen in mid-surge across the sides of hangars. There are no sand dunes — there is no sand, only a fine metallic dust that stirs occasionally and settles in drifts of iron-grey powder. The erosion is established, settled, the landscape in a long-sustained equilibrium between the forces that built it and the forces that gently dissolve it. It is a place that has existed this way for eons, its own state of being, neither decaying nor renewing but simply persisting in a slowed metabolism.

And there is life. Abundant, strange, quiet life. The fauna are the city’s pulse. They are not organic in any Earthly sense — more like mobile rust-forms, creatures of layered oxide and flexible ceramic, jointed and segmented, everything from thumbnail-sized scrapers that harvest the mycelial lace to lumbering hexapods the size of houses that stride between the empty blocks on piston legs, their bodies a collection of bolted plates and glowing indicator lights. They are silent. They make no vocalisations, only the mechanical sounds of their motion: a faint whirring, the occasional clink of a joint, the soft hiss of pneumatics. In the aggregate, these sounds merge into a low, pervasive background texture like the breathing of the city itself. They are everywhere, yet you might walk for an hour and see only a few, distant, moving with purpose along predetermined paths, following gradients of electromagnetism or thermal updrafts that we cannot sense.

Some are small and quick, swarming over the warmer surfaces in great numbers, their bodies iridescent with oil-slick colours. Others are vast and solitary, standing motionless for years, then relocating half a step in a week, attending to some endless internal calculation. They do not seem to eat or reproduce in any observable way; they are simply present, part of the ecosystem of the built environment, perhaps originally tools or workers of the ones who constructed this place, now feral and self-sustaining. The mycelial network connects some of them, acting as a data bus and a nutrient delivery system, feeding them the slow harvest of corrosion and decay.

The light is normal, a diffused amber glow that casts soft shadows, never harsh, because the atmosphere is thick enough to scatter but permanently overcast by a high layer of something — maybe metallic vapour, maybe a by-product of the city’s ancient breath. There is no day-night cycle; the light is constant, unchanging, as if the planet is tidally locked and this plateau always faces the sun at a slant. The temperature is warm and unwavering, a permanent late-spring afternoon that has held for geological ages.

The soundscape is one of almost-silence. Mostly quiet, the city absorbs its own noise. The hum of a distant engine, running on some inexhaustible geothermal tap, is so low and steady that it becomes more felt than heard, a vibration in the soles. Occasionally, a structural groan echoes from deep below, the tectonic adjustment of a tired beam, but it’s swallowed quickly by the vast emptiness. There is no wind to whistle, no water to drip. The quiet is a physical presence, a pressure on the eardrums, broken only by the near-subliminal clicks and hisses of the fauna as they go about their slow business.

This is a place of slow dynamics. Change happens over ten-thousand-year arcs. The erosion is patient, the creatures’ migrations are graduated, the industrial processes — whatever they still produce — run on cycles too long to perceive. A factory might thrum to life once a millennium to extrude a single perfect beam, then fall silent again. The city is not dead; it is a machine running at a time scale mismatched from human perception. The weirdness of it is absolute but understated, a profound alien logic that has settled into a routine so vast it feels like stasis. The sparse scatter of visible elements against the monumental scale creates a kind of resonant emptiness, not desolate but content, like a library holding its breath between readers.
