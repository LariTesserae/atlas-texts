The air does not move so much as it sweats. A heat deep enough to blur the distinction between breath and broth presses in from all sides, and with it comes the wet—not rain, exactly, though droplets bead on every surface, but a pervasive, condensing damp that feels like the world is exhaling through its skin. The light is the color of tarnished pewter, a dim and uniform glow that seems to seep out of the sky itself, though there is no visible sun, only a low ceiling of cloud or something cloud-like that undulates with a sluggish, muscular rhythm. Beneath that, stretching to every horizon, lies the Floor.

The Floor is, and is being, made. It is a construct of staggering scale, a continent of hand-worked material that has no name in any living tongue. Imagine a plain of fired clay, but not flat: it rolls in long, shallow swells and dips, like a sea swell frozen in terracotta, every contour shaped by intent. The material is a reddish, iron-rich mud, baked to the hardness of stone yet still somehow porous enough to bead with moisture. In some places it is smooth as slipware; in others it bears the marks of tools—fingerprints the size of river channels, the scrape of a wooden rib, the coil seams of giant pots. The surface is warm to the touch, almost feverish, and the heat rises from it in shimmering ghosts that twist and vanish. There is no natural substrate anywhere. Every grain of this world, from the finest silt to the distant mesas, has been brought here and built up. It is a total made thing, a ceramic planet or the floor of some kiln the size of a moon.

And it is unfinished. That is the first rule you sense: this place is still in the act of becoming. Erosion, here, runs backward. Rather than wearing down, the landscape is emerging, still raw and embryonic. In the middle distance, a ridge is rising. You watch it happen. The mud swells from underneath like dough proving in an oven, pushing up a long, low escarpment that cracks at the crest, oozing fresh, wet clay down its flanks. The cracks hiss with steam. This is not violent uplift but a kind of slow, ceaseless parturition, a geology of midwifery. Farther off, a hollow sinks into being, as if the ground is inhaling. The whole vast pan is in a state of turbulent creation, never settling, always adjusting. It is as restless as a storm sky, but expressed in solid earth. The dynamism is chaotic but not random—there is a sense of purpose, of hands you cannot see, shaping.

And yet, it is mostly quiet. The sounds that do break the hush are low and intermittent: a deep, basso groan as a new valley forms, the crackle of cooling crust, the plink of a single drop of water falling from a low overhang into a shallow basin that wasn't there a moment ago. The silence is not empty but full of the tension of something being born. Occasionally, a faint tapping drifts across the expanse—the sound of tool on clay, rhythmic and far away, though no maker is visible. It stops, then starts again, never quite locatable.

Water is everywhere, but it does not flow in rivers. It weeps from the ground itself. In the low swales, the clay exudes a thin, silvery scum of water that reflects the dim sky like a polished mirror. It is warm, near body temperature, and faintly alkaline, smelling of wet earth and something metallic—like old coins. You see no springs, no channels; the water simply appears on the surface the way sweat appears on skin, gathering in the shallow dips that the land is constantly making and remaking. Sometimes a pool will suddenly drain as the ground beneath it opens a throat and swallows, leaving behind a ring of fine, glittering salt that quickly gets swallowed by the next swell of fresh clay. The atmosphere itself is a saturated sponge; breathe in, and you taste mineral and moisture, as if you are inhaling the exhalation of a million tiny pores.

There is no vegetation anywhere. Not a blade of grass, not a lichen, not a stain of algae. The landscape is utterly mineral. But it is not dead, because it was never alive in that sense. Instead, you begin to notice other presences—structures. They are sparse, scattered across the immense emptiness like forgotten tools. Here, a pillar of stacked, unfired brick, each one bearing a handprint so large it would cover your chest. There, a low wall that curves in a perfect arc for a hundred yards and then simply stops, its end a rough stump of clay still glistening. Some of these forms are geometric: cones, stepped pyramids, shallow dishes the size of amphitheaters. Others are disturbingly organic: a series of twisted, branching tubes that look like fossilized intestines, rising waist-high from the plain before diving back under. All of them are made of the same red clay as the Floor, and all of them are being made by something. The technique is primitive—pinching, coiling, slab-building, the earliest methods of hand-craft. There is no evidence of the wheel, no metal, no fired glaze beyond the dull kiln-skin of the ground. It is a technology of mud and water and heat and hands.

But there are no animals here, no fauna at all. No birds cross the dim sky. No insects skitter on the warm clay. The only movement is the slow, ceaseless flux of the land itself and, if you watch long enough, the structures. A pillar leans, straightens, thickens at the base. A wall extends by a few new bricks, which appear to coalesce out of the wet air—moisture condensing into solid form, particle by particle, like frost growing on a pane, but in reverse time. The process is visible if you hold still and let your eyes un-focus. The world is being hand-built at the edge of perception.

And then the weirdness deepens. The rules begin to fray. Distance here is not constant. You will notice a spire far away that seems small, then you look again and it is huge and right in front of you, though you haven't moved. The horizon sometimes bends upward at the corners, as if the whole plain is the inside of a bowl, and the dim light comes from all directions equally. Shadows are wrong: a pillar might cast two shadows, one sharp and one blurred, pointing in opposite angles. Or none at all. The air occasionally thickens into a gel, holding you gently, then releases. Time, too, is unreliable. You might watch a valley form in what feels like minutes, while a single drop of water hangs on a ledge for an hour. The landscape is a dream of clay, and the dreamer is not quite sane, or not bound by the physics you know.

In the quiet, you begin to pick out a sound like breathing—vast, slow, the inhale spanning a minute, the exhale a little longer. It is not the wind; there is no wind. It seems to come from the ground itself, or from the whole sphere of the world. It is the sound of the making, the breath of a demiurge shaping its first pot on a wheel too large to see. And you, standing on the warm, damp, endless Floor, are perhaps a mote of dust in that breath, or a witness, or the next brick waiting to be laid. The landscape does not care. It just continues to become, sparsely populated with its own unfinished thoughts, hot and wet and dim and turbulent, in a quiet that is the most patient noise you have ever heard.
