The place has no name, because there is no one here who would name it. If you arrived—and you did not arrive, there was no journey, you are simply present now in the way that facts are present—you would first notice the light. It is a dim light, a gray-gold light, and it does not come from a sun. It suffuses the air itself, which is not air but a vapor thick with water and spores and the microscopic breath of plants. The atmosphere condenses on every surface, on every curve and ridge of what you might call the ground, though the ground here is not made of soil or stone. The ground is made of other ground that has lived and died and lived again.

You are high. Not high as on a mountain peak with a view of lesser things, but high in the sense that you are within a vast elevated plateau of living mass, a continent of vegetation that has risen so slowly, over such unthinkable time, that the concept of elevation relative to sea level has lost all meaning. There is no sea. There is no bedrock within reach. The entire world, as far as the dim light penetrates, is plant—plant as architecture, plant as geography, plant as the substrate of all existence.

The terrain undulates in broad, lazy swells. Hills that are not hills but the accumulated trunks of ancient growth forms, now compressed into a spongy, fibrous material that holds water the way a soaked cloth holds water. The surface is never dry. Water seeps from everything: from the weeping faces of exposed cross-sections where some slow collapse opened a wound; from the dangling curtains of aerial roots that hang from every overhead structure; from the pocked, porous skin of the living mounds. When you step—and you do not step, you are drifting, observing—the ground yields and water wells up around the impression, filling it immediately, a perfect cast of your absence. The water is warm, body-warm, and it carries in it a faint sweetness, the dissolved sugars of millennia.

Sound. It is mostly quiet, but what sound exists is continuous and low. A deep, almost sub-audible hum, the composite of a trillion tiny processes: water moving through capillary channels, cell walls stretching and relaxing, the slow exhalation of gases from breathing surfaces. Occasionally a sharper sound punctuates the drone—a distant, wet crack as some massive frond or plate, having grown too heavy with accumulated moisture, shears away and falls. The fall takes a long time. The atmosphere is thick, and things descend through it with a languid, dreamlike slowness. The impact, when it comes, is muffled, a soft thud absorbed by layers of living cushion.

You begin to perceive the scale. It is vast. The hummocks and swales repeat in every direction, fading into the luminous haze without a horizon line to break them. There is no sky, only a ceiling of vegetation so high and so dense that it has become its own weather system. Up there, in the permanent twilight of the canopy, moisture condenses into a perpetual fine drizzle that never reaches the lower levels as rain but instead drifts down as a cooling mist. The mist moves in slow, visible currents, like a second, thicker atmosphere flowing beneath the first. This place is large enough to have its own interior climate, its own slow-circling winds that carry spores and heat and the smell of humus from one region to another.

The strangeness gathers as you perceive more. This is not a forest. It is not a swamp. It is a single continuous living entity, or a community of entities so thoroughly interwoven that the distinction has no meaning. The vegetation—2.7 out of 3, total vegetation, pure plant ecosystem—is everything. The "trees," if you can call them that, are not individuals with trunks and crowns. They are columns of densely packed vascular tissue that merge seamlessly with the ceiling and the floor, like the pillars of a cathedral designed by no mind. Between them, filamentous networks span the gaps, some as fine as spider silk, others as thick as cables, all of them glistening with condensed moisture. These are structural, communicative, nutritive—the mycelial analogue of a kingdom that never evolved fungi because the plants themselves took on that role.

The density is palpable. There are many presences per volume. The space is not empty space; it is filled, saturated. Layers of leaf and tendril and air-root and fruiting body overlap and interpenetrate. To look in any direction is to look through multiple planes of translucent tissue, each one a slightly different shade of deep green, olive, amber, or the bruised purple of oxygen-depleted vascular fluid. Light filters through these layers, diffracting, so that the whole visible world has the quality of looking through a thick slice of jade held up to a clouded sky.

And yet, for all the density, there is movement. Fauna, abundant fauna, but fauna that has evolved to be of this place, which is to say it is as much plant as animal. Creatures drift through the saturated air, their bodies little more than gas-filled bladders trailing fine cilia. They photosynthesize and digest and move all at once, blurring every boundary. Slower things creep along the surfaces: flat, lobe-shaped beings that flow like amoebae but are structured from cellulose and lignin, leaving faint trails of enzymatic dissolution behind them. Nothing moves quickly. The dynamic of this world is slow, a 1.2 out of 3, patient processes driven by weak gradients. The temperature is warm and constant. The water cycle takes eons. Growth and decay are so nearly in balance that the landscape seems frozen in time, though it is actually a single continuous slow-motion event.

You become aware of the ancientness. It is 2.7 out of 3, primordial, settled-into-final-form. This place has been like this for geologic ages. All the sharp edges of the world have been worn away, not by wind or water in the abrasive sense, but by the relentless, gentle pressure of life layering over life. Every surface is rounded. Every cavity is filled. There are no cliffs, no ravines, only the soft, draped, upholstered contours of a world that has been entirely digested and rebuilt by the botanical. The very concept of erosion here is organic—not the stripping away of material, but its constant reprocessing. The oldest parts of the landscape are not rocks but the compressed heartwood of ancestors that lived before the atmosphere thickened to its current state, now buried miles deep in the massif, slowly, slowly hydrating and softening into a kind of lignite peat.

You notice the scattered built elements, rated 1.3 out of 3. They are the most difficult to see, because they have been almost entirely absorbed. Here, a straight line in a world of curves: the edge of a wall, or what was once a wall, now furred over with absorptive rhizoids and serving as the skeleton for a cascade of epiphytes. There, a geometric opening, a doorway or a window, its lintel sagging under the weight of centuries of accumulated living matter, its interior filled with a dark, still pool of water and the blind, pale fronds of things that grow in total darkness. The structures are small, single-buildings, widely scattered, and they are so ancient that they might be natural formations—were it not for the occasional hint of symmetry, the ghost of an angle, the faint trace of a material that is not and never was alive. Metal, perhaps, or fired clay. A technology level of nearly zero remains now. Whatever beings built these things had a technology, once, but that was before the vegetation took the world, before the long, slow, warm, wet equilibrium settled in. Their legacy is these faint marks, these partial remains, these calcium-rich nodules in the great body of the plant-world, slowly dissolving and being repurposed as nutrients.

There are no devices. No tools. The creatures here have no need of them. Their biology is their technology. The landscape itself is the only machine, a vast, slow, photosynthesizing, water-pumping, gas-exchanging engine that has been running without interruption for millions of years.

The atmosphere presses in. It is not oppressive, but it is enveloping. The dim light, the warm saturated air, the low hum, the softness underfoot and overhead and on every side—it has the quality of an embrace. The landscape feels like sleep feels, when sleep is deep and dreamless. It is the end state of a world that chose the path of maximum life, maximum moisture, maximum patience. It has no need to change. It has arrived. And it will continue, in its slow, quiet, weeping, breathing way, until the light itself, wherever it comes from, finally dims to nothing.
