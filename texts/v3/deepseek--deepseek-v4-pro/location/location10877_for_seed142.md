The plateau stretches out beneath a sky that has never known dawn. Above, a black dome presses down, unpunctuated by stars—just a seamless, absolute absence, as if the world were sealed inside a vault of dark glass. You stand on the edge of a vastness that feels less like land and more like the floor of some abandoned cathedral, its ceiling lost to an enormity of shadow. The air is warm, almost blood-heat, and perfectly still; it carries no scent of life, no moisture, only the faint metallic tang of old, dry metal and the ghost of oxidized stone.

Beneath your feet, the ground is a pavement of something that was once rock—perhaps basalt, perhaps a composite of fused industrial slag—but age has worked it into a uniform, fine-grained expanse, like the skin of a world that has been polished by eons of silent, patient sand. The erosion here has not carved canyons or sharp arêtes; it has smoothed. Every contour is a gentle, almost imperceptible undulation, a landscape settled into its final, lowest-energy shape. There are no dunes, no loose grains to shift in the wind—only this endless, hard, dimpled flatness, as if the whole region had been pressed under a cosmic iron while it was still warm.

You walk, and the sound of your footsteps is the only punctuation in a silence so deep it seems to swallow its own echoes. The light here is not light as you know it, but a kind of infra-glow: a faint, sourceless phosphorescence that seeps from the ground itself in ribbons of electric blue and dead amber, tracing the grain of the ancient stone like circuitry in a dormant machine. It pulses—so slowly you might not notice—with a rhythm that suggests some residual, idling metabolism of the world, too ancient to ever stop, too tired to ever quicken.

Out in the open distance, the first of the structures rises. It comes not as a sudden imposition but as a gradual thickening of the mirage-dark, a silhouette that resolves into something halfway between a cooling tower and a termite mound, honeycombed with hexagonal apertures and clad in flaking sheets of what must be iron, though the oxidation has bloomed into crystalline growths of deep violet and silver. These are the urban bones of the plateau: not a city in any living sense, but a scattered archipelago of industrial forms, each isolated by miles of empty plain, placed with a logic—or lack of it—that suggests purpose fossilized into puzzle.

You approach one. It stands on a low rise, the highest point for a day’s walk, and as you near, the silence inside it leaks out a subsonic thrum—not a sound, precisely, but a pressure in the chest, the ghost of a heartbeat from some vast, buried engine still cycling through motions it began before memory. The walls are tall, tilted, layered; they show strata of construction: courses of vitrified block, then a band of woven cable, then a horizon of rusted gear-teeth set end-to-end like masonry. This is not a ruin. Ruins are dead. This is something else—a machinery that has become geography, an industry that has eroded into a state of equilibrium with the world around it.

No vegetation softens the lines. There is no soil, no root-crack, no green tendril invading a seam. The absence of water is absolute except for a faint, crystalline haze that sometimes forms on the metal surfaces during the long, slow thermal cycles—a haze of metallic salts or perhaps acids, chemistry that never knew water as a solvent, now sublimating and recrystallizing in delicate, fernlike patterns on the iron. These are the only “plants”: fractal mineral efflorescences that grow an inch in a millennium, then dissolve and re-form in a cycle you will never live to see complete.

Animals do exist here, but you must learn to see them. They are rare—so rare that each encounter feels like a hallucination. One reveals itself now, as you pause by an aperture: a thin, flat creature, like a sliver of mica that has learned to flex. It inches along the edge of a shadow, propelled by a rippling fringe of silica hairs, and it leaves no tracks on the stone. It is blind, or perhaps it sees in the infra-glow, and it moves with the implacable slowness of a thing that has no predators and no hurry—a citizen of a world where nothing ever changes, where one meal may last a century.

You step through the aperture and into what must once have been a hall. The floor is a grid of metal plates, some warped by a heat that has long since dissipated. Overhead, the ceiling is lost in a darkness that the infraglow cannot touch. But here and there, hanging like stalactites, are coils of wire and glass that emit a faint, sizzling-static sound—the only real sound beyond the thrum, a sound like a distant radio tuned to the noise of an extinct universe. These are the voices of the place: not voices at all, but the whisper of residual current running through dead circuits, a kind of industrial tinnitus that fills the silence without breaking it.

Deeper in, the structure opens onto a space that is not a room but a view: a balcony or a loading dock overlooking the plateau from a height. And here, the scale of the landscape reveals itself fully. To the north, a line of similar forms recedes into the gloom, each crowned with a faint, phosphorescent halo where the infraglow collects around their edges. To the east, a darker mass resolves as a vast, flat-topped mesa—the remnant of some older city, now planed down to a perfect horizontal by the same forces that smoothed the plain. On its surface, the geometry of streets and foundations is still visible, but only as slight discolorations in the stone, like a shadow city pressed into the world’s memory.

The temperature never shifts. The dark never lifts. The sounds never crescendo. There is no weather, no wind, no dew point, no season. The plateau is a snapshot that has been held for so long that the snapshot itself has worn smooth. Time here is not a river; it is a crystal, frozen and faceted, and you are a small, warm mote wandering through one of its internal flaws.

You sit on the edge of the dock and let your feet dangle over the plain. Below you, a chute descends into darkness, perhaps a ventilation shaft or an access way, and from its depths comes a faint, rhythmic clicking—like the turning of a ratchet, or the settling of old gears. It is a sound so regular and so slow that it might be the heartbeat of the machinery, or it might be a creature, or it might be nothing but the thermal contraction of metal that has been cooling for a million years. In this place, these distinctions have long since ceased to matter.

The weirdness here is not in the presence of the unfamiliar, but in the way the familiar has been stretched and hollowed until it becomes a ghost of itself. This is an urban landscape without citizens, an industrial complex without production, a warm world without sun, a high plateau without a valley below. It is a place that has outlived its own meaning and now simply persists, in a state of perfect, quiet, never-changing equilibrium.

And as you sit, you realize that the darkness is not empty. It is full of the weight of ages, and of a presence—not malevolent, not benevolent, but merely there: the presence of a world that has finished speaking, and now only breathes, in and out, in a rhythm so slow that civilizations could be born and die between one iteration and the next. You are the rarest thing here. You are change. And the plateau, in its ancient, silent way, is waiting for you to leave so it can resume its dream.
