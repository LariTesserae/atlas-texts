The land drops away, not in cliffs but in long, careful shelves of stone, as if the earth had been shaped with patience and enormous weight. This is the First Hollow, a settlement folded into a natural amphitheater at the base of a broad, old watershed. The low country around it gathers water like a cupped palm. The air is thick with it, a warm, pressing humidity that never quite resolves into rain but never dries, either. It condenses on every surface: black rock, iron, red brick, the fat leaves of vine, the pipes that run between buildings. Water drips. It runs in channels cut along the narrow lanes. It wells from seams in the stone and slides down the faces of retaining walls. At the edge of the Hollow, where the town gives way to vegetation, the ground is marshy, seeping, alive with the slow movement of water toward lower places.

The town itself is small—a hamlet in footprint, a cathedral in ambition. Its buildings stand low and wide, constructed from stone and dark metal, some of them sunk into the terraces of the amphitheater, others built out from the rock as if they had grown there. The architecture is layered: older stonework at the base, patched with brick, then overlaid with iron braces, pipework, corrugated metal. Steam exhausts rise from several structures and merge with the general mist. The place is old enough that its edges have blurred; walls lean into the hillside, streets have buckled and been repaved with whatever was at hand, stairways descend into water and continue as stepping stones. It has the look of a place that has been continuously repaired rather than built, and never finished.

The Hollow is industrial in the way a boiler room is industrial: functional, dense with equipment, but not vast. There is a foundry near the center, a low drum-shaped building of firebrick and black iron, its mouth a round door like a kiln. From it comes the deep thump-and-hiss of a steam press, a rhythm that pulses through the ground and up through the soles of the feet. Pipes run from the foundry to other buildings—workshops, a power house, a pump station half-submerged at the basin's edge. The streets are narrow and often wet, laid with scored metal plates to give footing. Overhead, cables and steam lines crisscross between buildings, some of them leaking small jets of vapor.

The plants have learned to live with the machinery. They are many, but not crowded; they grow in distinct clusters, each with room to breathe. Vines climb the exterior of the foundry and hang in green curtains over its vents. Moss grows on the shaded sides of the stone walls, spongy and dripping. Ferns push up through the gaps in metal grates. Around the marshy edges of the Hollow, the vegetation thickens: stands of tall, broad-leaved trees with pale bark, their roots submerged; thickets of reeds with fibrous stems; carpets of floating plants that drift over the standing water. The green is deep, almost blue in the dim light. It grows in the pockets where the water waits.

The light here is weak, and it comes from strange directions. The Hollow sits in the shadow of the watershed, so direct sun reaches it only briefly, and then it is cut and scattered by steam and leaves. Most of the light is ambient—reflected from wet stone, diffused through mist, filtered green through the upper canopy of vines. At the bottom of the bowl, the shadows are deep, almost purple. The foundry glows faintly through its own door, a dull orange that turns the surrounding walls to brass. Lamps flicker here and there, some electric, some oil, hanging from cables or set into alcoves in the stone.

No animals move through this place. No birds call from the vines, no insects tick against the lamps, no fish rise in the dark pools. The water carries no small life. The vegetation does not rustle with hidden movement. It is a place emptied of animal presence, not because it is dead, but because something else has taken their place in the cycle. The sound, this lack is filled by the sound. The Hollow is loud, but not with voices. The steam press booms in a slow, even rhythm. Pipes clank and hiss. Water runs constantly, a threading white noise that ties the other sounds together. From the pump station comes a deep, grinding churn as it forces water up into the reservoirs on the rim. Vents exhale. Metal ticks as it cools. Somewhere, a turbine spins with a high, thin whine. The echoes in the bowl make the sounds layer and overlap, so that each noise seems to come from several directions at once.

The place is strange. It is not that the machinery is unusual, or even that the plants are. It is the way the world behaves here. The water is warm. It emerges from the stone already heated, as if the hillside is a body with a fever. The steam does not rise as it should; sometimes it hangs in sheets in the still air, and at other times it moves sideways, rolling along the ground like low fog, pushed by drafts from the pipeways. The light from the lamps has a muttering quality, now and then dimming all at once, as if the whole town sighed. There is a pattern to the steam press, a long rhythm of thumps and rests, and the people who live here move to it, but there are no people. There is no one. The machinery runs itself, or it is maintained by something unseen, or it is simply that the Hollow has settled into a state where everything operates because it always has. The factories clank, the pipes deliver, the pumps cycle, and no hand is seen at any switch.

Empty-handed in the warm, loud half-light, the plants breathe. Water beads on every leaf and drips. The foundry beats its iron heart. The pumps inhale and exhale. The town sits in the bowl of the earth, dense with its own mechanisms, sparse with presence, and it does not sleep. It works. It sweats. It grows. And the marsh, endlessly fed by the weeping rock and the exhausted water, creeps inward year by year, testing the edges of the stone terraces, softening the ground beneath the oldest walls, making slow, patient claims on everything the builders left behind.
