Here, the silence is a physical substance, thick as velvet, draped over every surface. You are standing in a shallow bowl of rust-red earth, no wider than a cathedral nave, enclosed on all sides by towers that are not stone but something between flesh and architecture. They rise two, three times your height—organ-pipe clusters of hardened clay, saliva, and chitin, their flanks pocked with arches no larger than a fist. This is a single chamber of a larger city, a fitting room in a termite metropolis that sprawls across the plateau for miles, but here, in this grove-sized hollow, the world holds its breath.

The air is warm, resting against your skin with the pressure of a lingering hand, and bone-dry. No water has flowed here in a time too long to measure; the sky, a bleached-denim blue, offers no promise of rain. And yet the ground erupts in life—a dense, near-touching extravagance of forms that read as vegetation but challenge that word. Creatures of pure biology, no toolkit but their own slow matter, have shaped this place: the mounds themselves are living skins, constantly repaired by the quiet billions that dwell inside. Look closer: among the towers, rooted in the mineral scree, grows a lushness that needs no water, at least not as you know it.

The plants here—call them that—are succulents from a fever dream. Each one is a reservoir, a sealed vault. There are columns like stacked emerald coins, each coin a bladder aching with stored atmospheric dew harvested during the rare, fog-breathing dawns. Others spider from crevices in the mounds’ bases, leaves like polished jade tongues, plump and firm, their edges lined not with thorns but with rows of tiny, crystalline beads that refract the light into needles of pale gold. The density is overwhelming; every gap between the towers is filled with these swollen, silent bodies, layered in terraces of self-seeded pots—older plants crusting into brittle gray husks while new shoots push through their ribs. It is lush not in the feathery, rustling way of a rainforest, but in a braided, sculptural, overcrowded way, as if some principle of abundance has been turned to a very different chemical key. The color is deep green blushed with violet, carmine, ochre—all the subtle desert saturation that thrives on stored reserves and pulse-slow metabolism.

And it is young, this place. The features are sharp. The plateau recently fractured its skin; basalt tongues cracked and froze mid-flow, leaving scarp edges so crisp they seem cut yesterday. The termite towers rise with the same untempered freshness—their tips are still damp in places with fresh secretion, glistening dully in the normal, daylong light of a sun that hangs at a comfortable, golden angle. The silhouettes are spires, minarets, organic skyscrapers built without a blueprint, their surfaces worked into filigree by the ceaseless, slow traffic of jaws and antennae. This is a town, a settlement of instinctual urbanism, infrastructure of temperature control, fungal farms, nurseries, and royal crypts—all layered one upon the other, every anchor point seized by the roots above. There are no streets, only the breathing walls and the meandering, body-wide passages that flow between them.

The fauna is abundant beyond easy counting, but it teaches you a new kind of quiet. A column of termites moves along a seam, a ribbon of amber bodies flowing in absolute cohesion, their feet a whisper softer than dust settling. Over a succulent’s waxy slope, a beetle—the size of a thumb joint, its carapace a lacquered sunset—pauses, antennae twitching in the still air. It makes no sound. In the high, honeyed light, small lizard-like forms—all legs and papery skin—freeze on vertical termite flanks, their pulsing throats the only giveaway, and they too hunt in silence. Even the air itself seems edited, damped; the sparse, weirdly-formed seedpods that hang from the plants do not rattle, but split open in silence, releasing plumes of filament that drift like slow, patient smoke.

The weirdness creeps in as you stay. It’s the scale—this cathedral room bounded by living pillars—and the chemistry. There is a faint, pleasant bitterness in the back of the throat, a non-aqueous scent: resin, oxidized minerals, the suggestion of acetic acid from termite crops. It’s in the way the light falls, normal sunlight but made strange by the dense, fleshy leaves that filter it into emerald and ruby shadows. And it’s in the pace. Everything here moves through patient processes, gradual cycles. The mounds grow by microns each season; the plants swell and split over decades; the air itself seems to cycle only through the slow respiration of the sealed, biological city. The dynamic is a barely perceptible pulse—a weak gradient of heat from mound vents, the incremental shift of a leaf toward the light, the decade-long advance of a crack in a drying mud-cap. There is minimal flux, only the profound, slow-motion intimacy of a place where every presence has learned to use what it has, and let go of water, haste, and noise.
