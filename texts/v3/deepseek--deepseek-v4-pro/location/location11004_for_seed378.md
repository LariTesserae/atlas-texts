The high plain breathes. Every surface sheds a slick of warm condensation, every fissure in the rock gives up a ribbon of steam, and the light—there is so much light—comes from everywhere at once, a flat bone-white brilliance that never lets a shadow sharpen. You are standing on a balcony of sinter, a walkway of fused mineral curving out from the flank of what was once a geyser cone and is now a spire threaded with pipework. The silence is so complete that your own pulse becomes a foreign sound, a dim intruder at the edge of the inner ear.

This is a city, or what a city became here. Structures rise with the deliberate spacing of a settlement that grew into its own breath: industrial sheds with skins of rust-scaled iron, cooling towers whose hyperbolic flanks sweat endless beads of water, pipe-runs bridging the gaps between them on delicate gantries. Nothing clusters. A factory dome squats a quarter-mile from its nearest neighbour, the distance filled by a shallow lake of silica-rich brine that shimmers at the threshold of boiling. Steam curls from it in slow, fat coils. The lake is ringed by a necklace of silica terraces, each tiny pool cupping water the colour of milky sky. Sinter, everywhere—the architecture of this place is half-built and half-grown, mineral accretion mantling every girder, turning stairways into flowing stone cascades, blurring the line between machine and formation.

The air is a weight. Hot and drenched, it presses on the skin like a second body. When you inhale, you taste metal and the flat, clean tang of distilled water. No scent of green, no humus, no flower; vegetation here never caught hold. The ground is a crust of precipitated crystal over volcanic glass, broken in places by the slow heave of thermal currents. In the cracks, something else lives.

They move without sound. Fauna: abundant, strange, and utterly silent. On the sinter terraces, pale crabs the size of thumbs pick their way across the wet crust, mandibles working at the mineral film. They leave minuscule tracks that fill with water and heal over in seconds. In the brine lake, shoals of translucent worms with many lateral fins swirl in synchronized helices, each individual as long as your forearm, their digestive tracks visible as a dark thread. They break the surface without a ripple, only a tiny meniscus trembling. Above, on the thermal columns of rising vapour, floaters drift: flat, disc-like animals with stippled undersides that modulate their buoyancy by bleeding droplets of heated gas. They drift in silence, bumping gently into one another, then sliding away. The city is their reef.

The urban skeleton is industrial in the way a fossil engine is industrial—present but absorbed. Pipelines hum with a subsonic frequency you feel through the soles of your boots rather than hear. They carry geothermal brine from deep wells, circulating it through heat exchangers in the factories. The purpose of the place is legible: energy conversion, mineral extraction, perhaps once a manufactory for things that required intense, steady heat. But the machinery runs unattended. Panels of instrumentation still glow with steady amber light behind fog-beaded glass. Gears turn in oil baths so perfectly viscous that the metal never contacts metal; the only evidence of motion is the slow blink of a counterweight rising, falling, like a breathing lung. The factories are towns in themselves, multi-level galleries of silent industry, their floors scaled with sulphurous crystals that crunch ever so faintly if you step just wrong.

Erosion has been kind, or perhaps just slow enough to become a new form of design. The walkways are scalloped at the edges, worn by condensation drip into lace-like stone. Steel has oxidized into a velvety brown patina that holds the moisture like moss. Wherever water touches metal and air, a cauliflower-like mineral growth bulges outward—iron-oxidizing bacteria, the only kingdom that bridges the rock and the animal. They build small, brittle castles that collapse under a touch. In the absence of higher plants, these microbial mats shimmer in the brightest patches of light, a thin paint of rust and sulphur.

The light: it is not from any single sun. The cloud layer above is thin but continuous, a pearl veil stretched from horizon to horizon, and it diffuses the sun into a radiance that seems to well up from the steam itself. At night—there is night, though the glow dims only to a soft silver—the city illuminates itself with arrays of vapour-proof lamps that cast no beam, only a pervasive luminous fog. Shadows do not exist; everything is modelled by highlights on wet convexity. Distance fades not into haze but into a gradual increase in brilliance, until the farthest structures are ghosts in the glare.

Soundlessness is a rule here, a physical property almost. The steam that should hiss escapes from vents through layers of sinter with a porosity that breaks the turbulence into silence; the factory machines operate in vacuum-sealed chambers or are muffled by the heavy air itself. The animals have no calls—they communicate by touch, by chemical trail, by bioluminescent flicker that the pervasive light renders invisible. When the floaters drift overhead, their silent collision produces a faint puff of warm moisture against your upturned face, the only evidence of contact. The sparse density of the city means that even if a sound were made, it would die alone in the wet immensity.

Yet the landscape is active, a slow-motion conversation. Every few minutes, a geyser hidden in a nook among the sinter towers coughs—a soft, wet exhalation that raises a plume of white and then subsides. The brine lake laps unhurriedly at its silica shore, its level changing with the underground tides of heated water. The crab-things migrate in a wide, scattered wave from one mineral lick to another, their motion eerily coordinated by the micro-topography of the crust. The industrial processes continue: a valve somewhere rotates a quarter-turn, a bank of condensers chills the steam into a sudden sheet of water that drains along grooved concrete channels, the flow gurgling so faintly you might imagine it. The whole system breathes—geothermal cycle, industrial cycle, biological cycle—all intertwined, all quiet.

The strangeness rests in the combination: a hot, wet, industrial city without plants, where animals move in silence across mineral-grown architecture, in light that never allows a shadow. It feels like a world built by and for a purpose that has withdrawn, leaving its machinery to be colonized by life that has no need of voice. The very air muffles the boundaries between living and non-living. The sinter grows; the iron breathes; the worms turn; the pumps beat. And you, moving through it, are the loudest thing: your heartbeat, your footsteps making the only fractures in the quiet, each one a small violence that the steam immediately closes over, forgiving and forgetting.
