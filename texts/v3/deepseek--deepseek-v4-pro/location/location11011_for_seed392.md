The air is thick enough to chew, freighted with water that never quite falls and never quite clears. It beads on every surface — not dew, exactly, but a constant slick exhalation from the world itself. The heat presses like a second atmosphere, a weight that makes the lungs work for every breath. And it is bright: an omnidirectional radiance without clear source, as if the sky itself were a luminous ceiling set just above the highest rooftops.

The Settlement (no one here calls it anything else) stretches in all directions, a low-built sprawl constructed entirely of living bamboo. Every structure is bamboo — not cut bamboo lashed together, but bamboo still growing, still green, still flexing with water pulsing up from wherever the roots draw it. The inhabitants, generations ago, learned to train the shoots as they emerged: bending them into walls, weaving them into floors, knotting them into archways and domes and long, low galleries. The bamboo never stops growing, but it also never changes. The rooms are exactly as wide as they were a hundred years ago; the doorways exactly as tall. New shoots emerge only at the edges, and are immediately guided into whatever form the Settlement requires — a new sleeping platform, a water channel, a bridge over a patch of particularly wet ground.

The ground. There is no soil. Where the bamboo does not grow, the surface is a continuous pale membrane, slick and warm and slightly yielding, like the skin of some enormous sleeping thing. It slopes almost imperceptibly across the whole Settlement, dipping into shallow basins where water collects in perfectly still, perfectly transparent sheets, ankle-deep and blood-warm. The water never rises and never drains. It simply is. The basins are scattered at wide intervals, so that a person walking can travel for some minutes between them, the bamboo walls opening now onto a broad, empty, membrane-floored court, now onto a narrow passage where the stalks are woven so tightly that the light dims to a greenish twilight.

The animals are everywhere, but they do not move. They are frozen — not dead, not taxidermied, but held in some absolute stasis midway through a step, a breath, a glance. A deer-like creature with too many joints in its legs stands at the edge of a water basin, head half-lowered to drink, the surface beneath its muzzle showing the faintest suggestion of a ripple that never spreads. Clusters of what might be birds — their bodies are pendulous, soft-looking, covered in a fine pale fur — hang motionless in the air between bamboo stalks, wings outspread, their shadows fixed sharp on the green walls. Insects rest on the membrane in numbers that become visible only when you look closely: beetles with carapaces like hammered copper, moths with wingspans broader than a human hand, long slender things that might be mantids, all of them utterly still. The Settlement's residents step around them with the practiced ease of long habit. The animals are simply part of the architecture.

Silence, mostly. The bamboo does not creak. The water does not ripple or drip. The air moves only in the faintest currents, stirred by the passage of bodies. What sound there is comes from the residents themselves: their bare feet on the membrane, a soft percussive slap; their breath; the low murmur of their voices when they speak, which is seldom. They wear simple cloth, hand-woven from bamboo fiber, dyed in the muted ochres and dull greens that the local materials yield. Their tools are bamboo, hardened in some slow-fire process they keep secret, shaped into knives and awls and weaving pegs. They tend the Settlement in ways that seem more ritual than maintenance, pressing their palms to the membranes of the walls, checking the tension of woven floors, peering into water basins as if reading something in their depths.

The strangeness manifests most clearly at the edges. Walk far enough in any direction — and the scale of the Settlement permits this; it takes hours — and the logic of space begins to degrade. A passage that should lead back to the central water basin instead opens onto a gallery you have never seen before, its walls woven in a pattern that seems to writhe in the corner of your eye. A flight of steps, hand-carved into a single massive bamboo culm, ascends to a door that, when you look at it directly, is clearly just a wall. A person you passed a moment ago now stands ahead of you, facing away, though you did not see them overtake you and there was no branching path. The residents navigate this with the same calm they bring to everything. They speak of "neighbouring" certain areas, of places that are "still deciding." They do not seem troubled.

Once, a long time ago — no one remembers how long, because nothing here marks time — something happened. Or perhaps it is still happening, frozen like the deer at the water's edge. The world was being made, or unmade, or remade, and it stopped partway through. The bamboo is both the raw material of construction and the construction itself. The animals are both present and inaccessible, like specimens in a diorama that is somehow also the world. The heat and the wet and the brightness are constants, gradients that never resolve into weather, potentials that never discharge.

At the very center of the Settlement, in a wide open court where the membrane floor is slightly thicker and more resilient, there is a single object that is not bamboo. No one knows what to call it. It is roughly spherical, perhaps the size of a curled child, and it hovers a handspan above the ground. Its surface is the color of tarnished mercury and it is covered in fine, hairline fractures that do not seem to penetrate its depth. It does not rotate. It does not hum. But if you stand near it long enough, you begin to perceive that the fractures are not cracks but gaps — spaces where the object is simply not there, negative volumes that shift in relation to one another without the whole ever changing size. Looking at it too long produces a sensation not quite like nausea and not quite like grief. The residents leave offerings beside it: woven bamboo figurines, polished stones that must have come from somewhere else, little bundles of dried leaves whose purpose no one explains.

The Settlement endures. It is never changing, not because it is finished, but because it has been paused in the middle of becoming. The membrane sweats. The bamboo holds its breath. The animals wait, mid-gesture. And the people move through it all with their soft feet and their simple tools, tending a world that neither grows nor decays, a total construction suspended in the instant before the next thing happens.
