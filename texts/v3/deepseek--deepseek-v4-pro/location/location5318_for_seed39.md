The ground here has not yet learned the shape of itself. It is black basalt newly risen, edges so sharp they glint even in the light that barely arrives. This is a plateau heaved up from the planet’s interior not a thousand centuries ago, still steaming at the seams, a place where geology is a present-tense verb. You stand on a spine of rock at the roof of the world; the elevation tugs at your lungs, and below you, cloud seas churn in silence. The air itself feels thin and chilled, a permanent pre-dawn cold that numbs exposed skin within minutes. Overhead, the sky is a low, featureless lid of vapour, filtering sunlight into a diffuse grey glow—dim, like a long total eclipse that never ends.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It moves as a constant weep on every rock face, condensing out of the moisture-rich upwellings that spill from the vents. The temperature never climbs high enough to dry the stone; instead, the rock sweats. Droplets gather along the sharp edges, runnel down into invisible fissures, and pool in hollows of black glass. Sometimes a fine, cold drizzle falls from the clotted mist, not rain so much as a settling of suspended drops. The sound of water is a whisper heard in a thousand tiny trickles, a susurrus without source or end. Yet there are no rivers here, no lakes—the terrain is too freshly fractured, the rock still deciding its catchment. The water merely passes over the surface or sinks into the hot cracks, returning skyward as plumes of steam.

Vegetation is a rumor. In deep crevices where geothermal warmth meets perpetual damp, a grey-green crust of lichen clings, its growth measured in geological time. Elsewhere, the rock is bare. There is no soil; erosion has barely begun its work. The landscape is embryonic—knife-edges of obsidian, vent chimneys that look like the tangled pipes of an engine room, gnarled lumps of dacite that cooled mid-flow. The shapes are fresh from the forge, unrounded by weather, brittle and unaccommodating to life as we normally know it.

Yet life is here, and in strange abundance. The fauna has not waited for softness or soil. Around the steam vents—scattered miles apart like factory outposts—ecosystems seethe. Pale, blind, segmented creatures the length of a human forearm cluster in writhing mats, their plumed mouths filtering chemosynthetic bacteria from the mineral-thick exhalations. They are not worms, not shrimp, but something in between, sheathed in chitin that glistens with condensation. Over them scuttle larger forms, multi-legged and deliberate, their carapaces ridged with frost-tolerant insulation, picking among the filter-feeders with delicate tusks. Above, cruising the mist on silent wings, are sooty-grey fliers that resemble broad-winged bats or ragged birds; they navigate by sound, their clicks just below the threshold of hearing, diving to snatch prey from the vent mounds. The spaces between the vents are near-empty—vast, echoing halls of bare stone and drifting fog—but around each geothermal column, the predator-prey dance is constant and rapid, a biological pace set to the rhythm of the vents.

That rhythm is what defines the place. From a distance, the soundscape is mostly quiet: the faint drip of condensation, the whisper of wind over razor stone, the far-off cry of a winged hunter. But approach a vent field, and you enter a machine hall built by the planet. Geysers cough on schedules dictated by subsurface plumbing, huffing bursts of steam that roar like pistons. Fumaroles hiss through mineral-throated nozzles, some pitched high as a tea kettle, others a deep, juddering bass that you feel in your sternum. Boiling mud pots bloop and crackle. The combined effect is industrial—not metaphorically, but literally: the sounds are those of engines, of forges and foundries, of a factory floor operating at full tilt. There are no walls, no human traces, no built structures at all. But everywhere the geology has assembled itself into forms that mimic ducts, vents, stacks, and condensers, all built of precipitated silica and sulfur in yellows, whites, and burnt oranges. The ground trembles slightly with the power beneath.

The openness of the terrain amplifies the emptiness. You can walk for an hour across the frozen ripples of lava and see not a single living thing, only the distant white tufts of steam marking the next vent cluster five miles away. The sky is a bowl of dim pearl, the horizon jagged with newborn peaks that still hold the sharp angles of fracture. Scale is deceiving: those peaks might be a hundred metres high or a thousand; there is no vegetation to give them measure. The weather cycles rapidly—a bank of thicker cloud rolls up from the lowlands, bringing a minute of sleet that coats the rock in a transient silver skin, then clears, and the condensation resumes its patient work. The cold is constant, but it’s not a killing deep-freeze; it hovers near the point where water moves, a chill that preserves the wetness rather than locking it in ice. Patches of translucent slush form in shadows, but geothermal warmth bleeding through the crust keeps most surfaces above absolute freezing.

This is a landscape in the first days of its existence, an embryonic highland where the planet’s inner heat and outer chill meet in a perpetual, hissing negotiation. It is active without being chaotic, empty without being lifeless, and its voice is the voice of a vast, natural factory running unattended, building nothing but itself. The rules here are simple: rock rises, steam vents, water cycles, and life clusters at the edges of the forge, shifting in a slow, steady arc across the plateau as old vents die and new ones break through the skin of the world.
