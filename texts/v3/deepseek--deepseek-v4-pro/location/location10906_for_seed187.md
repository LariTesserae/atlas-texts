This is the hollow at the end of the low ridge, a pocket rinsed open by an age of seeping damp—a small, cathedral silence smothered in living skin.

The sound arrives before you do. It is a low, rhythmic thrubbing, like a flywheel spinning under the earth, overlaid with a tinny shirr of high-frequency trilling that never lets up. The whole air vibrates with it, a mechanical drone that makes your teeth ache, though there is no engine anywhere in the valley, no human thing for miles in any direction. Just the hollow, breathing.

You step inside. The space is roughly chapel-sized, a domed chamber that the ancient erosion has carved down to its calmest, final form. Every surface—the floor, the sloping walls, the deep-set ledges—is smothered in a seamless quilt of vegetation. Not a single grain of mineral shows through. The moss here is the architecture: emerald liverworts and silver-green cushion moss build up in layers so thick and old that the stone beneath has become a rumored skeleton, deeply buried, entirely irrelevant. The moss has become the ground. It gives under your boots with the soft, wet insistence of a mattress left out in the dew, and where it has grown up the walls, it hangs in pendulous drapes—some thin as suede, others swollen and pendulous like the ears of an elephant. It is a plant-as-substrate world, a vegetal totality, and yet the chamber feels hollow, almost unnervingly so. There are only a few thin, buttress-like ribs of rock—the last surviving knobs of the primordial limestone—widely spaced like the pillars of a derelict nave, each one wrapped in its own green pelt. Between them, the volume is mostly empty air, a vastness of cool damp space that your breath clouds into. Density here is a memory; you stand in a near-void held up by greenery.

The light is normal, a gray daylight that sieves down through a ragged oculus overhead where the moss-ceiling has parted. It lands without drama on the mattress floor, picking out shallow, nut-brown puddles that never quite evaporate. The air smells of wet wool and mushroom gills, and it is cool—fifty degrees, maybe, the kind of cool that seeps into your knuckles and stays. You feel the dampness as a perpetual condensation: every blade of moss is beaded with tiny droplets, and when you brush a wall with your shoulder, the water comes off in a cold smear. There is no rain, no stream; just this ambient, osmotic wetness that drips in slow, random intervals from the tips of the moss-drapes, a sound so scattered it barely registers against the roar.

The roar. It has a source after all, or two. The rhythmic under-throb is the hollow itself: when a puff of wind passes over the entrance—a low, round arch choked with hanging rootlets—the whole cavity acts like a bass flute, a Helmholtz resonance that drops to a fundamental so deep it feels like a diesel idling in the next room. The high-frequency shrilling comes from the fauna. The place is alive with them. Froglets, no bigger than your thumbnail, are embedded everywhere in the moss, their translucent bodies speckled like the liverwort. They call in synchronized pulses, a metallic chee-chee-chee that races around the walls in waves, perfectly timed to the wind’s frequency as if they are keeping the motor running. There are crickets too—tawny, long-antennaed things that rasp from the high ledges—and fat, wet-bodied moths that flutter on noiseless wings, their beaded eyes reflecting the gray light in dozens of tiny pinpricks. The frogs and insects together build a soundscape so deafening, so unrelenting, that it crosses over into a kind of factory-floor music: you can almost hear the clack of conveyor belts in the crickets’ rhythm, the hiss of a pneumatic press in the moths’ wingbeats. It is industrial without industry, a purely biological assembly line that has been running without variation for longer than any machine.

This is the ancient truth of the hollow: it never changes. The erosion that shaped it is done; the last grain of loosened limestone washed away centuries ago. The moss grows at a pace so slow it could be mistaken for geology—a millimeter a year, then dying and building up its own peat, cannibalizing its ancestors. The puddles hold the same depth, season after season, fed by condensation that never increases or falls. The frogs breed in a continuous, overlapping cycle, their tadpoles wriggling in the dimples of water, so that the population is always exactly at carrying capacity, no boom, no bust. The sound, that overwhelming blend of wind-resonance and animal chatter, is a standing wave of noise, a frozen equilibrium that you feel in your chest cavity. Nothing here has a gradient. Nothing here strives. It has all settled into its oldest-stable-state, an end-of-life calm so complete that even the loudness feels like a kind of quiet.

You are within a bounded place—a glade-sized microcosm, a single feature contained by its own living walls. There is no path leading here, no sign of human touch; the built score is a flat zero, and the only tech is the false-industrial echo your mind insists on hearing in the frogsong. Standing there, dwarfed by emptiness and smothered by sound, you sense the rules of this world: it is a plant-machine running on moisture and cool air, an engine with no moving parts, a factory that produces only its own endless, self-absorbed continuance. And then you notice the bones. Small ones—vole ribs, a bird’s wishbone—half-sunk into the moss but not decayed, preserved perfectly in the acid-humidity balance of the puddles. They are arranged in no pattern, just the random leavings of a hundred generations of small, abundant lives. They are the only sign that anything here ever ends, and even they are being slowly absorbed, incorporated into the soft green flesh of the eternal hollow, where the loudness never stops and never started, and the damp never dries, and nothing ever really changes.
