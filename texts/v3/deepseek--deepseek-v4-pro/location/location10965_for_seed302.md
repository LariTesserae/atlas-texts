The Shining Lattice stretches away in all directions, a plateau lifted so high that the sky is a pale, overexposed dome, the sun a motionless white coin pinned at zenith. It is bright—a light that seems to bleach the very molecules of air, leaching colour until the world is rendered in aluminiums and pewters, the wet shine of gunmetal. There is no cloud, no variation in that brightness, as if the atmosphere itself has been worn too thin to hold shadow.

Yet the ground is saturated. Water is everywhere and nowhere separate; it does not fall as rain but weeps from the rock in a constant, cold exudation. Every surface is slick, beaded, gleaming. Drops gather along horizontal edges, swell, fall with a soft plink that is immediately swallowed by the all-body roar. The sound is the first and last thing you know: a deafening, unvarying wash of noise that presses against the ears like physical cloth, a steady-state howl-scape that never rises or fades, never changes pitch. It comes from the rim—the plateau’s edge, miles distant but ever present, where an endless sheet of meltwater pours over the lip into a ring of abyss, a perpetual curtain plunging into cloud. No wind combs this place; the air is still, heavy with chill mist, and the roar is the sound of that falling water, a white noise so absolute it becomes a kind of silence.

The terrain itself is packed beyond any concept of emptiness. There is no soil, no loose gravel, not a single patch of bare flat stone. Instead, the floor of the Lattice is a dense tessellation of angular basins, tilted slabs, and sharp-edged channels, all formed from a single mineral crust the colour of tarnished nickel. Each basin is a shallow pool, perfectly rectangular or polygonal, as if the rock has crystallised along invisible rules. The pools are all exactly brim-full, never spilling, never draining; the water in them is glass-still, so clear it magnifies the fine dendritic patterns of the stone beneath. Between the pools, the ridges are knife-narrow and coated in a slick, translucent film—a vitreous glaze that looks frozen yet is cool to the touch, a solid sweat of silica or some other mineral exudate. The network is so tightly fitted that to walk here would be to step from ridge to ridge with no chance of placing a foot on open ground. The spaces between presences—pools, ridges, strange low hummocks of bubbled crust—are measured in inches. It is a landscape with no gaps.

Nothing grows here. Vegetation has never taken hold; there is no humus, no crack where a seed might lodge. The cool air carries no scent of life, only the clean, metallic tang of wet stone and the faint mineral breath of ancient water. Yet this is not a sterile place, for life is rare but not absent. The fauna is a scattering of persistent, quiet things. A white thread-like worm, no thicker than a hair, can sometimes be seen undulating beneath the pool surfaces, feeding on bacterial films that stain the rock a faint, improbable mauve. Elsewhere, tiny beetle-analogues with transparent carapaces cling to the underside of crystal overhangs, unmoving for centuries, their metabolisms idling in the timeless cold. Their presence is a punctuation, a glimpse of pale legs or a single black bead of an eye, then gone again.

The atmosphere is dense with moisture but not oppressive; the cool temperature keeps it fresh, a perpetual condensation that beads on skin and stone alike. Standing here, you would feel the chill seeping upward through your soles, the damp settling into fabric, the pressure of all that noise filling your skull. The light is so steady it becomes a property of the air, a thin high-altitude radiance that flattens perspective. Distant features—a cluster of spires, a stepped sequence of overflow basins—are as sharp and small as etchings, lacking the haze of distance. It gives the whole tableau the remote intensity of a miniature, a diorama sealed under glass.

The strangeness of the Lattice is a subtle thing. It lies in the uniformity of the pools, the hint of intentional geometry that is entirely natural, the way certain rock forms echo the convolutions of a brain or the struts of an engineered truss. Erosion is established, a settled-form equilibrium; nothing here is young. The water has been flowing over these surfaces for an unknowable span, and in that time every sharp edge has been softened to a fine radius, every channel smoothed to a perfect parabolic curve. Yet no change continues. The roar that suggests violent process is a deception: the water’s force has achieved a perfect balance with the mineral hardness, so that now it slides without wearing. The landscape is in a frozen-equilibrium, a state where time has become a constant, not a driver. The water moves, the sound persists, yet nothing—not a single grain—is displaced. It is a place that has outrun change.

The Shining Lattice is a world of one chord held forever: wet, bright, loud, and infinitely dense, a high-altitude paradox where all is motion and all is stillness, an open maze of mineral and water that asks nothing of the living and offers only the raw, unswerving fact of its presence.
