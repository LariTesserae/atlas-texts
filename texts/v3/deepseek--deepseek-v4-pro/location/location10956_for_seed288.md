The first thing that strikes you is the sound. Not a sound you hear so much as a sound you inhabit—a bone-deep thrum that vibrates up through the soles of your feet and resonates in your chest, a ceaseless, layered drone that fills the vastness like breath. The cavern is so large that your lamp cannot find its walls, only a suggestion of distance where the dim, sourceless glow of the ceiling dimples into darkness. The air is cool and perfectly dry, carrying a faint mineral tang, like old stone and something sharper, almost metallic. You stand on a floor smoothed to a gentle, rolling flatness—not flat like a plain, but worn into a shallow basin that undulates away from you with the patient, settled contours of a thing that has been exactly this way for an age that has no name. There are no loose stones. No sharp edges. The rock beneath you has the polish of wind-cut desert varnish, though there is no wind here, only the slow, invisible drift of air currents so sluggish they might be the cavern itself exhaling over centuries.

Out of that dimness rise the growths. “Lush” is the word that comes to mind, though they are nothing like the wet, green riot the word usually conjures. They stand in a scattered, stately procession, each one a tower of clustered, overlapping plates—like gigantic fungi sculpted from frosted glass, or the skeletons of long-dead corals swallowed by dust. Their surfaces are pale, muted things: bone-white, smoky grey, a faint blush of rust-orange near the base where some ancient mineral stain still clings. They are fractal, self-similar, each great trumpet or shelf or branching spire echoing the shape of the next in a kind of slow, architectural rhythm. This is the vegetation, though it draws nothing from sunlight nor water. It feeds on the air itself, on the fine particulate drift of mineral salts and the faint, steady exhalations from fissures far below. The plates are thin at their edges, almost translucent where they catch the dim light, and if you look closely you can see a faint, internal shifting—a sifting of fine dusts through vascular channels, the silent, continuous metabolism of a world that has never known rain.

There is space between these growths, a lot of space. A sparse, imperial spacing that turns each cluster into its own small kingdom, its own island of tangled architecture in a sea of bare, burnished stone. Groves might be eighty paces across, and between them the ground opens out into wide plazas where the thrumming sound gathers and reverberates, layered now with the click and chitter of fauna. And there is fauna—abundant, teeming, a constant flickering motion at the edge of vision. Small, multi-legged things scuttle over the plated surfaces, their carapaces mimicking the pale flanges of the growths so perfectly that they seem to materialize out of the texture itself, then vanish again. Things fly—or drift—on wings of stretched membrane, riding the slow air currents in lazy, spiraling descents, their bodies little more than grey filaments knotted around a dark eye. They are silent, but the scuttlers are not; they drum and tap on the resonant plates, a percussive language that echoes and multiplies until the whole grove sings with a constant, hissing chatter like a rainstorm of tiny bones.

And then there are the larger fauna, the ones that move slowly between the towers, the ones that built. You hadn’t seen the city at first, because it is made of the same material as the growths—indeed, it seems to be an extension of them. Blind, pale, segmented creatures, each the size of a small horse, have shaped the living plates over millennia, secreting a cement of mineral paste that hardens into fluted walls and arched passageways and spiraling ramps that coil up the sides of the forest towers. This is an urban landscape in the truest sense: a dense, intentional web of structures, layered and interconnected, built not with tools but with bodies, with patient, inherited choreographies that have not changed in hundreds of thousands of years. There are thoroughfares, broad and worn to a satin sheen by the shuffle of countless feet. There are chambers that open like vaulted halls, their ceilings lost in gloom, where the growths have been trained to form ribbed domes thick with the punctured holes of galleries above. The constructions have the organic, flowing look of termite mounds on a monumental scale, but they are older. Far older. The edges that once might have been crisp have been softened by the ages, eaten back by the same micro-flora that builds them up, until every surface is a velvet of fine, dusty bloom. This is a city that has been settled into its final form, an equilibrium state where construction and erosion are one and the same slow breath.

You walk one of the thoroughfares. It is wide enough for a hundred of the builders to pass, though you see only a few now, moving in solemn, unhurried lines, antennae tracing the air. The light here is a little stronger—a pale, phosphorescent lichen crusts the lower walls, giving off a steady, moon-colored glow that casts no shadows, only a gradual brightening and dimming of form. The sound is overwhelming this close to the towers; the combined percussion of millions of small bodies, the low drone of the growths themselves (you realize now that some of that thrum is coming from them, a deep, subsonic vibration as the plates flex and settle in the slow tide of mineral transport), and the occasional, startlingly loud trill of one of the flying creatures, a high, keening note that cuts through the drone and echoes for a long time across the open plazas.

It is all, in its way, normal. That is the strangest part. There is a deep internal consistency here, an ecology that has found its own balance over a span so vast that it reads now as inevitable, even familiar. The cool air, the dry, polished stone, the mineral forest, the city built of living bone—it all coheres. The fauna do not notice you, or if they do, it is without alarm, as though you are just another slow-moving shape in a world where all shapes move slowly. Their cycles are keyed to rhythms you cannot sense, chemical pulses in the rock strata, the infinitesimally gradual shift of air current corridors that might take a thousand years to alter. This place changes, but it changes at the pace of geology. Today is the same as yesterday, and yesterday was the same as ten thousand years ago. The builders build, the scuttlers drum, the dust sifts through the channels, and the drone goes on, and on.

You switch off your lamp, and after a while your eyes adjust. The cavern is not as dark as you thought. The ceiling, far above, is a negative sky, an immense dome of dull, dark stone, peppered here and there with pinpricks of brighter phosphorescence—like stars in an overcast night. The forest extends away in all directions, the pale towers diminishing into a haze of distance, until they are just a texture, a suggestion of architecture on the edge of perception. It feels like standing in a city square at twilight, if the square were the size of a valley and the city had been dead for a million years but somehow refused to stop living. The sound wraps around you, immense and intimate, and you realize you have stopped hearing it as separate from the place. It is the place. The drone is the voice of the ancient, the breathing of the primordial cave that has settled into its final, deafening song.
