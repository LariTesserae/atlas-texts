The first thing is the dark. Not a darkness that lifts, not a low light your eyes might adjust to, but a total absence, a physical press against the face of the skull. You can blink; nothing changes. You can wave a hand before your eyes; the air does not stir differently, the black does not thin. This is a rule of the place: photons do not live here. If you were to light a match, the flame would be a memory of heat on your fingertips and nothing more—no bloom, no flicker. Even the infrared glow of warm bodies must be swallowed, or else the world would have found a way to see. Light is zero. This is the first parameter you learn, because it rewrites every other sense.

Sound, then, becomes the shape of the land.

You are standing on a high, old plateau, a thing so vast it generates its own weather. The terrain under you is uneven but rounded, every hard edge worn down by an age of water. The rock—when your fingers find an exposed seam—is pocked and grooved, slick with a perpetual film that condenses out of the air. This is a wet world. Not puddled, not flooded; the water is a constant seepage, a weeping from every pore of the stone. The atmosphere is so saturated that the distinction between air and water blurs; you breathe through a warm, thick soup that tastes of minerals and something fungal, faintly acidic. The ground gives beneath your boots like a soaked mattress, a dense pile of living stuff that squelches and sighs. When you kneel and touch it, you find a springy mat of interwoven tendrils, nodules, and minute, bladder-like growths. It is lush: a deep-piled carpet that covers everything, a vertical as well as horizontal profusion. Stems and fronds rise into the dark, some thin as hairs, others thick and fleshy like the stalks of great mushrooms, but warmer, more animal. They crowd you, touching your face and arms with their wet, rubbery surfaces, and they go on: you can hear them, when a breeze stirs, rubbing together in a high, susurrant hiss, a noise that fills the distance and tells of miles of this growth in every direction.

This is a landscape shaped by deep time. The erosion parameter is “established,” and you feel it in the smooth bowls and grooves of the bedrock, in the way the waterways have carved their channels into a harmonious, almost lazy network. There are no sharp escarpments here, no fresh talus; this is a world that settled into its own skin eons ago. Walking, you descend a gentle slope into a broad, shallow draw, and your boots splash into a rivulet of warm water that runs ankle-deep over a pavement of perfectly rounded stones. The stream makes a low, continuous roar as it hurries toward some larger river, and the sound is layered with a million smaller liquid voices: drips from overhangs, the trickle of seepage down a slimy rock face, the occasional *plok* of a heavier drop falling from some high frond into a hidden pool. The ground is so thoroughly waterlogged that every footfall re-fills with a dark, silty seep. It is warm: not a stifling heat, but a steady, close warmth that makes the dampness feel like a second skin. You might guess the temperature holds around a constant, unvarying tropical night, kept so by the thick blanket of the atmosphere and the slow radiance of the earth’s own subterranean engines.

The scale of this world is staggering. The plateau is not a feature within a landscape; it is the landscape, a continental tableland broken only by canyons so deep and wide they are worlds unto themselves. Sound carries oddly here, distorted by the dense air. Sometimes a noise arrives before its own echo, or a distant rumble arrives with a curious flanging, as if the atmosphere has folded the signal. You can hear, far, far off, the thunder of a great waterfall where the plateau ends—a sound so low and continuous it is almost a physical pressure, a baseline hum that vibrates in the diaphragm. The canyon that receives that waterfall must be miles across, a gulf of darkness within the darkness. The air itself tastes of vastness: when the breeze shifts, it brings a faint, clean chill from those depths, a hint of colder rock and rushing water, and then the warmer, thicker smell of the dense growth closes back in.

This brings us to density. Despite the enormity of the space, the sensory world is saturated. There is no empty square inch. The vegetation is one part, but the fauna is another, and they do not respect the boundaries of your body. The air is stuffed with small, whirring things that blunder into you, their bodies soft and chitinous, sometimes accompanied by a faint electric tingle. They use high, rapid clicks—sonar, you realize—to navigate, and the noise is a constant, rapid tickertape that overlays everything. Larger creatures move through the dense growth with heavy, squelching footfalls, and their bodies displace enough air that you feel them as a pressure wave before you hear them. Once, a great something passed nearby, and the ground shook with a slow, deliberate rhythm; you felt the thud in your bones, and then a deep, hooting call boomed out, so loud it made the fronds tremble. The call was answered from what seemed like miles away, a series of descending notes that rolled across the plateau like a physical force. The “loud” designation is literal: this ecosystem has evolved to communicate and navigate in a lightless world, and sound is the primary medium. The dynamic is ferocious: every few minutes, the steady chorus of clicks and chirps is interrupted by a sudden flurry—a high panicked squealing, a wet crunch, a splash, then a new set of calls as scavengers and neighbors react. Predator-prey cycles are swift and ever-present.

Some of those sounds are not organic.

Wandering in what you guess is a straight line, your hand eventually meets something cold, smooth, and geometric: a metal beam, riveted and slick with condensation. Feeling along it, you find a lattice of pipes and a low, humming cylinder. It is a pump, or a compressor of some kind, and you can hear a sluggish piston cycling inside it: *thump-hiss, thump-hiss*. The metal vibrates with a contained, industrious energy, and the air around it is thicker, smelling of oil and hot iron. This is a piece of the scattered industrial presence. The tech level here is unmistakably machine-age—gears, steam, steel—but it is operating in the dark with no sign of its makers. Farther on, you stumble across a cluster of pipes that emerge from the ground and run overhead, supported by occasional stanchions; they are warm, and one has a slow leak, dripping a viscous, tarry substance into the undergrowth. You hear, in the distance, a heavier rhythm: the chuff of a larger engine, perhaps a generator, its exhaust stack adding a low, bronchial wheeze to the soundscape. These artifacts are scattered, never clustering into a town or a complex. A single tower with a ladder that ascends into the blackness; a small concrete bunker whose door has been swallowed by the vegetation; a derrick, motionless, its cables loose and singing faintly in the wind. They are like an archaeological layer that has not yet quite become a ruin, because the machines are still alive. You never find a body, a skeleton, a tool dropped in haste. Only the engines, running.

And then there is the strangeness, the rule-breaking that sets this world beside reality rather than inside it.

The water, you notice, doesn’t behave entirely as it should. When you cup it in your hands, it feels thick, a little syrupy, and it dries leaving a residue that tingles and then numbs the skin. Some of the rivulets run uphill for short distances, pulled by something other than gravity—a strange capillary effect that works against the slope, or perhaps a local spatial warp. The vegetation, when touched in certain places, recoils with a muscular snap, and sometimes it emits a sound like the ringing of a tiny bell, though there is no metal. The fauna, too, defy easy cataloguing. By feeling the carcass of a small creature you once tripped over, you found a body plan of repeating segments, each with a ring of short, articulated limbs, and its skin was covered with fine, toothy denticles that rasped against your palm. Another time, you felt a dense, cloud-like entity pass through the air—it was colder than the ambient, and it hummed faintly, and as it passed over your hand, your fingers went briefly blind to touch, as if anesthetized.

Then there is the deep sound—the one that underlies all others. If you stand still for long enough, if you master your breathing and stop the rustle of your own body, you can hear a note so low it is almost a thought: a planetary hum, perhaps piezoelectric stress in the vast basaltic roots of the plateau, or perhaps a standing wave in the atmosphere itself. It cycles slowly, a drone that peaks every few minutes, and when it peaks, every living thing in the thicket falls silent for a beat—the clicks stop, the calls pause, the engines even seem to hold their breath. Then it passes, and the din rushes back in like a tide. Whatever it is, it is the metronome of this world, a clock made of sound and stone.

The rules here are syncopated: light does not propagate, but sound and heat and vibration do, and life has colonized every niche of permitted physics. The high elevation means the atmosphere is a tiny bit thinner than the lowlands (if there are lowlands), giving the air a faint tangibility that makes deep breaths feel insufficiently full; but it is still dense enough to transmit sound with unnerving fidelity. The warmth, the wetness, the darkness, and the endless, ongoing noise conspire to create a place that feels simultaneously womb-like and skull-like—a close, nurturing, terrifying vastness where the only way to know the shape of the world is to scream and map the echoes. And something, somewhere, is always screaming.
