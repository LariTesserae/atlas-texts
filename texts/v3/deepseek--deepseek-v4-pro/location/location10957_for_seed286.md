The sky here is not sky, but a luminous silver-white ceiling that presses close, its edges lost in a permanent fog. You are submerged—not in water, but in an atmosphere so thick with moisture that it behaves like a liquid, buoying you slightly, filling your lungs with cool, dense air that tastes of wet chlorophyll and faint ozone. The light is diffuse, sourceless, as if the whole vault of heaven is a frosted bulb, and it casts no shadows. You float—or walk?—on a surface that is not earth but a woven mat of living tissue, pale grey-green and impossibly vast. It stretches in all directions, a gently undulating plain of fibrous flesh that is at once soil, pavement, and organism. This is a world where vegetation has become substrate, where the entire landscape is a single, planet-scale superplant, and you are a fleck of dust on its skin.

The topography lifts here and there into higher-standing plateaus, giving the realm an elevation that keeps it suspended above some unimaginable abyss below. You sense the drop more than see it: the mist thins at the edges of the mat, and if you peer over the ragged fringe, there is only a grey void descending into warmer, denser layers. This is a high place, a cool place. The temperature hovers at that precise point where your skin feels a permanent chill but never quite shivers—a steady, unvarying coolness, like the inside of a cave that has known only one season for geological ages.

The structures that rise from the mat are not built; they are grown. Enormous, chimney-like stalks—some slender and solitary, others clustered in geometric arrays—reach hundreds of meters upward before branching into whorls of gas-exchange fronds. They resemble cooling towers, smokestacks, refinery columns, but their walls are rugose bark, their apertures lipped with something like sphincter muscle. They are the lungs and reactors of this place. From them issue constant sounds: a deep, industrial drone that never wavers, a throbbing hum as if great engines churn inside the mat’s hollow core. Hisses of pressurized gas vent from sphincters in rhythmic sequence; somewhere a low, resonant clang repeats every few seconds, metallic yet entirely biological—perhaps two massive plant parts striking together in a slow, perpetual pendulum. The noise is loud, omnipresent, a factory floor operating at peak capacity without end. And yet, nothing is made here. There are no artifacts, no carved stones, no wires or rivets. The machinery is flesh and fluid, and it has run this way, unchanging, for epochs.

You notice the fauna then, because on a canvas so sparse—the great spaces between the towering stacks are immense, uncluttered by undergrowth—each living thing stands out. A flock of creatures cruises through the mist above: they are flat, undulating forms like manta rays cast in translucent membrane, their edges fringed with cilia that comb the fog for microscopic airborne life. They move in perfect formation, every individual following the exact same course as its ancestors did a million years ago, tracing an invisible circuit that never deviates. On the mat below, skittering arthropod-like beings the size of household cats pick their way across the spongy surface, their legs adapted to find purchase in the fibrous weave. They stop at regular intervals to rasp at nodules that grow in predetermined spots, consuming a waxy exudate that oozes in steady, metronomic pulses. The feeders’ paths are grooved into the mat—ruts worn not by change, but by an eternity of identical movement. They are abundant, these animals, yet the scale is so vast that the space between them remains dominant. There is a sense of solitude, of emptiness balanced with teeming life.

The whole ecology is a closed loop, a frozen equilibrium. Nutrients rise from the deep, dark layers below—a rain of organic detritus that falls through the mist and is caught by the mat’s underbelly, then broken down in fermentation chambers within the living substrate. Gases are fixed, released, fixed again; water is pulled from the saturated air and cycled through vascular systems that pulse with slow, hydraulic certainty. There is no season, no day-night cycle, no disturbance. Not a single cell divides out of turn, not a single organism evolves. The system reached its perfect state long ago and has simply continued, a frictionless machine. Even the sounds repeat without variation: the drone never shifts pitch, the hisses never miss their beat. If you stood here for a thousand years, you would witness the same cloud of rays passing at the same moment, the same droplets condensing on the same fronds, the exact same cool, damp, ozone-tinged air sliding over your skin.

This is the strangeness of it—this marriage of industry and biology, of loud, roaring process and utter stasis. It feels like a factory abandoned by its engineers but still faithfully running, except there were never engineers. The world itself is the factory, a continent-sized organism that mimics the rhythms of human-scale production yet is wholly, eerily natural. The technology is not in the form of tools but in the genetic memory of the superplant, which has evolved the most efficient possible chemistry and never had cause to change it. There is a hint of electricity in the air—perhaps piezoelectric crystals embedded in cell walls that generate tiny charges with each flexing of the mat, used for signaling or as a defense against microbial invasion. Tiny sparks sometimes flicker along the edges of the gas vents, giving the mist a faint, fleeting glow.

Time does not pass here. It is a place that has forgotten duration. The landscape is an eternal now, its details as fixed as a painting, yet it is alive, breathing, working. That is perhaps the weirdest thing of all: to stand in a space so dynamic in its microdetal, so loud and animal-filled and churning, yet to know that it is absolutely unchanging, a perfect sculpture of equilibrium that will outlast the stars.
