The climb takes you through moss, always moss—spongy hillocks of it, deep as a hand is long, drinking from air that cannot decide if it is cloud or rain. You ascend on stone that has not fallen in living memory but is nevertheless arranged like a paused rockslide, great tilted slabs the size of oxcarts, their edges rounded soft as old soap. No mortar holds them; gravity and patience do. Between them, water moves without hurry, appearing from nowhere, sliding over dark rock faces in films so thin they seem a property of the stone itself, a permanent weeping.

The sound is constant here, but it is not a roar. It is the close, granular noise of a thousand small drainings—trickle and drip, seep and whisper, the wet exhale of saturated ground. Beneath that, lower and slower, there is a deeper note: the ground-hum of falling water somewhere out of sight, a cataract in the gorge below whose voice has been worn down by distance to a felt vibration in the sternum. The effect is strangely intimate, as if the whole place were breathing around you, each moss bed a lung.

The vegetation would be a jungle if it could, but something holds it back. You feel that. The warmth and wet should conjure a tangle, a choked profusion, yet the growth here is sparse, deliberately spaced. Scattered trees with trunks of twisted, sinewy grain—musclewood, some call it—rise singly from the moss, never in groves. Their bark is dark as wet leather, and from their branches hang aerial roots that never quite reach the ground, swaying slightly with their own slow purpose. Ferns uncurl in the gaps between stones, each one a small event, a green explosion in miniature, spaced like exhibits in a gallery. The moss itself is the true canopy here, a continuous, breathing skin that rounds every edge, fills every declivity, and makes the rock-mounds look like the shoulders of buried giants sleeping under green pelts.

There is no visible water body—no pool, no stream you can point to—yet water is the first fact of the place. It condenses on your skin. It beads on the waxy leaves of the scattered, low-growing shrubs. It darkens the stone in permanent vertical stains. The ground under the moss is not ground at all but a lattice of wet roots and trapped debris, and your footfalls squelch with a sound like a mouth opening. When you pause, water fills your footprint behind you, clear and still, reflecting the pale sky in a disc the size of a plate. These small mirrors linger, evidence of your passage, until the slow hydraulics of the moss erase them over hours.

The air has weight. It is warm but not hot, the kind of warmth that carries a slight fungal sweetness, the scent of healthy rot, of constant gentle decomposition that never tips into stench. There is a floral note too, faint and intermittent, arriving in pockets—someone's bloom, small and white and far away, releasing its scent into the moving air only when the humidity drops a fraction of a degree.

You are high here. The view is not grand—the mists see to that—but you feel the altitude in the quality of the light, a thinness to it even through the cloud, and in the way the trees grow: stunted but not struggling, compact as bonsai shaped by centuries rather than shears. The plateau feels like the roof of something, a hanging garden suspended above the deeper world. Occasionally the mist thins and you glimpse the flanks of the range falling away into cloud-forest far below, a drop so sheer your stomach tightens, before the white closes in again and you are back in a room of moss and vertical stone.

Animal life exists here but does not announce itself. It is present only in signs: a scatter of small bones, bleached and fragile, tucked into the crotch of a root—the work of something that eats and leaves no other trace. A single track in a patch of bare mud, five-toed and the size of your palm, slowly filling with water. Once, a call—not a bird's, you think, but something wetter, a note like a drop of water falling into a deep vessel, a single resonant plink that echoes off the stones and then is gone. You wait, but it does not repeat. The fauna here is rare in the way ghosts are rare: possibly present, impossible to prove, moving just beyond the edge of the dense, dripping silence.

And within the silence, a structure. Not built—nothing here is built—but formed, or found, or perhaps simply the result of one slab falling against another in a way that suggests intention. A gap between two leaning megaliths opens into a low chamber, roofed by a third stone the size of a cottage. Inside, it is dry, or drier. The moss stops at the threshold, a clear line where the weeping air does not penetrate. The floor is smooth stone, subtly concave, holding a skin of water so shallow and still it is invisible until your foot breaks it into ring-widening ripples. The sound inside is different—sealed, close, the drip-sound muffled to a heartbeat. There are marks on the walls, not carvings but patterns of mineral deposit, dark against pale: dendritic, fern-like, the slow branching of manganese oxides laid down over centuries by water seeping through infinitesimal cracks. They look deliberate if you forget time, if you imagine a hand instead of a process. But the process is the only hand here. The chamber has waited longer than you have been alive to hold this moment of dry stillness. When you leave, the moss-line will remember your step as a compression, a small disturbance slowly rising back to level.

Outside again, the mist shifts and a shaft of actual sun, pale and watery, touches the moss for the first time in hours. The whole slope exhales visibly, a ghost of vapor rising from the green pelt, and in that moment you see how the light strikes droplets on every tip of every fern frond, a constellation of small suns held in suspension. It lasts for twenty heartbeats. Then the cloud closes, the light retreats, and the steady, intimate sound of water in its thousand small channels reasserts itself. The place returns to its long, slow equilibrium, barely registering that you are there.
