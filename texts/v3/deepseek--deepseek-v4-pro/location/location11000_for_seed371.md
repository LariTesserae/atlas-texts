It is not a place meant for standing still.

The walls rise sheer and glossy, twenty, thirty metres high, veined with dark mineral streaks that glisten in the only light there is—an intermittent, blood-orange pulse that comes from somewhere deep in the crevasse floor, flaring and dying in no discernible rhythm. The stone is black basalt, fresh as a wound, its edges so sharp in places they will open skin at a touch. This is geologically young rock, born yesterday in planetary terms, still settling into itself, still shedding flakes the size of dinner plates that sheer off without warning and crash into the undergrowth below. The sound of falling stone is constant, a sporadic percussion under the deeper roar.

And the crevasse is choked with green. Not the green of sunlight and soil, but something more desperate. Mosses thick as felted wool drape every ledge, every fissure, drinking the condensation that never quite becomes rain. The air is a living thing, saturated, cool enough that you can see your breath even as you sweat. Ferns uncurl in slow motion from vertical walls, their fronds beaded with water that trembles and falls with each vibration. There are plants here that have never seen the sky. Broad-leaved things with waxy, dark flesh, their roots gripping the raw stone like fingers. Vines loop across the narrow gap above, so dense in places they form a second, quaking ceiling, a suspended mat of vegetation that drips continuously onto everything below. The crevasse is only a few metres wide at its broadest, and the vegetation has woven itself into a tunnel, a throat, a green intestine in the rock.

The water is everywhere and nowhere. No stream runs along the floor—the floor is too broken, too recently shattered, a jumble of knife-edged boulders and sudden black drops. But moisture sheens every surface. Condensation runs in rivulets down the walls, gathers in the cup-like leaves of the canopy plants, plinks into hidden pools that echo in chambers unseen. Occasionally, a pocket of accumulated water gives way somewhere above, and a cold, hard shower falls for thirty seconds, a minute, driving the sound up to a deafening pitch before ceasing as suddenly as it began.

And the sound—the sound is the dominant fact of this place. It is not one sound but a composite, a dense, layered noise that seems to push against the eardrums. The bass note is a low, rhythmic thudding, a mechanical heartbeat that travels through the stone itself. It comes from below. Deep in the crevasse, partially buried by the rockfall and the vegetation, an engine runs. A large one. Its casing is matte black, non-reflective, studded with cooling fins that vibrate visibly. Pipes as thick as a man's arm emerge from it and disappear into the walls of the crevasse, into boreholes that are still sharp-edged, unweathered. Warm air shimmers above the engine casing, causing the nearest ferns to waver and recoil. The machine is industrial-grade, built for a purpose now opaque, and it is working. It has been working for a long time, unattended, its rhythm slightly off, a misfire in one of its cylinders creating a syncopated knock that prevents the brain from ever quite tuning the noise out.

The light comes from the engine's exhaust vent, a downward-facing port that glows with the heat of its operation. The orange pulse is a byproduct of some internal cycle, flaring brighter every few seconds as a piston completes its stroke, then dimming to a dull ember glow. It is the only illumination, and it paints the crevasse in a hellish chiaroscuro. Shadows leap and collapse. The wet leaves flash emerald for a moment, then vanish into absolute black. The moss appears to crawl with inner fire. There is no sky visible above the vine ceiling. There is no natural light at all.

No animals live here. The noise alone would preclude it. The engine's thud is felt in the bones, a constant assault that would drive any breathing creature to madness or flight. There are no insects burrowing in the moss, no bats hanging from the vine ropes, no small scurrying things in the crevices. The vegetation is silent on the animal level—no rustle of wings, no chittering call. The only movement is the slow, inexorable growth of the plants themselves, the drip of water, the tremble of the engine, and the occasional, violent fall of a newly sheared plate of basalt. The place is an engine block with a garden growing through it.

And yet it is not static. It is turbulent in its own botanical, mineral way. The vines grow visibly, or near enough—a frond that was closed when you looked away is half-unfurled when you look back. The stone is actively fracturing as it cools, thermal stresses from the engine below meeting the cool damp air above, creating a cycle of contraction and expansion that keeps every surface unstable. Water undermines a root-hold. A slab loosens. The entire crevasse is in a state of slow, noisy collapse and regrowth, a brawl between the living and the mineral, refereed by the thudding machine. It is a small theatre, this place—you could walk its length in under a minute, if you could walk at all across the shattered floor—but it contains a density of process, a sheer number of simultaneous, interacting events, that feels like standing inside a running motor. A green, wet, dark, and relentlessly loud motor, building and destroying itself in the same breath.
