A shallow basin stretches open beneath a sky that has forgotten brightness. It is a young land, raw and jagged, as if the earth’s skin split here only a handful of centuries ago and the wound still weeps. The terrain is a forest of obsidian shards, each one a blade of frozen volcanic glass, some no taller than a hand, others rising overhead like fractured monoliths. They cluster so tightly that you cannot walk three steps without turning sideways, and their edges—still sharp, unbitten by time—catch your sleeve, your skin, leaving little offerings of blood on the dark stone.

Moisture is everywhere. The air itself feels thick, a cool broth of condensation that drifts in slow, visible currents. Fog doesn't merely roll through this place; it lives here, a permanent resident, condensing on every gloss-black surface into beads and rivulets. The basin floor is barely above the water table, so a thousand tiny seep-springs ooze up between the shards, feeding an intricate lacework of shallow pools. No true rain falls, but the atmosphere weeps constantly—a post-rain landscape stalled forever in the moment after the deluge. Every surface glistens; every hollow holds a puddle; the ground underfoot squelches with a cold, mineral mud bereft of any humus.

No plants grow here. There is no soil to root in, only glass and fine-grained scoria dust that packs into the crevices. The world is purely mineral—a black, wet geology without green. Even the faintest smear of lichen is absent, though in a few sheltered niches there are stains of rust-colored microbial film, so thin and slow-living they barely register as life. You might mistake them for oxidation. In that near-absence, the landscape feels like a place before life, or after it, or parallel to it entirely.

And yet it’s not empty. The dense-packed shards create a kind of vertical labyrinth, every cubic meter packed with smooth, reflective planes angled in all directions. Vision is limited to a few arm-lengths by the fog, but within that reach you see layer upon layer of dark glass, reflections of reflections, your own fractured silhouette ghosting back at you from a dozen surfaces at once. This density of presence—stone, water, vapor—gives an almost claustrophobic richness to the open space.

Sound dominates. It is a loud world. Water drips. Drips from every edge, every point, every overhang. The pings and plinks fall into pools with a ceaseless pattering, and because the shards are so many and so close, each drop echoes, bounces, resounds. The acoustics are strange: the glassy surfaces reflect not just light but sound, so a single drip can cascade into a chorus of diminishing echoes, a metallic whisper that trails off for seconds. Add the deeper notes—the gurgle of water forcing its way through underground fissures, the occasional groan of a settling shard, the low, almost sub-audible thrum of the earth cooling—and the basin becomes a symphony hall for geology. It is never quiet. Even in the stillest moment, a hum pervades, a musicality that feels intentional, as if the stones are singing to themselves.

Dimness seals the scene. The fog scatters what weak light filters from above into a directionless, silvery murk. No shadows, no beams. Just a perpetual twilight, a flat gray that deepens to charcoal at the far edge of perception. At night, the dark becomes absolute, but for a faint bioluminescence that sometimes kindles in the pools—a pale blue-green glow, brief and quivering, born from the microbial films or perhaps from something else. Something stranger.

There are animals, but they are rare and secretive. A glint of movement might resolve into a blind, translucent amphibian clinging to a vertical slab, its skin the same wet glass as its perch. Pale, eyeless insects skate across pool surfaces on legs finer than hair. They make no sound of their own, moving through the constant acoustic rain like ghosts. Occasionally a larger shape—a sinuous, slow creature that seems half water itself—uncoils from a flooded crack and vanishes before you can be sure you saw it.

Scattered among the shards are marks of other presences. Not animals. Small, crude shelters: three-sided huddles of obsidian slabs leaned together, barely taller than a crouching child. Their arrangement is deliberate but ancient, edges now slightly softened by condensation but still too sharp to touch. On a few broad-faced shards, faint petroglyphs have been pecked: spirals within spirals, repeated arcs that mimic the sound waves rippling through the air. These are not tools—there is no metal, no cordage, no evidence of complex making—just stones moved by hands or something like hands, long ago, leaving behind a whisper of intent. Whether they were worship, territory marks, or something else is illegible, swallowed now by the patient mineral order.

The whole basin operates on a rhythm so slow it borders on stillness. The fog thickens and thins across cycles measured in seasons, decades. Water levels rise and fall imperceptibly, reshaping the pool networks over centuries. The shards themselves, for all their sharp youth, are gradually, gradually rounding; a sound-echo today is a hair’s breadth different from one a thousand years prior. This dynamic is not one of flux but of deep-time breathing, a place where change is a mere thought passing through the landscape’s mind.

And then there is the weirdness—the rule that sets this place slightly askew. The obsidian here has an odd property: it stores sound. Not all sound, but certain frequencies, certain resonances. Walk between two tall shards, and you might hear a fragment of an old conversation you never had, a rhythmic tapping that fell silent before this land was formed. The petroglyphs seem positioned to catch and release these echoes; stand before a spiral, and the stone sings back to you in a voice of dripping water and grinding lava. The phenomena are faint, ambiguous, easy to dismiss as tricks of the fog. But the rare fauna orient to these points. The blind amphibians rest against the carved surfaces, throats pulsing in time with vibrations no living throat produced. It is as if the basin is a record, a phonograph playing a composition of its own birth.

Time here feels different. Not slower—more like it’s looped, folded, the young stone already old with borrowed sound. The cool, wet air presses in, and the glass shards crowd around, and the endless drip-ping-drip echoes, and you realize the loudness is not chaos but chorus, a dense, saturated music that the land is making for itself, for no human ear. You are the rare creature, the faint mark, the anomaly passing through.

And then you pass on, and the basin seals its fog behind you, and continues its slow, loud, glistening song.
