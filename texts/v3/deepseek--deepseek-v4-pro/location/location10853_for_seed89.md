The walls lean close, and in the dark, distance is measured by sound.

You are inside a throat of stone—a vertical shaft, or perhaps a collapsed cavern, or perhaps a place that has never decided which it is. The walls are not smooth but tufted and knobbed with accretions that have no name in any language you know: nodules of mineral flesh, slick with perpetual condensate, each one a slow-growing exhalation of the rock itself. The passage is narrow enough that you could touch both sides at once if you stretched your arms wide, and that narrowness is constant, wrapping around you like a held breath. This is a small place, a room-sized place, though "room" implies flat floors and corners, and here there are none—only the organic pinch and swell of a space that was never made but rather happened, over spans of time that have no meaningful relationship to a human life.

The air is warm. Wet. It presses against your skin with a living insistence, the temperature of a mouth. Water does not flow here so much as it migrates: beads travel down the walls in slow, trembling lines, merging and parting, catching on the mineral excrescences before releasing and continuing their descent. The sound of this movement is everywhere—not a drip, which would imply regularity and separation, but a constant low seethe, a watery whisper that never pauses. Occasionally there is a deeper note, a *glup* from somewhere below where a larger pocket of water shifts its weight, and the sound travels up through the stone like a word spoken through bone.

The shaft is densely inhabited.

You cannot see them, not at first. The darkness is not the darkness of a closed room but the darkness of pure depth, of light that has never reached and never will. It is absolute, a pressure in the eyes. But you can hear them. The walls seethe with life. In the water-slick crevices, in the pores of the stone, clinging to every nodule and overhang, creatures move and breathe and conduct their slow business. Most are small—finger-length, hand-length—soft-bodied things that never evolved eyes because there has never been anything here to see. They communicate in wet clicks, in the susurrus of flesh against stone, in the faint percussive pops of mouths opening and closing on air thick enough to drink.

Some are larger.

Every few minutes, something heavy shifts in the darkness above you. You can track its progress by the rain of dislodged water droplets that patter across your shoulders, by the wet dragging sound of a body inching down the shaft, by the way the smaller sounds temporarily hush as it passes. These larger forms do not seem to notice you, or if they do, they do not care. They are following their own cycles—slow migrations that might take years to travel a hundred vertical feet, grazing on the mineral nodules, scraping the biofilm that coats every surface with radula-like tongues.

The smell is dense: wet limestone, copper, the musk of countless living bodies packed into a vertical crevice. Underneath it all, a faint organic sweetness, like something fermenting in the deep rocks.

This place is not young. Everything about it speaks of duration so extended that the concept ceases to mean anything. The walls are grooved and polished by the passage of bodies over millennia. The floor—insofar as the narrowing pinch of stone beneath your feet can be called a floor—is worn into shallow basins, each one cupping a dark pool of mineral-rich water. The erosion here is not the violent erosion of rivers or glaciers but the patient, cellular erosion of life itself, of secretions and gentle abrasions, of water that never freezes and never dries, working stone grain by grain across geologic time.

There are no tools here. No marks of hands, no shaped stones, no charcoal smudges, nothing that would indicate that anything with intent has ever passed this way. And yet: the way the shaft branches, about twenty feet above where you stand, is wrong. One of the offshoots is too regular, its opening too circular. The lip where it meets the main shaft is smooth in a way that the rest of the stone is not—not manufactured, exactly, but *worried*. Like something with patience and a rasping mouth has spent generations widening this passage because it preferred this route over the others. The technology of flesh, applied to stone. A kind of primitive shaping that has no tool but self, no plan but instinct, no timeline but the life of the species.

The fauna hangs in layers. Near your feet, in the thin film of water that never quite drains, threadlike worms twist in constant undulation. Higher, on the wet walls, soft polyps extend and retract in a rhythm that might be breathing or might be feeding, their bodies translucent enough that you can see the shadow-movement of fluids within. The larger grazers, the ones that click and drag, occupy the middle reaches. And above them all, near the unseen opening of the shaft where faint air currents stir, things with thin membranes catch the updraft and sing—long, wavering tones that filter down through the darkness like something half-remembered from a dream.

The temperature does not change. It holds at that steady, unsettling warmth, the warmth of internal spaces, of bodies, of deep rock that has never known a winter. You realize, after a time, that the warmth is itself a form of sound: it hums in the stone, a vibration too low to hear but present in the way the water shivers on the walls, in the way the smaller creatures align themselves to it. Something deep below generates heat—a thermal spring, perhaps, or a pocket of decaying matter the size of a cathedral, or simply the accumulated body-heat of millions of lives too numerous to count, packed into fissures and shafts and crawl-spaces that web the mountain for thousands of feet in every direction.

Time moves differently here. The water takes lifetimes to travel from the high entrance to the sump far below. The creatures measure their existence not in seasons—there are no seasons—but in the slow accretion of the mineral nodules, in the gradual shifting of the passages as bodies wear new routes through old stone. The loudness of the place is, you understand now, the sound of enormous patience expressed densely: every click and whisper and slither and *glup* is part of a chord that has been sounding, uninterrupted, for epochs.

The shaft continues downward into tighter dark and upward into the unknown source of the faint air-current that touches your face. The walls glisten. The things on them move in their blind, slow rhythms. The water whispers. The warm stone hums.

You are a visitor here only in the most conditional sense. The place has no concept of visitors. It has only inhabitants, and its own deep time, and the dense, wet, living dark that holds it all.
