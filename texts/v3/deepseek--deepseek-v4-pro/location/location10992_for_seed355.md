You descend into the basin through a pass that shouldn’t exist. The way down is flanked by what might once have been watchtowers, now slumped and softened like old bone, their edges chewed by an eternity of damp heat. The air thickens with every step, until breathing feels like drinking. By the time you reach the valley floor, the sky has vanished — not veiled, but replaced — by a ceiling of motionless, gray-lit vapor that traps the heat against your skin. It is hot, the kind of hot that makes stone weep. And everywhere, stone does weep. Condensation crawls down every surface in slow, silent rivulets, sheeting the city in a perpetual film of warm water.

This was a city. Is a city. The difference here is not a line but a blur, because the reality of the place has grown uncertain. The buildings rise around you without any crisp edges, their architectural grammar both deliberate and deeply wrong. Walls taper at angles that seem to shift when you aren’t looking directly at them. Stairways spiral down into plazas where the paving stones undulate in gentle, non-random patterns, as if the ground once flowed like taffy and then set in a fit of geological dreaming. Doorways open onto sheer drops, or onto rooms that are full of thin, empty air and a sense of something having just left. It is urban in the truest sense — there are streets, avenues, amphitheaters sunken into the dimness, bridges spanning canals of black water — yet nothing here was ever built by a tool. The stone is seamless, each block joined to the next without mortar, more grown than carved, as though the entire settlement was exhaled from the earth in one long, ordered breath and then abandoned to the weight of eons.

And eons have worked upon it. The erosion is deep and settled, a patient gnawing that has rounded every cornice and polished every balustrade until they feel like damp soapstone under your palm. This is not ruination as decay; it’s ruination as completion, the form finally reaching the state it was always intended to occupy. The city is sparse in its arrangement — wide boulevards stretch between the structures, so wide that the far sides melt into the fog before you can see them clearly. Open plazas lie cracked and uneven, their emptiness pressing in on you with a soundlessness so complete you hear your own pulse as an intrusion. A few pale, fleshy things grow in the gaps: not plants exactly, but organisms that have abandoned chlorophyll for something else, fanning out in slow-motion fronds the color of old ivory, their surfaces slick with a mucus that might be dew or might be something the stone itself exudes. They are sparse, though. Much of the city is bare, wet rock and the dark mirror of standing water.

Water is the only active citizen here. It pools in the gutters, deep and still and faintly warm, feeding a labyrinth of canals that divide district from district. Occasionally a bubble rises from nowhere, breaking the surface with a sound that is less a pop and more a whispered syllable, as if the city is trying to speak through its own drowned throat. Steam vents hiss from cracks in the pavement — geothermal breath from a heat source deep below, keeping the air at a perpetual, sweltering 40°C even in the utter absence of sun. The hot rocks beneath you make the atmosphere shimmer in the rare moments when a pocket of thinner mist passes through, revealing glimpses of buildings that loom at the wrong perspective, their upper storeys bending out over the street like curiosity.

There is no animal life. No scuttling, no birdsong, no insect drone. The stillness is so absolute that you can hear the water condensing — a constant, high-frequency susurrus like a rain heard from a mile away. Sound itself behaves oddly here: a dropped stone’s clatter arrives a full second late, and from a direction opposed to the source. Your own footsteps follow you with a half-second delay, as if the air is still deciding whether to admit your presence. This is the weirdness at work. Reality has worn thin in the valley, rubbed raw by the heat and the wet and the sheer passage of time until the boundary between what is and what might be has begun to fray. At the edge of your vision, the fog sometimes coils into shapes that resemble figures, but they dissolve the moment you turn your head. The light — such as it is — comes from no single point. It is a directionless, amber-gray luminescence, as if the mist itself is faintly phosphorescent, holding onto the memory of a dawn that broke here millennia ago and never fully set.

The city is a dynamic place, but its dynamism is that of weather cycles and tectonic patience. The fog thickens and thins on a rhythm all its own, breathing in and out of the streets in slow tides. Water evaporates from warm plazas, rises a few feet, and condenses again on the undersides of arches, dripping endlessly back down. The geothermal vents shift their positions with the slowness of a glacier, hissing in new spots each century. This is a landscape in motion, but a motion that takes geologic time to see.

You walk through a market square where the stalls are nothing but low, eroded platforms that might have once been basalt but now feel like sponge. The canal alongside it is perfectly clear and perfectly black, depthless. You kneel and touch the water — it is warm, and when you pull your fingers back, they are not wet in the way water wets. Instead, a thin, cold sensation lingers, and the skin of your fingertips tingles as if touched by a faint electric charge. The reality breakdown is not violent; it is subtle and pervasive, a wrongness that seeps into your bones like the heat. You realize, with a certainty that has no logical source, that the city is not empty. It is occupied by its own absence, by the echo of a population that has been erased not from history but from the possibility of history. The buildings are not ruins; they are memories of buildings, and the mist is the breath of something that is still dreaming them into existence.

You leave before the dream decides to notice you. The basin watches you go, silent and wet, its hot exhalations following you up the pass until you break out into real sky again, gasping for dry air that you no longer entirely trust. Behind you, the city sinks back into its gray light, active and quiet and utterly, breakably real.
