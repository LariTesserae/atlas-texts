The first thing is the sound. It has no source you can turn toward, no distance or direction. It is simply there—a vast, bottomless roar that presses on the eardrums and works its way into the hollows of the jaw, a noise so total that it becomes a kind of silence. The wind owns this place. It sweeps across an unbroken plain at such a constant, urgent pitch that the air itself seems to be undergoing a slow, violent tearing. And beneath the wind, riding under it like a second pulse, is a deeper hum: a subsonic drone that comes up through the soles of your feet, through the bone, a vibration that trembles in the chest long after you stop moving. Here, to be is to be inside a sound—deafened, filled, swallowed.

The ground is wrong. Your boot sinks into a surface that gives more than soil should, a firm but spongy resilience that pushes back with the memory of pressure. When you kneel, when you press a palm into it, the world dimples. A little cloud of vapor puffs from the crushed nap, carrying a smell that is sweet and fungal and dark. The warmth of it surprises you—a body heat, a living warmth that has nothing to do with the sun. This is not earth. It is a mat, a continuous living sheet that runs to every horizon, a single, unbounded organism made of billions of tiny, interwoven plants. Up close, it is a jungle in miniature: a chaos of overlapping leaflets no bigger than a fingernail, translucent hairs beaded with dew, minute trumpets and cups and spore-stalks in ochre and ruby and pale lime. But stand, and it blends instantly back into an impossible, uniform emerald, an emptiness of green that stretches as far as the eye can find.

You are on a high and ancient surface, a plateau lifted into the sky by forces that ceased their work in the world’s childhood. The elevation is extreme—high enough that the unacclimated lung soon aches for thinner air, high enough that the midday sun burns pale and cold in a deepened blue-black zenith. And yet the air temperature is warm, unexpectedly heavy and soft, carrying a near-palpable moisture. Water is a presence here but not an event: rain almost never falls, yet the land is perpetually damp. Banks of fog the size of seas roll slowly across the plain, condensing on every surface, beading on the fine fur of the mat, soaking it drop by drop. The entire plateau drinks from these wandering clouds, and in response the vegetation has grown into a lushness that has no need for trees or bushes—only this deep, saturated, all-covering green, thicker and more intense than any meadow.

The landscape is a thing of terminal erosion, a place that has been worn down to its final, resting shape. Once, in a time so deep it hardly counts as time, mountains stood here—jagged, furious spines. But wind and frost and the patient, millennial rub of the mat itself have planed them away. Now the terrain is a ghost of that old relief: a series of almost-imperceptible swells and shallow hollows, the faint, rolling memory of an ancient ocean swell frozen in stone. There are no cliffs, no canyons, no sharp edges of any kind. Only this endless, gentle undulation, and occasionally—rare as islands—a dark hump of bedrock that has survived the wearing, its surface polished to a mirror gleam by eons of dust-laden blast.

On those outcroppings, the only hard ground for miles, you find the scattered marks of people. The built world here is barely a whisper: a low ring of stones wedged into a crevice, laboriously shaped by hand; a scatter of obsidian flakes, their edges still sharp; a single, upright post of some iron-dense wood now soft as wet paper, set into a drilled socket. The technology that made them was primitive—edge and fire and twisted fiber, nothing more. No metal, no wheel, no permanent shelter. You might walk for three days and see no other sign, then crest a gentle rise to find a few small cairns, or a shallow pit carved into a rock dome, lined with a bed of dried mat-fiber. The emptiness of the land is a physical thing. The density of presence—living, built, or otherwise—is almost null. Vast volumes of space hold nothing but the wind and the mat and the far-off curve of the earth.

Fauna, like human marks, is a rarity. There are things that live in the mat—tiny, pale, soft-bodied creatures that tunnel between its layers and never surface—but to see an animal is a small event. A single bird, high and dark against the cloud-banks, may take an hour to cross the visible sky, and its passage leaves a wake of silence in the mind. The sound, after all, never stops. It is the true constant, the deafening baseline against which all other sensations must be measured. Not just the roar of the wind, but a steady, percussive underburden: the mat itself breathes in its slow way, and as it breathes, pockets of fermentation gas build and release with wet, tympanic bursts, deep and resonant, as if some vast creature were muttering just below the threshold of hearing.

That breathing is the clue to the landscape’s strangest and most defining trait: its turbulence. This ancient, flattened, apparently static world is in fact a place of violent and constant change. The mat is alive not just in the sense that its cells divide, but in the sense that it moves. It rises into low domes over the course of weeks, fed by some deep metabolic pressure, then subsides, swallowing and digesting whatever rested on it. A boulder placed on the surface will be gone within a few seasons, ingrown, incorporated. The scattered human builders knew this; their constructions cling only to the bare rock, and even there they are regularly shaken, cracked, and toppled by the slow heave. The air itself is a kind of storm-state: the wind shifts direction in an hour, in ten minutes, in a sudden, spiraling fury, whipping the fog into vortices that race across the plain like white ghosts. The sound changes with it, a ceaseless chaotic composition of howls and low booms and the higher, harsher hiss of spray swept from the mat’s surface.

And then there is the strangeness that settles over everything like the mist—an oddness you cannot quite pin into a single image. At night, under the normal, crystalline light of the stars, the deeper fissures in the exposed rock begin to glow with a faint, blue-green luminescence that pulses in time with the hum underfoot. The wind, when you stop struggling against it and simply listen, seems to carry patterns—repeating phrases of pressure, almost a syntax. The few structures on the rock slabs are aligned, you notice after many days, not to any cardinal point, but to something local: an acoustic node, a line of magnetic distortion, a place where the mat’s subsurface rhythms intersect. There are no carvings, no drawings, but the polished faces of the oldest stone have been etched by grit into branching, dendritic forms that exactly mimic the growth habit of the mat, as if the rock itself were learning the shape of life.

This is a world in its final, mature state—not young, not middle-aged, but settled into a form that has not substantially changed in millions of years and will not change until the sun itself alters. It is a place of extreme elevation and extreme age, of lush emptiness and deafening solitude, where the ground is a single, breathing being and the air a continuous, turbulent anthem. Stand long enough, and you begin to feel it not as a landscape you are in, but as something you are on—a living skin of the planet, warm and damp and roaring, waiting under the long light to absorb you gently back into the green.
