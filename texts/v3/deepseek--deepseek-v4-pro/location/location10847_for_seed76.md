Beneath a sky so bright it seems to bleach the edges of things, the High Steppe stretches away in all directions, a continent lifted toward the stars. There is no water here—not a drop, not a bead of condensation, not even a ghost of vapor curling from a nostril. The world is dry in a way that feels elemental, as if moisture were a myth the ground has long forgotten. The air is thin and cool, just above freezing at the zenith of the day-cycle, and it carries a faint tang, metallic and sharp, like the taste of distant lightning on a world that never knew rain.

The ground itself is a single weathered plate of mineral crust, pale as bone meal, crazed with a network of shallow fissures that have existed so long they seem less like cracks and more like the settled, contented wrinkles of old age. Nothing is sharp; every edge has been softened by aeons of wind that whittles at the patience of stone. This is a place of deep time, where erosion has reached an equilibrium, where the land no longer struggles against the sky but has made its peace with it. There are no mountains, no valleys—just the immense, almost imperceptible curvature of a high plateau the size of an ocean basin, its boundaries lost beyond the horizon’s white glare.

And scattered across it, at such distances that your mind might mistake each for a solitary world, stand the Shrouds.

They are lush—achingly, improbably lush. From a twisted, woody core that rises like a goblet stem from roots sunk deep into the thermal belly of the crust, each Shroud erupts into a cascade of fleshy, translucent tissues. Their forms are sinuous, layered, like frozen fountains of viscera, or the gills of a mushroom god turned inside out and left to bask. They vary in hue: some shine a vitreous jade, others a honeyed amber, a few a deep venous purple that seems to drink the light rather than reflect it. The surfaces glisten not with water but with a slow-oozing serum—a cool, syrupy acid that beads along ridges and drips with the solemn regularity of a pulse. From a distance, each Shroud is a dense, extravagant burst of life, a celebration of organic abundance in this otherwise sterilized expanse.

But between them, there is only silence and the wide, white floor. The Shrouds are sparse, each separated from the next by a walk of hours or days. The ground between is bare, save for faint, wandering trails of a darker mineral residue—likely the leavings of the rare creatures that crawl or flit from one Shroud to the next. These organisms are rare enough that you might stand for a full day-cycle and see only a single glint of movement: a flattened, silver-furred hexapod undulating along a trail, or a creature like a paper-thin kite riding the thermals that rise from the organism-pumped warmth of a Shroud’s core. Their sounds are negligible—a soft rasp of chitin on crust, the brief shiver of a membrane catching wind—settling back immediately into the immense quiet. It is a quiet so complete that you can hear the slight crackle of your own joints, the whisper of your clothing, the faint rush of your own interior fluids that mark you as profoundly alien here.

Despite the stillness, this is an active world. The Shrouds themselves embody a slow, vegetal pace of life, but not a passive one. Their fronds shift incrementally to track the blazing white sun across the sky; their pores open and close in a tidal rhythm, inhaling the thin atmosphere and exhaling a faint, foggy discharge that quickly dissipates. This exhalation is part of a vast, slow weather cycle: the Shrouds pump trace gases into the high air, creating tenuous haze layers that sometimes crystallize into glittering veils, chemical cirrus that refract the light into arcs of ultraviolet and soft mint. No rain falls, but a kind of dry precipitation occurs—a gentle sifting of microscopic crystals that settle like diamond dust on the backs of the Shrouds, feeding them essential minerals.

The landscape’s weirdness is not in the grotesqueness of form, but in the inversion of expectations. Lushness without humidity—a riot of tissue thriving in absolute aridity. Coolth without frost—the serum within them resists freezing, its chemistry anarchic by water’s standards. The Shrouds’ metabolism is non-aqueous, built on a solvent that runs in veins like liquid stone, sluggish at the ambient temperature but perfectly adapted. They are gardens of acid and polymer, and the bright light feeds them through pigments that glitter like mica, not like chlorophyll. Standing beside one, you might hear it: a deep, subsonic thrumming, the sound of liquid being drawn up from mineral depths many kilometers below, a circulatory system that moves to the beat of a world’s slow pulse.

This is a landscape of distances and solitudes, of an ecology that has cleaved sparse and lush into a single, harmonious paradox. The High Steppe does not accommodate the human scale. It is a place shaped by forces that have time to waste and no need for witness. The only true sound, when the wind ceases and the Shrouds pause their breathing, is the faint, silvery hum of the planet herself, spinning on beneath the blank, bright vault—a noise like a held breath, or the memory of an ocean that never was.
