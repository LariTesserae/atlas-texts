You walk the high back of the world, where the air is thin and cool enough to numb your fingers but not to freeze, and the light comes from everywhere: a pale, hammering glare thrown off a white sun too large and too close, and mirrored again from the endless ground. The ground itself is the first thing that tells you this place is not like any other. It is not soil, not stone, not sand. Underfoot it gives slightly, a tight-stretched resilience, like walking a trampoline made of old leather. It continues to every horizon, a single unbroken surface, tan and gray and the colour of bone, with a texture like the hide of some impossibly huge animal—creviced, dimpled, here and there drawn taut over what might be buried ridges or the ribs of a dead world. The scale of it means you could walk for days and the only change would be in the angle of the light and the pitch of the sound.

That sound. It is the first true assault. Deafening was the parameter, and it is. A continuous, multi-layered roar that is not wind alone, though wind is part of it—a low-frequency booming that throbs in the chest, a high-pitched keening that saws at the skull, and over it all a constant crackle and hiss like a thousand arc-welders striking at once. The surface is alive with sound. Vents open and close across the leathery ground in slow pulses, puckered mouths that exhale plumes of gas with a papery rattle, then suck inward with a gulping roar. These exhalations rise as trembling columns of heat-shimmer, and they are the source of the industrial smell: not oil or smoke, but something sharper, like ozone and ground metal and the electrical tang before a lightning-strike. You look closer at one of these vents and see its edges are complex, banded with rings of what might be muscle or might be chitin, and within is a darkness that pulsates with a deep orange glow, a biological furnace.

The vegetation is total. There is no alternative substrate. Every inch of the continent—and it is a continent, large enough to birth its own weather—is covered by this single vast entity, or rather by a skin of many entities knitted so tightly that they are one. It is a pure-plant ecosystem, but "plant" here is a term of convenience for something that does not use water, that photosynthesises not with chlorophyll but with a dark reticulated pigment that drinks the blinding light and converts it with a soft, pervasive hum. The hum is part of the roar. It rises and falls as the light shifts, and here and there the surface itself moves—not in the slow creep of a glacier, but in sudden, whip-fast ripples, like a shaken cloth, as if the whole continent were a single organism caught in a dream of constant, chaotic change. The turbulence of the place is built into its biology.

In the distance, things rise. Sparse. The density parameter: much space between presences. These presences are monumental—stacks and chimneys of the same material as the ground, but grown upward, fluted and twisting, like termite mounds designed by a mad cathedral architect. They rise hundreds of feet, spaced so far apart that each is a solitary island in the shimmering air. Some vent thicker gases, black and coiling, that form miniature weather systems of their own, brief thunderheads that crackle with static and dissipate in minutes. Others are silent and dark, their sides pocked with openings from which fauna emerges and into which it retreats.

The fauna is abundant. You hear it even through the world-roar: a layer of clicks, whistles, and sudden shrieks that cut the air. Creatures ride the howling winds, paper-thin gliders with multiple wings that fan and fold, their bodies little more than nerve-bundles and muscle-strips, riding the thermals and the jets of vent-gas with manic energy. They swarm in sudden tornadoes of life, thousands of them whirling around a single chimney, feeding on the particulate matter it spews, then scatter as the chimney’s pitch changes—perhaps under attack, or simply in a cycle. On the ground itself, the sparse but continuous mat is grazed by slow-moving crawlers shaped like discs of iron-grey shell, their undersides flickering with electrical-blue cilia that rasp the leather surface and seem to digest it into a pale, crumbling dust. They leave silvery trails that heal within minutes, the skin of the continent knitting back with a faint, ultrasonic scream that is part of the general din. Predators come from the air, diving with folded bodies to impale the crawlers on stingers, and then the ground itself responds: around the kill, the leathery skin puckers and rises, wrapping the carcass in new growth, absorbing it back into the system in a matter of hours.

The erosion is established. For all the frenetic change—the constant venting, the sudden collapses of chimney-stacks that fall in a slow-motion avalanche and are utterly reabsorbed within a day, the storms that whip the skin into temporary dunes of itself—the landscape is in balance. It has been this way for eons. The bedrock, if there is bedrock beneath the living membrane, is never seen. The membrane is the world, and it has its own steady state of dissolution and regrowth, a Solaris-like cycle of creation and destruction that never tips into final ruin. The cool temperature, the high elevation, the absolute dryness: these are constants. There is no water. Not a drop. The chemistry is non-aqueous, based on some thick, oily medium that flows in the deepest layers of the living skin, and the "plants" extract what they need directly from the mineral dust trapped in the atmosphere or from the underlying crust, metabolising silica and metal into their tissues. Their colour is the colour of bone, of old ivory, with here and there a rusty stain of iron.

And yet there is technology. Industrial, the parameters said, and it is. No built structures, no made artifacts, no sign of a maker—but the whole system is a factory. The vents are stacks. The chimneys are fractionating columns. The ground is a vast, electrified membrane that hums with a steady, purposeful current. The creatures are components; the sudden swarms of gliders are perhaps waste-cleaners, the slow grazers are feedstock. The entire continent operates with the scaled, mechanical logic of a factory floor, a bazaar of chaotic but organised production, where the product is not something one holds, but energy, information, a continuous roar of data embedded in the sound. The deafening noise is not random: it carries patterns, spikes, rhythms that shift with the light and the wind. If you stood long enough, you might begin to parse it, to feel the industrial processes unfold in a language of vibration.

The light remains bright, nearly constant in its hammering, and the temperature stays cool. No day or night; some other cycle governs the fluctuations. The sparseness means that between the chimneys and the occasional heaps of shed shell-material, there is nothing but the endless drumskin of the world, vibrating with its own internal thunder. It is a place that makes no provision for the observer. It is untouched, undeveloped by any outside hand, and it is utterly alien in its combination of sparse emptiness and total vegetal cover—a desert of living leather, a cool highland of biological industry, a deafening, turbulent, bright-lit plane where the rules are not the rules of Earth but a coherent other physics. And it goes on, continental, planetary, beyond any horizon you could reach.
