**They are called the Lantern-Bound.**

They do not have a name for themselves any longer. The word that once meant “people” has slowly worn down, like the knotwork on every wall, until it simply means “those who remain in equilibrium.” There are never more than two hundred and twelve of them. Never fewer. When one dies, a child is born on the same night, in the same hour, delivered in absolute silence so the rhythm of falling water is not disturbed. The newborn is always pearl-pale, blind at first, exactly like the little amphibians that click across the stone. Sight comes later, softly, as though the eyes themselves are afraid of arriving too loudly.

They are slender, almost weightless, with skin the color of wet limestone and faint luminous veins that trace the same spirals carved into the architecture. Their hair is never cut; it grows in long moss-green strands that they weave with living liverworts so the strands appear to be part of the walls when they stand still. Most of the time they *are* still. They have perfected the art of becoming background. You can pass within arm’s reach of one and mistake them for a curtain of hanging moss until they exhale, and a single droplet trembles on their lower lip.

Their faces are narrow, tranquil, and ageless. After thirty or forty years the features stop changing, as though the damp has cured them. The oldest among them look like statues that have learned how to breathe. Only their eyes move with any frequency—slow, deliberate sweeps that chart the same circuits the silver-dusted moths have flown for centuries. When they look at you (if they choose to), the gaze is neither warm nor cold. It is measuring. They are checking whether you, too, have become part of the saturation yet. Whether you have begun to understand that movement is a kind of violence here.

They wear robes the color of the luminous lichen, stitched so cunningly with threads of living root that the garments appear to grow from their shoulders. The cloth is never fully dry. It clings, it beads, it glistens. Beneath it their bodies are almost sexless, softened by the endless moisture and the long refusal of haste. Desire exists, but it is conducted like everything else: in near-total silence, over many nights, with the patience of moss colonizing stone. Conception, when it happens, is treated as a geological event.

They do not speak often. When they do, the language has thinned to something between breath and water-sound. Vowels are held until they tremble on the edge of becoming droplets. Consonants have mostly dissolved. A full sentence can take nearly a minute to fall from the tongue. They consider this courteous. Anything quicker would be an imposition on the air.

Their names are not words. They are durations. A particular pattern of held breath, a certain number of heartbeats, the precise rhythm in which someone taps two fingernails against a root. You learn a person’s name by listening to them exist near you for weeks. The one who tends the black glass Wick is known only as the Pause Between Bells. She has not spoken aloud in eleven years. She does not need to. The slow bells inside the glass speak for her.

Every adult has a duty that has been performed in exactly the same way by the same bloodline for so long that the task and the person have fused. One lineage polishes the pale stone buttresses with nothing but the oil from their own skin and the softest parts of moth wings. Another lineage counts the shrimp in the basins each night and returns any that have wandered to the exact same pebble they left. A third lineage simply walks the concentric rings, always widdershins, always at the same speed, carrying a single droplet of water on a leaf. If the droplet falls they begin again. No one has seen them fail in four hundred years.

They are not monks. They are not ascetics in any way that seeks transcendence. They have already arrived. This is the place that is *enough*. Every gesture is a form of maintenance. Every stillness is worship. They do not pray to the black glass shaft; they protect its right to remain incomprehensible. When the distant bells inside it sound—once every seventy-three days, always at the same hour—they all stop whatever they are doing and bow their heads until the final tone fades back into the root-mass. Even the moths land. Even the blind amphibians cease clicking. The silence afterward is so perfect it feels like being forgiven for something you have not yet done.

There is no grief here, not as the outer world understands it. When the time comes for one of them to die, they walk to a particular hollow in the seventh layer, lie down inside the living wood, and simply stop participating in the act of being separate. The moss accepts them within days. Their bodies do not rot; they become vivid. Tiny orchids bloom from their mouths. The family does not mourn. They adjust the rhythm of the droplets on that particular root so the new absence becomes part of the music.

If you stayed long enough, you would begin to see that they are not *people* living *in* the Hollowed Lantern.

They are the Lantern’s memory of what it is like to have a pulse.

And the Lantern, in its slow, black, ringing way, is learning how to forget that it was ever anything else.
