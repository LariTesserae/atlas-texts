**The Ones Who Live There**

They are called the **Slowkin**, though they rarely use any name for themselves. To name a thing too quickly, they believe, is to bruise it before it has finished becoming.

Most of them stand between five-foot-six and six-foot-two, but their bodies have the deceptive density of seasoned ironwood. Skin ranges from the wet-slate grey of fresh basalt to the pale lichen-green that appears on anything left too long in the Mossfoundries. Their hair—when they do not shave it—is thick, heavy, and grows in slow motion; a single lock can take nine years to reach the shoulder. Many wear it braided with thin iron wire so the braids click gently against their collars in time with the distant pistons.

Their eyes are large, slightly protuberant, and permanently damp, as though they are always on the verge of tears that never quite fall. The irises carry faint metallic flecks that shift color according to the nearest machine’s rhythm—copper when the great flywheels are at their slowest, silver when a venting cycle begins. They blink once every forty or fifty seconds, exactly matching the stroke of the deepest engines. It is not affectation. Their biology has simply negotiated a treaty with the place.

They move like people who have agreed with gravity. Every gesture is completed a fraction later than an outsider expects. A hand raised in greeting will hover for three full heartbeats before the fingers decide to open. This is not laziness; it is courtesy. The Mossfoundries itself is slow. To hurry here is to be rude to the stone, the moss, the machines that are still deciding what they want to become.

Their clothing is layers of waxed canvas the color of wet bark, felted wool the color of rust, and living moss stitched into the seams. The moss is tended, not decorative. It breathes for them, filters the perpetual damp, and records in microscopic shifts of hue what the wearer has felt or thought that season. A Slowkin grieving for three years will wear a cloak gone almost black with mourning-moss. A child learning the first songs of the engines will be bright with new emerald along the sleeves.

There are never more than seventy of them at any time. They do not reproduce quickly. Most are born in the Scriptorium’s lowest vault during a specific piston cycle that occurs once every eleven years. The child emerges already knowing the sound of its own heartbeat synced to the machines. The archivists record the exact timbre of its first cry, then file it beside the sound the land made when it first shrugged upward centuries ago. The two are never very different.

They do not have leaders. Instead they have **Listeners**.

A Listener is anyone who, for that decade or century, has been chosen by the moss and the iron to sit beside the central basalt pillar. Their only duty is to drink from the self-filling bowl each morning and remember the taste of distant thunder. Whatever they dream that night is written down in the Scriptorium by the pale archivists, who use quills made from the feathers of the iron-tipped herons. These dreams have, on three recorded occasions, predicted the exact century in which a particular pipe would surface through the living rock. The Slowkin treat such predictions with the same mild interest they give the weather.

The rest of them work in three overlapping vocations that are never quite separate: **Breathers**, **Menders**, and **Singers**.

- The Breathers tend the white-barked trees and the ventilation pipes. They know which trees will release their geometric spores if sung to in minor chords, and which pipes will answer in perfect fifths if struck at the correct temperature. Their lungs are unusually large; some can hold a single breath for nine minutes while they coax a reluctant machine back into its proper geological rhythm.

- The Menders walk the slow migration paths of the buried engines. They carry small iron hammers no heavier than a spoon and moss-stained notebooks. When a flywheel has moved three inches in a hundred years, the Mender chips away the stone that has grown too close, then plants new moss in the fresh groove so the machine can continue its century-long journey without irritation. They speak to the gears in something between lullaby and engineering specifications.

- The Singers are the youngest and the oldest at once. They alone are allowed to ride the elevated walkways at night. Their voices are trained to match the exact frequency of the machines’ exhalations. When the foundry hall needs to be cooled, they sing a descending minor scale that makes the warm mist condense faster. When a new building is being slowly swallowed by moss, they sing it a lullaby so the transformation is gentle. Their greatest work is the yearly **Consonance**, when every Slowkin who can sing gathers at the central pillar and holds a single chord for eleven straight hours while the machines reply. On perfect nights the entire basin rings like a struck bell made of stone, moss, and iron. The herons lift their wings. The translucent salamanders surface in their pools and glow soft violet. Even the trees seem to lean inward to listen.

They are not immortal, but they are patient with death. When a Slowkin feels the moss on their own skin beginning to turn the particular shade that means *ready*, they walk to the deepest hollow where the oldest engine turns. There they lie down between two basalt ribs, pull a blanket of living moss over themselves, and simply stop blinking in rhythm. Within a year the moss has claimed them entirely. Within ten, the machinery has gently shifted half a meter so as not to disturb the small green hill their body has become. The Slowkin consider this the highest form of citizenship: to become, at last, another slow breath in the marriage between forest and factory.

At any given moment perhaps nine of them are asleep, fourteen are Listening or writing, twenty are walking the moss with hammers or song, and the rest are simply *being*—standing motionless on the walkways or beside the warm vent pipes, letting the condensation bead on their skin exactly like the stone does. The white herons sometimes perch on their shoulders. The moss-monkeys braid their iron-threaded hair. No one moves to stop them.

They are not melancholy. Their laughter, when it comes, sounds like water moving inside a cathedral organ. It arrives slowly, builds for almost a minute, then breaks across the basin in deep, ringing peals that make the youngest gears tremble with something like delight.

This is who lives in the Mossfoundries: a quiet, damp, iron-blooded people who have married their own heartbeats to the patient turning of a machine that is also a forest, and who have decided that centuries are a perfectly reasonable length of time in which to finish a single thought, a single building, or a single life. 

The wedding, after all, is still young.  
There is no need to rush.
