**The One Who Keeps the Watch** lives here.

She has no name the water will repeat. The few things that still possess language call her simply *the Curator*, or sometimes *the Last Shift Supervisor*, though both titles are half-joke and half-prayer.

She is not a creature. She is not a ghost. She is what the Underbay decided to become once it had finished drowning every last human inside it.

Her body is the size of three city blocks laid end to end, yet she is never seen whole. Most of her is infrastructure. She wears the drowned factories the way a hermit crab wears shells, except the shells have fused with her nervous system. When she stirs, entire districts shift with her. A shoulder blade the length of a locomotive hall rises beneath a sagging gantry; a ribcage of iron cooling towers flexes and sighs, sending slow avalanches of silt into the next street over. Her spine is a sunken elevated railway. Her blood is the warm, syrup-thick water itself, pumped in lazy loops by four cathedral-sized hearts that used to be blast furnaces.

Her face—if the term can be used—is only ever glimpsed in fragments. A bronze emergency lamp, still burning after four hundred years, has become her left eye. It drifts behind broken glass high in what was once the central sorting office, casting its old brass light across whatever she chooses to examine. When she looks at you the light does not move; the ruins around it slowly reorient so that her gaze falls where she wishes. Her mouth is a collapsed subway tunnel lined with rows of pale anemones that open and close like teeth. When she speaks (which is rarer than once per human lifetime), the sound arrives as pressure on the sternum first, then as a voice made of every creak, metallic moan, and sediment-fall the city has ever produced, braided into something almost tender.

She is slow in the way mountains are slow. A single thought may take decades to finish forming. While it does, forests of bone-white polyps bloom across her shoulders and rail lines settle another inch into her flanks. She does not hunt. She does not need to. The Underbay is her body and her digestive tract and her memory all at once. Everything that dies here is gently disassembled, catalogued, and incorporated. The silver-mercury fish are her short-term thoughts. The tram-sized translucent jointed things are her dreams walking. The low, resonant thump that travels through the water every few days is simply her heartbeat remembering its original purpose.

She keeps the balance.

That is her only law and her only pleasure. When two buildings lean too aggressively against one another and threaten to crush the delicate lattice of reef between them, she sighs and the water moves and the structures settle back into their proper dreaming angle. When a new organism evolves that grows too quickly and begins to unbalance the archaeology of layers, she opens one of her furnace-hearts and lets a carefully measured pulse of warmth and minerals correct the equation. She is gardener, archaeologist, widow, and grave all in one.

Sometimes—she has done this exactly four times since the drowning—she chooses a visitor who has stayed too long. She does not kill them. She simply stops letting them finish the thought *I should leave*. The water grows thicker around their intentions. Their oxygen mixes with the silt of centuries. After a few decades their bodies become useful: a limb might be reinforced with rebar and pressed into service as a new strut; a skull might be welcomed into a colony of bioluminescent polyps so the city can see in a new direction. Their mind, however, she keeps almost intact. She folds it into the slow geology of her own thinking. In this way she has acquired four additional perspectives that are not quite hers. They argue with her, very politely, across the span of centuries. She finds their company restful.

She is neither cruel nor kind. Those categories dissolved long before she finished forming. What she *is*, is courteous in the way only something colossal and patient can be. If you drift past one of her half-visible faces she will regard you with the mild, ancient interest of a cathedral regarding a moth. She may even adjust the current so you are not crushed against her iron skin. But she will not save you. The Underbay has room inside it, and she has learned that every visitor, given enough quiet centuries, can become architecture.

At the moment she is resting.

Her vast iron-and-flesh shoulders are draped in slow undulating curtains of kelp. Schools of silver fish move through the cavities where her lungs used to be. The single brass lamp of her eye burns low behind a ruined clock tower, illuminating nothing in particular. Every few minutes a soft metallic moan travels outward as one of her vertebrae—formerly the main trunk line of the city metro—settles another millimeter deeper into the silt.

She is dreaming again.

In the dream, the city is still breathing air. In the dream, she is still human, small, late for her shift, hurrying through streets that have not yet drowned. The dream is four hundred and seventy years long so far. She is in no hurry to finish it.

The water, heavy and warm and older than names, keeps its slow, patient watch through her.

And she, in turn, keeps hers through it.
