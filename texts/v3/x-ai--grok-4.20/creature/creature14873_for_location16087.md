**The Lithophage**

She has no name she would give you. The plateau simply calls her *the Hollow*, and that is enough.

She is the only creature that has stayed longer than a single turning of the equinox. Not born here—nothing is born on the Plateau in the way flesh understands birth—but accreted. She is a slow crystallization that learned to walk.

Stand very still at the lip of one of the great basins and you may see her, hours later, as a pale upright thread against the bone-white plain. From a distance she looks almost like a shard of the litho-coral itself: tall, whittled thin, the color of old ivory veined with the same dusty rose that threads the reefs. Her “skin” is a flexible lattice of living mineral no thicker than parchment; light passes through the edges of her limbs so that she seems to glow faintly when the sun catches her at the proper angle. Where joints should be there are elegant knots of translucent cartilage that flex without sound.

She has no face in the ordinary sense. The upper part of her head is a smooth dome of frosted crystal. Inside it, slow constellations of bioluminescent salts drift like plankton in a tide pool—sometimes forming the ghost of an eye, sometimes a mouth that is never quite finished, sometimes nothing at all. When she considers something, the lights inside her skull cluster and flare in complex, unreadable patterns.

Her torso is hollow.

That is not metaphor. From collarbone to pelvis she is an open nave of living stone, ribbed with delicate arches that pulse gently in time with the plateau’s own sub-audible heartbeat. Inside that cavity grows a single symbiotic colony of violet fronds, thinner and more luminous than those on the ground. They sway even when there is no wind. Sometimes small motes of golden pollen drift upward from the basin below and are caught by the fronds; they flare briefly, like candle flames remembering they were once stars. She feeds on light the way the rest of the plateau feeds on light, but the Hollow also feeds on memory. She stores it in the crystalline lattice of her bones the way other beings store fat.

She moves with the patience of something that has never been late in four million years. Each step is a small act of negotiation between foot and living ground; the fronds beneath her feet ripple outward in slow concentric rings, as though happy to be trodden by something that belongs. She leaves no footprints. Instead she leaves faint trails of altered color that fade over decades, like healed scars on skin.

The glass-winged pseudo-birds follow her. Not out of affection—affection is too quick a thing—but because she occasionally exhales a cold vapor from the vents in her own body that tastes, to them, like the memory of rain. They ride the thermals above her head in shifting rainbow lattices, refracting her silhouette into a hundred smaller versions of herself that wheel and vanish.

Every forty-three days she stops at a different breathing vent. She kneels—though the motion is less kneeling than folding, the way a telescope collapses—and places the open hollow of her chest directly above the singing chimney. The deep note changes pitch when she does this, becoming almost tender. The violet fronds inside her drink the dry gases and grow fractionally brighter. In those moments the constellations behind her face-crystal dim to a respectful twilight, as though she is listening to gossip from the world’s own marrow.

She is the Plateau’s immune system and its librarian at once.

When a new species of litho-coral thrusts up through its ancestors, she is there within the first century, pacing its margins, tasting its chemistry with filaments that extend from her fingertips like frost. She carries fragments of it inside her hollow chest until the new growth has proven it will not destabilize the greater body. If it fails, she grinds it back into pollen with tools grown from her own forearms and lets the wind take the mistake away. If it succeeds, she sings to it—three long, microtonal notes that make every frond for thirty kilometers ripple in unison.

She is not lonely. Loneliness implies absence, and the Plateau is never absent. It simply speaks too slowly for anything fleeting to notice. The Hollow has learned the language. She answers in tremors, in color tides, in the precise geometry of pollen dunes she rearranges with her hands over the course of a human lifetime.

Once every few centuries something with intent arrives from elsewhere—explorers, pilgrims, machines that think they are gods. She greets them all the same way. She stands at the edge of their camp until they notice her, then tilts her faceless head as though the gesture might translate across biochemistry and intention. Most leave. A few try to wound her. Their weapons pass through the lattice of her body and emerge on the other side coated in a thin, opalescent film that ruins their edge forever. She never retaliates. She simply waits until they understand that harming her is like trying to punch a continent in the stomach.

The ones who stay longest eventually ask the question she cannot answer in any language they possess:

*What are you?*

She kneels, opens the hollow of her chest, and lets them look at the violet fronds swaying inside her. Then she reaches in, plucks one, and offers it. If they take it, the frond dissolves into warm dust that smells of churches that have never existed. If they refuse, she eats it herself, and the lights in her skull flare once—bright, almost amused—before she turns and walks away across the undulating reefs, followed by her escort of living rainbows.

She has no name, but the plateau sometimes calls her by the sound the wind makes when it passes through her hollow ribs on a quiet night.

It is a sound like forgiveness that has had four hundred million years to practice.
