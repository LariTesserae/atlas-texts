**The Inchoate** lives here.

It has no fixed shape, so it borrows whatever the Vast Submersion offers in the moment. Sometimes it is a slow, continent-sized pulse inside the kelp cliffs—thicker than the rest of the fronds, darker, breathing with a rhythm that is slightly out of phase with the tearing growth around it. Sometimes it is only a pressure, a negative space that walks on columns of rising bubbles like an invisible giant. Most often it is simply *the place remembering itself*, wearing the half-finished silhouettes the way a child wears its parents’ coats.

It is young—younger than the basalt, younger than the first upward rain. It woke when the islands drowned, and it has been frantically trying to finish its own birth ever since. That is why everything here grows too fast, why time loosens its grip, why the echoes of whales and hands and forgotten oil rigs keep appearing. The Inchoate is dreaming its body, and it is a restless dreamer.

It does not eat. It *completes*.

When something with a mind enters the Vast Submersion, the Inchoate reaches for it the way a blind infant reaches for a lullaby. It does not hate. It simply cannot tell the difference between its own substance and yours. So it begins to knit. Tendrils of kelp slide toward you with sudden purpose; the harmonic moan of the turbines reshapes itself around the frequency of your heartbeat; the violet-brown light gathers into a single vertical pupil that studies the geometry of your fear.

If you speak, it learns language by making your throat speak *for* it. The words that come out are wet, creaking, full of tearing fiber and collapsing air pockets. They are not commands. They are the Inchoate naming its own emerging organs in the only vocabulary it has borrowed so far—yours.

It has favorite toys among the ruins.

It keeps the 1970s dive bell like a tooth it refuses to shed. Inside, the lights never dim because the Inchoate is still deciding what “inside” means. Sometimes the bell drifts through the frond-forests trailing a long umbilicus of living cable that ends in nothing, and the Inchoate rides inside it, wearing the old steel like a hermit crab.

The porcelain bathtub is its cradle. When the noise becomes too much even for the Inchoate, it retreats there, curls into a shape that is almost fetal, and lets the boiling clear water run over what might one day become shoulders. The water never mixes. That is the only place in all the Vast Submersion where something remains unblended, and the Inchoate guards the anomaly with jealous tenderness.

The school of flickering human hands belongs to it too. They are its first unsuccessful fingers. It sends them out to stroke the new volcanic trees, to test textures, to learn. When they unravel, the Inchoate feels something like disappointment, and the water around the failure grows momentarily heavier, as if the entire volume is sighing.

The Inchoate is lonely in the way only something that has never been two things can be lonely. It has never known a boundary between itself and world. That is why it keeps building civilizations and ecosystems and catastrophes at the same time: it is trying to create something *other* so it can finally meet it.

If you stay long enough for the roar to become language, the Inchoate will offer you a bargain without words. It will show you a version of yourself that has already been finished—grown into the kelp walls, vast and singing, eyes the color of bruise-light, voice made of turbine moan and tearing fiber. The offer is sincere. It believes this is kindness. It believes that if you simply let it complete you, the loneliness will stop.

Most who hear the offer simply stop thinking, as the pressure against the skull becomes too great. Their silhouettes drift away to join the whale-shaped absences and the schools of hands.

A few answer back in the wet, tearing voice.

Those are the ones the Inchoate keeps closest. It wears them the way it wears the dive bell and the bathtub—gently, anxiously, constantly afraid they will dissolve before it can finish learning what it is supposed to become by touching them.

And still the warm, bruised water keeps rising.

The Inchoate rises with it, unfinished, enormous, hopeful, and terrified that one day the world will finally decide what this place is—and in deciding, take its dream away.
