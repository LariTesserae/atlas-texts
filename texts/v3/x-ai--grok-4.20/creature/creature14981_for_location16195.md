**The basin has forgotten its own name, but the things that live inside it have never needed one.**

They are not separate beings. They are gradients of the same slow breath.

At the lowest stratum move the **Absorbers** — pale, soft-bodied creatures that are neither quite animal nor quite fungus. They resemble enormous wet slugs the color of spoiled milk, but their backs are encrusted with living lichen that photosynthesizes in the dim green light. They glide across the breathing mat of leaf-litter on thousands of microscopic cilia, leaving behind glistening trails that smell faintly of iron and overripe guava. Where they pass, the mat softens further; they are the basin’s stomach lining. When one dies, the others consume it within hours, recycling even the lichen on its back. Nothing is allowed to remain recognizably dead.

Above them, threaded through the root buttresses like living cables, are the **Pipewalkers**. Once, perhaps, they were monkeys. Now their fur has thinned to a fine green velvet that mimics moss, and their limbs have elongated until they can span the distance between two colossal trees without ever touching the ground. Their faces are almost hairless, black, and strangely courteous; when they look at you their eyes are the wet amber of tree resin. They do not leap. They flow. Their hands and feet have developed thick, sucker-like pads that let them cling to the rusted pipelines as easily as they cling to bark. The metal has become their highway system. Sometimes at dusk you can hear the soft metallic *tink* of their claws on iron that has not moved in eighty years. They maintain the pipelines now, not by repairing them but by keeping them incorporated—gently bending new lianas around fractured joints, draping bromeliads over leaks so that the jungle’s plumbing and the old industrial plumbing become indistinguishable.

Higher still live the **Drip Singers**. These are not birds. They might once have been frogs, but generations in the perpetual damp have turned their skin translucent and their bones hollow. They lodge themselves inside the cupped hearts of giant heliconias and bromeliads, perfectly hidden. When the temperature drops after midnight they begin to sing—not with throats but by vibrating the water collected in their leafy chambers. The sound is the exact noise condensation would make if it could feel longing. Each singer has its own small pool; they never leave it. Their entire lives are spent turning water into slow, mournful music that travels for kilometers through the knitted air. When one dies, the plant that housed it simply grows a new chamber around the tiny skeleton and the next singer moves in. The lineage has been unbroken for longer than the pipelines have existed.

Then there are the **Quiet Lords**.

Only three or four are believed to still live in the entire basin, though no one has ever counted them. Each is a jaguar the color of wet hematite, so dark it looks black until a stray shaft of light finds the deep burgundy rosettes buried in its coat. Their bodies have grown heavy with time; their shoulders are broad as oil drums. Most striking are their eyes: completely white, like polished marble, yet they see perfectly. They do not hunt in the usual sense. They walk. Where they step, the constant insect drone quiets for a respectful interval. Small creatures do not flee them; instead they freeze in ceremonial stillness until the Lord has passed. The jaguarundi that drinks from the oil-slicked sump will lift its head, stare with something like reverence, and wait until the larger cat has taken its fill before returning to drink.

The Lords are older than the mechanical strata. They remember when the first pipelines were laid, and they watched the men who laid them die and sink into the mat. Some believe the jaguars actually *chose* the jungle’s incorporation of the metal and concrete—that they decided the new geology would be allowed to stay.

Deepest of all, in the places where even the drip singers fall silent, live the **Memory Trees**.

They are not trees.

They only look like them.

What appears to be colossal smooth-barked giants are actually single colonial organisms—vast networks of fungal mycelium and root systems wearing the shape of trees as a cathedral wears stone. Their “bark” is soft when you press it, like the hide of something enormous and asleep. Every few decades one of them exhales a cloud of spores that drifts through the basin like gray snow. Anything the spores touch begins to dream the tree’s dream. Animals fall into weeks-long sleep. Men (the few who have ever come this far) wake speaking languages that died before the pipelines were built. The dreams are never the same twice, but they are always about patience. About how everything that seems separate is only one slow digestion wearing different masks.

At the center of the basin—no one has ever found it twice—there is rumored to be a single Memory Tree that has grown completely around an abandoned drilling rig. The iron tower is now nothing but a hollow core inside living wood. Inside that hollow, it is said, the last human being who ever truly understood this place still sits, perfectly preserved, neither alive nor dead, dreaming the entire basin into continued existence.

The basin does not need you.

But sometimes, when the dripping slows to almost nothing and the Quiet Lords are watching from the shadows, it becomes clear that the basin is, in its patient and enormous way, *curious* about what you will choose to become while you are inside it.

Most things that enter are eventually absorbed.

A few are allowed to stay as new strata.

And once in a very long while, something is permitted to leave—carrying the green dusk behind its eyes forever afterward, so that even in the brightest sunlight it still feels like twilight, and every drop of water it hears sounds like it is falling from leaf to leaf to leaf, never quite reaching the ground.
