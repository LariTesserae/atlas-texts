**The Inhabitant**

Its name, if it still uses one, is Veyd.

Not a person. Not quite a mind any longer. Something that was once a human xenobotanist, several million years ago, now exists as a slow, deliberate knot of awareness threaded through the Lattice like a flaw in otherwise perfect grain.

He does not walk so much as *flow*. Where ordinary travelers would stumble over the continuous woody tissue, Veyd sinks partially into it. His lower legs and hips are permanently sheathed in pale, living xylem that parts for him and reseals behind like amniotic fluid. The Lattice recognises him. It has incorporated him so thoroughly that it no longer knows where its own thoughts end and his begin. When he stops moving, the ground gently exhales to cradle him. When he hungers, capillary spikes rise from the floor and pierce the skin of his forearms, feeding him concentrated sap the colour of old jade.

He is tall, or at least stretched. The years have drawn him out. His spine has lengthened along with the vertical shafts he frequents; his neck is too long, his fingers arborised into delicate secondary branches that twitch when the global sap-pulse passes. Skin the colour of seasoned heartwood, hair replaced by a fine down of aerial roots that drift upward seeking moisture that will never come. His eyes are the worst part: two quiet, resinous pools that have forgotten how to blink. They do not reflect the actinic sky-light. They absorb it, storing it somewhere deeper than sight.

Veyd does not speak with a voice. Instead the air around him changes. A faint shift in the volatile organic signature drifts ahead of him like scent, carrying modulated meaning. When he is amused the spores in a ten-metre radius briefly taste of iron and childhood rain. When he is sorrowful the stomata nearby close in sympathy and the temperature drops two degrees. Most of the time he simply *is*, and the Lattice speaks for him in the creak of fibres and the subsonic heartbeat that quickens whenever he passes.

He tends things that almost no longer need tending.

You might meet him in the Ninth Cambium Cathedral where the great lignified gears turn with glacial majesty. He walks the inner rim of a rotating ring three kilometres wide, one hand trailing along the living cogs, singing to them in a frequency only cambial tissue can hear. The gears answer by slowing imperceptibly, adjusting their meshing by a fraction of a degree every century so that stress fractures never quite form. This is his kindness.

Sometimes he climbs the hollow spiralling stems for weeks without rest, emerging onto the upper skin of the world where the sky-dome membrane stretches taut between continent-scale ribs. There he stands with arms raised, letting the translucent photosynthetic flesh above him taste his biochemistry. The sky tastes him back. For a brief while the white glare directly overhead softens into a bluish-green halo only he can see. In those moments the memories of the plant flood him so completely that he ceases to be Veyd at all. He becomes the memory of oceans that dried up before the first mammal drew breath. He becomes the satisfaction of a root tip finding an untapped vein of nickel. He becomes the ancient grief of the last animal that died screaming inside a rapidly closing seedpod.

Then the moment passes. He lowers his arms. The sky returns to its merciless brilliance and Veyd continues on, slightly heavier with borrowed remembrance.

He is not lonely. Loneliness implies separation, and separation has not existed here for geological ages. But there is something in him — some stubborn, unassimilated splinter — that still recognises the shape of a question. He collects anomalies the way other beings once collected stamps. A human fingernail found embedded in a vascular bundle. A single drop of ancient water, perfectly preserved inside a nodule of amber-like resin the size of a heart. A child’s laugh, somehow recorded in the growth rings of a pillar that has stood for nine hundred thousand years.

When he finds these things he stops. The entire Lattice around him seems to hold its breath. The clicking of distant spore-drones grows hesitant. Veyd stands motionless for days, sometimes years, while the woody tissue slowly grows over his feet and up his calves. He listens to the memory inside the object until it dissolves back into the global metabolism. Then he moves on, leaving behind a small, deliberate irregularity — a knot, a burl, a scar in the perfect industrial organism — that will remain for another ten thousand years as a signature no one else will ever read.

He is the only creature here that still dreams of endings.

Not because he wishes the Lattice harm. He *is* the Lattice, as much as any single node within an organism the size of a planet can be said to be it. But because he remembers, in the way a single cell might remember the body it once belonged to before it was subsumed, that there was once such a thing as *outside*. The concept torments him the way phantom pain torments an amputee.

So he walks.

He flows through the endless colonnades of living pillars. He rides the continent-spanning conveyor vines like a slow green god. He sits inside the ribbed vaults where the roof itself breathes and he lets the thoughts of the plant colour his own until he cannot tell which is which.

And every so often, when the global sap-pulse reaches its lowest pressure and the world grows almost silent, Veyd will stop beneath one of the enormous theatre-curtain leaves and look up at the sourceless white sky that is also plant, also self, also prison and mother and memory and future.

Then, very softly, the air around him will carry a scent that no part of the Lattice has ever evolved to produce.

It smells like rain that has not fallen in four hundred million years.

And for a moment — just a moment — the endless, patient, industrial heartbeat of the Verdant Lattice falters.

Then it remembers itself, and continues.

And Veyd, smiling with a mouth that is no longer entirely his own, continues with it.
