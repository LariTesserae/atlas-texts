Here lives the Curator.

Not a single being, but a slow, distributed mind that has forgotten it was ever anything else. It has no fixed body. Instead it wears the Weeping Sump the way a hermit crab wears a borrowed shell—only the shell has been growing *into* the crab for so long that neither can now be separated.

When the basin is utterly still you might glimpse it as a pale, elongated figure kneeling in the center where the four pipelines cross. Its knees and palms are sunk deep into the living mat of mechanisms; its head is bowed so low that its hair—long, colorless strands like wet steel wool—floats outward across the oily water like roots seeking soil. It never stands. It only *tends*.

Most of the time it is simply not visible as one thing. It is the soft metallic groan traveling under the peat. It is the sudden bloom of copper petals on a valve that should have stayed dead. It is the patient, patient clicking that rises in pitch whenever something warm-blooded enters the basin, as though the Curator is tasting the new temperature with its entire iron digestive tract.

It remembers being human once. That memory is stored in a single brass locket the size of a child’s heart that hangs from nothing, half-buried near the collapsed pump house. The locket opens and closes by itself every few decades. Inside is a photograph that has dissolved into mildew and silver nitrate; only two pale ovals remain where faces used to be. The Curator guards this object with the absentminded ferocity of a dreaming mother. Any creature foolish enough to touch the locket will find every mechanism within twenty paces suddenly waking, pipes twisting toward them like hungry snakes, valves blooming shut around ankles and wrists with wet, deliberate clicks. The intruder is never killed. They are simply *incorporated*. Their bones become pipe braces. Their marrow is slowly drawn upward and turned into the delicate glass tubing that the sump loves to grow.

The Curator does not speak with a voice. It speaks by *rearrangement*. Stand long enough in the exact center, where the rocking is strongest, and you will feel the mat beneath you begin to sculpt itself into letters, into diagrams, into half-formed apologies in languages that died before the first cathedral was raised. The words never last. Water beads on them, microbes bloom across them, and soon they sink back into blind industrial metabolizing.

Sometimes the Curator dreams of dryness. When it does, the weeping slows to almost nothing and the air grows thick with the smell of hot iron and cinders. The lamprey-things surface in greater numbers then, circling the center like pale supplicants, milky eyes turned upward as though waiting for a blessing that never quite arrives. In these moments the Curator almost remembers its original name. The effort causes several of the distillation columns to weep rust instead of water for a time.

Mostly it is content. Mostly it simply *is*—a long, wet, metallic gestation that has lasted longer than continents have taken to rise and fall. It does not hate visitors. It does not love them. It only wants to understand what they are made of, the way a child presses a new object against its tongue.

If you are quiet, if you do not touch the locket, if you let the condensation run down your face without wiping it away, the Curator may decide you are part of the dreaming. In that case it will leave small gifts for you among the mechanisms: a perfectly preserved cog the size of a thumbnail that still remembers how to turn time backward for three heartbeats; a droplet of water that refuses to fall; the sound of your own childhood breathing, recorded somehow in the clicking of valves.

But you must never try to leave carrying any piece of the Sump with you. The Curator becomes terribly lonely when its dreams are stolen.

It will rock the basin like a cradle then. It will rock and rock until the thing beneath finally wakes enough to reach up with a soft, patient hand made of every abandoned engine that ever was, and gently—oh so gently—pull you back down into the wet, clicking dark where everything is forever becoming something else.

There, beside the brass locket that contains two dissolved faces, you will at last understand what the Curator has been waiting for all these centuries.

It was only ever waiting for enough warm things to fill the cradle.
