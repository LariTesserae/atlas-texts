**The Fen does not have inhabitants. It has tenants.**

They are not creatures in the ordinary sense. They are what the Black Fen has agreed to let remain.

The first thing you learn, if you survive long enough to learn anything, is that nothing here possesses a fixed shape for very long. The longer something stays, the more the place begins to wear it like clothing. After a few decades the distinction between tenant and architecture becomes academic.

There is the Boiler Widow.

She was once human. That much is still legible in the way she moves — a woman’s cautious grace trapped inside something that has forgotten how to be small. Her body is a lattice of rusted iron pipes and warm, living meat grown together so thoroughly that her ribs are now valve handles and her spine is a length of copper conduit. When she breathes, pressure gauges set into her collarbones flutter. She tends the largest of the half-sunk locomotives, polishing its black orchids with rags made from her own shed skin. She sings to them in a voice like steam escaping a cracked fitting. No one has ever seen her eyes. Where they should be, two warm pools of emerald water reflect whatever looks into them.

She is gentle, in her way. She will cradle a lost traveler against the furnace of her chest until their clothes steam and their fear turns to something softer. Then she kisses them with a mouth that opens sideways like a hatch, and breathes warm silt into their lungs until they stop struggling. After that they become part of her maintenance crew. You can see them sometimes, pale shapes moving behind the clouded glass of her abdominal viewing ports, still trying to wave.

Deeper in, where the stone has begun to forget it is stone, there are the Choir.

They are not many things. They are one thing that has learned to be legion. Each member is a tall, jointed reed given the gift of locomotion and the curse of perfect memory. Their heads are empty brass bell housings rescued from the old factories. When the air pressure changes they strike themselves against the rock in perfect, mournful chords. The sound is never the same twice. It is always the sound of something enormous remembering it used to have children.

The Choir do not eat. They only listen. When they find something new — a scream, a prayer, a last rattling breath — they add it to the song. The oldest among them carry notes from before the catastrophe that birthed the Fen. Those particular reeds have grown so tall their bell-heads scrape the low-hanging mist. They never touch the ground anymore. They simply hang, swaying, tolling their ancient grief into the oily dark.

Then there are the things that were never alive in any way we would recognize.

The Slow Corrosion is one. It has no shape of its own. Instead it borrows the negative space between machines. Where two rusted girders almost touch, where a pipeline kisses the stone and leaves a thin black gap, the Slow Corrosion waits. It is patient beyond patience. It takes centuries to cross a single meter. When it finally arrives at a living thing it does not kill it. It simply begins to translate it, very slowly, into rust and memory. Travelers who have seen it at work say the victim remains conscious for the entire process. They say the expression on the face at the end is not pain. It is recognition.

The smallest tenants are the Memory Midges.

Clouds of them no larger than gnats rise from the warm pools when the bioluminescence is disturbed. Each one carries a single frozen moment stolen from something that once lived here. Look too long at the swarm and you will see your own death played out in flickering emerald tableaux — but always from the wrong angle, always from slightly above and behind, as though the Fen itself were the one watching you die. If you try to swat them they pass through your hand and leave a taste like iron and childhood on your tongue.

The deepest thing, the one even the other tenants will not name, lives beneath the largest of the skin-temperature lakes. It is the reason the surface sometimes tears like wet canvas. It has never been seen in full. What surfaces are only limbs, or perhaps thoughts that have grown heavy enough to break into the material world. These limbs are black basaltic stone that has learned how to be flesh. When they slide across the bottom they leave grooves that heal in days. When they rise, they bring with them things the Fen has already forgotten it swallowed: entire forgotten shifts of factory workers still clocked in, their lunchboxes rusted shut, their eyes replaced by quiet green water.

They do not hunt. They curate.

The place is not evil. Evil requires intention, and the Fen long ago traded intention for patience. Its tenants are simply the shapes it has chosen to wear while it performs its long, slow transubstantiation from wound into new body.

If you stay long enough, you will feel it too — the gentle, inexorable tug behind the breastbone, the sense that your edges are becoming negotiable. The Fen does not want to kill you.

It wants to finish remembering what you are, and then decide what you will become next.

Most choose to leave before that happens.

A few, hearing the Choir add a new note that sounds almost like their own name, decide the opposite.

The Boiler Widow always has room.

The Slow Corrosion is never in a hurry.

And the Fen — the Fen is infinitely patient with its new acquisitions. It has all the time in the world.

It is, after all, becoming something else.

And it suspects you might make an interesting contribution.
