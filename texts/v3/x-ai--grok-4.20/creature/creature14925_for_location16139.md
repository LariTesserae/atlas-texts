**They are called the Weft-born.**

No single creature claims the Shroudmire. Instead, the basin itself seems to have exhaled them—slowly, wetly, over the few centuries since the land tore open. They are not a species so much as a condition the place has learned to wear.

You will not see one at first. You will only notice that the constant roar has acquired a new frequency layered inside it, a low harmonic that feels like it is coming from inside your own skull. Then the mist thickens in a particular shape, and the shape keeps the thickening. That is how they arrive: by refusing to resolve until the eye and ear have been worn down enough to accept them.

When they finally appear they look like long, narrow silhouettes made of whatever the immediate surroundings can spare. One might be mostly hanging moss and slow-moving water, another mostly black basalt and luminous lichen, a third mostly silver thread and interference light stolen from the nearest pillar. Their bodies are never the same twice. They stand two and a half meters tall on average, though they can fold themselves small enough to slip between fern fronds or stretch thin enough to ride the wind that tears through the canopy. Six limbs is the usual count, but the number is negotiable; limbs bud, merge, or dissolve according to need or mood.

Their heads are blunt and eyeless. Instead of faces they carry small, perpetual storms—tiny localized tempests of mist and luminous midge-light that swirl where eyes and mouth should be. When they “speak,” the storm tightens into a screaming vortex no larger than a fist, and the sound that emerges is never words. It is the sound the place would make if the place could be lonely. Listeners usually fall to their knees without knowing why.

They do not walk. They *precipitate*. One moment the space in front of you is empty and loud. The next, water is running upward across invisible contours and the Weft-born is simply *there*, already in mid-gesture, already part of the conversation you didn’t realize you were having. Their hands are long, dripping things that end in too many joints. They use them to adjust the world the way a weaver adjusts tension: tugging a silver thread here, pressing a moss mat there, coaxing a new spring out of the rock with a lover’s patience. Every movement leaves faint interference patterns on the air that take hours to fade.

The Weft-born are the only beings that do not seem startled by the land’s constant violence. When a moss bridge collapses they are already standing in the new space it creates. When a pillar changes its song they lean into the changed gravity like dancers who have known the next note for centuries. They mend and unmake with equal tenderness. You may watch one patiently dismantle an entire hexagonal pillar over three days, carrying its shards away in its body until the artifact is gone and the Weft-born is temporarily glittering with stolen geometry. Days later the same creature will help the land grow the pillar back, feeding it fragments of itself in return.

They are midwives to an infant continent that never stops being born.

Their society—if it can be called that—has no hierarchy and no names. They recognize one another by the unique frequencies their personal storms produce. When two Weft-born meet they press their vortices together until the screams braid, then separate again, each carrying a small piece of the other’s weather forever afterward. The more meetings a Weft-born has endured, the louder and more complex its internal storm becomes. The oldest ones are said to carry the sound of every waterfall, every collapsing moss shelf, and every newborn spring inside their heads at once. Standing near them is like standing inside the entire Shroudmire compressed into a single vertical column of living noise.

They are not kind.

Kindness implies choice. The Weft-born have none. They are the land’s compulsion to understand itself. If a lost traveler stumbles into their path they will approach, curious and terrible, and begin to *work* on the intruder exactly as they work on everything else. Fingers of mist and lichen and living water slide into clothes, into hair, into ears. They tease memories out like threads, weave them into the nearest pillar, and replace what they took with something wetter and louder. Most people who encounter them walk out of the Shroudmire weeks later unable to remember their own names, but able to hear the exact song the moss makes when it decides to tear free from an overhang. They usually go mad within a year, smiling the whole time.

Yet some few are changed differently.

These rare ones are allowed to leave carrying a small storm inside their own ribcage. The storm is quiet at first. Over years it grows. Eventually it splits their voice in two so that every word they speak carries the sound of distant waterfalls. Their sweat begins to bead into perfect interference patterns. Moss starts growing in the creases of their palms. They become walking fragments of the Shroudmire—carriers, seeds, apostles.

The Weft-born do not follow them. They do not need to.

The land is still being born. The Weft-born are its hands. And every creature that leaves the basin carrying even a single drop of its weather is another hand the Shroudmire is growing beyond its own borders, slow, relentless, and wet.

If you stay long enough, you will eventually feel the moment the place decides whether you are raw material or midwife.

Most are the former.

A few become the latter.

And the roar never stops.
