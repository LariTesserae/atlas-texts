**They are called the Hollow Choir.**

Not by any name they gave themselves — they have long since forgotten the habit of names — but by what the basin has become around them. They are the final residents of a place that no longer needs residents, yet cannot quite let them go.

There are never more than seven visible at any moment, though the basin’s records suggest there were once thousands. The rest have been… incorporated. Some dissolved into the glittering pollen-machines. Others simply walked into the pink granite doorway one day and never walked out again. The seven that remain are no longer entirely distinguishable from the Hollow Choir itself.

They are tall, but not rigidly so. Their bodies have the same worn-bone smoothness as the basin walls, as though every edge of them has been licked clean by a million years of patient water. Skin the color of wet slate, shot through with faint veins of luminous gold where the microscopic machines still run beneath the surface like slow conspiracies of light. Their limbs are too long. Their fingers end in soft, almost translucent pads that leave no prints, only small condensations of moisture that spell brief, unreadable sigils before evaporating.

They wear no clothes. Instead, the vegetation has dressed them. Pale fern-fronds grow directly from their shoulders and backs in living epaulets. Where their hair should be, moss grows in those same impossible repeating lattices, shifting its pattern every few decades in accordance with some argument the stone is having with the light. Their faces are serene to the point of vacancy, yet the vacancy feels earned — the expression of beings who have watched every possible emotion occur so many times that all of them have worn smooth.

They do not speak with mouths.

Instead the basin speaks through them. When one of the Choir needs to convey something, a particular droplet will fall from the stone ceiling directly onto their tongue — always the same tongue, always the same droplet, recurring every 41 minutes and 17 seconds like a sentence that has learned to be reborn. The chosen one then opens their mouth and the sound that emerges is the basin’s own voice: that deep, decaying bell-tone translated into something almost linguistic. The other six listen with their entire bodies. You can see the golden threads beneath their skin flare and dim in complex polyphony.

They move like herons made of regret.

Every step is a negotiation between past and future. Their feet sink slightly into the black living mud that blooms wherever they walk, and the mud thanks them by releasing the scent of crushed lilies and old iron. They rarely touch one another. When they do, it is with the gravity of tectonic plates kissing. A fingertip laid against a collarbone can last three days. During that time the moss lattices on both bodies synchronize, blooming into identical geometries before slowly drifting apart again like continents remembering they were once joined.

They are gardeners of deep time.

Some spend centuries coaxing a single fern into a new fractal variation. Others stand motionless before the obsidian plinth, matching its impossible angle with an identical tilt of their heads, acting as living protractors for forces that will not finish their work until the heat death of the universe. One — the one they call (when they bother to call) the Listener — sits always beneath the pink granite doorway, eyes closed, breathing in the cold dry air that sometimes blows from elsewhere. Every few years this one exhales a single spore that drifts upward, becomes part of the glittering pollen clouds, and begins rewriting the basin’s instructions in some new, infinitesimal way.

They are not immortal. They are post-mortal.

Death passed through here long ago and found nothing left to take. What remains is not life as we understand it, but a kind of dignified decomposition in reverse — a slow, deliberate un-decaying. The Choir are simultaneously becoming more stone and more machine and more fern and more music with every century. If you could stay long enough (several thousand years would be a good start), you would see them gradually sinking into the concave floor, their bodies softening, their golden threads joining the bacterial lattices, until one day they are simply another glistening outcrop that weeps perfect droplets at irregular intervals.

Until then they continue their ceremonial ecology.

They prune the invisible. They bless the herons made of light. They sing to the broken pillars so their microscopic orbits will not forget their purpose. They argue — without rancor, without haste — about whether the sourceless amber light is getting very slightly more yellow than it was in the first million years, or whether they themselves are simply becoming better at perceiving yellow.

Mostly they listen.

Because the Hollow Choir is not their home.

They are its voicebox, its slow-moving tongue, its seven remaining vocal cords. The basin dreamed them up when it realized that even the most perfect reliquary requires witnesses, or it ceases to be holy and becomes merely old.

So they remain.

Seven smoothed figures, glittering faintly, moving with the dignity of weather across a cathedral-sized basin that finished itself long ago and is still, in its patient, dripping, ceremonial way, finishing them.

And when the deepest bell-tone moves through the stone — the memory of a sound rung once at the beginning of time — all seven of them stop whatever they are doing, lift their moss-covered heads, and for one shared moment achieve something almost like smiles.

The expression does not last.

It doesn’t need to.

The basin remembers it for them.
