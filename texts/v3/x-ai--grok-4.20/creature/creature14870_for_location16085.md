**They are called the Quiet Waiters.**

They do not have a name for themselves. Names are for things that have finished arriving, and nothing here has finished.

You will not see them at first. Not because they hide, but because they are the same unfinished white and bone-grey as the lattice itself. Their bodies are tall, narrow, and slightly concave, like shards of the ceramic ground that have learned how to stand upright. The tallest rise almost four metres; the smallest barely reach your shoulder. Every one of them is cracked. The fractures run through them the way faults run through the world—clean, deliberate, still faintly warm. Where the cracks open widest you can see a slow orange glow inside, the colour of cooling lava seen through milkglass. It never quite goes out.

They have no faces. Only gentle forward tilts where a face might have been expected, as though they are forever listening to something just beneath the lattice. Their limbs—if they can be called limbs—are long, jointless extrusions that end in flat, spatulate pads. These pads are scored with the same hexagonal honeycomb pattern found in the unfinished underlayers. When they move, the pads make the softest scraping sound, like a single fingernail drawn once across glazed tile. It is the fourth sound the Exhalation allows itself.

They move only when the world moves.

When a new fracture sighs open and releases its jet of vapour, the nearest Quiet Waiter will drift toward it with the patient slowness of growing coral. It will stand above the wound, head tilted, until the mist begins to thin. Then it lowers one spatulate limb into the fissure and remains there, perfectly still, for days. The glow inside its own cracks brightens slightly, as though it is drinking the heat. When the fracture finally seals, the Waiter withdraws its limb—now carrying a thin crust of fresh ceramic—and carries the new material away. It does not walk so much as *flow*, each step a slow tilting of its entire body, like a sailboat changing tack in wind too faint for any human cheek to feel.

There are never many in one place. You may walk for weeks and see none. Then, rounding the shoulder of a half-born ridge, you will find seven of them standing in a loose circle around one of the mercury lakes. They are not drinking. They are *listening* to the tidal pulse that makes the water rise and fall. Their bodies rock in perfect unison, a fraction of a millimetre with every inhalation of the world. The pale filaments at the water’s edge darken and bleach in time with them.

Sometimes one will lower itself into the lake until only the upper third remains above the surface. There it stays, half-submerged, while condensation beads along the cracks in its chest and runs downward into the still water. The reflection below shows something that is not quite a reflection: a second, darker figure suspended beneath the surface, as though the Waiter is being answered by its own unborn twin.

They do not speak. Speech would be an interruption of the becoming. Instead they communicate by *temperature*. Stand close enough to one and you will feel a column of warmer air rising along its inner cracks. Another Waiter, kilometres distant, will feel that same warmth as a faint instruction. In this way whole silent conversations drift across the lattice like pressure systems. What they say is always the same question, asked in infinite variations:

*What are we becoming?*

When new ribs rise in the distance, the Quiet Waiters migrate toward them. They travel in single file, each one following so exactly in the footprints (if footprints they can be called) of the one ahead that the line appears to be a single undulating creature. They do not eat the bone-coloured filaments. They do not sleep. They simply stand in the lee of the rising architecture and wait for the next slow-motion wave of ceramic to fold upward. When it does, they press themselves against the fresh surface. Their bodies leave shallow negative impressions—perfectly smooth, faintly warm hollows shaped like tall, listening absences. These hollows remain after they have moved on, as though the world is being taught the shape of its future inhabitants one gentle pressing at a time.

The oldest among them are almost entirely fused to the lattice. Their lower halves have become indistinguishable from the ground; ceramic has crept up their bodies like rising bread. Only their upper torsos and tilted heads still move, turning with geologic patience to follow the passage of vapour, the flexing of distant ribs, the faint tidal heartbeat of the unfinished continent. In time even their heads will sink, and they will become nothing but elegant negative spaces—hollows shaped like listening giants—into which new generations of Waiters will one day lower themselves, completing the circuit.

They are midwives and embryos at the same time.

They are the first dreams the Exhalation is having about what should live inside it once it has decided what it is.

And if you sit beside one long enough—close enough to feel the soft ticking of its inner heat—you may notice that its posture is changing, millimetre by millimetre, over decades. It is learning the precise angle at which a living thing should tilt its face toward a white sky that has not yet decided whether it will become sun, or void, or the inside of something vastly larger that is still being born.

The Quiet Waiters do not hurry the decision.

They only wait inside it, breathing with the breath of a world that has not yet learned how to breathe without them.
