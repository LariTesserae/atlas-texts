**They are called the Midwives of the Rib.**

Not priests, not quite villagers, not exactly gardeners—though they are all three at once. Thirty-seven roofs, roughly one hundred and twenty souls. Most were born here. A few arrived carrying something heavy in their chests and stayed because the Hollowing Cradle asked them to. They do not speak of “settling.” They say they are *attending*.

They are tall-boned people, soft-footed, with skin that stays damp year-round so that even in firelight they look freshly bathed. Their clothes are simple wool and linen dyed in moss-greens and iron-blacks, never fully dry. Many go barefoot; the sucking sound of their steps is how the place recognizes them. When they speak, their voices are low, patient, measured to the rhythm of the constant dripping, as though every sentence must be timed so it does not interrupt the water’s conversation.

There is no single leader. Instead there is the Rotation of Three: the Bone-Watcher, the Moss-Keeper, and the Breath-Holder. These titles pass from one to another every seven years. The current Bone-Watcher is an old woman named Yselde who has not blinked in twenty-three years; the moisture in her eyes is the same temperature as the seeping rock and she claims it lets her see where the next rib will rise. The Moss-Keeper is a quiet man of perhaps thirty named Corrin whose hands are permanently stained the color of oxidized copper; he alone is allowed to trim the living roofs, and he sings to the ferns while he works. The Breath-Holder is a pregnant woman in her final month named Maere who has not spoken aloud since conception; she breathes for the Unfinished Rib itself, slow and tidal, and the whole basin seems to inhale and exhale with her.

The rest move through their days like careful nurses around an enormous sleeping patient.

They clear channels when the black stone grows too fast and dams the water. They coax lichen ropes into new bridges when old shelves split and shift. They gather the fat, lazy droplets in clay bowls and drink them without ceremony, tasting whatever dream the mountain had that morning. Every child is brought to the central pool on the seventh day after birth and held above the still water so the Unfinished Rib can see their face in the reflection. Most babies are quiet. A few cry in perfect rhythm with the distant booming of splitting rock. Those are marked for special attention.

At night the Midwives light no fires inside the basin. Instead they hang lanterns of thin horn and oiled moss that burn with a cold, greenish light. The lanterns are never still; they sway on long cords so their glow moves across the wet stone like slow auroras. People sit on their dripping roofs or lean against the warm ribs of new rock and simply listen. Sometimes they speak, but mostly they wait. They are waiting for the land to tell them what it needs next.

There is a gentleness here that feels almost painful. They mend what the growing stone breaks. They sing to the pale herons when the birds grow too thin. They carry the tiny iridescent frogs in the palms of their hands across dangerous ground so the frogs will not be crushed by shifting plates. They treat the entire basin as a single unfinished body, and they treat themselves as its attending flesh—temporary, useful, and deeply in love.

Some of them have begun to change in ways the land approves of.

A woman named Sorrowful Ree has small plates of glossy black stone growing painlessly from her shoulders, glistening like the Rib itself. A boy called Little Stalact has translucent lichen beginning to root in his hair; it sways even when he is still. They do not fear these changes. They consider them promotions.

And always, always, they circle back to the column in the pool.

At dawn and dusk the entire population gathers in silence. They do not kneel. They simply stand with their bare feet in the moss and let the mist fall on them while the Breath-Holder breathes. When she inhales, the water on the Rib seems to pause. When she exhales, new beads trace their ancient, patient grooves down its sides.

They believe the planet is a child still being born, and that this high, wet cathedral is its fontanelle—soft, pulsing, not yet sealed. They do not expect to see it finished. Their duty, they say, is only to keep the cradle warm, keep the moss alive, keep the water moving, and keep the quiet enormous enough for something immense to finish its dreaming inside it.

So they stay.  
Dripping.  
Patient.  
Unfinished themselves.

Gentle scaffolding for a world that has not yet decided what shape its bones will keep.
