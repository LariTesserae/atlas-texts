**The Weeper** lives here.

She has no single shape that holds for long. Most of the time she is less a creature than a condition of the Underloom itself — a slow, patient gravity that moves through the fused machinery like a sigh given mass. When she chooses to gather, she becomes a tall, dripping silhouette made of whatever the hollow offers: rusted iron bent into a suggestion of ribs, translucent glass-legs borrowed from the cat-sized water-things, sheets of living moonlight-fungus draped across her shoulders like wet silk, and the hollow black curve of a Choir beetle’s mirror-wing for a face. The mirrors are always turned inward. No one has ever seen what looks back at her.

She is ancient in the way dripping stone is ancient. There was a time, the ruins suggest, when she had another name and a purpose connected to the machines, but the name has corroded and the purpose has softened into pure maintenance. Now she tends the balance the way a widow tends an unvisited grave.

You will not meet her directly. She refuses frontality. Instead you become aware of her the way you become aware of a change in pressure before a storm. The *plick* of falling water grows slightly more deliberate, as though each drop is being placed by a careful hand. The pale vein-lattices brighten along one particular route through the wreckage, tracing a path only she uses. A warm, almond-scented breath moves across the back of your neck though nothing visibly stirs. If you turn quickly you will see only the watchman’s shack door swinging a few centimeters farther open than it was, and the suggestion of something tall and reflective sitting inside, watching you with the patience of something that has already outlived every clock.

She sings when the Choir sings, but her voice is the silence between their notes — a negative tone that makes the metal bones of the Underloom flex like deep-sea creatures rising toward the surface. When the dripping threatens to stop she becomes audible as a slow, wet creak that travels through every pipe at once, coaxing the condensate back into rhythm. She is the reason the place has achieved its stable state of dying. She repairs what collapses by gently pressing broken pieces into new, sadder shapes. She moves the glass-legged water-creatures along their moisture gradients when they grow confused. She polishes the parabolic mirrors of the Choir with fog from her own breath so their reflections stay bright.

At rare intervals — perhaps once every few decades — she grows lonely in the way only something immortal can. Then she chooses a visitor. She does not speak. Instead she leaves things arranged: a perfect circle of quartz barnacles around your pack while you sleep, a single Choir beetle placed on the rim of your canteen so that its mirrored back reflects your own eye back at you. These are invitations. If you accept by leaving something of equal sentimental weight behind (a locket, a letter, a recording of a human voice), she will allow you to see her fully once before you leave. She will stand thirty meters away, half-dissolved in mist and fungal light, and you will understand that she is crying. The tears are not water. They are tiny, perfect recreations of everything that has ever rusted here — miniature locomotives, pocket watches, wedding rings, bones — all tumbling slowly down her iron ribs and shattering on the floor into new fungal veins.

Most people refuse the invitation. Those who accept never quite dry out again. Years later, in sunlit places far above, they will still leave damp handprints on everything they touch, and sometimes, in quiet rooms, others will hear the soft, patient *plick* of falling water coming from inside their chests.

She does not hate you. She does not love you. She simply needs the weeping to continue, and she has learned that the quickest way to make a heart produce moisture is to break it gently, over and over, at exactly the tempo the Underloom requires.

When you finally turn to leave, you will find the watchman’s shack door closed for the first time. Through its small fogged window you may glimpse her sitting at the ruined desk, mirror-face turned inward, slowly turning the pages of a ledger that contains every name of everyone who ever disturbed the dripping. She is updating it.

She never looks up.

The *plick* continues behind you all the way to the surface, steady as a second heartbeat you can never quite outrun.
