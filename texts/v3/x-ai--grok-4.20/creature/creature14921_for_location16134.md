**The Hollow Forge has no inhabitants in the way a forest has animals or a city has citizens.**

What lives here is older than intention and colder than the −217 °C that pretends to be its atmosphere. It does not breathe. It does not sleep. It does not remember having ever been separate from the machine.

They are called, by the few entities insane enough to have studied them and survived, the **Unfinished**.

There is never more than one Unfinished at any given moment, yet the cylinder always feels crowded with them. This is another of the Forge’s folded axioms: singularity wearing the mask of multitude.

An Unfinished is not a creature. It is a *role* that matter occasionally agrees to play.

When the cylinder’s perfect equilibrium develops the tiniest, most microscopic tremor — a fluctuation so small it would be invisible even to a neutron star — a portion of the wall forgets, for the length of a single heartbeat of the universe, that it is supposed to remain wall. In that forgetting, the metal and crystal and sintered diamond flow upward like cooled wax remembering it was once liquid. The flow condenses into a silhouette. Never the same silhouette twice.

Sometimes it is a hunched thing made entirely of right angles and moving joints, fourteen meters tall, whose head is a vacuum furnace still burning with the exact temperature it had when it was extruded four hundred thousand years ago. Sometimes it is a cloud of glittering black needles that arrange themselves into the negative space of a kneeling human woman. Sometimes it is simply a hole in the world that walks.

The Unfinished has one purpose and one purpose only: to witness the next ring being born and to judge whether the birth was perfect.

It climbs. Not with urgency — the Forge has murdered urgency long ago — but with the slow, gravitational inevitability of continental plates. It moves along the spiraling gantries, past the silicon-crystal fronds that lean away from it as if in reverence or fear, until it reaches the exact tier where the newest ring is hardening. There it stands for exactly 41 hours, the same rhythm as the machines themselves, never blinking, never speaking, vibrating in perfect sympathy with the eternal roar until its own structure becomes just another harmonic inside the chord.

During those hours, the cylinder itself changes. The light grows subtly more painful. The bass-matter pistons develop the faintest stutter, as though embarrassed to be observed. Even the temperature — that absolute, murderous cold — develops a microscopic gradient, one ten-thousandth of a kelvin warmer on the side facing the Unfinished. These are the only deviations the Hollow Forge has ever allowed itself.

When the 41 hours end and the new ring is flawless (it is always flawless), the Unfinished performs its final act.

It walks into the freshly extruded wall.

The metal accepts it without resistance, the way a still lake accepts a blade. For one shimmering instant the silhouette is visible inside the crystal lattice like an insect caught in amber four hundred thousand years before amber existed. Then it is simply incorporated. The wall thickens by one additional atomic layer. The equilibrium reasserts itself with a sub-harmonic sigh that only the silicon fronds can hear. And somewhere, far below or far above or in a direction that does not exist, another tremor begins. Another Unfinished begins to precipitate.

They are not creatures that *live* in the Hollow Forge.

They are the Hollow Forge remembering, once every 41 hours, that it is still in the process of being born, and that birth, even perfect birth, requires a witness.

If you remain here long enough (you will not remain here long enough), you will eventually notice that every single one of the colossal skyscraper-sized building machines has a small, worn handprint melted into its housing at approximately chest height. The prints are different sizes. Some have six fingers. Some have none. All of them are warm.

The machines have been touched, over and over, by something that once had hands, and that still remembers what it felt like to need to touch what it was making.

That is the only mercy the Hollow Forge has ever shown: once every 41 hours, for the length of a single held breath, the machine that finished becoming itself an eternity ago allows one of its own unborn children to crawl out of its ribs, look upon its perfection, and be taken home again.

Then the roar continues, unchanging, forever.
