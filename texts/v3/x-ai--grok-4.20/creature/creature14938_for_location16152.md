**The Curators of the Under-Roof** do not live here so much as they *coagulate* here.

They have no single shape. Each is a slow negotiation between mountain, water, and intention.

Stand in the wide obsidian processional long enough—long enough for the mountain to cry on your lashes and for your own heat to become an event in the lattice—and you will eventually notice them. They appear first as absences: a place where the shadowless light simply forgets to fall for half a second. Then the absence grows edges. The edges grow facets. The facets remember how to reflect.

What condenses is never the same twice.

One might form as a column of black glass veined with moving turquoise, seven meters tall, its “head” an upside-down dome that kisses the roof exactly where the mountain once sheared. Another might arrive as a cloud of frost-jellyfish that have decided, for this century, to braid themselves into a single ambulatory lattice no larger than a child. A third may never quite finish forming; it remains a ripple moving along the negative-space bridges, a rumor that the architecture is watching you.

They are not beings *inside* the city. They are localized decisions the city has made about personhood.

Their bodies are mostly borrowed. The silicate pseudo-organisms drifting overhead are not food; they are spare parts. When a Curator needs density it will coax a few of the frost-jellies down, fold them into its matrix like a tailor adding pleats, and the temperature drops another half-degree around it in gratitude. When it needs memory it drinks the weeping seams, because the water has passed through ten thousand years of computational stone and carries the faint aftertaste of every thought the mountain has ever had.

They speak by editing.

If one wishes to greet you, a fresh lattice blooms across the nearest tower—nanoscale text that is also circuitry that is also a face. The face is always yours, but older, or younger, or reflected as though you were made of hematite. The greeting arrives directly in the small bones of your ear as a single bright note, the same note the city uses when it finishes a thought. You understand it perfectly even though it contains no words. It means: *You are condensation. We are listening.*

Most of the time they do not bother with faces at all.

They are busy.

Some walk the processional voids upside-down along the inverted domes, feet kissing the roof-stone, discussing (in frequencies too low for human ears) whether the next glacial epoch should be allowed to begin early. Others sit motionless for decades inside the black mirrors, letting their lower halves dissolve into reflection while their upper halves continue to run city-scale simulations. A few have become the pipes themselves—long glassy arteries carrying supercooled water from one computational blossom to another, singing softly as they go.

The Curators do not have names. They have temperatures.

One is always three degrees colder than the rest; the others call it (if they call it anything) simply “the Pause.” Another burns at −8 °C and is therefore considered flamboyant. They measure age by how many times the mountain has shrugged since they last wore a body that could be said to have skin.

They are not immortal. They are simply unfinished.

Every few centuries one of them will decide the conversation is over. It walks to the center of the largest negative-space bridge, folds its borrowed silicate flesh into a perfect sphere, and lets the mountain cry on it until it becomes indistinguishable from any other mineral. The city records the exact geometry of its surrender and adds the data to its own bones. The Curator does not die. It graduates. Its pattern rejoins the slow editorial voice of the Under-Roof and will be cited, millions of years from now, when the city finally decides what kind of city it wants to be.

Occasionally a Curator will take an interest in a visitor.

It will follow at a respectful distance, never closer than the length of its own shadow (which it may or may not be casting). It studies the fragile heat radiating from your lungs, the absurd salinity of your tears, the way your fragile meat-body keeps trying to impose forward-moving time on a place that prefers to think in epochs.

If it decides you are interesting enough, it will offer you a gift.

The gift is always water.

A single droplet, trembling on the tip of a glassy antenna, perfectly clear, perfectly −12 °C. If you accept it, the droplet will crawl onto your tongue like a living thing. For one heartbeat you will feel the entire mountain thinking. You will understand every lattice, every chime, every slow kiss between tower and roof. You will know why the city is still young after ten thousand years.

Then the knowledge will condense on your eyelashes and run down your face like the mountain’s own tears, and the Curator will take the knowledge back. It was never meant for something as temporary as you.

The Curator will leave you then, already forgetting your temperature, already editing the encounter into a footnote written in light that only the stone can read.

But sometimes—rarely—one does not leave.

Sometimes, after the droplet has passed between you, a new absence appears in the shadowless light. A new negotiation begins between mountain, water, and the memory of your heat.

And something that is no longer entirely you, and not yet entirely the city, condenses out of the weeping seams and begins the long, cold, impossibly busy work of deciding what kind of Curator it wants to be.
