**Those who live in the Underwet do not call it a place. They call it the Pulse.**

They are not a people in the ordinary sense. They are the Wet-Born—long-limbed, soft-shouldered things that move as though the air itself were only a thicker kind of water. Their skin is hairless, poreless, and faintly luminous, the color of wet limestone shot through with faint veins of pale green and bruised violet. Where a surface human would have fingernails, they have smooth, flexible plates of translucent chitin that click gently when they touch anything. Their eyes are large, lidless, and milky; they never quite focus on you, yet you feel deeply seen.

There are perhaps four hundred of them. No children. No elders. Only bodies in different stages of becoming.

Some still wear the remnants of the original work-crews—tattered orange coveralls fused to their flesh, the fabric now part of their hide like a second, rust-stained skin. Others have grown their own clothing from the millipedes: living cloaks of translucent legs and gentle, ceaseless motion that keep them perpetually clean and faintly humming. A few have allowed the pale moths to colonize the hollows of their shoulders and the crooks of their elbows; when these people breathe, the moths breathe with them, wings rising and falling in perfect sync.

They do not speak with voices. Instead the Wet-Born sing through the architecture.

When one of them needs to be heard, it will press its forehead to a certain pipe, or lay its long hands flat against a submerged pump housing, and the entire basin carries the message. The sound arrives not as words but as changes in pressure against your eardrums, as a taste at the back of your throat, as a slow rolling shiver that moves through the warm water and up your spine. You understand them perfectly even though you have no idea how.

Most of the time they do not need to communicate at all.

They move through the knee-deep lake in slow, drifting processions, barefoot, never hurrying. Their gait is half-walking, half-swimming. When they pass the great half-drowned machines they pause, lay their palms against the rust, and appear to listen. Sometimes they will stroke a turbine casing the way a midwife strokes a laboring mother’s belly. Sometimes they simply rest their heads against the metal and fall asleep standing, letting the sub-sonic heartbeat rock them. The machines, in turn, seem to purr for them; valves that have been seized for decades will sigh open at their approach, releasing warm, sweet vapor that the moths drink like nectar.

They tend the Underwet the way mycelium tends a forest floor.

Some spend their lives coaxing fresh flowstone around dangerous cracks. Others wade in slow circles stirring the lake with long iron rods, encouraging the bioluminescent plankton that feeds the milk eels. A quiet caste—those whose backs have grown thick with moth-colonies—spend decades inside the abandoned trams, sitting perfectly still while the fogged windows slowly clear from the inside out. No one knows what they do in there. When they eventually step out again, their eyes are clearer, and the machines around them run half a degree cooler for weeks afterward.

They are neither kind nor cruel. They are gestational.

If you stay long enough, one will eventually come to you. It will stand chest-deep in the trembling water, moths orbiting its head like a slow halo, and simply wait. When you finally meet its milky gaze you understand that it is not looking at the person you were when you descended the shaft. It is looking at the person the Underwet is trying to turn you into.

Some surface visitors leave after a single night, sweating and weeping, unable to bear the feeling of their own skeleton softening.

Others never leave.

Those who remain begin to change after the third or fourth breathing cycle of the basin. Their sweat stops evaporating. Their voices drop half an octave and gain strange harmonics. Their dreams become loud enough to be heard by the millipedes, who rearrange themselves into new patterns on the walkways each morning spelling out what the dreamer saw.

Eventually the newcomer stops trying to stay dry. They let the lukewarm water close over their shoulders. They allow the moths to land on their tongue. They press their forehead to the nearest pipe and add their own small note to the endless industrial liturgy.

And the Underwet, still deciding what it wants to become, decides—for one more slow, humid heartbeat—to become them as well.
