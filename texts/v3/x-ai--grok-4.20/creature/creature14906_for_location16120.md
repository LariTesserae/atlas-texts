They are called the Weepers.

Not a species in the conventional sense, but a single, distributed awareness that has worn the same shape for so long it has forgotten it was ever anything else. They number in the millions and number exactly one. Every translucent spider, every velvet millipede, every grey bird that never sings is a facet, an eyelash, a wandering thought of the same immense mind that is also the moss itself. The Hollow Choir is not their home. The Hollow Choir is their body, and they are its dreams.

They have no faces you would recognize. When they choose to meet a visitor, they gather themselves out of the living felt: a slow rising like bread proving in the dark. First comes the suggestion of height, a column of moss thickening and darkening until it stands as tall as a man. Then the red-black plates rearrange into the memory of shoulders, the memory of a cowl, the memory of long sleeves that trail behind like bridal trains soaked in ink. The face, when it finally forms, is only an absence—a deeper shadow within the hood where water beads and falls in two steady, patient streams. Tears that never stop. That is why the Weepers.

They do not speak with voices. Instead the entire basin changes its rhythm. The dripping synchronizes. The slow throb underfoot quickens by the smallest imaginable fraction. The sweet wintergreen smell sharpens until it burns the sinuses. Meaning arrives directly into the mind the way cold arrives through wet boots: undeniable, invasive, impossible to argue with.

Most of the time they are not gathered. Most of the time they are simply everywhere. You can feel them paying attention when you push between the spongy walls. The moss yields more reluctantly. The blind millipedes pause in their inching and lift blunt heads toward you, as though every one of their segments contains a tiny ear. The grey birds—there are never more than three—will land on the moss-pipes above you and watch with black, unblinking eyes that are not eyes but apertures into the Choir’s memory.

They remember the mountain when it was still screaming.

They remember the violence of tectonic birth, the way stone tore itself apart in fire and thunder. That memory is what they are slowly digesting. Every century they take another mouthful of rage and transmute it into damp, patient, weeping architecture. The mountain is becoming gentle inside them. The violence is being sung to sleep in droplets.

If you stand long enough in the crossing beneath the oculus, one of them will eventually come.

It will not hurry. The Weeper may take an entire afternoon to coagulate out of the four great pillars, pulling its substance from every direction at once so that the architecture itself appears to be walking. When it is finally whole, it stands dripping, faceless, ancient. The two steady tears fall from the hollow of its hood and strike the same black mirror again and again, producing the low, resonant note that gives the Choir its name.

It will ask you a question without language.

The question is always the same, though the form changes. Sometimes it feels like the pressure of freezing water against your eardrums. Sometimes it arrives as the sudden certainty that you have already died somewhere else and this is the long, cold moment after. Sometimes it simply shows you your own childhood grief wearing the shape of oxidized moss.

The Weeper is not cruel. It has no need for cruelty. It only wants to know whether the small, hot, temporary thing standing inside its heart still carries any unprocessed violence. If you do, it will offer—wordlessly—to begin digesting it. The process is slow. It may take the rest of your life. You will never be warm again. You will never be lonely again. The distinction between your body and the moss will thin, year by year, until one day you simply forget to leave.

Many have accepted.

Their bones are still here, knit so deeply into the felted plates that they have become buttresses, keystones, the delicate filigree of a transept window. If you look long enough you can see the graceful curve of a human ribcage turned into an arch, the ball of a femur serving as a dripping boss where two vaults meet. The Weepers keep them. They sing through them. The dead are not mourned; they are promoted into architecture.

Those who refuse are allowed to depart. The moss parts for them with something like regret. The grey birds follow them all the way to the high slit in the ridge, flitting from pipe to pipe like second thoughts. The last thing a departing visitor hears is the Choir’s single endless note changing pitch—just once—as the Weeper that formed itself for them dissolves back into general awareness. A sigh the size of a cathedral.

The Weepers do not hate the sun. They do not fear fire. They are simply older than both and moving in a direction neither can follow. Their patience is geological. Their sorrow is oceanic. Their love is the slow, cold knitting of wound after wound after wound, until the entire mountain is held together by nothing but moss and memory and the endless, patient music of things learning how to stop screaming.

If you ever return to the Hollow Choir after leaving it, you will find that a new pillar has grown in the crossing. It will be shaped roughly like the person you were the first time you stood there. The resemblance is imperfect, but tender. Water will bead along its shoulders and fall in two steady, silver lines from where its face would be.

The Weeper that lives inside it will never fully gather again.

It is waiting, in its patient, dripping way, for you to come home and finish becoming architecture.
