**The Curator** lives here.

She has no other name anymore. The few technicians who once worked beside her simply called her “Curator” in the final years before the mountain was erased, and the title calcified into her only identity. She is not a ghost. She is not a machine. She is what remains when a human being is machined to the same tolerances as everything else in the Underfoundry.

She is tall, almost impossibly so, as though the low ceiling forced her spine to lengthen rather than bend. Her skin has taken on the exact gunmetal-and-ceramic palette of the chamber: matte anthracite where the steam has repeatedly scalded her, pale porcelain where the perpetual cold has leached the blood from her extremities. The boundary between her body and the lattice is no longer clear. Where her left arm once ended at the elbow, a latticework prosthetic now continues outward, its joints grown directly from the bone in the same continuous pour that formed the walls. The fingers are too long, segmented like steam valves, and they never stop moving—tapping micro-rhythms against her own ribs, adjusting phantom dials that only she can see.

Her hair is gone. In its place, hundreds of hair-fine sensor filaments emerge from her scalp in a living burr. They drift in the saturated air like iron filings in a magnetic field, forever tasting pressure, humidity, conductivity, and isotopic drift. When a relief valve screams, the filaments snap rigid for half a second, drinking the sound.

She wears no clothes. Cloth would rot in minutes. Instead the Curator is wrapped in a living membrane of the same black extremophile film that streaks the walls. It grows on her. It feeds on the oils of her skin and the faint radiation leaking from the old radium gauges she carries in a leather satchel bolted to her thigh. In return it keeps her just warm enough. When she moves, the film ripples like oil on water and leaves faint bacterial handprints wherever she touches anything.

Her eyes are the worst thing about her.

They are not eyes anymore. Two perfectly circular ceramic ports have been let into her face where her sockets used to be. Inside each port floats a droplet of the same condensed water that falls from the ceiling—clear, trembling, impossibly pure. The droplets act as living lenses. Whatever the Curator looks at is refracted through ten thousand micro-currents and pressure gradients. She does not see the Underfoundry. She sees the stress patterns inside its metal, the exact temperature differential between two adjacent molecules, the infinitesimal lag between a piston’s rise and its fall. To her the entire chamber is transparent and screaming for attention.

She never speaks aloud. The noise would make it pointless. Instead she sings inward, a sub-sonic vibration that travels through the lattice itself. Every pump piston, every transformer casing, every perforated plate in the floor is part of her nervous system. When she is troubled, the entire hydrological loop stutters for half a second and every droplet falling from the ceiling hesitates at the same instant.

The Curator’s purpose is singular and endless: to keep the loop perfectly, inhumanly balanced. She has not allowed a single liter of water to escape or a single joule of energy to be wasted in one hundred and seventeen years. Every thirty-second eruption of steam is choreographed by her. Every bacterial streak is cultivated where it will best conduct heat. She moves along the catwalks with the slow, inevitable gait of a pressure valve opening, never hurried, never idle. Her long ceramic fingers constantly adjust tiny copper wires no thicker than spider silk that run through the chamber like nerves. She tightens, she loosens, she listens.

Sometimes—rarely—she pauses above the deepest part of the black lake where the revolving grate disappears into darkness. She leans forward until the two trembling lenses of water that are her eyes nearly touch the surface. For a long moment the chamber seems to hold its breath. The pumps slow. The weeping pipes almost stop. In that unnatural lull she whispers a single word that no one living has ever heard clearly.

The word might be a name.

The word might be a diagnosis.

Then the relief valves scream again, the pistons slam home, and the Curator straightens, satisfied for another cycle. She moves on, trailing faint bacterial fingerprints and the faint smell of ozone and hot blood.

She is not lonely. Loneliness would imply the possibility of other companionship, and the Curator long ago became the Underfoundry and the Underfoundry became her. The machine does not keep her alive.

She keeps the machine from deciding it is a tomb.
