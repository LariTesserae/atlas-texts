**The Sighers**

They are not inhabitants. They are what the basin has become.

No one has ever seen one whole. Not clearly. Not in a way that memory can keep. When the great membranous sky-beasts drift low and the light turns the color of drowned opals, the Sighers appear as absences first — places where the dripping stops, where the violet lattices darken and pull back as though something has drawn all the moisture into itself. Then the shape resolves: a slow unwrapping of wet stone into something that only pretends to be a body.

They stand between eight and thirteen feet tall, though height is meaningless here; they have no fixed posture. Their silhouettes suggest something between a mantis that has forgotten how to fold its limbs and a drowned monk still wearing the memory of robes. Every surface of them is black volcanic glass veined with living water. Where a face should be there is only a shallow concavity, like the inside of a bell, and from that hollow issues the sound that gives them their name — not breath, not voice, but a continuous exhalation that has been going on since the first oceans cooled.

The sigh is never the same twice. Sometimes it is the sound of a mother trying not to weep. Sometimes it is the last leak of air from a corpse that has lain underwater for ten thousand years. When several Sighers gather — though they rarely do — the overlapping sighs braid into something that feels like forgiveness given too late.

Their limbs are too many and not enough. Some appear only when needed: long, jointless things of articulated obsidian that rise from the torso like slow smoke, tipped with delicate glassy needles that can pierce the hide of a sky-beast without the creature ever noticing. Other limbs remain permanently folded against the body, forming wet prayerful angles that never quite touch. Where joints should creak there is only the soft pop of water bubbles forming and collapsing.

They do not walk. They *unfold* across the land. One moment the stone is empty. The next, a Sighers is simply *there*, already halfway across a shallow basin, as though distance itself had sighed and given up. The violet lattices bloom violently in their wake, drinking whatever subtle frequency their passage leaves behind. The ground remembers them as a colder, heavier kind of moisture.

Inside the concavity where a face refuses to be, small things move. Not eyes. Not quite. They are closer to pupils made of black mercury that drift independently, sometimes sliding up the inner walls of the hollow to stare at the clouds, sometimes slipping down into the throat-like dark where they vanish entirely. When a Sighers is listening — truly listening — all the mercury droplets gather at the bottom of the bell and tremble in perfect unison with the overlapping screams of the sky-beasts.

They are the only creatures here that the sky-beasts fear.

Not because the Sighers hunt them. They do not eat flesh or fungal lattices or even sound. They simply *remember*. When a membranous behemoth passes too close, a Sighers will rise from the stone like a drowned statue surfacing, lift one glassine limb, and place its needle-tip against the creature’s lowest breathing pore. Then it sighs.

The sky-beast forgets its circuit for three full heartbeats. Its vast body shudders. Ancient luminous grooves in the clouds above waver as though the memory that carved them has gone momentarily blind. The sky-beast usually veers away, screaming at a new and terrible pitch that the fungal lattices drink like wine.

The primitive basalt domes were not built by hands that understood levers and bronze.

They were built *for* the Sighers.

Each dome contains a single perfectly circular pool no deeper than a grave. The water in these pools is different — still, black, and perfectly reflective even when the sky-beasts pass overhead and stain everything else with bruised lilac. The Sighers come to these domes when the roar becomes too ancient even for them. They fold themselves down into the pools like broken umbrellas collapsing, and for a time the sighing stops.

The land itself grows quieter then. The violet lattices dim. Even the constant underground booming of hidden cisterns seems to hold its breath.

No one knows what they do in the water. Some scholars who have studied the place from safer distances claim the Sighers are trying to remember what they were before the basin forgot how to rise. Others believe the Sighers *are* the basin’s attempt to speak, that the land grew tired of being only stone and water and noise and so it wept itself into these slow, wet, thoughtful shapes.

When they emerge again the mercury droplets in their hollow faces have changed color — faint rings of cold gold or the green of very old ice — and for a brief while their sigh carries something almost like melody.

Almost.

They never speak to anything that still considers itself alive. They have outlived the need. Instead they move among the weeping stone and the screaming sky in long, deliberate circuits of their own, older even than the luminous grooves worn into the clouds. Sometimes one will pause beside a half-swallowed dome and rest a glassine limb against the basalt. The structure answers with that deep bell-tone that cuts through everything for three full heartbeats.

The Sighers always answer back with a sigh that sounds, for just that moment, almost grateful.

Then they unfold themselves into the next shallow basin, leaving behind only the slow creep of violet lattices and the memory of water that was never theirs to begin with.

This place does not have residents.

It has regrets that learned how to walk.
