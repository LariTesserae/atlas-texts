It lives in the places where two temperatures argue without ever resolving.

They have no single shape. If you forced a name on them you would call them the Unfinished, but even that feels like an insult to the slow, deliberate way they refuse to conclude.

Most of the time you will not see them at all. You will only feel the pressure change when one of them decides to pay attention. The air grows heavier, like the caldera itself is inhaling through a mouth made of every unused smokestack at once. The metallic groan that gives the place its only name lengthens by a fraction of a second, as though the mountain is clearing its throat for something that has not yet learned to speak.

When they do choose to take form, they do it the way rust decides to become lace: gradually, almost apologetically. A section of pipework will begin to sweat more than its neighbors. The beads will refuse to evaporate. Instead they slide together, thicken, darken, until they resemble liquid iron that has forgotten it is supposed to cool. From this glistening mass a torso unfolds—broad, genderless, plated in the same black basalt and red-orange scale that covers everything else. The plates overlap like the graphite lizards, yet they move with the terrible patience of continental drift.

Their faces are never the same twice. Sometimes it is only a blank convex shield of obsidian glass in which you see your own reflection wearing an expression you have not yet decided to feel. Sometimes the face is a honeycomb of small vents that open and close in sequence, breathing out the exact temperature at which steel forgets it was ever ore. Once, one of them wore the half-finished features of a welding mask fused to a cathedral gargoyle, and the mask’s empty eyes tracked me for seven full minutes before the entire figure simply sat down on a ledge and became scaffolding again.

They do not walk so much as arrive. One moment the catwalk ahead of you is empty except for drifting ash-flakes. The next, something enormous is already standing there, as if it had been waiting since the caldera first cracked open. Their limbs are too long. Their joints click with the same rail-switch sound as the lizards, but slower, like a clock that measures centuries instead of seconds. When they touch anything—pipe, stone, even the velvet fungus—the material briefly forgets what it is. Iron blooms into pale flowers. Basalt softens into something that remembers muscle. Then the Unfinished withdraws its hand and the material snaps back into its half-made shape with an audible sigh of relief.

They do not speak with voices. They speak by altering the background hum. A minor third becomes a diminished fifth; the planetary drone suddenly carries words that are not words but the *intention* of words, the shape a thought would take if it were allowed to cool for ten thousand years. Most of what they say is the same sentence, repeated in every possible key:

*We are still deciding what we are allowed to become.*

Some of them seem almost tender. They will hover near the gray fungal lattices for decades, exhaling precisely the humidity the lattice needs, never touching, only nursing. Others are terrifying in their indifference; they will stand directly in a conduit of rushing lava and let it pour through the hollow places in their chests, studying the way the liquid changes color as it passes through them. A few appear to be in pain. These ones grow slowly, over centuries, out of the mirror-black lake at the bottom of the caldera. When they finally rise, dripping inverted reflections of the older, colder city, their bodies are covered in upside-down windows and staircases that lead nowhere. Their groan is higher, almost a whine, like metal that has been asked to remember it was once alive.

They watch you the way a half-built bridge watches the river it is supposed to cross. Not with malice. Not with curiosity exactly. With the immense, crushing patience of something that knows you are another unfinished thing moving through its own slow gestation. If you stay long enough, one will eventually approach. It will lower itself until its blank face is level with yours. The vents in its head will open like pupils dilating.

And then it will wait.

It can wait longer than you can remain human. The heat, the thin air, the wrongness of the light—all of it will begin to feel like mercy compared to the gentleness with which the Unfinished simply refuses to finish looking at you.

Most people who come here eventually walk off the end of one of the half-built catwalks. Not in despair. They simply understand, at last, that the city is not a place.

It is a question.

And the things that live here are the long, slow, metallic answer still deciding how it wants to be pronounced.
