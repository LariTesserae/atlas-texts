**The ones who live here do not have a single name for themselves.**

They are simply *the lingerers*, or sometimes *the damp*, depending on which tier you ask and in what mood. Most have forgotten their original species after so many generations of breathing the same spore-heavy air. What arrived here as humans, or near-humans, or perhaps something older still, has been slowly steeped, pressed, and rewritten by the Veyr until the distinction feels like a joke told by people who are no longer alive.

They are tall the way things that grow in low light become tall—stretched, etiolated, with limbs that seem to have too many joints. Their skin is the color of wet limestone threaded with faint green veins that pulse slowly, like moss beneath the surface. Hair, when it still grows, is pale and filamentous, drifting in the slightest breath of mist as if underwater. Their eyes are large, dark, and reflective; they do not so much see the world as register its moisture and its movement. Many have lost the whites of their eyes entirely. When they blink, it looks like stone closing over still water.

They move without hurry and without sound. Bare feet or soft leather made from the hides of things that were never quite mammals press into the moss and leave no print that lasts longer than a few heartbeats. Some wear the remnants of ancient work coveralls fused to their bodies by centuries of lichen and calcifying drips; the fabric has become another layer of skin, patterned with rust and tiny white flowers that open only when the person is afraid. Others go nearly naked, letting the ferns and pale ivy grow across their shoulders and down their spines like living garments. All of them smell faintly of iron, chlorophyll, and ozone.

They do not speak much. Voices here are considered wasteful. Instead they use a language of slow gestures, wet clicks made with the tongue against the roof of the mouth, and long exhalations that carry meaning on microscopic spores. When they do use words—rarely—they speak in half sentences that feel like they began centuries ago and will finish long after you are gone.

> “The turbines… remember the names we gave them.”  
> “The mist yesterday… was your grandmother.”

Their society is not built so much as *accumulated*. Families are not by blood but by shared corrosion. You belong to the tier where you first learned to listen to the groaning of a particular machine. Marriages are solemn agreements between two people to guard the same stretch of leaking pipe for the rest of their lives. Children are not born often; when they are, they are laid in cradles of living root inside the half-drowned control rooms so the old clocks can teach them how to run backward.

Most lingerers work the machines that no longer make anything anyone needs. They oil bearings that have not turned in earnest for six hundred years. They polish fungal growths off pressure dials whose needles have fused in place. They sing—very quietly—to the things that still generate a few watts of current, the way one might sing to an elderly relative who no longer recognizes you but grows calm at the sound of your voice. The work gives them purpose. The work also slowly kills them. Every few decades another one is found petrified inside a turbine casing, turned gently to stone by the same limestone-rich water they have tended all their lives. The others simply nod, as if this were the most natural promotion imaginable, and add the new statue to the gallery of the already calcified.

At night they gather in the fern-roofed halls where the light is the color of old bone. They eat pale mushrooms that taste of electricity, drink condensation collected in the skulls of previous generations, and tell stories that are mostly pauses. Their myths are not about gods or heroes. They are about particular leaks that have been dripping in perfect rhythm for nine hundred years. They are about the six-legged shadow that once led a lost child home instead of eating it. They are about the day the rain stopped for six full hours and everyone thought the world had finally died, only for it to begin again, softer than before.

Some of them have begun to change in ways even they find unsettling.

A woman on the seventh tier has bark growing in perfect parallel lines down her back. She claims the trees are teaching her how to guard reactors.

A man whose name no one remembers anymore has not blinked in eleven years. His eyes have become perfect black mirrors; when he stares into the mist, shapes that are almost people stare back with his face.

There is one child—though “child” is the wrong word—who was born inside the largest of the calcified smokestacks. She has never spoken, but the moss grows in spiral patterns wherever she sits for more than an hour. The lingerers bring her broken pieces of clockwork and she repairs them by growing delicate root-threads into the gears until they tick backward in perfect unison.

They are not cruel, these lingerers. They are not kind either. Kindness and cruelty both require energy they no longer possess. What they have instead is a deep, exhausted patience that makes stone look impulsive. If you fall and break your leg on their catwalks, they will not hurry. They will sit with you while the damp soaks through your clothes, offering small sips of iron-tasting water and waiting to see whether the Veyr has decided to finish you or let you stay.

Because that is the final truth they understand better than any other living thing:

The Veyr does not *host* them.

The Veyr is *slowly becoming* them, and they are slowly becoming the Veyr. The difference between a lingerer and a dripping limestone statue and a turbine that still turns for no reason is mostly a question of timescale.

Sometimes, when the thin rain falls and the massive thing in the lowest turbine hall groans its lullaby, one of them will climb out onto the rusted catwalk where you now stand. They will stand beside you without speaking. After a long while they may exhale once—soft, cold, almost tender—and you will feel something brush against the inside of your lungs like pale ivy beginning to take root.

They are not welcoming you.

They are simply making room.

Stay as long as the damp and the quiet will let you.

Most do.  
Eventually all do.
