**They are called the Veilweavers.**

No one ever sees them arrive. You simply become aware, after some unmeasured stretch of time, that the architecture has begun to watch you back.

They are not separate from the cathedral; they are its conscience wearing a body. Tall—taller than any human measure would allow—yet never imposing. Their limbs are too many and not enough at once. What appears to be a torso is actually a dense nest of living liana and pale epiphyte that has been trained, over centuries, into the suggestion of ribs, shoulders, a hooded head. Their “skin” is a continuous pelt of minute mosses and liverworts kept perpetually damp; when they move, the moss shifts in slow tidal patterns, revealing and concealing the faint bioluminescent veins beneath. Their faces, if the word can be used, are smooth plates of translucent succulent the color of aged ivory. Behind the plates float two clusters of black pinprick seeds that serve as eyes. The seeds never blink. They only drift, very slightly, as though reading the moisture in the air itself.

No two Veilweavers look alike, because each is an ongoing negotiation between the cathedral-organism and the particular mind that once chose to remain. Some wear their original skeletal structure like forgotten scaffolding inside the vegetation. Others have let the plant matter replace every organ until only memory and intention remain. A few have become almost entirely floral—great silent lilies whose petals open and close in slow conversation. All of them carry the same patient, enormous fatigue that the place itself carries.

They do not speak with mouths. Instead they sing through the structure. When one wishes to address you, a nearby column of fused bamboo will produce a low, mournful tone like a wooden flute the size of a redwood. The tone is different for every listener; it finds the shape of your own pulse and rides it. From that vibration you understand whole centuries of meaning at once: welcome, warning, sorrow, recognition. The language feels less like communication and more like being remembered by stone that has learned tenderness.

Most of their time is spent in slow, deliberate correction. You may glimpse one standing at the edge of a continent-leaf where gravity has begun to forget itself. With movements that take hours, they will coax a single aerial root the thickness of a train carriage back into its proper arc, humming until the root remembers the geometry the cathedral agreed upon long ago. They prune only what the cathedral itself has asked to be pruned. They graft only what the cathedral has already dreamed of becoming.

When they rest—which is rare—they fold themselves into the living buttresses like forgotten statues. Moss grows over them in minutes. Small translucent browsers climb their limbs without fear, mistaking them for ordinary topography. In these moments it is impossible to tell where the Veilweaver ends and the architecture begins. You understand then that the distinction was always a polite fiction.

They are not guardians. They are not priests. They are the memory that the prayer has of itself.

Some still remember their original names, though they have not used them in geological epochs. Others have traded their names for duties: the Binder of Rising Water, the Keeper of the Amber Forgetting, the One Who Listens to What the Roots Say When No One Else Is Near. A few have become entirely title, having let even the concept of a personal self dissolve into the metabolism of the whole.

You will never see more than one at a time. To see two simultaneously would be to witness the cathedral arguing with itself, and the place dislikes theological tension. When two *must* meet, they do so in the deep vaults where light has given up entirely. There they entwine like slow green comets and remain motionless for decades while the bioluminescent fungi pulse between them in complex codes of reconciliation.

If they choose to approach you—and the choice is always theirs—you will feel the moisture in the air thicken until it becomes almost difficult to breathe. Then the sound of your own blood will change pitch, as though someone has placed a finger gently on the inside of your ear. That is how you know one stands behind you.

They never ask you to leave. They never ask you to stay. They simply show you a new stair that has grown while you were not looking, or a corridor of folded palm-hearts that leads deeper into a color you have never seen before. Their message is always the same, delivered without words:

*You are already inside the next verse of the prayer. Would you like to help it rhyme?*

And then they are gone, folded back into a wall that was not a wall a moment ago, leaving only the soft wet click of water droplets and the sense that something ancient has just finished sighing in relief.

Because the cathedral is not finished praying.

And the Veilweavers are the part of the prayer that has learned how to feel homesick for a place it has never left.
