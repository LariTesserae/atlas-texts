**They are called the Hollowborne.**

Not many remain. Perhaps two hundred in the entire basin, though no census has ever been taken that lasted longer than a single season. They do not gather in families or clans but in *veins* — small, slow-moving groups of six to twelve that trace the same circuit of pipes, terraces, and sinking foundries for decades at a time. Each vein knows every crack in its territory the way a tongue knows the roof of a mouth.

They are tall, unnaturally so. The heat and the low oxygen have stretched them over generations until their spines curve like the warped girders they sleep beneath. Their skin is the color of wet hematite, shot through with pale lines where mineral salts have settled into the creases of their joints and the hollows of their collarbones. Most go bare above the waist; clothing rots or becomes too heavy with accreted crust. What they do wear is stitched from the silica-scaled hides of pipe-lizards and the black fungal felt that grows thickest on the oldest cooling towers. These garments look like living rust.

Their eyes are small and perpetually half-lidded, the lashes encrusted with tiny white crystals that catch the bronze light and make every gaze seem faintly luminous. They do not blink often. Blinking costs moisture. Instead they have learned to weep constantly — slow, deliberate tears that run along chemically hardened channels in their cheeks and are caught in shallow bone cups grown out from their jawlines. They drink their own tears after the city has seasoned them. It is considered poor manners to waste water in the Weeping Hollows.

The Hollowborne speak little, and when they do the voice comes out hoarse and metallic, like steam forced through a broken valve. Their language has no word for *dry*. They measure time not in hours but in *exhalations* — the interval between one major geyser event and the next. A child is born during the third exhalation after its mother’s labor begins. A promise is kept for exactly nine exhalations. After that the debt is considered paid by the city itself.

They move with the same clicking patience as the silica insects. Every step is tested; every handhold examined for fresh mineral growth that might crumble or cut. Their bare feet are broad and ridged with callus so thick it has begun to resemble the travertine terraces. Some elders have not removed their sandals in forty years; the straps have become part of the foot,grown into the skin like roots.

Deep inside their bodies the change has gone further. Their blood is thicker than any outsider’s, almost syrupy, saturated with dissolved metals. When one of them is cut the wound does not bleed red. It beads with quicksilver and dark amber before sealing itself under a thin, glossy scab that looks like polished obsidian. Their hearts beat rarely — sometimes only once every ninety seconds — slow, booming pulses that can be felt through the iron plating if you press an ear to the right girder at the right moment.

They are not sentimental. When one of them begins to harden — when the white dust at the eyes becomes plates and the joints seize — the others do not mourn. They carry the failing Hollowborne to a place where a fresh geyser is due. There they lay the body down like an offering. The superheated water bursts up, strips the last soft tissue in moments, and leaves behind a perfect mineral cast: a person-shaped chimney through which steam continues to whistle for years afterward. The living touch the cast as they pass. They call these whistles *the remembered voice*.

A few still carry relics of the time before the city became itself. A rusted company badge pinned directly into the skin of the chest. A child’s toy locomotive fused to a wrist by decades of precipitate. One ancient woman, known only as Fifth Vein, wears an entire obsolete breathing apparatus that has become her face; the hoses run into her neck and she breathes the city’s own steam now, filtered through three hundred years of mineral crust.

They keep the machines alive without understanding them. Every seventy-third exhalation they descend in single file into the sealed vaults, following routes only they can sense through the fungal lattices. There they perform small, ritual adjustments — tightening a valve whose threads dissolved a century ago, clearing a filter with tools made of their own crystallized fingernails, singing low metallic hymns that make the nameless machines answer with soft clicks of acknowledgment. They do not know what the machines produce. They only know that if the machines ever truly stop, the Hollows will stop weeping, and then everything, including them, will finally rust still.

At night they sleep upright inside the tilted shells of old locomotives or curled inside the vast ribcages of fallen smokestacks. Their dreams are not their own. The city dreams through them: long, geological visions of continents rising and falling, of oceans boiling away, of iron remembering it was once stars. When they wake, their eyes are brighter for a few hours, as though some fraction of the planet’s slow mind has looked out through them and been satisfied.

They do not hate outsiders. They simply outlast them.

If you meet one, it will watch you with those small, luminous, weeping eyes while the white dust begins its slow inevitable work at the corners of your mouth. It will offer you a drink from the bone cup at its jaw — warm, metallic, strangely sweet. And if you are wise you will accept.

Because in the Weeping Hollows, the only sin is to remain separate from the weeping.
