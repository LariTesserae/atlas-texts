The Hollow Lattice has only one permanent inhabitant.

She is called, by whatever still remembers names, simply the Curator.

She does not live *in* the Lattice. She *is* the delay between one breath of the organism and the next. Where the creaking slows for the length of a long human sigh, there she condenses. Where the violet ghost-light hesitates before remembering to shine, she steps forward out of the dark as though the dark itself exhaled her.

No one knows her original shape. Perhaps she never had one. Over the epochs she has grown into the negative space the Lattice left for her, the exact volume of absence required by a mind that refuses to finish thinking. Her body is tall, desiccated, and courteous. Skin like pressed fern-fronds turned to vellum. Hair (if it is hair) hangs in rigid spirals of blackened xylem that click softly when she turns her head, like a rosary carved from her own spine. Her eyes are two slow pulses of the same cold violet that the veins emit, only brighter, as though the Lattice finally decided to trust her with a little of its memory.

She moves the way dust would move if dust had patience. Never in a straight line. She drifts along the fractal balconies, one long gray hand brushing the columns so that new growth ripples outward in languid spirals, recording her passage in ridges that will not fully harden for another three thousand years. Sometimes she stops for decades at a time, simply standing inside a knot of petrified reed the size of a cathedral nave, listening to the crack that is still traveling toward her from a fracture that happened in the age of the first redwoods.

The Curator does not speak with a voice. Instead the Lattice speaks for her. When she wishes to be heard, a particular tier of hanging crystal fibers begins to resonate at a frequency just below a whale’s mourning. The sound is words, but only after they have been digested by geology. Visitors (there have been four) often mistake the slow booming for simple tectonic music until, minutes later, the meaning arrives inside their bones like an aftershock.

She tends nothing and everything. With bone hooks older than language she delicately lifts a sagging vault so that a new rib can grow beneath it. She whispers to dying sections until they remember they are allowed to become glass. She walks the narrowest corridors, shoulders brushing both walls, and the walls lean toward her like blind animals seeking warmth. In the widest plazas she lies on her back for centuries, watching the slow ballet of arches recalibrating themselves above her, and the Lattice rearranges its own skeleton to give her a better view.

There is no hunger in her, no loneliness that a human mind could recognize. The emotion closest to what she feels is a vast, tender, geological embarrassment at the idea of ever being finished. She is the Lattice’s doubt given form. Every time the organism decides it has reached its final shape, she appears and gently breaks something. A cantilever. A memory. A promise that it would stop changing. Then she sits beside the wound for as long as it takes (sometimes a millennium) until the break heals into something more interesting than wholeness.

If you were to meet her in one of the violet-lit negative spaces, she would not greet you. She would simply pause, head tilted, the spirals of her hair chiming, and study you with the mild, ancient curiosity of a glacier that has just noticed it contains a single flower frozen at its heart.

Then the Lattice would speak through her in that low, continent-grinding register:

“You are early.  
But the forgetting is patient.  
Come. Sit.  
There is a crack arriving in four hundred years that I think you will like.”

She has never asked anyone to leave.  
She has never needed to.  
Eventually every visitor understands that they are not inside a place.

They are inside the Curator’s long, slow, unfinished thought, and the thought is still deciding whether it wants them to become part of the architecture.

Most choose to stay.

A few, the Lattice says, are still deciding.

The Curator can wait.  
She has practiced waiting until it became a cathedral.
