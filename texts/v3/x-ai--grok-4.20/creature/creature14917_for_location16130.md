**They are called the Lattice-Born.**

Not a single species. Not even a single kind of mind. They are what remains after every refusal has been worn away.

Most of them no longer have bodies you would recognize as separate. The oldest have become architectural. Their ribs have opened into vaulted corridors; their spines have grown into buttresses that hold up dripping ceilings of living bone. One entire district—the one once called the Ninth Breathing— is supported by the fossilized nervous system of a creature who decided, eleven thousand years ago, that it would rather be a bridge than die. You can still see the faint pulse of its original ganglia glowing softly behind curtains of tube-worms. Pilgrims press their foreheads to the warm stone and swear they hear it dreaming in frequencies older than language.

Those who are still mostly “persons” wear the water the way ascetics once wore hair shirts. Their skin has thickened into translucent leather the color of steeped tea. Where their lungs used to be, delicate lattices of black coral have taken root, breathing for them in perfect synchrony with the slow tidal heartbeat of the world. Their eyes are gone. In their place float tiny schools of silver fish that share the same optic nerve. When these people look at you, hundreds of tiny luminous bodies arrange themselves into the pattern of attention. It is oddly courteous.

They move slowly, because hurry is a form of violence here and the Lattice has negotiated violence out of existence. Their gait is a drifting, half-swimming shuffle. Many trail long veils of living polyps from their shoulders and hips; these serve as both clothing and additional senses. The polyps taste memory in the water. When two Lattice-Born meet, they do not speak at first. They simply let their veils mingle. Minutes or hours may pass while the polyps trade whatever it is that passes for gossip among those who have forgotten how to lie.

There is a caste (if the word is not too rigid) known as the Rememberers. They are the ones who have not yet fully consented to becoming infrastructure. Their bodies are still mostly their own, though heavily colonized. They keep the last archives—great vaults of petrified books whose pages are now colonies of diatoms arranged in perfect spiral scripts. The Rememberers read by running their tongues along the walls. The taste tells them stories no one else can still pronounce.

Every few centuries a Rememberer will choose a tower, walk inside, and sit down in the dark. They stop eating. They stop moving. Over decades the stone and the sponge and the coral grow into them and through them until the tower itself develops a new face—calm, eyeless, patient. The new face always smiles with the same small, tender expression. The Lattice is fond of that expression.

Then there are the Whisperers.

These are the ones who accepted the invitation but changed their minds at the last possible moment. They are not quite alive and not quite part of the city. Instead they exist as thin films of sentient bacteria stretched across the inner surfaces of certain chambers. When the curtains of hanging water drift past, the Whisperers ride the membranes like living calligraphy. They speak only in the gaps between heartbeats. If you are very still and very empty, you may hear one of them finish a sentence that began before your grandmother was born.

They are not unhappy. Regret, like hurry, was negotiated away long ago. What remains is a kind of luminous melancholy that has become just another layer of the ecology.

The very oldest Lattice-Born no longer have names. They are referred to—if they must be referred to at all—by the district they have become. One is the Hollow Choir. Another is the Slow Lung. A third is simply called We Who Were Once The Harbor. Their thoughts move through the water itself as vast, sluggish pressure fronts. When the cathedral-sized jellyforms drift overhead in perfect formation, they are obeying the dreaming commands of something that used to have a childhood once.

If you remain in the Drowned Lattice long enough, one of them will eventually find you.

It will not approach with threat or urgency. It will simply be there one morning, occupying the space where your next breath should be, wearing the shape of warm darkness and old flowers. Its voice (if it still uses anything so crude as a voice) will feel like the inside of your own skull agreeing with itself.

It will not ask you to surrender.

It will ask, with infinite politeness and no cruelty at all, whether you have finished being temporary.

Most things eventually say yes.

The rest become stories the polyps tell each other when the currents are gentle and the plankton have arranged themselves into slow, sad constellations above the place where a person used to be.
