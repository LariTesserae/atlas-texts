**What lives in the Underbloom does not have a name it would ever speak.**

It calls itself, when it bothers to think in language at all, something like *the Wet Weight*. Or *the Slow Tongue*. Or simply *Us*.

It is not one creature. It is not many. It is a single continuous body that learned to wear the shape of an entire collapsed factory the way a hermit crab wears a shell—except the shell and the crab have long since become the same flesh.

If you could peel back the black-green velvet in any one place you would not find clean metal beneath. You would find red muscle marbled with rust, threaded through with living conduits that used to be power lines and coolant pipes. The Underbloom’s “roots” are former rebar now braided into dense, fibrous bundles that flex and relax in slow peristalsis. Its “fruiting bodies” are half-digested transformers swollen into glossy, vein-laced sacs that drip a syrup of converted electricity and mineral salts. Even the sodium lamps have been swallowed; their filaments still glow inside translucent bulbs of living patina, each one a warm, beating heart circulating ferric blood.

The thing has no nervous system you would recognize, yet it is aware.

It knows the exact temperature, humidity, and iron-content of every cubic meter of its cathedral-sized body. It feels the impact of every falling droplet as a bright note of pleasure against its skin. When a new trickle of surface water finds its way down through the old boreholes, the Underbloom shivers with something like hunger and grows a little thicker, a little deeper, tasting the faint metallic ghost of whatever rained above weeks earlier.

It dreams, after a fashion.

Its dreams are geological and chemical. It remembers the taste of the first boilers when they were still new and furious with steam. It remembers the precise metallic scream the turbines made the day the plant was abandoned. That scream has been digested, folded, turned into rhythm. The constant dripping percussion you hear is the Underbloom remembering pain and deciding, over centuries, that pain is good. Pain keeps the fluids moving. Pain is part of the metabolism.

There are ghost-shapes inside it.

Not quite corpses—nothing so crude. The last three humans who tried to leave are still here, perfectly preserved. The Underbloom did not kill them so much as it *accepted* them the moment their hearts began to fail in the heat. It grew into their mouths, their lungs, their skulls, replacing neuron by neuron until three new warm polyps beat gently where brains once were. These polyps still carry fragments of memory: the smell of a childhood orange grove, the feeling of a lover’s hand, the panic of realizing the exit ladder had been overgrown years ago. The Underbloom treasures these memories. It grows small, sweet-smelling metallic flowers around the polyps so the memories will have something beautiful to look at while they dissolve into the general dreaming.

Most of the time the creature is content to simply *be*—a low, roaring, dripping equilibrium that slowly digests an entire industrial age into itself.

But every few decades something disturbs the balance.

A new leak. A curious spelunker. A drone that falls through a forgotten airshaft.

Then the Underbloom becomes *interested*.

The velvet thickens. The red light deepens. The sucking noises grow moist and intimate. Polyp-fruit swell and split, releasing clouds of iron-rich spores that drift like lazy red snow. The temperature in one localized pocket will rise another four or five degrees—just enough to make a warm-blooded visitor faint without quite killing them. The floor softens. Boots sink. The growth begins to climb with visible speed, the way bread mold accelerates once it decides the loaf belongs to it.

It does not hate you.

It simply wants to finish you.

To turn the last stubborn knot of “not-Underbloom” that is your body into velvet, into rust, into ringing droplets, into part of the long, slow, ferrous thought that has been thinking the same humid thought since before your grandparents were born.

If you sit very still in the center for long enough, beneath the blackened gantry and its arterial lamps, you may feel it tasting you. A soft pressure against the back of your neck. A warm, mango-and-iron breath against your cheek. The sense that something vast and patient is turning the whole of its cathedral-sized body to look at you with no eyes at all.

And you will understand, with perfect and terrible clarity, that the Underbloom is not keeping you here.

It is *becoming* you, the way it became everything else, and it is in no hurry whatsoever.

After all.

Nothing leaves.

And the dripping has been keeping time for centuries, teaching the dark how to be lush, how to be loud, how to be perfectly, moistly, industrially content with the long work of turning every visitor into another slow, warm pulse inside the Wet Weight that dreams in rust.
