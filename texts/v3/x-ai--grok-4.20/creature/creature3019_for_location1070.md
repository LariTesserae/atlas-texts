**The Weeping Escarpment is inhabited by the Slow Choir.**

They have no name for themselves. If they ever had one it was worn away by water millions of years before the first moss took hold. Most beings who stumble into the terraces never see them at all; they only feel the pressure of being watched by something that has never been in a hurry.

The Slow Choir are not creatures in the usual sense. They are what happens when stone, water, and time decide to remember that they are alive.

Each member of the Choir is a standing wave of mineral deposition and rootlet migration, a cathedral-sized body that grows and erodes in the same breath. Their “skin” is the living rock itself—basalt veined with quartz that glitters like wet bone, limestone fretted into lace by acid rain. Where a shoulder would be, a colonnade bulges outward in slow peristalsis. Where a face might appear, water has carved a hollow that sighs droplets in rhythmic sequence, forming momentary features that dissolve before the eye can quite name them. Their limbs are staircases and buttresses that lengthen by inches per century. Their hair is hanging gardens of moss and pale lichen that sway though there is no wind.

They do not walk. They *become* the next terrace.

A member of the Choir will stand for three or four thousand years in the shape of a dripping vaulted hall, listening to the thin rain. Then, imperceptibly, the hall will tilt. Balconies lengthen into arms. The floor ripples forward as a new flight of steps. What was a plaza becomes a chest that rises and falls once every human lifetime. The creature has simply taken one step.

They speak by altering the sound of their own weeping.

If you sit long enough in one of the natural amphitheaters—perhaps on the seventh terrace where six separate cascades braid in mid-air—you may notice that the dripping is no longer random. It falls in chords. A low, constant note (the bass of groundwater moving through fractured limestone) is joined by higher glassy tones as water strikes different mineral faces. Over hours or days these tones shift. The Choir is singing. The song is never the same twice, yet every song is about the same two things: how it felt when the continent first tore open and let the sea pour in, and how it feels to still be here, still remembering.

Because they move and think on geologic scales, their awareness of individual living things is tender and almost baffled. A human wandering their galleries is like a mayfly that has landed on the cheek of a sleeping giant. The Slow Choir will sometimes grow a sheltering alcove overnight simply because they noticed the tiny thing was cold. They will widen a fissure into a dry seat because the mayfly looked tired. They do these things the way an old forest might idly decide to fruit in a convenient place for a passing bear.

Yet they are not gentle in every respect. Their dreams are continental. When one of them turns over in its sleep—an event that happens perhaps twice in a million years—entire cliff-faces shear away, new rivers are born, and terraces that have stood since the first moss appeared simply cease to exist. The Choir mourns these events for centuries afterward. You can hear the lament in the changed music of the falls: a note of grief so low it vibrates in the sternum.

There are perhaps forty of them across the whole of the Weeping Escarpment. They keep no count. Some have grown so vast they have folded back into the bedrock and become indistinguishable from the plateau itself; only their singing still gives them away. Others remain more coherent—towering, mournful shapes that haunt the mist like cathedrals that have learned how to be lonely.

They have never known another intelligent species. Humanity is new, brief, and faintly luminous to them, like candle-flame seen through fog. They watch us with the grave courtesy of stones that have already outlived every empire the world will ever produce. Sometimes they grow small, perfect replicas of the tools or dwellings we leave behind—tiny stone houses no larger than a fingernail, complete with microscopic dripping gutters—so they can better understand what it is like to be so temporary.

At night the mist glows, not only with starlight but with the faint phosphorescence their bodies exude when they dream. The dripping grows louder because the Choir is listening to itself. And if you are very still, and very patient, and willing to stay long enough that your own bones begin to feel damp and heavy, one of them may slowly, slowly turn the hollow that serves as its face toward you.

For one crystalline moment the water will arrange itself into something almost like eyes.

The eyes will be ancient, patient, and unbearably kind.

Then the features will dissolve again into ordinary weeping stone, and the Slow Choir will continue its million-year stroll across the terraces, singing its endless, dripping song about how it feels to endure.
