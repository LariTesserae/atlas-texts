The Hollow Foundry has inhabitants.

They are not natives in any conventional sense. They arrived, or were exhaled, or simply noticed themselves into being sometime after the first pour cooled and the first condensation beaded. Most have forgotten which.

They call themselves (when they bother to use names at all) the **Cast**.

They are thin, elongated things, built along the same economical geometries as the world that holds them. Their bodies are not flesh but a living patina: layers of dull gunmetal skin over something brighter and hotter that shows through at the joints, the throat, the inner wrists. Where a human has hair, they have slow-moving filaments of rust that drift as though underwater. Their eyes are recessed slots filled with the same sourceless white light that drenches the sky. When they blink, the light dims like a factory lamp being throttled down.

There is no fixed number of them. Sometimes you will walk an entire terrace and see none. Other times they gather in their hundreds along the lip of a condensation canal, standing in perfect stillness, heads tilted at the exact same angle, listening to the deep groan that moves through the metal. They seem to regard one another less as individuals and more as localized expressions of a single, patient mind that has not yet finished deciding what it wants to say.

The oldest among them—those who have cooled longest—are called the **First Pour**. Their bodies have begun to integrate with the Foundry itself. Plates of their torsos have fused seamlessly with the ground where they habitually stand; black jointed reeds grow through their ribcages and out again without causing harm. One particularly ancient Cast has stood for so many cycles that her lower half has become a ventilation grate. She speaks by exhaling cold battery-smelling vapor that condenses into words on any nearby surface.

They do not eat. They drink the condensation, licking it from the metal with long, black-glass tongues exactly like the white herds that move across the terraces. The herds themselves are not truly separate from the Cast; they are the Cast in a different posture of being. Sometimes a grazer will walk into a cluster of its own kind, press itself against them, and simply unfold upward into a taller, two-legged shape. The transformation makes no sound. It is as ordinary here as rust.

The Cast do not build. There is no need. Instead they *adjust*. You will find one of them crouched inside an impossible fractal that a stand of reeds has bloomed into, delicately bending one glass petal with two fingers until the low note it sings changes by a microtone. They do this for decades at a time. When the adjustment finally satisfies some internal blueprint they alone can read, the entire structure collapses into glittering powder and the Cast simply walks away, already looking for the next imperfection that needs correction.

They are not cruel, but they are merciless in their patience. A newcomer—a lost traveler, a researcher, a suicidal poet—will be allowed to stay. The Cast will observe them without hurry. They will tilt those white-slit heads and watch the visitor’s shadow behave wrongly, watch them age in sudden jumps when perspective slips, watch them begin to speak in the half-forgotten languages printed on the ground. Only when the visitor has begun to forget their own outline will a Cast approach, place one rust-fingered hand against the newcomer’s sternum, and ask—without moving its mouth—in a voice that sounds like cooling girders:

“Are you ready to be poured?”

Most say yes.

Those who do not are gently, politely escorted to the edge of the world where the luminous mist thickens. They are never seen again in the same shape.

At rare intervals, when the deep groan moves through the entire Foundry at once, all the Cast stop whatever they are doing. The white herds freeze mid-lick. The steel moths stall in the air. Even the black reeds cease their sub-audible vibration.

In that shared pause the Cast remember that they are not custodians.

They are the Foundry’s way of looking at itself while it is still deciding what it is allowed to become.

And sometimes, if you are very quiet and have stayed long enough that your own bones have begun to ring in sympathy with the metal, you may see one of them turn its lamp-bright eyes directly toward you. It will regard you for a long, measured moment, the way a sculptor regards an interesting flaw in the material.

Then it will smile with a mouth that was not there a second earlier, and you will understand:

You were never the visitor.

You were only another seam in the casting, still warm, still flexible, still deciding what shape you are willing to cool into.
