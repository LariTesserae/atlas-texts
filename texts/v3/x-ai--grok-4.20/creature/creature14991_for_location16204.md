**The Verdant Stair is not inhabited. It is tenanted.**

What lives here does not merely dwell inside the Body. It has become one of the Body’s slower, more deliberate thoughts.

They are called the **Stewards of the Ninth Petal**, though they have worn many names across the epochs the Body has taken to finish itself. To the casual eye they appear almost human—tall, attenuated, skin the color of wet heartwood, eyes like drops of resin still catching the last of some forgotten sun. But stand near one long enough and the illusion frays. Their ribs are not ribs; they are lateral branches that have learned to cage a heart. Their hair is fine root-fiber, twitching independently, tasting the air for changes in sap-pressure and spore-count. When they speak, the sound arrives a half-second late, as though the words first had to travel through kilometers of living wood before reaching their mouths.

There are never many of them. Never fewer than nine, never more than thirty-three. The Body itself seems to cull or recruit them according to rhythms no outsider has ever mapped. Some have walked these spiraling colonnades for four hundred years without aging past their thirtieth summer. Others appear as adolescents, already ancient behind the eyes, carrying the memories of Stewards who came before them the way a tree carries its heartwood.

They do not build. They *coax*.

You will meet one—eventually—on a terrace no wider than a village street, where a single colossal fungal column rises like a cathedral pillar shot through with slow amber lightning. The Steward will be barefoot, toes fused gently into the floor the way a vine merges with its trellis. Their hands will be buried wrist-deep in the living wall, fingers moving in the patient, microscopic gestures that guide capillary flow and cambium growth. They are not commanding the Body. They are listening so perfectly that the Body begins to dream in the shape of their listening.

When they turn to regard you, the expression is not quite kindness and not quite indifference. It is the look a centuries-old oak might give a mayfly that has landed on its bark: courteous, remote, faintly amused by the brevity of your panic.

They will offer you water from one of the black root-snouts. The water always tastes like whatever you needed most at that exact moment—sometimes iron and snowmelt, sometimes childhood rain on a tin roof. Drink, and you will feel the Body notice you. Not as prey. Not even as guest. As a temporary fluctuation in its enormous, patient metabolism.

The Stewards do not rule. They are the part of the Body that has learned to walk upright and speak aloud so it can argue with itself.

At night (though night is only a thickening of the marine gloom) they sing to the great diaphragms far overhead. Their songs are wordless, pitched so low they register first in the bones, then in the resin-lamps that brighten or dim in time with the melody. When they sing, entire terraces shift—slow as continents—re-aligning drainage grooves that have not needed adjustment for three hundred years. Beetles the size of shields click in resonant counterpoint. Frogs fall silent in reverence. Even the bats alter their flight paths into living musical staves across the dark.

Some Stewards have begun to root. Their feet have merged so completely with the floor that they can no longer leave their chosen column or balcony. They do not seem to mind. Their upper bodies continue their careful work while their lower halves become buttresses, archives, living buttresses swollen with the accumulated memory of every raindrop the Body has drunk since the last Ice retreated.

There is one among them—called only **the Thirteenth**—who has allowed the Body to grow *through* her chest. A braided liana as thick as a man’s thigh enters below her collarbone and exits between her shoulder blades, flowering once every seven years in perfect synchrony with the vast pale blossoms kilometers above. She no longer speaks with a human voice. Instead, the flowers on her vine open and release pollen that smells like whatever truth you are least ready to hear.

The Stewards do not fear death. When one of them finally tires, they simply lie down along a chosen ridge of petrified root-wood. The Body absorbs them over the course of a single decade—skin to bark, bone to heartwood, memory to sap. The next Steward to walk that same ridge will sometimes pause, press their palm to the new growth, and smile with faint recognition.

They are not gardeners.

They are the slow, deliberate dreams the Verdant Stair dreams about itself, given legs for a while so they can walk its own immensity and marvel at what it has become.

And they are waiting, with geological patience, for you to decide whether you, too, would like to be remembered by something that will still be breathing long after every human name has turned to moss and amber.
