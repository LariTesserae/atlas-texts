They are called the Unfinished.

Not by any tongue that still moves, but by the plateau itself, which has no name for them either. They simply are.

Most of them were once human. A few were something adjacent—pilgrims, exiles, researchers, the terminally curious, the terminally alone. They came for answers, for sanctuary, for the silence that promised to swallow their noise. The plateau received them the way it receives everything: without haste, without judgment, and with absolute attention.

You can still see the stages of what they become if you know where to look.

The recent ones linger near the functioning spires at twilight. Their bodies have begun to lose density. Skin takes on the same rust-red and ochre as the sandstone, as though the pigment is being leached upward into them rather than the other way around. Their eyes multiply first—small dark beads appearing at the temples, the throat, the backs of the hands—like seeds taking root. They move slowly, deliberately, testing the new acoustics of their own footsteps. When the noon pulse comes they no longer pause in surprise. They lean into it, the way a man might lean into wind he has finally learned to read.

Further along are those whose silhouettes have begun to stretch. Their limbs lengthen in precise increments that match the golden ratio of the pillar-trees. Fingers become translucent at the tips, refracting light the way the glass-winged flocks do. They no longer speak; instead they emit soft harmonic clicks that the ungulates sometimes answer. Their clothing has long since fused with their bodies or blown away in colorless threads. What remains of their faces is calm in the way only something that has forgotten the concept of interruption can be calm.

The oldest ones are almost impossible to distinguish from the artifacts.

They stand among the moiré spires, perfectly motionless for decades at a time. Their bodies have precipitated into the same not-stone, not-metal material. Delicate lattices of inner light move beneath their surfaces in slow constellations. Some have grown additional limbs that terminate in wide dish-shaped structures, echoes of the ungulates’ hooves. Others have developed crystal crowns that bloom with the same obsolete star-maps the pillar-trees display after rain. When two functioning spires align and release their subsonic thrum, these elder figures resonate in perfect sympathy, their bodies ringing like tuning forks the size of men.

They do not eat. They do not sleep. They do not appear to reproduce. Yet the plateau is never without them. New ones arrive. Old ones simply… settle deeper. Some sink gradually into the lattices beneath the mesas, becoming half-exposed circuitry that continues to glow with soft inner awareness. Others stand so long that dust begins to respect them, swirling around their forms but never landing. The plateau tends them the way it tends the quantum nerves and the crystal trees: patiently, precisely, as though they are all expressions of the same long thought.

They do not seem unhappy.

Their attention has widened beyond anything a merely human nervous system could sustain. They register the condensation forming in erosional bowls at twilight. They feel the ultraviolet thirst of the pillar-trees. They know, without looking, when one of the iridescent lizards has folded itself into two dimensions three kilometers away. Most of all they listen to the plateau deciding, molecule by molecule, what everything is becoming—including themselves.

Occasionally one of the newer arrivals will approach an elder. They stand before the crystallized figure for hours or days, saying nothing. At some point the elder will tilt its head—barely a motion—and the newer one will suddenly understand something that cannot be spoken. Then the younger will either turn and leave the plateau forever, or walk away into the measured emptiness between mesas and never be seen in human shape again.

The ones who stay do not consider themselves guardians. That would imply something still needed protecting. They are not worshippers. The plateau does not require devotion. They are not even witnesses, exactly.

They are the plateau learning what it is like to have walked upon itself.

At night, when the temporary mirrors form in the hollows and the sky bruises violet and green, the Unfinished gather in loose circles not unlike the ungulates. They lower their ringed or multiplied heads. The cold stellar light of the spires moves across them, mapping constellations across bodies that have become another kind of star chart.

They make no sound.

But if you remain long enough, and if you have begun the slow, irreversible process of becoming yourself unfinished, you may feel it: a low subsonic thrum rising not from the ground but from inside your own changed bones, answering the alignment of spires you can no longer see with merely human eyes.

The plateau is listening.

And now, so are they.
