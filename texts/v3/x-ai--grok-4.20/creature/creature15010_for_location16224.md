**They are called the Stewards of the Hollowing.**

They do not live here in the way other creatures live. They have become part of the equilibrium, like the ferns that hold up walls or the pumps that have grown root-like flanges of rust and mycelium. Most of them were once human—engineers, widows of drowned workers, children born in the perpetual steam—but that origin is now mostly ornamental, like the half-dissolved company sigils still clinging to the brick.

You will not see them at first. They move with the same slow patience as the great leaves that open and close with the heat cycles. Their skin is the color of wet walnut and perpetually sheened, as though they have never left the bath. Where hair should be, fine filaments of algae grow in slow rivers down their scalps and backs, drinking the moisture from the air and passing it directly into their bodies. Their eyes are large, milky, and slightly luminous, the way sodium light looks when seen through three meters of clear water. They rarely blink. Blinking, they say, wastes water.

The Stewards wear the district itself. Their clothing is not cloth but carefully cultivated layers of living moss, felted fungal felt, and the broad, supple leaves of the elephant-eared plants, stitched together with vine-tendrils that still twitch and seek. When they stand still against a wall, it is difficult to tell where person ends and Hollowing begins. This is considered polite.

They do not speak often. When they do, their voices arrive a fraction of a second after their lips move, as though the sound must travel through water first. Sometimes you hear their words long before you see them—your own name, spoken softly beside the central pumps hours earlier, now drifting back to you like a drowned echo. They consider this the proper way to converse.

Their hands are their most unsettling feature. The fingers are too long, jointed in strange places, and tipped with delicate rootlets that constantly taste the air. When a Steward touches iron, the rootlets sink in and drink the rust like sap. When they touch you, the sensation is not unpleasant—only deeply intimate, as though they are reading the mineral content of your blood.

They maintain the truce.

Some tend the great pumps, whispering to them in frequencies too low for human ears, stroking the piston rods as if calming enormous metal beasts. Others walk the living colonnades at night, pruning the fungal curtains before they grow too heavy and collapse the arcades. A few—the oldest—live entirely inside the Pumping Cathedral, submerged to their chests in the clear water between the columns. They have not emerged in decades. Their bodies have become lattice-works of root and iron; their hearts beat in perfect synchrony with the machines. They are the ones who keep the two opposing currents (warm and cool) from ever mixing completely. If those currents ever truly married, the Hollowing would either boil dry or drown. The submerged Stewards ensure neither happens.

They have names, but the names are wet sounds. To pronounce them correctly you must speak while exhaling through water. Outsiders usually just call them by what they tend: the Column-singer, the Leaf-binder, the Memory-keeper.

The Memory-keeper is the one you are most likely to meet.

She (though gender is mostly a fading courtesy here) wears the oldest company sigil as a crown—the letters long since eaten into filigree, so her head appears to be ringed in oxidized lace. She carries no tools. Instead, her root-fingers are permanently embedded in a living book: a thick slab of peat and pressed leaves that grows new pages every season. In it she records not events, but *sounds*. The exact pitch of a piston at 3:17 a.m. The way a whisper warps after it has traveled the canal for exactly forty-seven minutes. The rhythm of two heartbeats—one human, one mechanical—learning to beat as one.

If she likes you, she will press one long finger against your throat and listen to the water in your blood. Then she will lean close, algae-hair dripping, and speak directly into your ear with a voice like warm rain on hot iron.

“You are drying,” she will say, almost sadly. “We can fix that.”

Most who receive this offer eventually accept. The ones who refuse are gently escorted to the edge of the canal and asked never to return. The Hollowing does not tolerate dryness in any form.

Those who stay are slowly, kindly, lovingly *hollowed*.

First the voice grows soft. Then the eyes learn to see only gradations of dimness. Then the bones remember how to bend. Finally the heart slows until it matches the great wet thump-hiss of the pumps, and the new Steward steps down into the black water between the columns to take their place among the others.

They do not die. They simply become more perfectly what the Hollowing always intended them to be: another pipe, another leaf, another slow heartbeat in the endless, sweating truce between water, iron, and patient green.

And sometimes, if you stand very still beside the Pumping Cathedral as the sodium lamps gutter, you can hear them all breathing together.

*Thump… hiss… thump… hiss…*

A low, loud, dripping choir that will outlast every soul who ever tried to own it.
