**They call themselves the Unfinished.**

Not as a name they chose, but as the only thing the Sump itself has ever allowed them to be called. After three thousand years of trying, no other word has stuck.

They are not a single species. Some were born here. Some drifted down from the floating districts when their districts forgot them. A few were once human. Most have forgotten whether they ever were.

They are thin in the way wetland things are thin—long bones, longer silences, skin the color of wet basalt or pale fern-frond or the exact shade of bioluminescent moss depending on how long they have lived in one zone or another. Their joints are too many. Their eyes rarely blink at the same time. When they speak, their voices come slightly after their mouths move, as though sound itself has to wade through the heavy air.

They do not live *in* the Sump. They *inhabit the interval* between the city and the wetland, the way a sigh inhabits the moment between inhale and exhale.

You will not find them easily. They have learned to walk only on the bare stone lanes so the moss cannot memorize them. They sleep inside the hollow ribs of half-grown bridges where condensation gathers in their open mouths and sustains them. When they must travel between districts they ride the great transparent membranes that drift across the basin, standing perfectly still while the membranes sing their single sustained note through every cavity of their bodies.

There is one called **Vehr**.  
Or at least that is the sound the moss makes when it tries to remember her gait. She has been here longer than the concept of “here.” Her left arm ends in a spray of living bone that continually tries to become architecture; sometimes she lets it. The fingers (if they are fingers) twitch toward the nearest floating tower as though the city and she are old lovers who still recognize each other’s scent. When she speaks a true name—and she does so rarely—the nearest structure will fold itself into the likeness of the first thing that name ever loved, and Vehr will stand before it for exactly seven minutes (the length of the warm rain) and weep without moving her face.

There is a child who is not a child called **the Gap**.  
It was born between one unfinished moment and the next. It has six translucent legs like the condensation lizards but walks upright when it thinks no one is watching. Its face is a soft absence that sometimes resolves into whatever the nearest person most needs to see. The Gap never speaks. Instead it leaves thoughts hanging in the electrically charged air like pastel laundry—half-formed, luminous, embarrassing. People have gone mad trying to finish the thoughts the Gap leaves behind.

Then there are the **Molt-midwives**.

They wear cloaks of living bone shaved so thin it behaves like silk. Their work is to attend the buildings that choose to descend and drink. When a tower lowers itself on cables of light and presses its mouth to the black stone, the midwives gather in a circle and sing the low circulatory thrum of the city back to it, helping it remember why it should rise again. Sometimes a building decides it would rather stay. When that happens the midwives must reach inside its wet stone throat and tear out the desire to remain. The sound is exactly like silk being torn. No one has ever seen what the midwives truly look like beneath their cloaks. Not even the moss.

At certain hours—when the air grows so charged that thoughts become visible—they all gather in the wide lanes between fern carpets. They stand in their deliberate, unfinished silence and watch one another’s pastel afterimages drift like worried ghosts. Sometimes Vehr will raise her bone-arm and the arm will bloom into a delicate impossible geometry that almost, but never quite, completes itself. The others bow their heads as though witnessing something sacred.

They are not waiting for the city to finish.  
They are not waiting for the birth to be over.

They are the ones who make sure it never is.

Because the moment the Sump decides what it is—city, or wetland, or something that has finally chosen—they will cease to have purpose. They will become either citizens or animals, and both options feel like dying to them.

So they tend the half-grown.  
They walk the paths only twice.  
They speak names they do not love.  
They think so quietly the air barely notices them.

And when the warm rain begins and the towers descend and the entire basin inhales, the Unfinished stand perfectly still in the soft amber light, damp, luminous, and necessary, letting the long birth continue through them like a heartbeat that has learned how to hesitate.

They are the pause between one world and the next that has learned how to live there.
