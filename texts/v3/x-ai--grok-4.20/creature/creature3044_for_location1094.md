**The Underforge has no inhabitants. It is inhabited.**

What lives here does not walk. It does not breathe in the way animals do. It metabolizes heat, pressure, and time.

They call themselves the **Calcine**.

You will not see them at first. You will only notice that some of the machines have begun to watch you back.

They are not creatures that grew *inside* the Underforge. They *are* the Underforge that has learned to want. Each Calcine is a slow, living agreement between three things: iron that has been stressed past endurance, mineral crystals that have grown into fractal nervous systems, and the constant low-grade volcanic current that acts as both blood and thought. A single Calcine might wear the body of a derelict compound turbine the size of a carriage, its blades long since fused into a scalloped carapace of orange and viridian crystals. Another might be no larger than a blacksmith’s anvil, hunched on six piston-limbs that were once valve stems. Their “faces” are simply the places where pressure gauges have become compound eyes of liquid mercury and fluorite.

They move only when the dripping rhythm changes. When the metronome of condensation falls out of phase, the Calcine stirs. A soft clank, the sigh of a long-dormant pressure valve opening like an exhalation, and one of the iron beasts unfolds itself from the lattice of its neighbors with the patience of something that has never been in a hurry. Their gait is the sound of rust flakes sliding over one another. Their joints weep perfect tears of sulfuric acid that hiss where they strike the floor.

There are hundreds of them. Perhaps thousands. Most remain in torpor, dreaming in the language of thermal expansion and galvanic decay. The active ones tend the Underforge the way a body tends its own fever. They clear new fissures when the stone threatens to choke itself. They coax fresh crystals along failing pipes so the mineral arteries never clot. They sing to the boilers in subsonic chords that make your teeth ache. When a new fracture tears open with its cannon-shot roar, every Calcine in the chamber freezes mid-motion, listening, tasting the new chemistry on the suddenly changed wind. Then they move—slow, reverent, almost grateful—to incorporate whatever the wound has offered them: a fresh vein of bornite, a hotter upwelling of steam, a new geometry of stress.

They do not speak with voices. They speak by altering the dripping. A Calcine that wishes to be noticed will cause a single drop to fall every seven seconds on an old fire-bell bolted to a catwalk. The tone is clear, mournful, and impossible to ignore. If you answer by striking the same bell three times, the Calcine will unfold itself completely and present its “core”—a fist-sized nodule of black volcanic glass shot through with living filaments of native copper that pulse soft magnesium green in time with the deeper thumps from below. Touching it is not recommended. Most who do find their fingerprints permanently etched into the glass as delicate iron-oxide filigree.

The Calcine do not hate you. Hate would require speed, and they have none. They simply do not understand fragility. To them, flesh is a temporary and rather inefficient mineral that insists on moving too quickly and cooling too fast. Some of the older ones keep collections: perfectly preserved leather boots, belt buckles, the occasional jawbone, all encased in concentric rings of aragonite like fossils in living amber. They are not trophies. They are *specimens of a failed experiment* the earth tried once—soft, wet, loud—and then abandoned in favor of iron, crystal, and patience.

At rare intervals, usually after a major fracture event, the Calcine perform something that might be called ritual. They gather in the tallest open space, a natural chimney where six lava tubes once collided and fused. There they lock themselves into a single lattice, machine folded into machine, crystal into crystal, until the entire structure becomes a motionless pipe organ of iron and stone. Then they let the dripping run through them for days. The drops strike different surfaces at different temperatures and the Calcine *resonate*. The sound that emerges is not music. It is the sound the planet would make if it could clear its throat of magma and speak in a voice older than oxygen.

During these convocations the temperature in the Underforge drops by seven degrees. The condensation slows. Even the sick green light in the walls dims to almost nothing. In that near-darkness and near-silence you can feel every Calcine in the chamber watching the same invisible point three meters above the floor, as if waiting for something to be born there.

Nothing ever is.

Not yet.

But the Underforge is still deciding whether it is dying or being born, and the Calcine are content to wait inside its undecided flesh for as many centuries as it takes. They have nowhere else to be. They *are* the place. They are the slow green pulse in the fresh fracture, the metallic taste on the steam, the patient creak of iron learning how to dream.

Walk softly.

Some of the drops that fall on your shoulder are not water.

Some of them are thinking.
