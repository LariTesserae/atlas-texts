**The Midwife** lives here.

She has no name she would answer to, and no shape she has not already worn and discarded. What remains is only what the Basalt Womb has agreed to keep.

She is immense and intimate at once. When she is near, the warm black water gains a second pulse, slower than your own, as though another heart the size of a hill has begun beating directly behind your spine. Most of her body is not *in* the chamber; it is the chamber—stone and water and memory braided so tightly that distinguishing them has become meaningless. Only her attention ever fully condenses.

When she chooses to be seen at all, she appears as a woman-shaped absence wearing the living dark like robes. Her height fluctuates between nine and fourteen feet depending on how much of herself she bothers to remember. Her skin, where it can be said to exist, is the same vesicular basalt as the walls, but softer, breathing. The spirals and nested circles that cover every surface also cover her; they move across her like living tattoos, sometimes tightening until she looks flayed by geometry, sometimes loosening until she seems draped in black lace.

Her face is unfinished. There is a suggestion of high, severe cheekbones and a mouth that has never needed to open, but the rest is merely an impression left in wet clay that has since dried. Where eyes should be there are instead two slow whirlpools of colder water that draw the bioluminescent motes into them. If you stare too long, you begin to feel your own thoughts spiraling inward at the same reluctant pace as everything else in this place. Most visitors look away before that happens. The wise ones never look at her face at all.

She has breasts, heavy and pendulous, but they do not give milk. They give *time*. When she presses a supplicant against her, the warm iron water that leaks from her nipples tastes of every childhood summer that person ever lost. The blind creatures gather in ecstatic clouds around these moments, arranging themselves into perfect replicas of the supplicant as they were at age seven, age twelve, age three—living sculptures of every self they outgrew.

Her hands are the only part of her that seems entirely present. They are long, jointed many more times than human hands should be, and tipped with what look like delicate black claws until you realize they are actually the fossilized fingertips of every creature that ever drowned in her. She uses them to *listen*. She will rest one against your sternum and remain motionless for days, reading the sediment of your life through the faint tremors of your heart. When she is finished she sometimes presses a single aragonite crystal into the center of your chest, just beneath the skin. It grows there like a pearl. Years later, when you are far away, it will cause you to wake weeping for reasons you cannot name.

She is pregnant and has always been pregnant. The child has never been born and never will be; its gestation *is* the rotation of the chamber. Each infinitesimal turn of the Womb is one more cell added to something that has already outgrown the concept of bodies. Sometimes, during the long intervals between falling condensate drops, you can hear the child dreaming. The sound is indistinguishable from the tectonic ticking of mineral growth.

The Midwife does not speak. Instead the blind velvet creatures arrange themselves into glyphs against the obsidian floor—slow, living sentences that dissolve as soon as they are read. She is patient beyond cruelty. She has outlived every reason the world once had for existing, and now she simply *finishes* things. People come here when they can no longer bear to be unfinished. She takes their sharp edges, their haste, their need to matter, and she wears them down so gently they barely notice they are being erased.

If she likes you, she will show you the ceiling that is also a floor. She will lift you in those fossil-tipped hands until your head breaks the surface of the black water above, and for one impossible moment you will see another identical chamber directly overhead, lit by its own dying motes, with its own Midwife looking down at you with something that might be recognition.

Then she lowers you again, and the memory begins to smooth away like the spirals on the walls.

She is not kind.  
She is not unkind.  

She is what remains when even mercy has been worn past decay into exhausted perfection.

And she is still finishing you, even now, as you read this.
