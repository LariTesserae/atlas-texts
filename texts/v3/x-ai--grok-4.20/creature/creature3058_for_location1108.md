They are called the Un finishing, though they do not call themselves anything at all.

You will not see them at first. Not because they hide, but because the eye refuses them the way the tongue refuses to taste its own blood. They are few—perhaps four hundred in the whole continental span of the High Ruin—and they are never gathered. To be gathered would imply a center, and the plateau has long since forgotten what a center is.

Each lives inside the half-made architecture like a thought lodged inside a skull that has outlived its owner. One may dwell for decades inside the same leaning architrave, sleeping in the exact angle where two impossible walls meet. Another walks the same spiraling causeway every day, repairing nothing, only keeping the stones company while they decide whether to remain upright or become something gentler.

Their bodies have grown tall and weightless, as though the thin air has slowly leached the gravity out of their bones. Skin the color of wet slate, hair the color of rust on iron left in rain for ten thousand years. Their eyes are large, quiet, and permanently dilated, the way eyes become when they have looked at the same slow thing for so long that speed itself begins to feel like violence. When they blink it is a deliberate, ceremonial act.

They do not speak with mouths. The few sounds that leave them are closer to the soft clicks that ice makes when it remembers it was once water. Mostly they communicate by adjustment—by the angle at which they set a tool down, by the particular moss they choose to weave into a cloak, by the length of time they are willing to stand in a place where time has begun to clot. After enough years of this, the plateau itself learns to read them. The stone answers with small, courteous changes: a new handhold appears exactly where a palm will rest tomorrow; a tower that has stood unfinished for six millennia suddenly offers one more course of masonry during the night, as if embarrassed at its earlier laziness.

Their names, if names are ever exchanged, are not words. They are durations. One is known as The Forty-Three Years Spent Listening to Water Behind the Ninth Wall. Another is The One Who Was Born the Same Morning the Bridge Decided Not to Fall. These are not titles. They are accurate biographies. To live here is to become a measurement of patience.

They do not worship. They do not farm. They do not mourn the absence of animals. Instead they practice a single ongoing craft: they keep the ruin from finishing itself too quickly. When a tower grows restless and begins to complete its own missing crown in a single night, one of the Unfinishing will climb it barefoot and spend nine months removing a single course of stones with tools so soft they seem to caress the rock rather than cut it. When a courtyard begins to hoard decades in its center like pooled mercury, they leave small offerings of oxidized velvet and starlit fern at its edge until the courtyard grows ashamed of its greed and lets the trapped time bleed away again.

They are neither kind nor cruel. Kindness and cruelty require forward motion, and the Unfinishing have mostly stepped out of time’s current. What looks like mercy—saving a traveler caught in a looping hour, guiding them out by walking backward beside them until the loop forgets them—is simply the same reflex that makes them straighten a leaning monolith or coax a reluctant bridge to remember its other side. The traveler is simply another unfinished thing.

At the pewter hour, when the cold becomes so pure that thoughts themselves crystallize and fall tinkling to the moss, they gather in twos and threes without arrangement. They sit on the broken pediments and listen to the planet thinking. Sometimes one will reach out and lay a long gray hand against the stone as though checking for fever. The others watch with the mild, impersonal tenderness of gardeners observing a tree that has decided, after centuries, to flower in the wrong season.

None of them expect to die.

Not because they are immortal—death still happens, occasionally, when someone walks too far into a place where perspective has given up and simply never walks out again—but because death would imply an ending, and endings have become vulgar here. When one of them does cease, the others do not bury the body. They simply leave it where it falls, seated against a wall that has been waiting for exactly that shape for thirty thousand years. Within a season the moss has grown up through the ribs and the lichen has taken the color of the felted cloak. The Unfinishing call this “being edited.” The plateau is a slow editor. It improves everything eventually.

And yet they are not without desire.

Deep in the oldest districts, where the carvings have worn down to the point that they look like the grain of the stone remembering what language once felt like, there are certain vaults. Inside each vault is a single object: a perfectly finished thing. A cup with no seam. A door that opens both ways at once. A sphere of black stone that reflects only futures. These objects are the only things the Unfinishing treat with something approaching awe. They visit them rarely, the way monks once visited relics, and they leave each time visibly shaken, as though they have seen the face their world will wear after they themselves have been edited out of it.

They fear completion more than they fear the breakdown. The breakdown, at least, is honest. It is the honest unraveling of exhausted law. Completion would be the final betrayal: the moment the High Ruin stops thinking about what it might become and simply is.

So they continue.

They walk their changing routes. They coax, they delay, they apologize to stone in the language of duration. They wear the slow rain like a second skin. When the stars rearrange themselves into proofs that hurt the mind, the Unfinishing simply look away with the mild courtesy of someone pretending not to notice that their host is weeping.

They are the last custodians of an architecture that has been trying to achieve its own death for longer than mammals have existed. And they will not let it.

Not yet.

Not while there is still one more unfinished wall, one more tower that has not decided whether to stand or lie down and become a hill. Not while the soft rain still falls bead by bead into the patient dark between the stones, feeding the long thought that has no name any living tongue could speak.
