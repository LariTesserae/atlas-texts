**The Rememberers** live here.

They have no faces you would recognize as faces. What they wear instead is the slow argument itself, grown into and through their bodies until it is impossible to say where the argument ends and the creature begins.

There are perhaps nine thousand of them. Or nine. Time is elastic in the Throat, and population is measured in geological consent rather than bodies. Some have stood in the same hexagonal fracture for four hundred years without moving their feet; mosses have bridged the gaps between their knees, and the iron arteries of the world have grown delicate glass tendrils through the hollows of their collarbones. Others drift with the membranous herds, lashed to the creatures’ sails by living cables of their own nervous tissue, humming the same decade-long chord so that the herds never forget the melody.

They are not native. Nothing here is native. The Throat does not permit natives; it only permits immigrants who agree to forget where they came from.

The oldest among them is called (by those who still bother with names) **Mother-of-Pipes**. She was once something like a woman, though the word has rusted off her long ago. Her ribcage has become a lattice of black crystal and living reed; when the great low note moves across the shelf she resonates with it until her teeth — the few that remain — chime like bells. Steam from the world’s own arteries condenses on her shoulders and runs down her back in mercury rivulets that never quite reach the ground. She has not blinked in three centuries. There is no need. The landscape blinks for her.

The Rememberers do not speak with voices. They speak with weather.

When one of them wishes to convey sorrow, a particular species of sapphire moss on their arms releases a chemical that changes the fluorescence of the night air for seven miles around, turning every guilty shadow the color of old grief. When they are amused, the silica swarms thicken in the air until the sunlight fractures into a private rainbow that only the intended recipient can see. Love is expressed by allowing one’s own body to be slowly colonized by the other’s favorite lichen until the two of them become a single hanging garden swaying between ridges.

They do not eat. They *negotiate*. A Rememberer will stand for weeks in front of a six-legged obsidian tortoise, forehead pressed to its mossy brow, trading slow thoughts and colonies of bacteria until the creature yields a single bead of nutrient-rich lymph. The exchange is so courteous it looks like prayer.

The youngest Rememberer is barely two hundred years old and still retains the vague outline of a human shape beneath the accretions. They call him **Last-to-Forget**, though he has already forgotten his original name and most of the reasons he once had for needing one. His skin is still soft enough to bruise when the tubular reeds scream. He wanders the mirror-pools at the times when both suns are hidden, staring at his reflection as though it might suddenly remember something he has lost. The pools are patient with him. They show him versions of himself that never quite happened — a man with different eyes, a woman with his mouth, a child made of frozen mist — and he thanks them by bleeding a little into their perfect round surfaces. The blood becomes new singing worms by morning.

At certain convergences — when the two suns and the planet’s slow tectonic heartbeat align — all the Rememberers who can still move gather on the highest glassy ridge. They press their bodies together until they form a single living ridge of flesh and crystal and pipe and moss. Then they sing the world back into memory.

The note is so low it registers first in the iron arteries buried in the crust. The pipes answer. The silica swarms rise in glittering pillars. The membranous herds drift into formation like slow applause. Even the vegetation stops screaming for seven full minutes, which is the closest thing to silence the Throat has ever known.

In that silence the planet remembers that it is old.

And the Rememberers remember that they are the planet’s tongue — the last wet syllable still being shaped, still being tasted, still refusing to be finished.

They will stay until the argument is over.

They will stay long after.

Because the Throat That Remembers Snow does not want witnesses.

It wants co-authors.
