**They are called the Slow Choir.**

Not a tribe, not a people in any surface sense. They are the ones who learned how to breathe in the same rhythm as the city’s settling. Most surface scholars would call them human, but only in the way a fossil is still the shape of what once lived. Their bodies have lengthened over uncountable generations; limbs slightly too long, necks slightly too flexible, skin the same pale, translucent gray as wet limestone. When they stand still they are almost impossible to distinguish from the mineral draperies around them.

They do not speak with voices. Their language is mostly pressure and pause. A hand placed against a wall at the exact moment a groan travels through the stone. A shared exhalation timed to the ringing of a water-drop. When they do use sound it is low, melodic, and rare—like someone humming inside a bell. The name “Slow Choir” is what they allow outsiders to call them, because it is the closest surface words can come to describing how they sing with the city’s own slow violence.

Every adult carries a tuning shard: a fist-sized piece of living selenite grown inside their own body, usually just beneath the sternum. It is not jewelry. It is an organ. When the city begins to rearrange itself, the shard vibrates in sympathetic resonance, telling the bearer which direction the next shift will travel. The most respected among them can read the difference between a tower that will slump in decades and one that will avalanche in days.

They do not have leaders. They have listeners.

The greatest listener of any generation is simply called the Echo. The current Echo is a woman named Veyr whose skin has begun to grow tiny selenite crystals along her spine, like a slow mineral sunrise. She has not spoken aloud in forty years. It is said she no longer needs to; the city speaks through the tuning shard in her chest and she answers by choosing where the Choir will sleep that season. They follow her the way glass eels follow condensation currents.

Their shelters are never built twice. When they arrive at a new ledge or basin they weave temporary lattices of bone, crystal fiber, and braided fungal silk—structures so light that when the floor tilts, the entire camp simply lifts, slides, and is gently deposited elsewhere like a leaf on moving water. The moment a scaffold begins to feel permanent they burn it. Permanence is a kind of insult here. The Underbright taught them that lesson with the bones of their ancestors.

They keep no written history. Instead they keep resonance maps—immense, room-sized tapestries of crystal thread and beetle-carved stone beads that hang in the tallest vaults. Each bead represents a major shift the city has made. The threads record how the Slow Choir moved in answer. Reading one is less like studying history and more like listening to an earthquake remember itself in music.

The Choir does not worship the Underbright. They consider worship a form of resistance, an attempt to stand outside the conversation. Instead they participate. When a district folds, they are there to help it fold more gracefully, prying loose dangerous overhangs with their iron tools, guiding glass-eel migrations so the iridescent trails mark safe new paths. When a new chamber is extruded from the living rock they are the first to enter it, singing it into usefulness before it can decide to become a tomb.

Children are born in the iris hours—those brief moments when a plaza suddenly opens into a lower layer. The newborn is placed against the fresh stone so the first thing it feels is the city’s pulse. If the infant cries, the Choir considers it a good omen: the child has already learned to argue with the world. If it is silent, they worry. Silence can mean harmony, or it can mean the child is already halfway to becoming part of the mineral skin.

They are not immortal, but they die so slowly that death itself seems to have adopted the local tempo. When one of them finally lies down for the last time, the Choir does not bury them. They simply leave the body where it chose to rest. Within a year the corpse will be completely incorporated—first by the glassy fungi, then by the restless limestone. The tuning shard is harvested from the sternum and passed to the next singer strong enough to carry it. In this way the Choir remains only ever one generation deep, and yet older than continents.

At any given time there are perhaps four hundred of them scattered across a buried city the size of a mountain range. They may not see one another for decades. Yet every time two groups meet, they spend three full days in absolute silence, simply pressing their tuning shards together so the city can tell each half what the other half has learned while they were apart.

If you stand on the pale elephant-hide limestone long enough, and if you are very still, you may eventually notice them watching you. Not with hostility. Not with curiosity. They watch the way the glass eels watch: as one more moving current in the endless mineral conversation. Sometimes, if the city is calm and your breathing falls into the correct rhythm, one of them will step out of a shadow that was not a shadow a moment earlier. They will place two fingers against your sternum, just where a tuning shard would sit, and listen.

Then they will either walk away without a word, or they will begin to hum the next note of the city’s long, flattening song.

And if you are very lucky—or very unlucky—they will invite you to hum it with them.
