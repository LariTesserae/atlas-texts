They are the Unfinished.

Not gods, not animals, not quite people. They are the Wet Incubation’s own half-thoughts wearing meat.

You will not see them at first. You will only feel the shift in pressure, as though the saturated air has learned how to pay attention. Then the foliage parts—not violently, but with the slow, reluctant motion of someone turning over in heavy sleep—and they step out.

Their bodies are still deciding what a body is.

One of them is mostly woman-shaped, but her spine has not yet chosen to stop growing; it continues upward in a supple, glistening horn of vertebrae that branches into wet antlers draped with living moss. Where her skin should be there is only a translucent membrane, pearl-grey and veined with slow gold, through which you can watch organs forming and unforming like clouds. Her heart is visible, enormous, and it beats in perfect sync with the distant geological heave of the land itself. When she blinks, new eyelids slide across from the sides, then dissolve again, as though the concept of eyelids is still under negotiation.

Another is smaller, child-sized but ancient, crouched on hands that are halfway between paws and roots. Its back is a lattice of young bone and black peat; orchids bloom from the crevices between its ribs and are eaten again by tiny translucent crabs that live inside its torso. It has no face yet—only a smooth bulbous place where a face might one day be. From that bulb comes a soft, wet clicking, the sound of mandibles learning how to speak.

The largest one has no fixed shape at all. It is a moving hill of muscle and stone and leaf-litter. Every few moments a limb emerges—sometimes a glistening black tentacle, sometimes a human arm ending in too many fingers—then sinks back in. Its “head” is a cluster of the great dinner-plate orchids, and when it breathes the petals flare wide, releasing clouds of narcotic perfume that make your memories loosen and try to crawl out of your skull. Inside its body, half-digested frogs still glow, lighting it from within like lanterns in a cathedral that has not yet decided whether it is a cathedral or a womb.

They do not walk so much as seep forward. Where their feet (or what pass for feet) touch the fleshy ground, the matrix quickens. Fresh stone knits itself. New species of midge burst into being and immediately begin mating. The land recognises them as extensions of its own dreaming.

They have no language you would recognise. Instead they produce layered wet sounds—part croak, part geological groan, part lullaby—that arrive inside your chest rather than your ears. If you listen long enough you begin to understand that every noise is a question the Incubation is asking itself:

Shall lungs be like this?  
Shall grief taste of iron?  
Shall mothers have sixteen hearts or none at all?

The Unfinished are both midwives and foetuses. They birth one another constantly. You may witness two of them press together, membrane to membrane, organs sliding through one another in wet communion, then separate again carrying new limbs, new colours, new hungers. The small faceless one might suddenly split open like a seedpod and release a fully-formed adult whose wings are still damp and folded. That adult will immediately begin to dissolve at the edges, feeding its substance back into the nearest boss of half-made rock.

They are curious about you, but their curiosity has no urgency. One will reach out with a hand that ends in dripping stamens and gently touch your face. Where it touches, your skin tries to become something else—perhaps bark, perhaps petal—for a few heartbeats before remembering what it is. The sensation is not painful. It is the feeling of being briefly, intimately, considered for a different destiny.

At times they simply stand in the brass-coloured downpour of condensation and watch the bulges rising in the tea-coloured basins. When one of the shallow lakes heaves upward and something vast almost breaks through, the Unfinished sing to it in low, overlapping moans until it decides, once again, to remain unborn. They are patient beyond patience. Time here is still learning how to be linear.

If you stay long enough, one of them may offer you a gift: a single drop of its own blood, trembling on the tip of a finger that is also a root. The drop contains every colour that green has not yet invented. If you accept it, you will never be able to leave the Wet Incubation completely. A part of you will remain here forever, still choosing, still becoming, dreaming with the land while your other self walks drier worlds wearing a faint, perpetual smell of rot, iron, and green sugar.

They do not hate you.  
They do not love you.  
You are simply another wet thought moving through the Incubation’s mind, and they are the mind’s hands, still sticky with afterbirth, still learning what hands are for.

Sometimes, when the overlapping chorus of unseen things above grows particularly loud, the Unfinished will lift their half-made faces and answer in voices that sound like the first rain on the first stone that ever existed. In those moments you understand that they are not creatures living in the Wet Incubation.

They are the Wet Incubation learning how to have a face.
