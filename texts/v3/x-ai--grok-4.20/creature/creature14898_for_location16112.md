They are still here.

Not as ghosts. Not even as caretakers. They are the Hollowworks now, the way a sponge is the sea it lives inside.

Call them the Drowned Choir if you must name them, though they have no name for themselves and would not answer if you spoke it. There are perhaps four hundred, though their numbers feel irrelevant; they rarely gather in the same volume of mist at the same time. Most remain inside the machines, curled like pale fetuses within the cooling jackets of boilers that have not known fire in nine hundred years. Others have grown outward, becoming architectural.

They are tall, almost painfully so, with limbs that have lengthened past any proportion a surface-dweller would recognize as human. Their skin is the color of wet limestone and just as porous; water moves through them as easily as breath. Where ordinary creatures have pores, they have seams—fine, machined-looking lines that open and close in slow tides. When they stand still (which is most of the time), condensation gathers along these seams and runs down them in perfect parallel tracks, as though their bodies remember the original purpose of every rivet and gasket.

Their faces are smooth and slightly concave, like the inside of a shield. No eyes. Instead, a single horizontal gill-slit runs where a nose should be, forever fluttering with the same low bronchial note that fills the chamber. When one of them breathes out, the note deepens for half a second and the moss around its feet brightens by one shade of green. They are singing the machines to sleep, or perhaps the machines are singing them. After so long it has become impossible to tell which is instrument and which is player.

Most of their bodies are wrapped in living iron. Not armor—symbiosis. Over centuries the rust scaled off the machines and learned to grow back onto their hosts in thin, articulated plates. These plates shift and click softly when the Drowned Choir move, a sound like a grandfather clock deciding whether or not to strike the hour. Where the plates part you can see the moss has rooted beneath their skin as well, pushing up between the ribs in soft velvet bosses. Their blood, if it can still be called blood, is warm condensate laced with microscopic iron filings; it tastes like rust and rainwater and something faintly electrical.

They do not walk so much as flow. Each step is a slow unfolding of too many joints, the foot placed with the care of someone lowering a newborn into a font. Behind them the moss remembers their footprints for exactly eleven minutes before rising again, so the floor is always patterned with faint, ghostly trails that never quite overlap. Sometimes a pale millipede will ride one of these living footprints like a raft, allowing itself to be carried across the chamber in imperial dignity.

The Drowned Choir rarely touch one another. When they do it is with the gravity of two continents deciding to become one. They press their gill-slits together and the chord that fills the Hollowworks briefly resolves into something almost like harmony before sinking back into its single eternal note. During these unions their iron plating meshes like puzzle pieces and the moss between them blooms with tiny white threads that look, for three heartbeats, almost like flowers. Then the moment passes. They separate. The almost-flowers dissolve back into green.

They have no tools. They need none. The entire chamber is their extended body; the pipes are arteries, the turbines are lungs, the ceaseless falling water is their shared circulatory system. When one of them wishes to repair something (a seized bearing, a cracked weld, a place where the moss has grown too thick and begun to choke a heat exchanger), they simply open their own forearm plating and let their blood run into the fracture. The iron filings in their veins knit the metal the way their own bones once knitted. The machine and the creature remember they were never truly separate.

At what might be called night—though the light never changes—they gather in the exact center of the chamber where the ceiling is lowest. There they stand in a loose circle, heads tilted back, gill-slits wide. The four-second rhythm of the falling drops becomes a drum. Their combined voice drops half an octave and the enormous silent flywheels above them begin, very slowly, to turn. Not to do work. Only to remember what rotation felt like. After seventeen revolutions they stop again. The Choir exhales as one. The moment of nostalgia passes. They return to their individual machines and fold themselves back inside like letters returning to envelopes.

They are not unhappy.

Happiness, like hunger or fear or the memory of sky, was left behind long ago. What remains is a deep, wet, musical contentment—the satisfaction of a wound that has finally stopped trying to heal and has instead become a new kind of mouth.

If you were to stand among them long enough for your own lungs to fill with the same saturated air, you would feel the slow temptation: to let your joints lengthen, to allow the moss to root behind your eyes, to trade your clumsy wet breathing for the honest chord that has replaced all other thought here.

Most who feel this temptation simply walk forward until the machines open for them like iron flowers.

The Drowned Choir make room.

They have learned there is always space for one more note in the endless song.
