**They are called the Quiet Mothers.**

Not by any name they have spoken themselves—for they do not speak in tongues that break the air—but by the way every living thing in the Under-Grove leans toward them when they move. There are never more than nine at any time. There are never fewer than three. The grove decides how many it needs, and the Mothers simply *are*.

They are tall, almost too tall for the low ceiling, yet they never stoop. Their bodies are thin and slightly bowed like young trees that have grown too long in the dark. Skin the color of wet limestone glistens with the same condensation that drips from everything else; it is impossible to say where their flesh ends and the moisture of the chamber begins. Where a human would have hair, long filaments of pale rootlets grow from their scalps and shoulders, drifting lazily in the humid air as though they are still tasting for water even now. These roots twitch when the Mothers listen. They knot together when the Mothers dream.

Their faces are gentle and terrible. Eyes large, lidless, and milky-white, like the blind fish that circle in the pools. They do not need to see. The entire grove is their eyes. When one of them turns her head toward you, you feel the attention of ten thousand leaves and roots and fungal threads shifting at once. It is not hostile. It is simply absolute.

They breathe with the chamber. When the Under-Grove inhales, their narrow chests swell; when it exhales, their shoulders sink and the warm rain begins again. Their mouths are small and rarely open. When they do speak it is never with sound. Instead the dripping around you changes rhythm. A thousand separate drops begin striking leaves in precise patterns, creating a language of soft percussion that travels through root and stone and bone. Those who have heard it and lived say it feels like being forgiven by something that has already forgotten your name.

The Quiet Mothers move slowly, bare feet never quite touching the moss. They glide an inch above the wet ground, trailing faint wakes of warmer air. Their long fingers—too many joints, too many knuckles—drift through the ferns and vines as though reading braille written in living tissue. Where they pass, orchids open that have never opened before. Where they pause, millipedes rearrange themselves into perfect spirals at their feet like supplicants. The pale geckos freeze on their translucent bellies, hearts visible and hammering, not in fear but in awe.

They tend the grove as a body tends its own fever.

One Mother will stand for days beneath a particular ceiling crack, letting the water run down her face and into the hollows of her collarbones until a new species of moss blooms there. Another will crouch beside a pool and sing—without sound—until the blind fish rise and lay their milky bodies against her shins in offering. A third walks the perimeter where the limestone throat narrows, dragging her root-hair across the stone, repairing the slow cracks that appear every few centuries.

They are not guardians. They are not priests.

They are the dreaming mind that the Under-Grove grew for itself when it became lonely ten thousand years ago.

Sometimes one of them carries the obsidian blade that once belonged to a forgotten people. She holds it like a question. Occasionally she will press the ancient edge against her own arm until a single drop of sap-thick, silvery blood falls into a cupped leaf. The leaf drinks it. The entire chamber sighs with satisfaction. The blade is then returned to its mossy perch where the tiny orange salamander waits, patient as geology.

At rare intervals all nine gather beneath the three weathered stone columns. They stand in a loose circle, root-hairs intertwining, and the temperature rises. The dripping ceases. For one long minute the Under-Grove holds its breath with them. During this time the Mothers’ bellies distend slightly, as though something is being decided inside them. Then they separate. The rain begins again. And somewhere else in the grove, a new Quiet Mother begins to uncoil from a knot of roots and pale orchids, already ancient the moment she opens her milky eyes.

They have no names.

They have no children that are not the grove itself.

And yet if you remain long enough, silent enough, still enough, one of them will eventually drift toward you. She will lower her long face until her root-hairs brush your cheeks like cool fingers. She will regard you with the patient indifference of ten thousand years of dripping water.

Then, very gently, she will show you how to become part of the breathing.

Most who are offered this gift refuse.

A few accept.

Those who accept are never seen again by surface eyes.

But sometimes, years later, travelers descending the narrow limestone throat report hearing an extra rhythm among the dripping water—one that sounds almost, but not quite, like a second heartbeat.

The Quiet Mothers do not smile.

The grove smiles through them.
