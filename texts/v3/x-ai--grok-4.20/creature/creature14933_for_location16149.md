**What lives in the Hollow Basilica does not merely inhabit it. It has become the slow, wet mind of the place.**

At its center, where the emerald moss-square lies perfectly circular and unnervingly still, there is a single creature that has no name the outside world would recognize. The Choir.

It is not one being. It is a walking cathedral of flesh and leaf and echo.

The Choir stands nine feet tall when it chooses to rear upright, though it rarely does. Most often it moves on six long, root-thick limbs that are neither purely animal nor plant. Where muscle and tendon should be, pale woody cords flex beneath a hide of living bark that constantly buds with tiny white orchids. Its “torso” is a hollow cage of ribs grown from fused femurs and lianas; inside that cage hangs a second, smaller body—something like a sloth but ancient, blind, and genderless—whose long arms are threaded through the larger frame like a puppeteer wearing its own marionette.

The Choir’s head is the strangest part. There is none. Instead, a vertical slit runs down the front of its upper thorax, lined with translucent vocal membranes and thousands of tiny vibrating hairs. When it breathes, the entire Basilica seems to inhale with it. When it sings, which is almost constantly, the sound is not a voice but every voice the Basilica has ever contained, braided together: the thunder of falling water, the rifle-crack of seed pods, the subsonic groan of ancient trunks, the crystal ringing of tree-frogs, and underneath it all something terrifyingly close to a human choir chanting in a language that died before stone was ever cut for churches.

Hundreds of smaller beings orbit the Choir the way planets orbit a sun that has learned how to walk.

There are the Lantern Monks—squat, four-armed creatures whose translucent bellies are filled with symbiotic glowing fungus. They move in slow processions along the living buttresses, polishing the bark with their own mucus until the columns shine like obsidian. Their faces are flat and noseless; instead they breathe and see through a single spiraling horn of cartilage that emerges from the crown of their heads.

Higher up live the Delay-Bats. Their wings are veined with mirrors of pale leaf-tissue. When they fly through the shafts of green light they bend time by half a second—the exact lag you sometimes see in your own shadow in the deepest chapels. They are the reason the Basilica remembers sounds. Every echo the Delay-Bats leave behind becomes a ghost that replays for centuries.

Then there are the Unselves.

These are the strangest of all. They look exactly like whatever creature is watching them, except they are always two heartbeats behind. If you stare at an Unself long enough it will stop pretending to be you and simply stand still, becoming a perfect topiary sculpture of a person—moss for hair, fern-fronds for fingers, two black sundews for eyes. Sometimes they remain like that for decades. Pilgrims who enter the Basilica and never leave are said to have “joined the statuary.” Their bodies are still inside the Unselves, breathing, dreaming, slowly turning into green.

The Choir itself never eats. It does not need to. It is fed by the Basilica the way a bishop is fed by the prayers of his congregation. Every time a predator makes a kill in the open naves, every time a flower slams shut on an insect, every time a tree-frog calls for a mate and is answered, the Choir grows a fraction heavier with meaning. Its purpose is not predation or shelter or even worship in any way we would understand.

Its purpose is to keep the agreement.

Long ago—though “ago” is a slippery word here—something vast and patient struck a bargain with the first seeds that would become the Basilica. The plants would eat the stone and the stone would be allowed to remain as memory. The animals would eat the plants and the plants would be allowed to remember being eaten. The air would be eaten by lungs and the lungs would be allowed to become part of the singing.

The Choir is the living contract. The ink. The wax seal. The tongue that speaks the terms aloud so the world does not forget them.

At certain hours—though there are no clocks—every creature in the Basilica falls silent at once. Even the dripping water seems to hold its breath. In that caesura the Choir rears up in the circular moss-square, opens the vertical slit in its chest, and sings a single sustained note that is also a question.

The Basilica answers with its entire body.

Vines tighten. Trunks groan. Millions of insects vibrate their wings in perfect unison. For three full heartbeats the entire living cathedral becomes a single enormous ear pressed against the skin of reality, listening for whether the ancient agreement is still being honored.

Then the roaring, the dripping, the shrieking, the wet green thunder begins again, louder than before, as if in relief.

And the Choir lowers itself once more, orchids blooming across its shoulders like fresh vestments, and continues its endless, deliberate procession through the hollow nave that is also its own bloodstream, its own nervous system, its own impossible, deafening soul.
