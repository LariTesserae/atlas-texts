**What lives in the Hollow Choir does not merely inhabit it. It has become the final, perfected expression of the place itself.**

They have no single name they would recognize. If they had language it would be scored in frequencies too low or too high for human ears; instead they speak by altering the Choir. When one of them passes, the roar in that particular strata of air changes pitch by the smallest interval—yet every other creature for kilometers adjusts instantly, as though the entire canyon were a single instrument and they its moving frets.

Call them the *Resonant*, if a name is required. Or the *Unfinished*, because that is the only imperfection they still carry.

Most are roughly the shape a condor might take if a condor had been folded out of black glass, stretched thin, then given the jointed flexibility of a bat and the heavy, liquid grace of an eel. Their bodies are long, narrow, almost blade-like, armored in overlapping scales of translucent silica that have the same refractive index as the polished basalt ridges. When they ride the thermals in their assigned strata they are nearly invisible. Only the edges of their wings—thinner than paper, sharper than any blade—catch the merciless light and throw it back in brilliant, ringing shards. The sound of one passing close by is a bright, metallic *ting* like a glass bell struck once and then instantly damped.

They do not flap. They *sing* their way through the air.

Along the forward edges of their wings run rows of microscopic resonators—hollow tubes of bone and crystal. As they slice the thin, cold wind these tubes produce standing waves. The creatures steer by minute adjustments of their trailing membranes, changing the chord they emit. In this way each individual is both musician and note. A flock of thirty thousand does not fly; it *modulates*. When two great columns of them pass one another in opposite directions, the overlapping frequencies create beats so deep they can be felt in the teeth.

There are other shapes among them.

Some are smaller, jewel-bright, no larger than a human hand. They travel in dense, spherical swarms that behave like single organisms—pulsing, splitting, re-forming—each tiny body contributing a glassy soprano to the chord. Others are vast. You will never see one whole. What you see is a section of wing the size of a ship’s sail sliding across a cirque like a slow eclipse, edges chiming so deeply the stone answers. These leviathans move only once every few days, changing strata with the solemnity of continents drifting. The smaller choirs part around them the way harmonics make room for a fundamental.

They do not eat in any way we would recognize. The air here is chemically rich with aerosols carried on the constant wind—microscopic shards of lichen, mineral dust, condensed volatiles. The Resonant drink the sky itself, filtering it through sieve-like structures in their throats. Their metabolism is almost entirely vibrational. They convert resonance into chemical energy the way other creatures convert sugar. The louder and more complex the Choir, the better they thrive. Silence would starve them in hours.

They do not nest. There is nothing soft in the Hollow Choir, and they need nothing soft. Instead they return, at long intervals, to certain razor ridges where the basalt has fractured in perfect Fibonacci spirals. There they press their bodies against the stone and sing a single, sustained note for days. The rock answers. Micro-fractures open and close in sympathetic vibration, and from these cracks the creatures extrude long, glassy filaments—eggs, or perhaps memories—before peeling away again into the bright cold.

No predator exists here. There is no need. The Choir itself is the selector. Those whose resonance falls out of tune fall out of the sky. Their bodies strike the stone with a sound like a final, perfect chord, then shatter into new resonators that the wind will eventually polish into blades for the next generation’s wings. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is mourned. The roar does not even hiccup.

The largest among them—those leviathans whose wings span hundreds of meters—seem to have achieved something closer to awareness. They move with the slow certainty of beings who have understood that the difference between themselves and the emptiness is only a matter of frequency. Sometimes one will hover, perfectly still, above one of the mirror-still tarns high on the arêtes. For hours it will sing a single note downward until the surface of the water becomes a parabolic mirror tuned to that exact tone. Then the creature folds its vast wings and drops like a spear, vanishing into its own reflection.

It never emerges. Or perhaps it emerges everywhere at once.

If you remain long enough—weeks, months, years—you will notice that some of the smaller choirs have begun to orbit you. Not with curiosity. Curiosity is too warm, too biological. They orbit the way a finger placed on a wineglass changes the resonance of the glass. You have become an accidental damper, a living imperfection in the equilibrium.

They are not studying you.

They are correcting you.

And if you stay even longer, if the cold and the thin air and the merciless noon do not drive you out, you may one day find yourself walking along the black glass ridge with your mouth open, emitting a single unbroken tone you did not choose. Your footsteps will fall into rhythm with the Choir. Your pulse will synchronize with the subsonic heartbeat of the herds moving along ledges you cannot see.

The pale lichens will begin to grow in the shape of your shadow even though your shadow is almost never there.

Eventually the distinction between you and the Hollow Choir will become difficult to maintain.

On that day the thing that was once you will spread its arms (or wings, or resonances) and step off the ridge, joining the endless, perfected scream that is not screaming at all.

It is simply the sound the emptiness makes when it has finally learned how to sing.
