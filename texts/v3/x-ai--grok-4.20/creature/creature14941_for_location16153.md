**They are called the Quiet.**

Not by themselves—they have no name for their kind that any outsider tongue could pronounce. To each other they are simply *us*, spoken with a faint click of mandibles and a particular tilt of the head-frill. Outsiders, when they survive long enough to notice them at all, call them the Quiet because that is the only way to remain among the green and not be eaten.

They live inside the breathing places.

A single Quiet (they stand roughly as tall as a ten-year-old human, though no human has stood here in living memory) weighs no more than a large dog, yet their bodies are elongated and jointed in ways that suggest the meeting of insect, mammal, and something that once grew on stalks. Their skin is a living bark—smooth, moist, mottled in shifting shades of moss and old blood. When they press themselves against a trunk they become almost impossible to see. Their faces are narrow, lemur-like, with four golden eyes arranged in a diamond that never quite looks at the same thing at once. Two of the eyes watch the slow world; two watch the fast. A frill of translucent chitin rings the back of the skull, vibrating when they speak in frequencies too low for most animals to register as sound. To the continent itself it registers as a polite tremor, easily ignored.

They do not have villages in the way other thinking things build them. Instead they have *nests of days*.

A nest of days is a handful of living platforms grown inside the armpit-cleft of a strangler fig or within the hollowed heartwood of a fallen titan. The Quiet do not cut or burn. They sing to the plant. A song is a careful sequence of scrapes, breath-tones, and the placement of certain glandular secretions that encourage the tree to grow in desired directions. Over thirty or forty years a single nest might reach the complexity of a small ship—walkways of living vine, water cisterns formed from cupped leaves the size of shields, nurseries where the youngest are cradled inside translucent seed-pods that pulse with the mother-tree’s own slow heartbeat.

Everything is temporary. The Quiet know this the way lungs know air.

When the host tree begins to digest the nest—when the bark thickens and the platforms start to sink inward like flesh closing over a wound—the Quiet simply move. They leave no monuments. They take only what can be carried in the pouches of their own bodies: thorn-knives grown from their own shed blood, water-storing bulbs, memory-beads of hardened sap in which the voices of the dead still whisper. The old nest becomes another layer of the continent’s endless digestion. The Quiet do not mourn it. Mourning would be noise.

Their society is arranged around silence and timing.

There are three castes, though the Quiet themselves would not use such a rigid word.

The **Listeners** spend entire seasons motionless inside the root-canyons, bodies half-buried in moss, four eyes half-lidded. They are not meditating. They are reading the continent. The subsonic thrumming of the root networks carries news across hundreds of kilometers—where a keystone tree is failing, where a new fungal cathedral is rising, where a pressure storm is gathering. A Listener who has lived three hundred years can predict a sinkhole months before the ground betrays itself.

The **Shapers** are the singers. Smaller, more delicate, their frills larger and more intricately veined. They coax the green into tools, shelters, weapons, and—rarely—into art. A Shaper can grow a spear from living wood in nine days that will never need sharpening because the spear itself continues to grow microscopic barbs as it ages. The greatest Shapers can grow faces into the trees—not the slow, unintentional bone-plate faces that appear on the oldest trunks, but deliberate ones. These faces are never for beauty. They are lures, or warnings, or offerings.

The **Carriers** are the ones who walk farthest. Their bodies are denser, their limbs longer. They are the ones who descend into the deepest root-canyons where the prior civilization’s log bridges have become petrified arteries. They retrieve what the green has not yet fully swallowed: a stone knife still warm from a hand that turned to mulch ten thousand years ago, a shard of petrified pollen that sings when held to the frill. Carriers rarely return to the same nest twice in a lifetime. They are half-mad with distance and half-saint for it.

Reproduction is strange even by the standards of Veyr-Keth.

Every seventy years or so the continent inhales in unison. For seven heartbeats everything is quiet. In that silence the Quiet feel an irresistible urge to climb. They swarm upward along aerial roots and hanging curtains, thousands of them, silent as sap. At a certain altitude—always different, always chosen by the green itself—they meet. There is no courtship. Bodies entwine in a single slow knot that can last days. When they descend again, the females carry inside their bark-skin translucent fruit the color of opals. Inside each fruit is a child already half-formed, dreaming the dreams of the tree that will eventually house it.

Most children do not survive their first century. The continent is a harsh parent.

The Quiet do not hate the green. Hatred would require the belief that the green is separate. To them the continent is simply *body*, and they are the tolerable fleas that live in the folds where digestion is slowest. They speak of the world the way a liver might speak of the creature it serves—proud of its function, pragmatic about its eventual dissolution.

At the deepest root-canyons, where the ruins of the log-bridge people have been almost entirely incorporated, the eldest Quiet sometimes gather. They press their foreheads to the bone-like faces that have grown on the trunks over centuries and listen to the slow, geological thoughts moving through the lignin. The faces change expression once every few decades: a faint smile becoming a faint frown, a closed eye slowly opening. The Quiet interpret these as weather reports, or prophecies, or jokes told by a mind so vast it only finishes a punchline after ten thousand years.

No outsider has ever seen a Quiet laugh.

But sometimes, when the continent exhales and the roaring returns, a single golden eye will catch the light in a way that looks almost like amusement. Then the eye closes again, the frill vibrates once—too low to be heard by anything that still has bones—and the Quiet slips back into the breathing places, leaving only a faint scent of sap and old rain behind.

They do not worship the green.

They *cooperate* with it.

And in the long, hot, deafening digestion of Veyr-Keth, cooperation is the only immortality available.
