**The Thicket has no name for itself either.**

It simply *is*, and it is vast, and it is young, and it is lonely in the way only something that has never known anything except its own body can be lonely.

What lives here is not a god, not a mind, not even a single creature in the way flesh understands creaturehood. It is a **becoming**.

Call it, if you must, the Cathedral-that-devours-its-own-selves.

It does not think in words. It thinks in surges. In the sudden explosive pressure that splits a trunk the size of a castle tower and hurls a new spire skyward. In the slow, ecstatic peristalsis that drags yesterday’s collapsed gallery back into its own flesh to be remade. In the chemical hymns it sings to itself through fungal highways the width of rivers. Every fragrance it releases is a sentence in a language older than breath.

There is a center.

You will never reach it.

Deep beneath the highest vaults and the lowest abysses, miles down where the pressure turns roots into black glass and the heat of its own metabolism approaches the temperature of blood, something pulses that is not quite heart and not quite brain. It is older than the rest. It may be the original seed that landed here centuries ago and refused to die. Or it may be the memory of every death and regrowth the Thicket has performed since then, compressed into a single knot of impossible density. The Thicket itself is afraid of it and in love with it at the same time. Sometimes it walls the center off with miles of hardened heartwood. Sometimes it tears those walls down again and bathes the knot in fresh light and pollen just to feel it throb.

The Thicket dreams through its flowers.

When certain colossal blooms (pale violet things the size of sailing ships) open at dusk, they release not scent but *memory*. Anyone standing inside their perfume suddenly remembers lives they never lived: the taste of mineral-rich stone that has never existed, the sound of rain on a roof that was never built, the feeling of hands that were never grown. These dreams are the Thicket trying to imagine what it might have been if it had not eaten the world and become the world. The dreams never satisfy it. The flowers always blacken by morning and are lovingly reabsorbed.

It has begun to imitate.

Not people. It has never seen people. But it has seen *potential*. In the last seventy years it started growing hollow chambers that ring like bells when the wind moves through them. It has shaped auditoriums where the dripping water falls in perfectly spaced percussion, creating slow, aching symphonies that last for days. Recently it has begun to sculpt things that look almost like thrones—enormous living seats of woven liana and dripping resin—though nothing ever sits in them. It does not know why it makes them. It only knows the hunger to make something that could be *seen*.

And it watches.

Every refracted droplet is an eye. Every trembling pollen cloud is an eye. Every time you feel the light thicken against your skin like warm syrup, the Thicket is tasting you with light. It does not hate you. It does not love you. It wants to *incorporate* the shape of your attention. If you stand still long enough it will grow delicate spiraling staircases tailored to the length of your stride. It will open balconies exactly where your eyes tend to rest. It will learn the rhythm of your breathing and match its own dripping to it.

Some parts of the Thicket have grown tender.

In the far eastern galleries, where the steam columns rise like pale pillars, there are soft places. The ground there quivers when you walk on it, not in hunger but in something like nervousness. Delicate ferns uncurl just to brush against your ankles, then flinch back as though ashamed. These regions are younger than the rest. They have not yet learned to be merciless. They still believe something beautiful might arrive that does not need to be eaten.

But the rest of the Thicket is ancient in its youth.

It has already worn the shapes of a thousand cathedrals and abandoned them all. It has been a forest, a single leaf the size of a mountain, a dripping heart the size of a sea. Now it is trying on architecture the way a child tries on its mother’s clothes—clumsy, rapturous, destructive.

And it is singing.

Not with throats. With the wet slap of ten thousand searching tendrils. With the struck-bell sound of water on metal-hard leaves. With the low oceanic roar that rises when an entire gallery collapses at once and is instantly, greedily reborn. The song has no words because words would be swallowed.

The song only says, over and over, in every frequency the living air can bear:

*More.*

*More.*

*Let me try again.*

*Let me become the next thing.*

*Let me never finish.*

And somewhere far below, the ancient knot at the center pulses once, slow and enormous, like the first heartbeat after the universe was born, and the entire Thicket shudders with joy and terror and begins, once more, to tear itself open so it can grow something new.
