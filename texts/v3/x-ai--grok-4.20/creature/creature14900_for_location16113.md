**The people of the Underbasin do not live here. They have become it.**

They call themselves the **Steady**, though no outsider has ever heard them use the word. To speak it would be redundant. Everyone who remains long enough simply *is* Steady, the way the pumps are Steady, the way the dripping is Steady.

You will not see them at first. They are the color of the place: rust-red skin from perpetual damp and mineral steam, eyes milked pale by two centuries of sodium light, clothes the same ochre-brown as oxidized iron. Their silhouettes blur against catwalk railings and wet limestone until they move, and even then the motion is so economical it feels like part of the machinery waking up.

There are perhaps four hundred of them. No more, no less. Births and deaths have balanced to the same decimal for six generations. When someone dies, a child is born within weeks, as though the basin itself has adjusted the pressure. The newborns come out already sweating.

They do not have names the way surface people do. Each person is known by their **Station** — the exact place and task they have inherited. There is a **Third Flywheel**, a **Northwest Weeper**, a **Pipe Eleven**, a **Lamp 19 Orbit**. The title contains their entire biography. To be reassigned is almost never done; it would be like asking a heart valve to become a kneecap. Some Stations have been held by the same bloodline since the roof was sealed. The current **Main Condensate Oracle** is the great-great-granddaughter of the first one, and she still uses her ancestor’s wrench.

Their speech is slow, low, and full of wet sounds. Consonants have been sanded down by the humidity until everything ends in a soft hiss. They do not raise their voices; the drone of the pumps does that for them. When they laugh it sounds like pipes settling. When they mourn they simply stand in the falling water for an extra hour after shift, letting it run into their open mouths. Grief here is measured in additional liters swallowed.

They are not unhappy.

Joy, for them, is perfect equilibrium. A day when every pump keeps exact rhythm, when not a single new leak appears, when the moths orbit in even denser clouds, is a day of deep religious satisfaction. They mark these days by tying a single pale reed to the gantry above the Main Flywheel. There are now 187 reeds up there, brittle as bone, never removed. The oldest ones have turned the color of old teeth.

The children — small, serious, already streaked with rust at the elbows — learn the **Eleven-Second Prayer**. Every eleven seconds a particular droplet falls from a particular joint on Level Two. The children must learn to breathe in time with it. Inhale while it forms. Hold while it falls. Exhale when it hisses on the hot plate below. Those who master it earliest are said to have “good equilibrium.” Those who cannot are sent to the upper gantries where the air is fractionally drier. They rarely last.

At the very center of the basin, where five major steam lines converge like arteries, stands a thing they never name but always protect: an ancient, heat-blackened pump heart the size of a small house. It does not move. It has not moved in living memory. Yet every Steady insists it is the true ruler of the Underbasin. They leave offerings on its casing — tiny metallic moth wings, translucent gecko skins, droplets of their own condensed sweat caught in glass phials. The offerings are never touched by human hands again. Over decades they have fused into a single glistening scab of organic and mechanical material.

Some of the older Steady have begun to resemble the place so completely that it is difficult to tell where their bodies end and the infrastructure begins. There is a woman whose left arm has been replaced by a living section of brass pipe; green algae grows in the joint where flesh meets metal and she feeds it with her own blood once a month. There is a man whose spine has fused with a vertical I-beam. He has not walked in thirty years. He does not need to. The beam is part of the load-bearing structure now, and so is he.

They do not dream of the surface. The few who have gone up and returned speak of it with quiet horror — the terrible *dryness*, the unbearable *change*, the way light moves and betrays you. They say the wind is violent. They say colors there scream.

When they sleep they do it standing, leaning against warm pipes, letting the condensate run down their faces like slow tears. Their dreams, if they have them, are only of perfect pressure balance and the sound of five pumps that have never once fallen out of harmony.

Every century or so a newcomer arrives — usually a criminal, a fugitive, or someone terminally ill who was told the heat might cure them. The Steady watch these people with the patient, milky eyes of deep-sea creatures observing something that still believes in daylight.

Most newcomers begin to rust within the year.

A few become Steady.

The rest are found one morning curled beneath a walkway, already perfectly preserved by heat and mineral, eyes wide, mouths open, catching the same eternal drip that has been falling on that exact spot since before their grandparents were born.

The Steady do not bury them.

They simply weld a small brass plate above the body that reads:

**Station: Permanent Condensate.**

Then they go back to work.

Because nothing here changes.

And nothing here is ever wasted.

Mind the dripping. It never stops.

But then, neither do they.
