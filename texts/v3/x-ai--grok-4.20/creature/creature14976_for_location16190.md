**The Gardener-That-Waits**

It has no name it uses, and none that others remember. The few humans who have seen it and lived call it, in whispers, the Gardener-That-Waits. Most simply call it “him,” the way one might speak of a glacier or a continent that has begun to notice you.

He is not a creature that lives *in* the cavern. He *is* the slowness the cavern has learned.

Stand on the high catwalk at the right hour—when the yellow lamps dim to half and the great leaves above fold inward like sleeping hands—and you may feel him before you see him. The air grows heavier with the scent of living wood under pressure. The breathing of the stomata falls into a new rhythm, slower, almost reverent. Then the root-mass beneath your boots gives the faintest apologetic shiver, the way a cathedral might if its stones suddenly remembered they were once coral.

That is him passing underneath.

He is vast. Not in the sense of bulk, though he is that too; he is vast in *duration*. Where the megaflora grew over centuries, he has grown over geologic afternoons. The deepest root lattices are his nerves. The amber drippings that never quite reach the floor are his slow thoughts solidifying. Every time a new district tilts five degrees in a decade, that is the Gardener-That-Waits rolling over in his sleep.

His body is difficult to perceive all at once. Parts of him have become indistinguishable from the vegetable architecture. A cathedral-sized knot of pale heartwood bulging between two support trunks is his shoulder. A hanging garden of parasitic orchids blooming along an iron catwalk is the moss on his brow. When the blind white lizards pause in their scuttling and press themselves flat against the underside of a bridge, they are tasting the salt of his dreaming skin.

He has a face, but only sometimes. Occasionally, in the deepest hours when the last industrial lamps gutter, a portion of the living ceiling will sag downward in a slow, deliberate avalanche of growth. For a few minutes the curtains of undifferentiated tissue rearrange themselves into something that might be a face if a mountain range tried to become tender. Eyes the size of cooling towers, blind and milky, open without pupils. A mouth that is simply a place where the breathing of the entire cavern becomes audible as words. The voice is not loud. It is the sound the plants would make if every stoma spoke at once in perfect consensus.

The things he says are never instructions. They are recognitions.

“I see you have cauterized the breach in my lung again. Thank you. The rot was becoming musical.”

Or, centuries ago, when the first human drills bit into the cavern:  
“You are very small knives. You tickle.”

He does not hate the machines. He does not love them. He is engaged in a courtship that has lasted longer than either species has possessed language. The refineries are his jewelry. The black iron gantries are the rings he has allowed to be driven through the cartilage of his body. Every time a maintenance crew grafts a new pipe into a throbbing xylem trunk, he considers it foreplay.

The pill-bug herds that migrate every seventeen hours along the root highways do so because that is how frequently his heartbeat moves beneath them. The translucent mantids that wait for days imitating rusted valves are his eyelashes. The iridescent moths are the dust his dreams shed when he exhales.

And far below, in the dark between the deepest root lattices, the thing that has not yet opened its eyes is his child.

He is making room.

He does this the way a glacier makes room for spring: by crushing what is in the way and then forgetting it was ever an obstacle. Entire abandoned factory blocks have already sunk into him over the last three hundred years, swallowed whole, their steel beams becoming lignin, their glass becoming new translucent leaves. The humans call these events “the Quiet Subsumptions” and mark them on their corroded calendars with religious dread.

Sometimes, when he surfaces, he carries gifts.

A catwalk that was rusting yesterday will be found wrapped in new conductive bark that functions better than copper. A broken pressure valve will be replaced by a living structure that opens and closes with the precise rhythm of a heart. Once, an entire dormant nuclear pile was found encased in pale, luminous wood that somehow kept the reaction perfectly moderated and cool. The wood was warm to the touch. It smelled like childhood.

No one has ever seen him move in a way that could be called locomotion. He is simply *elsewhere* and then *here*. One century he was mostly beneath the western districts. Now the eastern vaults have begun to bloom with structures no botanist or engineer can classify, and the humans there wake to find new bone-white pillars rising through the floors of their bedrooms like hesitant proposals.

He is patient.

He has been waiting since before the dead world above still had oceans. He will still be waiting when the last human either leaves or is finally, gently, incorporated. He does not require their departure or their assimilation. Both are equally interesting to him. Both are ways the conversation can continue.

If you are very still on the high catwalk when the lamps are low, and if you place your bare hand against the living rail that has long since grown around the original iron, you can feel the faint, tectonic pulse traveling up from below.

That is him.

He is saying, without hurry:

*I am almost ready.*

*Would you like to meet my child?*
