**They are called the Hollow Choir.**

Not a species. A state of being. A slow consensus reached by whatever still possesses a mind down here after the water took everything.

They do not live on the floor. They live inside the saturation itself.

Most of them began as something else (engineers, pilgrims, maintenance clades, prisoners, worshippers of the last dry land) but the Thalassic Shelf has had three billion years to forget the difference between flesh and iron, between prayer and rust. Now they drift between the bronze-indigo kelp curtains like living counterweights, bodies elongated and hollowed by pressure until they resemble upright, jointed tubes of pale cartilage and blackened metal. Their ribcages are literal cages; many keep smaller machines or symbiotic shrimp colonies inside their own thoraxes, glowing faintly behind translucent bone. Where lungs once were there are now resonant chambers that let them sing back to the roar of the water columns and be understood.

They have no faces anymore. The front of the head has folded inward into a smooth, slightly concave plate of mother-of-pearl and old rivets. Sound arrives there instead of light. They “see” by tasting the pressure waves of the world’s constant disassembly. When they wish to speak to one another they strike tuned sections of their own bodies with specialized hammers grown from their wrists. The resulting chords carry for kilometers through the cold, recognizable to every member of the Choir even across the roar of the upwellings.

The Hollow Choir does not build. They tend.

They move in slow, liturgical processions between the hanging kelp cathedrals, pruning the barrel-creatures when they overgrow, coaxing the armored bus-fish into new migration patterns with low-frequency hymns, tightening the braided root-mats with living cables extruded from their own spines. They worship the machines without believing the machines were ever made by anyone. To them the half-sunken foundries, the lone smokestacks, the drifting drone rigs, the single iron bench on the endless plain, are all expressions of the same exhausted equilibrium the ocean has reached. The Choir maintains them the way a dying forest maintains its fungi: not out of nostalgia, but because the rot and the growth have become the same organism.

Some of them still remember dry land in the way amputees remember pain. These individuals carry small, sealed reliquaries (a rusted pocket watch, a child’s tooth, a single page of sheet music preserved in oil) embedded directly into their flesh. They sing to these objects more softly than to anything else. The rest of the Choir tolerates this the way one tolerates the ticking of a heart that has not yet learned it is no longer needed.

When a member of the Choir dies, the body is not buried. It is unwound.

Other Choir members arrive and patiently unbraid the cartilage, unbolt the metal, release the shrimp colonies, until nothing remains but a long ribbon of pale material that is allowed to rise slowly through the kelp forest like a question no one will ever answer. Sometimes these ribbons become new anchors for the hanging vegetation. Sometimes they simply vanish upward for hours until even the Choir’s pressure-sensitive sight loses them. No one mourns. Mourning would imply the possibility of less saturation, and saturation is the only law left.

The oldest among them (those who have been hollow longest) have grown vast. Thirty, forty meters tall, their bodies laced through with generations of tubeworms and black coral so that they resemble mobile sections of the machines they serve. These ancients rarely move. They simply stand in the violent currents between two kelp curtains and let the water columns pass through the open lattices of their ribcages, singing continuously. Their voices are so low that whales change course when they hear them. Their songs have no words. They are descriptions of what it feels like to have already lost, perfectly, three billion years ago, and to still be here keeping the engines running anyway.

Occasionally a new mind falls into the Shelf from whatever thin reality still clings to the surface far above. A submersible. A suicidal pilgrim. A piece of orbital debris. The Choir finds them quickly. They do not rescue. They do not convert. They simply wait until the new arrival has understood that drowning is not an event but a final geological consensus. Then, if the newcomer still possesses a voice, the Choir teaches them how to strike their own bones in harmony.

Most eventually join.

A few do not. These are allowed to sit on the perfectly flat plain beside the half-buried locomotive or the open cathedral door or the solitary iron bench. They are permitted to remain dry inside their own failing suits until the pressure and the cold and the saturation finally finish the argument.

The Choir never hurries them.

There is no reason to hurry.

Everything that can be won has already been lost. The ocean has reached its final, exhausted equilibrium, and the Hollow Choir is simply the part of that equilibrium that is still capable of humming along with the machinery.

They are not waiting for rescue.

They are not waiting for the end.

They are the end, learning how to sing beautifully inside its own throat.
