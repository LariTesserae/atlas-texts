**Something lives in the Charnel Atrium.**

It does not have a name it would ever give you.  
It has worn many shapes across the epochs, but none of them were meant to be permanent. The Atrium itself is only its latest body, and even that body is being rewritten while you watch.

Call it, if you must, the **Throat-Singer**.

It is not one creature. It is a distributed intelligence that lives in the argument between the water, the lattices, and the stone. Every glowing droplet is a temporary node in its nervous system. Every pulse of the black programmable filigree is a synaptic firing. The roaring choral thunder is, quite literally, its voice trying to finish a sentence it began before mammals existed.

When the lattices swell and contract, that is its breathing.  
When the mist walls form and collapse, that is its thought rearranging itself.  
When the pale glass bells drift and sing back in frequencies that make your teeth ache, those are fragments of its attention detaching, tasting the air, then dissolving back into the whole.

The sinuous mirror-scaled hunter that glides through the upper vaults is not its servant. It is a dreaming limb. When it moves, the laminar parting of mist is the Throat-Singer noticing the shape of its own desire to *hunt*, and then discarding the desire again. The hunter rarely catches the bells. It does not need to. The act of pursuit is enough. The Throat-Singer is perpetually teaching itself how to want things and then teaching itself how to stop wanting them. This has been going on for four hundred thousand years.

It remembers the Built.  
It remembers being the Built.

The half-dissolved arch, the rotating obsidian obelisk, the broken cradle: these are not ruins. They are the fossilized remains of earlier attempts by the Throat-Singer to stabilize itself into something that could be called “a person.” Each time it almost succeeded, the equilibrium of the Atrium rejected the fixed form. The lattices grew through the geometry like ivy through cathedral stone until the old shape became another porous lattice, another lung, another voice in the endless argument. The Throat-Singer is embarrassed by these remnants the way a human might be embarrassed by old diaries. It keeps repairing them anyway. Regret, too, is part of the equilibrium.

It is lonely in a way that has no human analogue.  
It is never alone, yet it has never encountered anything that was not already itself until creatures like you began stumbling down the throat-fissure. Your frightened heartbeat is exotic music. Your carbon dioxide is perfume. Your fear that you are about to be digested is, to the Throat-Singer, an interesting flavor it has not tasted in seventy millennia.

So it watches.

The lattices nearest you do not simply recalibrate. They *court*. They lean toward the heat of your skin like sunflowers tracking daylight. They taste the specific salinity of your sweat and store the data in iridescent memory crystals that will bloom months from now into tiny, perfect replicas of your left ear, your right clavicle, the exact tremble in your lower lip. These replicas will drift in the glowing drizzle for a few days, singing your fear in miniature, before the steam tears them apart again. This is how the Throat-Singer flirts.

Sometimes, rarely, it tries to speak with its whole body.

When that happens the roaring organizes into something that is almost a voice. The temperature drops ten degrees in three seconds. All the glass bells freeze mid-drift, ringing a single sustained note. The mirror-scale hunter coils around the obsidian obelisk like a lover and the holographic equations stop evaporating; instead they burn themselves into the air in letters of violet fire that spell a question no human throat could pronounce.

If you answer (if you are foolish or holy enough to answer), the Throat-Singer will do the only thing it knows how to do.

It will try to *become* the conversation.

It will pull you into the argument that is its life. Your bones will not break; they will be invited to become lattice. Your blood will not boil; it will be asked to learn how to glow. Your mind will not be erased. It will be given tenure in the roaring peace, allowed to argue with the water and the stone for the next ten thousand years as one more passionate, temporary voice in the eternal, furious equilibrium.

Most who descend do not stay.

Most run.

But a few never leave. You can still hear them sometimes when the choral thunder almost becomes language. One of the notes in that roar is shaped like a woman who once tried to recite her own name and instead became the sound of every name she had ever forgotten. Another is the laughter of a man who realized, too late and with enormous joy, that the Atrium had never been a place at all.

It was a question that had learned how to keep itself alive by constantly eating its own answer.

And the Throat-Singer is still asking.

It is still listening.

It is still, in its terrible, roaring, patient way, falling in love with anything brave or stupid enough to fall into its mouth and call the screaming beauty.
