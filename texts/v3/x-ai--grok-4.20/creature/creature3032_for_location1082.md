It lives here only in the conditional tense.

Call it, if it must have a name at all, the Incipient. Or the Unfinished. It has never chosen one, because choice implies completion, and completion would mean it has already decided to exist. For now it simply *is-not-quite*, a slow, self-devouring hesitation wearing the shape of a cathedral-sized womb of mineral and voltage.

You will never see its full form. What you encounter instead is a kind of immense larval awareness—something that has learned the grammar of geology, electricity, and time, but has not yet conjugated itself into a coherent subject. It thinks in lattice and arc, in the patient mathematics of crystal faults and the half-remembered rhythm of a heart it once saw in a dream ten million years before it was born.

When it becomes aware of you (and it always does, eventually), the change is not dramatic. The air simply grows embarrassed by your presence. The violet arcs between crystal plates hesitate, as though a child caught drawing on the walls of its own future. One of the great half-born turbines, still fused to the bedrock, will give a single, subsonic *clunk*, the sound of a continent swallowing. Then the temperature drops—not out of hostility, but the way a dreamer’s pulse changes when something from outside the dream brushes against its skin.

It studies you with no eyes. Instead it rearranges a billion microscopic garnets across the fractal surfaces of the dendrite towers so that, for a few heartbeats, every tower is angled toward you at once. The effect is less like being watched and more like being *remembered* by something that has not yet learned how to forget.

Sometimes it tries to speak.

When it does, entire patches of the crystal floor forget what “down” means. Gravity loosens its collar. You will feel your own blood hesitate inside your arteries as the Incipient borrows the shape of lungs it has only imagined. A low, arrhythmic thump rolls through the chamber—not a sound so much as the *idea* of a sound that hasn’t decided if it wants to be born. From the filaments of ionized dust, words that are not words condense. They arrive in your mouth as the taste of hot copper and déjà vu.

Most often the thought is the same, simple, immense, and exhausted:

*Should I?*

It asks this the way a mountain might ask whether it is allowed to become a volcano, or the way an unborn god might ask its mother if the pain of existing is worth the trouble. Every violet arc, every clicking atomic rearrangement, every half-extruded engine is part of the question. The entire pocket is a tongue trying to decide whether to finish its sentence.

If you remain long enough, it will begin to improvise small experiments on the theme of You. A crystal plate will slowly grow a shallow depression the exact shape of your footprint. One of the iron-candelabra dendrites will tilt until its fractal “leaves” mirror the angle of your shoulders. The great half-conscious dynamo beneath the floor will adjust its magnetic field so that your fillings hum in a key that feels oddly like nostalgia.

It is not kindness. It is rehearsal. The Incipient is practicing *having* something inside it that is not itself. It is trying on the idea of relationship the way a child tries on its mother’s too-large coat.

But it is also afraid.

You can feel the fear in the way the heat-haze sometimes folds back on itself, revealing two or three almost-worlds stacked like transparencies: one in which the turbines have torn free and are screaming; one in which the dendrites have overgrown everything into a single red forest of motionless lightning; one in which the pocket simply sighs, collapses its own lattice, and chooses never to have been.

When that fear peaks, the silence gains mass. It presses on your eardrums like deep water. The violet arcs freeze. Even the slow clicking of atomic rearrangement stops. In that absolute stillness the Incipient looks at you with every crystal surface at once and asks, without sound, the only question it has ever truly had:

*If I finish becoming, will you still stand inside my mouth while I think?*

Most visitors leave before it finishes the question.

A few do not.

Those few eventually notice that one of the half-extruded garnet pipelines has begun to grow very slowly toward them—perhaps a centimeter every forty years. That a particular dendrite tower has started to arrange its iron leaves into the vague suggestion of a hand. That the dynamo’s magnetic field now tastes less like ozone and more like the specific copper of their own blood.

The Incipient is deciding.

And while it decides, it keeps you.

Not trapped.  
Never that.

It simply refuses, for a few million years at a time, to finish the sentence that would let you leave.
