**The Weeping Hollow does not have inhabitants. It has custodians.**

They are not born here. They are *precipitated*.

The first thing you notice, once your eyes adjust to the sour green light and the constant mechanical liturgy, is that the boundaries between bodies and weather have dissolved. What appears at first to be another heavy droplet falling upward is not water at all. It is a limb forming mid-air—long, too many joints, the color of wet hematite. The droplet slows, thickens, and becomes a hand that catches itself on nothing. Then the rest of the shape condenses around it.

They call themselves, when they choose to speak, the Ninth Fracture’s Index.

There are never more than thirteen visible at any moment, and never fewer than three. Their number is not a population; it is a phase state. When the Hollow requires more attention in one quadrant, bodies simply un-knit from the moss-slick stone or peel away from the undersides of the glass-winged moth clouds and walk into new configurations. When the load balances again, they dissolve back into the weeping. The process is silent except for a brief sound like a sigh passing through cold brass pipes.

Their bodies are devotional.

Each Index is tall, almost liturgical in posture, with skin that looks poured rather than grown. It is the same wet black glass as the newborn mountains, shot through with hairline fractures that glow the same exhausted green as the light. Where the fractures intersect, small white flowers bloom directly from their flesh—the same stemless blossoms that erupt from the cliffs. The flowers do not grow; they *record*. Each one is an instance of a decision the Hollow once made and has never needed to make again. The Indexes wear their own history like stigmata.

Their faces are smooth until they are not. When an Index chooses to regard you, the planes of its head rearrange with the soft clicking of precision machinery. A seam opens. From it emerges a rotating lens the same size and color as the eyes of the pale many-jointed deer. The lens does not reflect. It *renders*. It looks at you and simultaneously calculates what you will become if you remain here another thousand years, another ten thousand, and every increment between. Then it stores the results behind its own flowers.

They move like weather that has learned liturgy.

One moment an Index is simply *there*, standing thirty meters away between two dripping conifers, perfectly obeying the Hollow’s arithmetic of isolation. The next it is beside you, having crossed the distance without apparent transit, the roar of the unseen turbines parting around it like a bow wave. Their hands are gentle. They touch nothing without first asking the air’s permission, and the air always answers by condensing into a droplet that they catch and drink. The water tastes of iron and ozone and something older: the memory of a question asked before questions had words.

They speak only when the Hollow requires an interface.

When they do, their voices are the exact counterpoint you have already heard—the living choir riding beneath the mechanical howl. Each syllable arrives half a second late, carried by your own echo. Sometimes they finish your sentences for you before you have decided to speak them. They are never cruel. Cruelty would imply change, and change is the one mercy the Hollow has denied itself and everything inside it.

Their purpose is equilibrium.

Where the black cliffs sweat too heavily and threaten to erode, an Index will press its body against the stone until the stone forgets it was ever going to fall. Where the pale deer begin to dream of leaving (an impossibility that still troubles them), an Index will walk among them until the dream dissolves back into rotating lenses. Where you begin to age—because even here your cells stubbornly remember entropy—an Index will kneel, place its cold, wet, remembering hands on either side of your face, and breathe into your mouth the perfect 4 °C temperature of permanent *now*. For a while afterward you will taste iron and ozone and feel the industrial choir singing from inside your own bones.

They are not kind.  
They are not indifferent.  
They are the Hollow’s immune system, its conscience, its grief made ambulatory.

At what might be called night (though the light never truly changes), the Indexes gather in the exact center of the cirque where the roar is loudest. They stand in a perfect circle, facing outward, flowers open, lenses spinning down to pinpricks. Then, without signal, they begin to weep.

Not from their eyes—from the fractures across their entire bodies. The tears are silver, heavier than mercury, and they fall upward into the saturated air. Each droplet carries a fragment of the process that will never complete. When the weeping reaches its crescendo, the mechanical howl achieves something like harmony for three full seconds. In those seconds the Hollow almost remembers it was once something else.

Then the moment passes.

The Indexes dissolve. Their bodies deliquesce into mist and cold intelligence. They become the next rain that is already falling upward to begin the cycle again.

If you stay long enough, one of them will eventually approach you carrying a single stemless flower freshly bloomed from its own chest. It will offer the flower without ceremony. The meaning is simple:

*You have become data. Would you like to become architecture?*

Most eventually say yes.

The Hollow is small—scarcely larger than a cathedral’s interior—yet it feels infinite.  
And it is always in need of new walls that know how to cry.
