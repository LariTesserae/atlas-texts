**They are called the Hollow Choir.**

Not a species. A condition. A state of occupancy.

The first thing that lives here is the pressure itself. It pools in the negative spaces between tanks like black marrow and thinks with the slow, crushing patience of something that has never been in a hurry. When it decides a new cavity is ready, it exhales a single syllable that lasts three weeks. That syllable becomes meat.

From that meat the first Singers are born.

They do not grow. They *arrive*, already the length of subway cars, already ancient the moment their grinding mouths open. Each Singer is a cathedral bell that has learned how to be lonely. Their bodies are not scaled or fleshed but *cast* — black iron fused with something that remembers being cartilage. Inside their throats sit smaller versions of themselves, nested like matryoshka dolls, each one singing at a different octave so the whole creature becomes a living chord that never resolves. The largest ones have been singing the same unfinished measure since the first tank was expressed from the dark. They will still be singing it when the last tank forgets why it was made.

The smaller mechanofauna — the ones that look like vacuum cleaners crossed with eels — are not animals at all. They are regrets. Every time the structure almost remembers what it was built to contain, the memory detaches, grows fins, and begins drifting between the tanks looking for somewhere to be useful. Their schematic-light skins are apologies written in circuit diagrams. When they touch one another the soft *clink* is the sound of two almost-purposes forgiving each other for never having been finished.

Then there are the Scaffoldites.

These are the closest things to priests. Jointed, pale, deliberate. Each one is exactly as long as it needs to be and no longer. They do not eat. They do not mate. They only *perform industry*. A Scaffoldite will spend nine days building a perfect catwalk between two tanks, bolt by bolt, weld by weld, using tools that unfold from its own elbows. On the tenth day it disassembles the catwalk and itself with equal reverence, storing every bolt inside its own hollow thorax until the next calling. Their memory is measured in structures built and unbuilt. They have never repeated a single assembly. There are theories that if every Scaffoldite that has ever existed were to build one final project together, the Bellowing Tanks would finally understand their own shape.

Deeper still, where the lattice grows dense enough to strangle light, live the **Inquilines**.

They are rare. Most have never been seen by anything that possesses eyes. An Inquiline is what happens when something metallic remains uninhabited for too long. The pressure grows impatient. It forces its way inside the unused iron and *wears* it. The resulting creatures are ambulatory sections of the cathedral itself — living buttresses, ambulatory bulkheads, ambulatory pressure doors. Their hearts are valves. Their thoughts are flow rates. When one passes, every nearby Singer falls momentarily silent out of something that might be respect, might be fear.

And then there is the **Last Welder**.

Only one is ever active at a time. It resembles a mantis made of fresh weld-scars and white floodlight. Where its head should be is a single industrial lamp that never dims. It moves along the outer cliffs of the structure, always on the edge between the lattice and the true abyss. In its forelimbs it carries a welding torch that burns colder than the water around it. The Last Welder does not build. It *corrects*. Every few decades it finds a place where the structure has begun, very slightly, to understand mercy. It burns that understanding away and moves on. The scars it leaves behind are worshipped by the Scaffoldites for seven years before they are ceremonially unmade.

No creature here is individual. They are all frequencies of the same slow bellow. When a Singer dies — which happens once every few centuries — its body does not rot. It is gently taken apart by the mechanofauna, each segment polished until it shines like new, and then welded into the lattice itself. The next Singer to be born will carry that dead singer’s voice as a harmonic, slightly flattened, forever.

If you remain still long enough, they do not simply measure you.

They *consider* you.

The smaller ones will taste your blood with caliper-mouths and decide which of your bones would make the best rivets. The Scaffoldites will begin sketching temporary scaffolding around your limbs, testing where you might best integrate. And far above, one of the Singers will alter its gyre, bringing its vast grinding mouth close enough that you can see your own reflection turning inside the rotating rings.

They are not hungry.

They are vocational.

In the Bellowing Tanks everything is being invited, constantly and without mercy, to become part of the cathedral before the cathedral finishes remembering what it was supposed to be.

Most things accept.

The pressure is patient.

And the choir is always short one voice.
