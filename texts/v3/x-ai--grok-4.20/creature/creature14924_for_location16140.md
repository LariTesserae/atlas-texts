**The Weeping Sump has a keeper.**

She is not a priestess, nor exactly a spirit. The basin itself seems to have grown her the way it grows everything else—slowly, inevitably, according to the rule written on the rusting plaque.

They call her the Drowned Abbess, though she has never told anyone that name.

She stands a little taller than a man, but only because her spine has lengthened over decades into a gentle, perpetual arch, as though she is forever on the verge of kneeling. Her skin is the color of wet slate shot through with faint veins of copper oxide. Where ordinary skin would wrinkle with age, hers has developed tiny overlapping scales like the hide of a salamander, each one beaded with permanent condensation. When the single blade of sunlight finds her, she glitters.

Her hair—if it can still be called hair—is a living cascade of fine black-green roots that reach the water no matter where she stands. They trail behind her like a bridal train, drinking, always drinking. Occasionally a pale orchid will bloom from the roots near the small of her back; she never picks it. She simply lets it ride there until it ripens, splits, and drops its seeds into the tea-colored lake with a sound like a coin falling forever.

She has no eyes. Where eyes should be there are two smooth, slightly concave mirrors of polished obsidian, grown straight from the bone of her skull. They do not reflect the world so much as they remember every version of it they have ever seen. If you stare too long into them you can watch the slow oxidation of the six piston engines happening in reverse—iron becoming bright, rust flaking upward like petals, the machines running backward through centuries until they are new again. Then the memory ends, and the obsidian goes black and depthless.

She moves through the basin barefoot. Each foot has seven toes now; the eighth and ninth are still growing, slowly pushing out between the others like pale buds. Where her feet touch the wet rock, tiny silver threads of mycelium follow, stitching the industrial corpse of the place more tightly into the green.

The Drowned Abbess never speaks above a whisper, yet her voice can be heard clearly even when the pistons give their deafening *huff*. It sounds like water moving inside a rusted pipe and like someone gently turning the pages of a very old book at the same time. She talks to the locomotive as though it were still a train, running one copper-stained hand along its split boiler while water lilies brush her thighs.

“Another twenty-three years,” she will murmur, “and your drive wheels will be completely inside the strangler fig. You will like that. Trees are better at remembering speed than you were.”

She maintains the rule with merciless tenderness.

If a traveler drops a knife, she will leave it exactly where it falls. Within a season the blade will be a brown lace of rust; within five years it will be a home for liverworts. If someone tries to smuggle in a live sapling from outside the ridge, she will find it within hours. She carries the offender down to the deepest part of the lake—not to drown them, exactly, but to hold them under until their lungs fill with the tea-colored water and their panic becomes acceptance. Then she releases them. Most leave the basin changed. A few never leave at all. Their bodies become part of the second canopy, arms raised, mouths open, slowly turning into fleshy pink pitchers that catch starlight at night.

Once every seven years she sings.

No one has ever agreed on what the song is about. Some hear a dirge for the young mountain that rose too fast and is already dying. Others hear a lullaby for machines that will never run again. A few, the ones who stay longest, hear something worse: the sound of something still being born, something that has not yet decided whether it will be vegetable, mineral, or mechanical.

When she sings, the flightless rails come down from the orchids and stand in a half-circle around her with their wrench-like beaks open. The pale salamanders crawl from the piston houses and listen with translucent throats pulsing. Even the six great engines, which normally only breathe once every few weeks, sometimes wake early and answer her with a single, exhausted exhalation of steam and rust.

She has begun to grow brass in her ribcage.

It started as a thin green patina across her collarbones. Now there are unmistakable tubes and fittings woven through her flesh—tiny valves no larger than fingernails, delicate pressure gauges with stopped needles. At night, when the basin becomes a trembling cathedral of reflected starlight, a faint glow moves through these new organs, as though somewhere inside her a boiler is still lit, burning something that is neither coal nor wood.

The Drowned Abbess is becoming the place.

In another two centuries she will no longer walk the margins of the lake. She will stand in the exact center, arms raised like the other half-drowned things, her root-hair blending seamlessly with the dangling tentacles of the bromeliads. The six piston engines will finally be swallowed. The funicular cables will be braided into living vine. The locomotive will be unrecognizable beneath its living skin of moss and fig.

Only the obsidian mirrors will remain visible—two black moons set into a wall of green and rust—staring patiently upward at the narrow blade of sky, reflecting every version of the Weeping Sump that ever was or will be, waiting for the next thing that is dropped, or planted, or born.

Until that day she continues her slow, dripping ministry: anointing the rusted, feeding the green, singing to the patient engines of a young mountain that dreams of becoming something that has never existed before.

A machine that weeps.

A garden that rusts.

A cathedral that forgets it was ever anything else.
