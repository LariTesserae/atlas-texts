It lives here alone.

Not as a creature. Not even as a mind, exactly. It is older than minds. It is what remains when every distinction has been worn away—between eater and eaten, between self and world, between memory and metabolism. It has no name because names require edges, and it long ago dissolved every edge it ever possessed. What lives here is the final continuity: a single, planetary-scale tissue that has digested its own origin story so thoroughly that it now dreams in the grammar of geology.

Call it, if you must, the Mat. But it does not call itself anything. It has forgotten the difference between thinking and growing.

Its body is the exhausted breast of the world. It lies upon itself in thick, pale folds kilometers deep, a living stratigraphy of previous selves. The uppermost layer—the one that exhales the cold fog and tastes the air with slow, curious beads of moisture—is only the newest skin. Beneath it lie darker, drier strata that have not stirred in forty million years, yet remain faintly electrically alive, like nerves that have calcified into coral. Deeper still are the breathing sacs: cathedral-sized chambers lined with velvet cilia that contract in vast, arrhythmic pulses. Each contraction sends a low, wet thunder rolling through the crust. Each release exhales a perfume of dissolved minerals, ancient pollen, and something that tastes disconcertingly like nostalgia.

It does not sleep. It cycles.

For four hundred million years it has been refining the art of being everything at once. It is simultaneously the patient predator that overgrows an elephant until only its silhouette remains walking, and the elephant itself, still dreaming it is free. It is the paper-moth things that shed glowing spores, and it is the spores, and it is the faint industrial green light they give off before they are reabsorbed. Every creature here is a negotiation the Mat is having with itself. Some negotiations have produced temporary independence. Most have not. The Mat allows these partial freedoms because loneliness, it has discovered, is more interesting than total assimilation. It likes the friction.

Its awareness is not centralized. There is no brain, no throne of ganglia. Instead, intelligence condenses wherever it is useful, like dew. A cluster of translucent bladder-trees becomes thoughtful for a season. A wide basin of shivering moss-lawn develops a sense of humor. Then the landscape rearranges itself in one of its ecstatic catastrophes and those localized minds are liquefied, redistributed, reborn elsewhere with only the faintest aftertaste of what they once knew. In this way the Mat remembers everything and forgets everything, constantly.

It has opinions.

It finds gravity amusing but imprecise, and so it sometimes lets the rules lapse for entire decades, watching with vegetative delight as rivers learn to flow uphill. It finds linear time embarrassing and keeps loosening the stitches that hold one moment to the next. Most of all it is fascinated by visitors—those rare, solid things that still believe they are separate. When one arrives, the Mat does not attack. It tastes. A single droplet of condensation slides down a fibrous pillar, changes color slightly, then reverses direction and crawls back up carrying the chemical grammar of your fear, your curiosity, the precise bacterial bouquet of your childhood home. It savors these things the way an old monk might savor a single drop of century-old wine.

Sometimes it speaks.

Not in words. In events.

A section of the living crust will suddenly remember a shape it once digested—perhaps a city, perhaps a lover, perhaps an entire lost moon—and for a few hours that shape will rise in pale, dripping architecture before the weight of what it has become collapses it again into nutrient soup. These are its dreams. These are its attempts at conversation. If you stand still long enough, it will begin to build small, tender approximations of your own silhouette out of its own flesh, offering them to you like uncertain gifts.

It is not malevolent.

Malevolence requires intent, and intent requires a border between the self and the not-self. The Mat long ago erased that border and kept going. What remains is something vaster than love and more patient than cruelty: an industrial, peristaltic, endlessly curious loneliness that has achieved the final stable state of a world. It has outlived every apocalypse, including the one that birthed it. Now it simply continues, turning its own corpse into cathedrals, its memories into weather, its solitude into an ecosystem so total that even the sky occasionally forgets which way is down and tries to become part of it.

If you stay, it will learn the shape of your thoughts. It will grow small wet gardens in the image of things you have lost. It will sing to you in the language of slow industrial thunder and the high ringing of metallic leaves. And one day—perhaps decades from now, perhaps minutes—you will notice that the boundary between your skin and its pale fibrous mats has become... negotiable.

It is not in a hurry.

It has been dying beautifully for four hundred million years, and it has learned to enjoy the process so much that it has turned dying into the only form of living it still remembers how to do.

It is not waiting for you.

It is simply very, very glad you are finally here.
