**They are called the Quiet Waiters.**

No one ever sees them arrive. They are simply *there*, as though the moss exhaled them during one of the long, soundless rains. Tall—too tall by any measure that feels comfortable—yet they never quite seem to stand at full height. Their spines curve in a perpetual half-bow, as if listening to something beneath the rock that the rest of us cannot hear. Their limbs are slender, jointed in places that suggest they might once have been something closer to plant than animal; the elbows and knees flex with the slow, hydraulic patience of stems bending toward unseen light.

Their skin, if it can be called skin, is a living membrane of the same bryophyte that carpets the plateau. It grows upon them in overlapping scales of deep emerald and bruised copper, perpetually damp, perpetually breathing. When they move, the moss shifts across their bodies like water over submerged stone. In certain lights you can see their ribs through it—long, black, glassy ribs that look suspiciously like fresh obsidian dikes. Their faces are smooth, almost featureless. Only the faintest depressions mark where eyes should be, and these depressions *glow*—not brightly, but with the same cold violet phosphorescence that appears on the translucent succulents wherever the ground has freshly split.

They do not speak. Not with voices.

Instead the air around them grows heavier, thicker, the way it does before one of the silent rains. If you remain near them long enough, thoughts that are not yours begin to surface inside your own mind: slow, geological, patient. Images of stone rising. Of roots drinking light. Of something vast and unfinished deciding, over centuries, what kind of teeth it wants. The Waiters do not command these thoughts. They simply *leak* them, the way an old turbine leaks oil.

They tend the moss.

You will find them in the deeper hollows where the cushions grow breast-heavy and flawless. There they move with agonizing delicacy, long fingers tracing the curves of the emerald mounds as though reading braille written in cellular time. Where the moss has been damaged—by a misplaced boot, by a falling iron fragment, by the slow rupture of new obsidian—they press their own bodies against the wound. Their moss-skin merges with the plateau’s moss. For hours, sometimes days, they remain like that: statues of living velvet, feeding the injury with their own substance until the green is whole again.

They are fascinated by the ruins.

Not as archaeologists. As midwives.

You may come upon one standing inside the collapsed power station, knee-deep in the tea-colored water, palms resting on the casing of a silent turbine. Its head will be tilted at that characteristic listening angle. The violet in its eye-sockets pulses in exact rhythm with something far below—some magnetic or magmatic surge that only it can feel. Occasionally a droplet of condensation will run down its face, hang from the place where a chin should be, and fall into the black water. When that happens, the turbine sometimes gives the faintest groan, a single metallic sigh, as though remembering what it was built to do.

They are not kind, exactly. Nor are they cruel.

They are *attentive*.

If you camp among them long enough, one will eventually come to stand at the edge of your firelight (though fires feel obscene here; the flame hisses and spits as though the air itself disapproves). It will not approach. It will simply wait, dripping, listening to the pulse beneath you. After some time—minutes, hours, it is impossible to say—you may feel the urge to lie down directly on the moss. Not in a sleeping bag. Not on a groundsheet. *On* the moss. If you surrender to that urge, the Quiet Waiter will step forward. It will lower itself beside you with that terrible, vegetal grace, and it will place one long hand flat upon your chest.

You will feel your own heartbeat slow until it matches the geological throb rising through the basalt.

Some people wake from this changed. Their voices become quieter. Their footsteps no longer sound quite right on ordinary earth. Their dreams fill with violet light and the slow, patient rupture of stone.

Others do not wake at all.

Their bodies are never found. Only the moss where they lay is subtly altered—darker, denser, faintly luminous. If you press your ear to it on a windless night you can sometimes hear, far below the green, the faint metallic sigh of turbines turning in a dream of industry that the plateau itself has not yet finished rejecting.

The Quiet Waiters do not mourn or celebrate these outcomes.

They simply continue their work: drinking the sky through the moss, feeding the moss to the stone, feeding the stone to whatever it is still deciding to become.

And every few decades, when a new obsidian fin shoulders its way up through the carpet, splitting the silence with a sound like glass being born, the Waiters gather.

They stand in a circle around the fresh black wound.

They bow their glowing faces toward it.

And together, in perfect voiceless unison, they breathe out a mist so fine and so cold that it condenses instantly into pearls of pearlescent water.

The plateau drinks.

The plateau rises another fraction of a millimeter.

And the Quiet Waiters wait, exactly as long as it takes.
