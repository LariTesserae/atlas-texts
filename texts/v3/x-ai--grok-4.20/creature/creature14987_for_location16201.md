**The Wetworks has no single inhabitant. It has many, and they are all the same creature wearing different skins.**

What lives here is not quite a person, not quite a machine, and not quite a plant. The locals call them **the Soaked**, though never to their faces. They do not mind the name. Most have already forgotten their own.

You will not see them at first. They are the color of wet concrete and bruised iron; their silhouettes blur into the dripping walls the way breath blurs on cold glass. Only when one of them moves do you realize the moss on that particular stretch of wall has eyes. Slow, patient, amphibious eyes the color of motor oil.

They are tall, most of them, because the Wetworks keeps stretching them. Their limbs have grown too long from years of reaching through scaffolding and between half-drowned girders. Their fingers end in soft, flattened pads like the undersides of newts, excellent for gripping slick rebar or stroking the warm flank of a sleeping crane until it sighs and shifts its enormous weight for them. Their skin is not skin. It is a living interface—half rubbery lichen, half ferro-cement—forever beaded with condensation that never quite falls off. When they bleed, the blood is translucent and smells of lilies left too long in rainwater.

Their faces are the hardest part to look at. The features have been gently erased and redrawn so many times by humidity and forgetting that each Soaked carries a different compromise of a face. One may have the high, proud cheekbones of an old factory owner, but the mouth has become a soft gill-slit that opens sideways when they speak. Another keeps the jawline of a missing engineer, but where the eyes should be there are now two smooth, reflective pools no deeper than a coin. They reflect whatever the Wetworks wants you to see: your own face underwater, or the silhouette of a machine that hasn’t been invented yet.

They do not sleep. Instead they enter long, dreaming stands—leaning motionless against a wall or hanging by their padded hands from conveyor bridges while the moss slowly grows over them. During these stands the city finishes its thoughts through them. You can hear it if you get close: a low wet clicking inside their chests like a typewriter submerged in a bathtub. When they wake again, new streets have usually appeared overnight, or an old smokestack has quietly become a cathedral.

Some still wear scraps of their previous lives the way other people wear jewelry. A rusted foreman’s badge grown into the flesh of a collarbone. A woman whose hair has become living copper wiring that sparks softly when she is amused. A child (though “child” is a difficult word here) whose left arm ends in a lovingly maintained torque wrench that opens and closes with the slow pulse of a heart.

They speak rarely, and when they do the words arrive half-drowned. Their voices sound like someone trying to talk while being gently held underwater by something that loves them. Sentences surface, then sink again. Whole ideas are abandoned mid-breath because the Wetworks has changed its mind about what should be said.

They are not cruel. They are not kind. They are unfinished, like everything else. Some still try to maintain the old machines, murmuring to blast furnaces as though calming nervous horses. Others have given themselves entirely to the vegetation and can now only be told apart from the hanging gardens by the fact that they occasionally weep clear, fragrant sap when they remember their mothers’ names.

At night they gather in the mirrored courtyards where the fish-tools surface. They sit in concentric rings, knees drawn up, letting the turquoise water lap at their ankles. Sometimes they sing. The song has no words and no fixed melody; it is simply the sound the city would make if the city could clear its throat and apologize for existing. The metallic moths rise in slow spirals around them like dark incense.

If you stay long enough, one of them will eventually approach you. It will not speak at first. It will simply stand close—close enough that you feel the cold sweat of its body mingling with your own—and study your face the way a dreaming building studies its scaffolding.

Then, very softly, it will ask whether you have any parts you no longer need.

Because the Wetworks is still deciding what it wants to become, and it has grown fond of the shape of your hands, or the way your voice cracks when you are afraid, or the particular rhythm of your pulse. It would like to try those on for a while.

Most people only stay a season.

The Soaked have been here since the first foundation stone was poured. They have become the negotiation itself: the wet argument between rock, machine, and water given slow, patient, sorrowful flesh.

They do not mind if you leave.

They have all the time in the world, and they are still deciding what they want to become next.

Sometimes, on particularly quiet nights, you can hear the entire basin breathing in unison with them. A slow, damp, geological inhale.

And then the long, patient, moss-softened exhale that sounds almost like a name no one has agreed upon yet.
