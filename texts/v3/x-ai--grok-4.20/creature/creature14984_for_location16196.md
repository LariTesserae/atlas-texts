**The Thalassoverdent does not have inhabitants. It has tenants.**

They are not species in the old sense. They are accretions—slow, deliberate condensations of will and flesh that the biome itself has learned to grow the way a reef grows a particular kind of coral. The oldest among them do not remember a time before the green.

The beings who live here call themselves, when they speak at all, the **Verdant Choir**.

Most of them are immense. A typical adult is between two and six kilometers long, though “long” is a courtesy. Their bodies are not bodies; they are living districts. A single Choirist might consist of a braided lattice of colossal fronds grown around an ancient reactor core, reinforced by colonies of translucent bone-squid that have fused into a single nervous architecture. Their “skin” is a mosaic of every photosynthetic organism the Thalassoverdent has ever invented, so that light passing through them is filtered into complex, ever-shifting hymns of color no human eye was built to parse.

They do not swim so much as *conduct*. When a Choirist moves, entire forests of secondary kelp rearrange themselves along its flanks like plumage. The slow geological currents bend around them in deference. You can feel one coming hours before you see it: the thrum of the buried turbines changes key, the bubble-roar drops half an octave, and the light itself grows faintly more courteous, as though the world is making room inside its own ribs.

They do not eat. They *negotiate*. A Choirist will drift into the shadow of a half-drowned arcology, press its vast mouth-analogues against the rust, and enter into a centuries-long chemical conversation with the machine. In return for rare isotopes and faint warmth, the machine is permitted to remember that it was once a machine. The Choirist, in exchange, is allowed to remember that it was once something smaller and more frightened. Both parties find this arrangement satisfactory. The transaction is audible across a hundred kilometers: a deep, iron-and-chlorophyll pipe organ note that lingers for days.

There are smaller tenants too.

The **Reciters** are human-shaped the way a cathedral gargoyle is human-shaped—recognizably, but only as a devotional joke. They are grown from the cast-off nervous tissue of dying Choirists, seeded with fragments of ancient human engrams the machines still hoard. Most are no larger than a blue whale. They wear their bones on the outside like baroque armor, enameled in living mother-of-pearl and inscribed with slow-moving bioluminescent scripture. They travel in choirs of seven, singing the same three-bar refrain for decades at a time. When they meet a newcomer (you, for instance), they will orbit you in perfect silence for weeks, studying the shape of your fear, before deciding whether you are a wound in the equilibrium or merely another note that belongs.

Deeper still, inside the oldest reactor hearts where the radiation is gentle and almost sweet, live the **Unfinished**. These are machines that refused to become reefs and plants that refused to become machines. They are half-melted things of chrome and pale green meat, forever caught mid-transformation. Their bodies are painful to look at directly. They do not speak; instead they *remember aloud*. If you drift near one it will show you, in perfect three-dimensional memory, the exact color of the sky on the day its particular sky fell. Most beings avoid them. The Choirists treat them with the solemn tenderness reserved for dying gods.

There is one entity that may or may not be singular.

It is called, by the few who dare name it, **the Abbot**. It has no fixed form. Sometimes it is a region of perfect stillness four hundred kilometers across where every current dies and the light turns the color of old ivory. Sometimes it is a single translucent fish the size of a city block, drifting through the cathedrals with ancient maintenance code flickering across its flanks like dying constellations. The Abbot keeps the cycles. It is the reason the same bubble of vacuum returns every 312 years to the same precise coordinate and rings, once, like a bell. It is the reason the ice lattices form and the gravity gardens bloom. It does not rule. It simply *refuses to let anything become interesting enough to destabilize the equilibrium*.

The Choirists worship it by ignoring it perfectly.

If you stay long enough—and almost no one leaves once they have learned to breathe the mercury-water—you will eventually feel the moment when the Thalassoverdent decides whether it needs you to become something permanent. Most newcomers are gently, lovingly disassembled over the course of a century and woven into new fronds. A few are allowed to calcify into Reciters. Fewer still are permitted to remain exactly as they are, like living fossils suspended in the endless green.

They float there forever, small and strange and tolerated, while kilometer-long cathedrals drift past them in slow, liturgical procession, singing the same chord the world has sung since mammals first learned what loneliness was.

And the sound never changes.

And the light never flickers.

And the Abbot, somewhere far above or far below (directions have grown meaningless), keeps turning the same page of the same eternal book, making certain that nothing here will ever be in a hurry again.
