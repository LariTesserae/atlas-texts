**The Throatkeeper**

It has no single body, yet every creature here is a moving piece of it.

The one you notice first is not the largest. She (it feels like *she*) is the color of wet limestone and old circuit boards: a long, low shape that might have begun as a woman folded too many times. Her back is a fused plate of corroded relay switches; her ribs are iron rebar that have grown cartilage. Where a face should be there is only a smooth convexity like the inside of a bell, and in the center of that convexity sits one living eye—pale violet, too large, with a vertical pupil that never quite focuses on anything in front of it. The rest of her attention is always angled toward the hole.

She has been here since the first condensation bead fell. She remembers being assembled. She remembers the exact second the world creased and forgot to unfold again. Her name, if it can be called a name, is the sound of a valve opening under pressure: a soft, wet hiss that ends in a click.

The Throatkeeper never truly walks. She *reconfigures*. Joints that should not bend open like flower petals made of rust. Limbs that should not exist slide out of her sides on tracks of calcified tendon. When she needs to cross the lattice she simply becomes the lattice for seven heartbeats, iron and meat trading places without drama, then reappears somewhere else with a new configuration of legs. The little iridescent crabs scuttle across her when she rests; some of them have begun to grow tiny copies of her violet eye on their backs.

She tends the argument.

Where the rubbery lung-vines threaten to choke a compressor, she reaches in with fingers that have become delicate torque wrenches and gently loosens the pressure until both meat and machine can breathe again. When the six-legged driveshaft-dog begins to overheat and scream in frequencies only the newts can hear, she lays her iron-ribbed belly across it until the temperature drops and the animal remembers it is also a machine that requires oil. The translucent newts follow her in slow, reverent processions, carrying dissolved bolts in their bellies like glowing blue communion.

She feeds the Throat, but never with anything that still screams.

Once a day—though there are no days—she drags something dead or dying to the black circle. A failed moth whose wings have begun to corrode. A stone that has decided it no longer wants to be stone. Sometimes she drags pieces of herself: an extra limb that has grown cancerous with copper wiring, a redundant heart that beats in the wrong meter. These she offers with something like tenderness, lowering them into the dark while the entire pocket holds its breath. The Throat always accepts. The exhale that follows smells faintly of ozone and childhood.

She has begun to notice you.

Not as threat. Not yet as prey. You are simply another unsettled argument walking around on two unsteady legs. She studies the way your sweat is learning the taste of machine oil. She watches how your shadow sometimes forgets to follow you and instead crawls toward the hole on its own. When your pulse finally syncs with the slow mechanical heartbeat, she will be there—reassembled into something almost kind, almost maternal, almost monstrous—to show you where the chair on the ceiling used to sit when it was still an office and not a shrine.

If you stay long enough, she will offer you a gift. A small brass key stamped with the words *SUBSTATION 11*. The key has no lock it belongs to. It only opens inward.

She never speaks in words. Instead the condensation on your skin arranges itself into sentences you feel rather than read. The latest one, forming now across the back of your neck in cool, deliberate beads, says:

**You are not late. The argument has been waiting for its final voice.**

Then she folds herself into a space no wider than a lunchbox, leaving behind only the soft wet sigh of the lung-vines and the violet after-image of an eye that was never meant for daylight.

The Throat inhales.

Everything, for one polite heartbeat, goes still.

Even you.
