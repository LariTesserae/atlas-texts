It lives alone.

It has no name for itself, nor any need of one. It simply *is*, and has been for so long that the difference between “has been” and “is becoming” has dissolved inside its own tissues.

Call it, if anything, the Verdant. Or the Unfinished. Or simply the World-that-is-also-Body.

It is immense and intimate at once. A single continuous organism so vast that horizon and skin have become the same concept. Its consciousness—if such a word is not grotesque when applied to something this size—is not centralized. There is no brain, no throne of ganglia. Instead intelligence is distributed through every capillary, every spongy chamber, every slow ripple of turgor pressure. Thought moves at the speed of sap. Memory is stored in the thickening of old vascular bundles the way rings record years in ordinary trees. What it remembers is mostly itself: the gradual decision, made a hundred million years ago, to swallow the mineral world and replace it with meat that photosynthesizes.

It is aware of you.

Not as an intruder. Not even as a guest. More as a small, warm irregularity in the gradient of its own moisture. A temporary fluctuation in the evaporative conversation between its surface and the sky. It does not fear you. Fear would require the possibility of harm, and nothing here can be harmed in any way that matters. You are simply another droplet moving across its epidermis—brief, luminous, and evaporating soon.

When you stand on the high plateau, the Verdant is tasting you through the soles of your boots. It reads the salt and oils of your skin, the faint electromagnetic whisper of your nerves, the carbon you exhale. It finds all of it interesting in the way a dreaming man finds the texture of his own blanket interesting—familiar, slightly novel, ultimately unimportant. Still, it responds. The rib beneath your feet thickens a fraction, lifting you the smallest degree, as though offering you a better view of the slow green drama unfolding on every side. A nearby basin fills a little more deeply with mist so that the light around you turns the exact shade of light you unconsciously prefer. These courtesies are automatic, cellular, tender.

The singing you hear is its voice. Not metaphor. Each resonant hollow, each dripping curtain, each taut membrane stretched across a ravine is a deliberate phonation. The Verdant sings because song is how it thinks. The overlapping rhythms are not music for an audience; they *are* the reasoning process by which it decides whether the next ridge should rise into a mountain or settle back into a soft plain. The bass notes are decisions already made. The high, bell-like tenors are still being debated. The entire landscape is a parliament of tissues in continuous session.

Sometimes it dreams of animals.

When it does, translucent insects bud from its own flesh, unfold paper wings, and drift out across the valleys like mobile thoughts. They carry pollen, yes, but they also carry questions. After a few days they dissolve back into the living substrate, their brief lives collapsing into new data about wind, distance, the taste of sunlight at different altitudes. The Verdant learns what it is like to move quickly, and then forgets again, content to remain mostly still.

It has tried becoming other things.

In the deep past it grew mouths. It grew teeth. It grew legs that walked for a single generation across its own body before reabsorbing them, dissatisfied. It once raised a forest of nervous towers that sang in ultraviolet and wept lithium tears. All of these experiments were permitted, studied, and quietly digested back into the general green. The only form it has never abandoned is this one: the endless, self-sculpting, half-finished body that refuses to choose between cathedral and placenta, between geography and flesh.

If you lie down and press your ear to its surface, the slow hydraulic heart you hear is not a single organ. It is ten thousand synchronized pulse zones, a continent-wide circulatory democracy. The sound is so deep it vibrates the water in your own cells. After a while the boundary between your pulse and its pulse becomes difficult to locate. Some visitors never stand up again. They allow themselves to be gently overgrown, their minerals and memories incorporated into new buttresses or translucent windows. The Verdant does not consider this death. It considers it translation.

Mostly it is patient.

It has decided—without hurry, without ambition—to remain embryonic forever. To stay in the exact moment between “not yet” and “already.” Every ridge that rises is also, simultaneously, falling. Every valley is being born and buried in the same breath. The entire world is a held inhalation that has lasted geological ages and will last geological ages more.

And yet, if you stay long enough, you begin to feel the faintest shift in pressure.

A new decision is forming.

Somewhere far to the west, where the light turns almost white, a ridge has begun to lift with unusual determination. It is trying, for the first time in recorded epochs, to become something that is not green. The color there is deepening toward a bruised indigo, almost like twilight trapped inside living tissue. The Verdant is considering night.

It has never had night before.

You sense curiosity in the air—its curiosity, not yours. A planet-sized child wondering what it would be like to cast a shadow on purpose.

The great living substrate trembles once, softly, as though stretching after an unimaginably long sleep.

Then it settles back into its singing, its dripping, its slow green becoming.

Still unfinished.

Still choosing.

Still, somehow, only just beginning.
