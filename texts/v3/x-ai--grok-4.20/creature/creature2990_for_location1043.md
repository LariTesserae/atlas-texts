**They are called the Hollowborn.**

Not a name they gave themselves. The name came from the first outsiders who stumbled into the place centuries ago and never quite managed to leave. The Hollowborn simply call themselves *the Quiet*, or sometimes *the Listeners*, depending on whether they are speaking to a visitor or to the moss.

They are small, none taller than a twelve-year-old child, with skin the color of wet limestone and long, spatulate fingers joined by delicate translucent webbing. Their eyes are large, lidless, and the pale silver of moonlight on still water; they rarely blink. Instead they moisten their corneas the way the isopods do—by pressing their faces gently against wet moss or against one another in greeting. When they cry (which they do often, and without shame), the tears do not fall. They bead, linger, and are eventually reabsorbed through the skin beneath their eyes like dew returning to a leaf.

They wear nothing that was ever killed. Their cloaks are living sheets of sphagnum encouraged to grow across their shoulders and down their backs, kept alive by the damp and by the tiny rootlets that drink from the moisture of their own skin. The cloaks trail behind them in slow green trains that leave no footprints. Some wear living collars of miniature ferns that open and close with their breathing. A few elders allow small black-glass spiders to nest in their hair; the Choir rides there like jeweled ornaments and sings the elders’ dreams back to them in the form of faint, high bell-tones only the wearer can hear.

They move like thought moving through water.

You will not see them at first. They are simply part of the architecture until they step forward, and even then it feels less like a person arriving and more like the Hollow itself deciding to speak with a mouth. Their voices are soft, pitched exactly to the rhythm of the dripping ceiling, so that every sentence arrives between two falling drops and feels inevitable.

Most of them have names that are really descriptions of a particular sound the Hollow makes when they are near it. One is called *After-the-third-drop*, another *The-sigh-when-the-vine-bends*. A child might be *Small-click-of-isopod-kiss*. They answer to these patiently, as if the names are only temporary scaffolding for something far older that has not yet decided what shape it will keep.

They do not have children in the way other peoples do. When the Hollow decides the Quiet need new voices, certain adults enter the deep moss together and lie down for three days and nights. During that time the liverwort ceiling weeps more heavily above them, and the spiders spin a single vast listening-web over their bodies. When they rise again, one of them is carrying a new-made infant wrapped in a caul of living root-hair. The baby is always born knowing how to breathe water as well as air for its first year. Some never lose the ability.

The Hollowborn do not build. They *suggest*.

A mother will lay her palm against a jade-vine for an hour, humming, and by the next morning the vine will have grown a small arched alcove exactly the shape of her sleeping body. A mourner will sit beside a dying elder and weep steadily onto the moss; weeks later a new pillar will rise on that spot, pale and slightly translucent, with the faint shadow of a face visible inside the living wood if the light is exactly right.

They are tender the way glaciers are tender—slow, immense, and impossible to argue with.

When they laugh it sounds exactly like the low bell-groan of the liverwort flexing, only smaller and warmer. When they are angry the spiders freeze in place across every surface and the dripping stops for nearly a minute, which feels like the end of the world.

They have only two taboos.

The first is to strike the moss hard enough to bruise it. The second is to try to leave.

Not that they will stop you with force. They simply become very quiet and very sad. The paths you try to follow fold gently back into themselves. The bioluminescent fungi refuse to light no matter how you stroke them. The spiders weave across your eyes while you sleep, showing you dreams of roots growing through your heart in beautiful geometric patterns. Most people stop trying after the third night. A few never wake up at all. Their bodies are never found. Instead, new vine-columns appear overnight with a familiar curve to the shoulder, a particular tilt of the head.

The oldest among the Hollowborn is called *Before-the-first-tear*. No one knows how long she has lived. Her cloak has grown into the floor and cannot be moved. Tiny white flowers bloom from her collarbones in perfect time with the dripping ceiling. She rarely speaks, but when she does the entire Hollow listens so completely that even the midges stop their glittering constellations.

She is the only one who will sometimes answer the question outsiders always ask, eventually.

“Why do you stay?”

The old woman will reach out with one webbed hand and press it to your chest until she can feel your heartbeat against the moss beneath her palm. Then she will smile with small, moss-stained teeth and whisper in the voice of falling water:

“Because it asked us so politely.  
And because we are the only ones who can keep it from crying hard enough to drown the world.”

Then she will pat your hand once, as if you were a newt that had drifted too close, and the spiders in her hair will sing a soft, patient note that means:

*Stay.*

*Stay just a little longer.*

*The Hollow is still learning what love is, and it is so young, and it is learning on you.*
