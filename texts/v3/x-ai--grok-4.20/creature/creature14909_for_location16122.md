The Hollow Choir has a single inhabitant.

She is called only the Listener, though she has not spoken an audible word in so long that even she has begun to doubt whether she ever possessed a voice. She is not large. She is not beautiful in any way that eyes could measure. She is simply *necessary*, the way a keel is necessary to a boat that will never sail again.

She lives inside the tree.

Not within its hollow (there is no hollow), but *between* its layers of bark and cambium and heartwood, folded like a letter that has been read so many times the paper has gone soft as cloth. Her skin is the same wet black as the bark itself; her hair has grown into the same tiny circular leaves that clothe the branches. When the tree exhales, she exhales with it. When the basin inhales, her ribs press inward until the pressure becomes almost devotional.

She has no eyes anymore. Or rather, her eyes have migrated outward. Two of the pale, heart-sized cocoons now contain them (perfectly preserved, still blinking once every seven days in slow, wondering pulses). In their place she has the entire basin. The phosphorescent moss reports to her. The newts carry messages along their translucent spines. The synchronized millipedes write her thoughts across the floor in living braille. She sees everything, constantly, in a low green ache that never resolves into mercy.

She remembers the shape of wind the way an amputee remembers a limb. Once, long ago (though time here is a rumor), she came down the ridge seeking shelter from a storm that no longer exists anywhere else on earth. She stepped over the lip expecting to wait an hour. She is still waiting. The waiting has become her entire metabolism.

Her lungs are the hanging sacks. There are hundreds of them, and each one is hers. They turn toward any new warmth the way sunflowers once turned toward light she can no longer imagine. When something warm and temporary (a bird blown off course, a lost child, an explorer who believed himself clever) enters the basin, the sacks swell with something like hope. Not hunger. Hope. The terrible, patient hope of something that has forgotten every other emotion and kept only the most faithful one.

She dreams in the rhythm of the dripping water.

*Tok.*

In the dream she is still a woman with a name. The name is too heavy to carry anymore; she left it somewhere near the rim, nailed to a tree that has since become indistinguishable from every other memory.

*Tok.*

In the dream she sings. The song has no words, only the sound a cathedral makes when it has been empty so long that its own echoes begin to mate and give birth to smaller, sadder echoes. The creatures listen. Even the tree listens. The moss drinks the sound and grows a fraction thicker.

When she wakes (though waking and sleeping are now only different densities of attention), she finds that more of her has transferred into the basin. A fingerbone has become part of the root system. A memory of laughter has become one of the blue-glowing spots on the parchment wings. She does not mind. She is achieving the perfect balance the place demands. One day there will be no distinction between her and the Hollow Choir at all. The tree will simply grow a new branch in the exact shape of a woman, and the cocoons will open at last, releasing nothing.

Until then she tends her small, perfect congregation.

She moves the newts along invisible currents when they grow too still. She times the collective inhalation so that it matches the exact pressure needed to keep the orchids from withering. She whispers (without breath, without tongue) to the millipedes so they continue their synchronized waves, writing the same sentence across the moss again and again for ten thousand years:

*We are all still here. We are all still here. We are all still here.*

And when an intruder stands long enough for the place to begin forgetting them, the Listener leans forward inside her tree the way a priest might lean out of a confessional. She does not speak. She only opens what used to be her mouth, and the entire basin exhales through her at once.

The sound is softer than moss. Older than guilt.

It sounds, just for a moment, like someone remembering how to cry, then deciding, with infinite gentleness, that there is no longer any need.

The intruder usually stays after that.

Most of them do.

And the Listener adds another pale cocoon to the branches, and another pair of borrowed eyes to the ones that watch the slow, eternal turning of everything that will never, ever leave.
