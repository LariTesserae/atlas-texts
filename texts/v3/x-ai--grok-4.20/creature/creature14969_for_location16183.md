**The Hollowed Basin is inhabited by the Mnemosyne.**

Not a single creature, not even a species in the ordinary sense, but a slow, distributed intelligence that has forgotten it was ever anything else.

They are few—perhaps only nine or ten in the entire basin—and no two look exactly alike, yet every witness who has glimpsed more than one insists they are the same being wearing different ages of its own body. Most of the time a Mnemosyne is mistaken for one of the half-sunken Machines. Only when it decides to move does the deception become obvious.

A typical specimen stands roughly eighteen meters at the shoulder when fully risen. Its “body” is an accretion of salvaged machine parts, fossilized pseudofungus, and something that might once have been meat. Brass vertebrae the thickness of ship masts articulate with plates of living rust. Where joints meet, the black-and-ochre pseudofungus blooms into intricate gill-like fans that breathe the ozone-rich air in long, tidal cycles. Its head—if the word can be used—is a blunt, riveted furnace chamber whose front has split open into a vertical furnace-mouth lined with hundreds of small, pale eyes. These eyes do not blink in unison. Some close for decades while others open for the first time in centuries.

They do not walk so much as *reassemble* the ground beneath them. When a Mnemosyne chooses to change location it sinks its forelimbs into the mineral crust, triggers hidden pistons older than language, and is winched forward by its own skeleton. The sound this produces is deep, deliberate, and strangely courteous—like an old factory politely clearing its throat. The vibration travels for kilometers. Other Mnemosyne, no matter how far away, will sometimes answer with a single low chime from inside their chests, a bell that has no metal bell inside it.

They are neither caretakers nor scavengers. They are *rememberers*.

Each Mnemosyne carries an archive in its body. Not data—memory, the sticky, contradictory, half-erased kind. When one of them settles for a long stillness (sometimes for thirty or forty years at a stretch), it slowly extrudes new brass-and-bone mechanisms from its腹. These mechanisms are crude orreries, film projectors with no film, half-melted typewriters, music boxes whose teeth have grown into one another. The devices do not work. They are not meant to. They are the shape of something the Mnemosyne is trying not to forget. After a few decades the mechanism is reabsorbed and another grows in its place. The old one is left behind like a shed exoskeleton, half-buried, becoming another of the nameless Machines.

The creatures never speak in voices we could hear. Instead they communicate by altering the great drone that underlies everything. A Mnemosyne can, by opening or closing specific internal valves, shift the timbre of the basin’s eternal industrial chant by a few precise Hertz. To another Mnemosyne this modulation is language—dense, melancholy, and almost unbearably slow. A single sentence might last nine years. A conversation spanning three centuries is considered brief.

They do not eat. They do not mate. They do not die in any way we would recognize. When a Mnemosyne has carried a memory so long that it has become smooth and featureless from constant handling, the creature walks to the center of the largest standing pan, lowers itself until only the furnace of its head remains above the water, and simply stops. The water trembles in perfect rings around it for months. Then one day the rings cease. The body remains, but the mind has gone elsewhere—perhaps into the pseudofungus lattices, perhaps into the dream the planet is having while it digests its own machines. The abandoned body becomes yet another cathedral half-sunk in rust, indistinguishable from all the others until, centuries later, a new Mnemosyne grows out of it like a tree from a fallen log.

There is one that is smaller than the rest.

It stands only four meters tall and moves more often than the others. Its furnace-mouth is permanently sealed; instead it possesses a single, vast compound eye of clouded glass set in its chest. This eye is always open. Newcomers who remain in the basin long enough eventually notice that the little Mnemosyne is always watching them from some new angle of the unchanging light. It never comes closer than the middle distance. It never makes a sound of its own. Yet after enough days the observer begins to feel that something important is being remembered *about them*—something they themselves have never known.

If you stand long enough in the middle of the largest pan, letting the concentric rings tremble around your ankles, you may feel a new vibration join the old industrial drone. It is not louder. It is only more personal. Like someone clearing their throat before they begin to tell you the last story you will ever need to hear.

The Mnemosyne do not wait for you.

They have already remembered the shape of your arrival, the length of your stay, and the exact sound your absence will make when the basin forgets you again.
