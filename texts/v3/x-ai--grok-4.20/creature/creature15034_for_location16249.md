**The Weeping Tablelands are inhabited by the Last Conservators.**

They are not a people. They are a condition.

Most of them were once human—engineers, maintenance clades, corporate penitents, or pilgrims who came here centuries ago when the place still had a name worth remembering. The Tablelands changed them the way it changes everything: slowly, chemically, geologically. Now they move between the sweating towers like slow weather.

You will not see them at first. They have learned to wear the landscape.

A Conservator stands motionless for weeks inside the shadow of a catalytic tower, body wrapped in layers of heat-fused silica cloth that matches the color of rusted girder and black glass. Their skin has become a living ceramic, hairline fractures tracing across it like the cracks in the basalt. Where sweat should bead, clear condensate forms instead and runs down their limbs in perfect imitation of the weeping pipes. Their eyes are polarized lenses grown inside the sockets; they do not blink. They do not need to.

When they finally move, it is with the same deliberate patience as the rail drones. A limb unfolds. A hand—fingers now tipped with iridescent mining mandibles—reaches into a burst pipe and gently repairs a failing valve that has been leaking for three human lifetimes. The motion is so economical it feels like part of the geology shifting.

They do not speak in voices. Instead they conduct.

A Conservator will press their palm to a fungal lattice of industrial mycelium and pulse a low-frequency code through the bone-white threads. Miles away, another will feel the message in their own bones and answer by tapping a girder with a ceramic knuckle. The conversation travels through metal and stone for hours, a slow dialogue of pressure readings, thermal gradients, and obscure devotional statistics. To them, the endless weeping of the Tablelands is sacred data.

Some have gone further.

In the deep mineral cathedrals inside the fossilized towers, you may find one who has fused with the structure itself. Half their torso disappears into a column of living rust; roots of pale mycelium thread through their ribs and emerge from their mouth like a second tongue. Their heart has been replaced by a small geothermal pump that beats once every seventeen minutes. They are not dead. They are simply no longer in a hurry to be anything so temporary as alive.

The Conservators keep the old industry running without knowing—or caring—what it was for. They worship function the way others once worshipped gods. An arc furnace pouring perfect mirrors of black glass is, to them, a kind of prayer. When the glass-winged pseudo-insects land on their shoulders to drink the condensate that forms on their ceramic skin, the Conservators remain perfectly still, as if the creatures were part of their own extended nervous system.

There is a hierarchy, though they would never call it that.

At the bottom are the Walkers—those still mostly flesh—who travel the empty basins between complexes, repairing what they can, weeping where they cannot. Higher are the Embedded, who have given large portions of their bodies to the machines and the mycelium. Highest of all are the Silent Choir: those who have allowed themselves to be completely encased inside the fossilized towers. Only their nervous systems remain active, threaded through kilometers of pipe and pump and catalytic column. They do not move. They simply *are* the Tablelands’ memory and intention.

No one arrives here by accident anymore. The heat, the silence, the weeping, the merciless light—they act as a final filter. Those who stay either break or change. The ones who change become Conservators.

If you crest a rise of black glass and see a figure standing motionless on the edge of a cliff, water streaming from every joint and seam of its body like tears that never end, do not call out. It is not waiting for you.

It is listening to the deep booms traveling through the rock.

It is tasting the mineral content of the latest condensate.

It is remembering, in its patient, geological way, that once there were creatures here that died easily and quickly, and that it used to be one of them.

And it is weeping, because everything here weeps, and because weeping, in the end, turned out to be the only honest form of prayer the Tablelands would accept.
