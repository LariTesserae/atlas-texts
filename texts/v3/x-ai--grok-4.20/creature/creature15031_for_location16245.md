The ones who live inside the Weeping Lattice do not have a name for themselves. Names require speech, and speech is a violence against the roar. They simply *are*, the way the pillars are, the way the dripping is.

They are small, almost child-sized by the measure of outer lands, yet their hands are grotesque in their generosity—broad, spatulate, with palms so thickly calloused they look like river stones polished by ten thousand years of current. The skin of those palms is perpetually damp; moisture beads on them exactly as it beads on the basalt, and when they press their hands to the rock they seem, for a moment, to be made of the same substance. Their bodies are hairless, poreless, the color of wet slate shot through with faint veins of paler quartz. Their eyes are large, lidless, and milky; they do not see light so much as they register shifts in the green radiance of the rice-white insects. When the swarms thin, the people’s eyes dim to the same faint glow, as though the light has simply moved inside their skulls.

They breathe through their skin. Their narrow chests barely rise. Most of the time their mouths stay closed, lips sealed by a natural film that keeps the constant moisture from flooding their lungs. Only when they are alone in the deepest hollows do they open their mouths, and then it is only to sing—low, sub-audible vibrations that make the fungal ropes tremble and the water on the walls arrange itself into momentary, shivering runes.

They call the act of shaping the stone *remembering*. Not building. Not carving. *Remembering*.

A young one (though age is difficult to judge here) will stand before a bulging mass of still-soft basalt for days, palms flat against it, eyes half-closed. The stone is still dreaming the memory of fire. The person does not force a shape; they remind the rock what it once almost was. Then they pour water in precise, ritual lines. The water is never cold. It is body-warm, almost blood-warm, drawn from the slow pools where the eyeless fish drift. Where the water touches, the stone sighs, softens, slumps. The person waits. Sometimes for weeks. When the new hollow has opened far enough, they step inside and press their entire body to the inner surface, as though returning to a womb that has just learned their outline. The stone closes slightly around them, remembering their shape, and a new room is born.

Every adult carries a small clay bowl of living fungal filaments at their belt. When the filaments are braided and left to dry they become stronger than hemp and nearly as flexible as muscle. These living ropes are used to climb, to lower one another into fissures, to hang cradles in which the very young sleep while the dripping falls on their translucent eyelids. The ropes themselves sometimes twitch, as if still remembering when they were only mold.

They have no leaders. Instead there are *Listeners*—those whose palms are largest and whose inner ears have grown so sensitive they can read the minute seismic gossip of the entire basin. A Listener will stand for hours with both hands and their forehead pressed to a pillar while the rest of the people wait in concentric rings around them. When the Listener finally steps back, their milky eyes are full of slow green light. They do not speak. They simply walk to a particular bulge of stone and lay their hands upon it, and everyone understands: this place will become a stair tomorrow. This other place will become a grave.

The graves are not holes. They are upward growths. When someone dies their body is carried to a chosen pillar and pressed into a shallow depression that has been coaxed open. Warm water is poured. The stone flows over them like black honey, swallowing them whole. Within days the pillar has grown a new, slightly heavier bulge, and the person is simply *remembered* into the lattice itself. Sometimes, years later, a Listener will press their palm to that exact spot and smile with closed lips, as though hearing an old joke.

The children—those who have not yet sealed their mouths with the protective film—learn by climbing. They swarm the spiraling ramps and fungal cables like pale salamanders, their enormous hands finding purchase where no purchase should exist. Their laughter is the only sound that ever competes with the water, and even then only for moments. The adults never hush them. The roar itself seems to gentle slightly around a child’s voice, as though the world, in its endless embryonic state, still has room to be surprised by something new.

At what outsiders would call “night” (though the light never changes), the people gather in the largest of the natural bowls. They do not sit. They stand in waist-deep warm water, bodies touching, and press their palms to one another’s backs and chests. In this way they pass the day’s vibrations through the entire community: the news of a new room finished, the rumor of a millipede migration, the slow, patient grief of a pillar that has finally hardened too much to shape. The sensation moves through them like a wave. Sometimes the entire gathering will begin to sway in unison, a single wet organism breathing with the rhythm of the dripping.

They are not happy.  
They are not sad.  
They are saturated.

And in their saturation they have achieved something few waking worlds ever manage: they have made peace with the moment of becoming and have decided, with enormous, dripping calm, never to finish.
