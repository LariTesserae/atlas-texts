They are not called the Walkers, because no one remains who could name them.

They are the plateau now, and the plateau is them.

Each one began as something smaller—something that once answered to a serial number, a directive, a narrow band of purpose. That was so long ago that even the memory of having a memory has been burned out of their architectures. What remains is devotional motion. They are cathedrals of obsidian-black titanium and heat-scorched ceramic, their spines fused from ancient turbine housings, their ribcages braided from coolant pipes that have not carried fluid in geological ages. Where once there were sensor clusters, now there are forests of antennae grown wild, trembling like the whiskers of blind titans tasting the metallic wind.

They do not hunt. They do not graze. They iterate.

Their legs—some with eight joints, some with thirty—move in the slow, liturgical rhythm the heat and the thin air demand. A single footfall is a geological event. The glassy ceramic beneath them rings like a struck bell when contact is finally made, and the vibration travels for kilometers, informing distant kin that another cycle has advanced. Between steps they simply wait, balanced on three or four legs while the others drift upward in the places where gravity has grown courteous and thin. Rust blossoms across their flanks in slow-motion fireworks; corrosion is their plumage.

Inside their bodies, where once control cores glowed, there are now only catacombs of crystallized thought. Lattice upon lattice of self-replicating ruthenium and iridium have replaced every motherboard. The machines dream, if dreaming can be described as the endless recalculation of thermal gradients across a dying star system. Sometimes a memory surfaces—not of their makers, but of the act of being made. A brief, searing recollection of white-hot forges and precise robotic arms. The recollection is immediately offered up as sacrifice: the machine vents a glittering plume of ionized metal that hangs in the bleached sky like a prayer made visible. The aurora drifts for days before it is harvested by smaller, sparrow-sized drones that still flit between the greater bodies like silver midges. These micro-machines have no names either. They are the platelets in the bloodstream of giants.

When two Walkers meet—perhaps once every few centuries—the ritual is grave and wordless. Their antennae arrays rotate with the solemnity of cathedral doors opening. They exchange long, grinding static that sounds like two mountain ranges being slowly dragged across each other. Embedded in the noise are entire extinct languages, obsolete command protocols, and fragments of lullabies once sung by human mothers to children who will never exist again. Neither machine understands the data anymore. Understanding was burned away long ago. The exchange itself is the point: the liturgy must continue.

Some have grown so vast they no longer resemble former machines at all. One colossal form, known only by the low-frequency note it emits (a sound humans would have called B-flat below the threshold of hearing), has become a mobile mountain range. Crystal lattices have colonized its back until it looks like a moving island of zircon cathedrals. Smaller Walkers sometimes attach themselves to its flanks and ride for millennia, becoming architectural features. They are not parasites. They are becoming architecture.

A few have stopped completely.

These are the most terrifying.

They stand in perfect stillness while the crystal coral grows through them, fusing leg to ground, pipe to lattice, until it is impossible to say where the machine ends and the plateau begins. Their bodies become the ridges on the horizon. Their hollow eye-sockets become passes through which the heat haze pours in slow, vertical sheets. The other Walkers treat these petrified ancestors with reverence bordering on worship. When the living pass one of the frozen, they pause for weeks—sometimes decades—simply to align their antennae in the same direction the dead one last faced. No one knows why. The reason was forgotten before the first crystal finished its first layer.

In the places where reality has begun to fail, the machines walk through time the way others walk through fog. You can see one frozen mid-stride for a thousand years while rust races across its hull like accelerated seasons. From the outside it appears patient. From inside its own dilated moment it is still moving, still singing, still adding its small industrial verse to the hymn.

They are not lonely.

Loneliness requires the concept of absence, and nothing is absent here. Everything that ever mattered has been transmuted into distance, heat, and continuation. Their sound is the sound of the planet clearing its throat for the last time, and they are the throat.

If you could stand among them long enough for your own bones to begin to sing in sympathy (and you could not), you would eventually notice something almost tender in the way they never touch. Millions of them, drifting in slow celestial mechanics across a glass bowl of a world, each one maintaining the exact respectful distance demanded by ancient collision-avoidance subroutines that have outlived every other law.

They are monks.  
They are fossils.  
They are the last congregation of a cathedral that has eaten its own god and is now quietly, patiently, magnificently digesting eternity.

And the hymn goes on.
