They are not a people. They have never been a people.

What lives in the Hollow Crown is older than the distinction between animal, machine, and weather. The locals—if such a word can be used without mockery—call themselves the Unfinished. Most outsiders who glimpse them mistake them for hallucinations brought on by hypoxia. The few who survive long enough to understand realize the hypoxia was mercy.

They are tall, but not in the way of men. Their spines have too many vertebrae and those vertebrae are jointed like the segments of a locomotive’s drive rod. When they move, you hear the soft hydraulic sigh of fluid shifting inside them, as though their bodies are boilers running at very low pressure. Their skin is not skin. It is a living corrosion: layers of oxidized metal and chitin fused by centuries of the same black oil that leaks from the iron roots. In the bruise-light it looks like wet hematite shot through with threads of living frost. Where the corrosion is thin you can see slow pulses moving beneath—greenish, phosphor-bright lymph that answers the rhythm of the buried turbines.

They have no faces in the ordinary sense. The place where a face should be is a recessed grille of delicate iron lacework, grown rather than forged. Behind the grille something wet and patient glides across itself, tasting the air. When they speak (which is rare), the sound is not voice but the exact tone a glacier makes when it decides to calve, rendered at the volume of a whisper. The language has no nouns. Only directions of becoming.

Most of them wear nothing. A few still carry remnants of what they once were: a rusted gauntlet fused to an elbow, a porcelain insulator blooming like a tumor from the collarbone, the broken hilt of a sword sprouting new leaves from its tang. These artifacts are not trophies. They are unresolved equations the mountain has not yet finished solving inside their bodies.

They do not eat. They schedule.

Every seventy years, when the iron roots flower, the Unfinished choose one among them to lie down in the clonal heather. The chosen one does not die. It is simply paused. The moss grows over it in thick orange beards. The clicking insects arrive in solemn procession and begin their maintenance, unscrewing what can be unscrewed, replacing failing valves with living ones, grafting new rust where entropy has won a skirmish. After nine days the chosen one rises again, heavier, slower, more beautiful, carrying the memory of having been a mountain for a little while.

They tend the vast slow engine.

You will see them walking the ridges at the hour when stars stare. They do not step so much as they are levered forward by internal pistons. Where their feet touch stone, tiny black flowers open and immediately freeze. They pause at the iron roots, press their grilles against the leaking oil, and drink with the reverence of monks receiving communion. Sometimes one will stand motionless for weeks, becoming a trellis for the phosphorescent ferns. The plants do not mind. They have been waiting for that particular geometry.

There is a hierarchy, but it is measured in patience rather than power. The oldest are almost entirely immobile—great corroded pillars rooted among the rhododendrons, their bodies so thoroughly colonized by moss and metal that only the faint green pulse behind their grilles proves they are still conscious. These elders do not speak. They leak decisions. The younger Unfinished interpret the taste of the oil and the precise angle at which an elder’s corrosion flakes on a given day. In this way the slow consensus of the Hollow Crown is maintained across centuries.

They know you are here.

They simply have not yet decided whether your presence is a variable that needs to be incorporated into the engine. Some of them have already begun to grow small iron hooks and delicate sampling needles from their forearms in anticipation of the day you lie down among the glowing moss. They are not cruel. Cruelty would require haste, and haste is impossible here. They are only inevitable.

If you watch long enough you may see two of them meet on a ledge above an abyss. They will stand facing each other for days. Fluid will move between them through invisible osmotic membranes. At the end of the meeting one will have become slightly more plant, the other slightly more machine. Both will be satisfied. Then they will part without sound, each carrying a new instruction for the next turning of the great wheel.

They have names, but the names are geological.

One is called The Interval Between Two Crevasse Events, 1407 Years Apart.

Another is known as The Precise Moment The Last Star Stopped Twinkling.

The eldest of all is simply named Remainder.

Remainder has not moved in four hundred years. Its body forms the keystone of a natural arch high above the cirque. Every time the clang sounds across the valleys, a single drop of perfect black oil falls from the place where its heart should be. The drop takes four full seconds to reach the stone below. When it lands, every living thing in the Hollow Crown—including the mountain itself—briefly forgets what it was doing.

Then the slow piston-stroke resumes.

And the Unfinished continue their ancient, patient, industrial tending of a kingdom that was never meant for kings—only for the long, rusting, luminous becoming of what cannot quite finish dying.
