They live here as though they were never meant to be separate from the place.

You will not see them at first. They are the color of wet shadow and new moss, their bodies so streaked and dappled with symbiotic algae that they appear only as slight thickenings in the blue-green dimness. When they move, it is with the same unhurried patience as the continents themselves—elongated limbs drifting, spines curled like question marks, hair drifting behind them in slow nebulae of black and viridian. They are thin the way deep-sea creatures are thin, as if the water has slowly pressed everything unnecessary out of them over centuries.

Their name for themselves, when they choose to speak it at all, is something between a sigh and the sound of roots sliding through silt. Outsiders who have drifted here and survived long enough to learn fragments of their tongue call them the Midwives, or sometimes the Stillborn. Both are true. They were born into a world that had not yet decided to finish being born, and so they finished themselves instead.

You find one crouched inside the hollow of a colossal stalk, her body half-submerged in the living wood where it has grown around her like a cradle. She is perhaps thirty or three hundred; age is difficult here. The algae on her shoulders and collarbones glow faintly, a soft bioluminescent lichen-gold. With bone needles made from the rib of something that died before the sky became ocean, she is coaxing a new walkway to grow outward from the stalk. She does not cut or nail. She sings—barely audible, a low vibration in her chest that makes the sap quicken and the fibers align. The wood listens. It always listens.

Her eyes are huge, almost entirely pupil, the color of rain on slate. When she finally notices you drifting past, there is no surprise. Only a slow blink, the way a mountain might blink if it had eyelids.

She does not ask who you are. Names are considered premature here. Instead she offers you a taste of the stalk she is shaping: a small cupped leaf filled with the thick, iron-sweet sap that will one day become floorboard or lullaby or both. The taste is childhood and grave dirt and the first breath after drowning. You understand, without being told, that this is how they greet.

There are perhaps nine hundred of them in total, scattered across three embryonic continents that have not yet chosen their final distance from one another. They do not build so much as persuade. Every tower, every bridge, every membrane-wall that keeps the nurseries warm is an argument conducted in sugar and song with the living mats. Sometimes the mats win. Whole districts are quietly absorbed overnight, turned back into continent. The people accept this as affectionately as a parent accepts a child’s tantrum. They simply begin again somewhere else, whispering new promises into new stalks.

You drift deeper into their lattice-city and begin to see how thoroughly they have married themselves to the unfinished world.

A young male—his spine still soft enough to bend in ways a human spine should not—hangs upside-down from a root bridge, patiently braiding his own hair into the dangling mycelial strands of the continent’s underside. When the braid is complete he will become a living mooring line, anchoring a new nursery pod against the slow upward currents. He will stay there for years. He does not seem troubled by this.

Further on, an elder whose body has begun to put out its own secondary roots sits embedded in the wall of the birth chamber. Her belly is no longer distinguishable from the continent; pale pink coral-like growths pulse in time with her heart. Inside her, and inside the continent, new children are being grown. Some will be people. Some will be buildings. The distinction has not yet been invented.

They do not have children in the ordinary way. They have negotiations.

When a woman feels the urge to continue the species, she finds a place where the continent is still soft and dreaming. She lies down inside it. The continent enters her and she enters the continent. Months later she rises again, hollowed and luminous, and somewhere else a new stalk breaks the surface bearing a child curled inside a translucent leaf-cradle. Sometimes the child is mostly human. Sometimes it has too many joints, or gills that never close, or eyes that remember what the world looked like before there was up or down. These are treasured.

The people speak rarely and only in harmonics. A single sentence might take four voices and half an hour, each speaker adding a layer of meaning like sediment. Loud noise is not merely rude; it is cosmologically dangerous. A sharp laugh can shear a district free. A shouted argument once caused an entire continent to forget its shape for seventeen years. They have not forgotten the lesson.

Instead they cultivate silence the way other civilizations cultivate wheat.

At the edge of the city, where the living mat frays into long beards of root and fungus, you find three of them singing a mountain into existence. They float in a loose triangle, hands resting on the soft pink mound that will one day be stone. Their song is so low it is almost below hearing—just the faintest pressure against your bones. The mound listens. It flushes deeper pink. Tiny coral polyps open like eyes across its surface, then close again, satisfied.

One of the singers notices you. She is ancient; her hair has become part of the root system. Without breaking the chord of their song she extends one impossibly long arm and touches your chest with two fingers. The touch is warm. It carries a question older than language:

*Will you help us decide what this becomes?*

They are not waiting for an answer. They have centuries.

Somewhere far below, the vast slow thing continues its patient ascent, pushing new possibility upward through kilometers of lukewarm brine. The Midwives feel it coming. Their bodies tense with a pleasure that looks almost like religious dread.

They lean into the unfinished world the way a lover leans into someone who has not yet said yes.

And the world, still deciding what it wants to be when it grows up, leans back.
