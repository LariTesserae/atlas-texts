**They call themselves the Veyri, though no tongue outside the Vale has ever spoken the name and lived to carry it elsewhere.**

They are not a people in the way other mountain tribes are. There are never more than thirty of them at any time. When a new soul is born, an old one is taken by the water within a turning of the moon. The mountain keeps the balance exactly. They do not question it.

You will not see them at first. They have become part of the Vale’s frantic equilibrium so completely that the eye slides off them the way rain slides off oiled moss. Only when one chooses to be seen do they resolve out of the wet stone and hanging green, as though the mist itself had decided to grow limbs and intent.

They are small, even by the standards of high-altitude folk. None stands taller than the pale cliff-deer. Their skin is the color of wet slate shot through with the faint iridescence of oil on water. Where hair should be they have only fine, living filaments of copper-green moss that moves independently, tasting the air for moisture and scent. Their eyes are large, almost entirely black, and perpetually wet; they never blink in the conventional sense. Instead the entire surface of the eye is refreshed by a slow, continuous film of condensation that rolls downward like tears that never quite fall.

They wear nothing woven. Clothing would grow too heavy. Instead they drape themselves in living curtains of the same oxidized ferns that hang from the cliffs. These garments breathe, drink, and photosynthesize. When a Veyri stands motionless against a wall for too long, the ferns begin to root into their skin. Most of them bear small colonies of fern on their shoulders and backs the way other peoples wear cloaks. Some of the eldest have become half-statue: their legs and lower torsos are indistinguishable from the dripping rock they lean against, bound there by thick mats of emerald moss that pulse gently with their heartbeat.

They move constantly.

Even when they appear to be still, something is always stirring: a finger tracing new rivulets in the stone, a slow circling of the head to keep the moss-hair evenly damp, the subtle shifting of weight from one foot to the other so that no single patch of skin remains in contact with the same droplet for too long. Stillness is death. They learned this long before the first hermit arrived. The hermits merely confirmed what the Veyri had always known in their blood.

Their speech is not sound. It is modulation of the constant roar.

They have learned to shape the waterfall’s voice the way a flutist shapes breath. By standing in precise places along the fluted cliffs and opening or closing certain crevices with their bodies, they can alter the pitch and rhythm of the falling water. What sounds to an outsider like random layered thunder is, to them, an ever-changing language of overlapping polyphony. A single Veyri standing on a ledge may be singing an ancient grief by directing a thin sheet of water across a resonant hollow in the basalt. Twenty of them together can make the entire eastern wall answer a question.

They do not build. They *negotiate*.

Every few years a new slab of rock calves from the caldera rim and smashes into the basin. The Veyri descend in a silent, scurrying swarm. Within days they have levered, braced, and coaxed the broken stone into new configurations that serve the water’s desire rather than their own. The leaning stone cells that look like hermit shrines are actually theirs—though they were built long before the first human ever set foot here. The skeleton in moss is not human. It is one of their own who finally chose the final stillness. The mountain accepted the gift.

At night they glow.

Not like the millipedes, which are crude chemical lanterns. The Veyri carry colonies of symbiotic algae and fungi beneath the thin outer layer of their skin. When the true dark comes, these living lights bloom in slow pulses across their bodies, tracing the same fractal patterns the water cuts into the stone. They look like living maps of the Vale itself moving across the cliffs. The marmots fall silent when the Veyri descend at night. Even the lynx draw back, recognizing something older than predation.

They have no leaders. Instead they have *Listeners*.

Every generation, one child is born whose moss-hair grows in perfect concentric rings. This child is given to the eastern wall at the moment the prismatic fire strikes it. For three days and nights the child stands where the rainbows burn, unmoving, while the rest of the tribe sings the water into protective curtains around them. Most do not survive. Those who do return changed. Their eyes become prismatic, refracting whatever light touches them into tiny personal rainbows. These are the ones who speak for the mountain when the mountain decides the balance has slipped.

They remember the hermits. They remember every hermit.

The seated skeleton in the moss cloak was called Brother Sefren. He lasted eleven years. On the final morning he simply sat down in the place now occupied by his bones and let the water have him. The Veyri watched from the cliffs for nine days as the moisture slowly escorted him into the green. When it was done they placed the circle of quartz inside his empty ribcage so the next hermit would know the mountain had been polite.

The Veyri do not pity the hermits. They are fascinated by them. Human beings are the only creatures they have ever encountered who choose to come here seeking stillness. To the Veyri this is an almost erotic form of madness, like watching someone deliberately step into an avalanche to see what silence feels like. They leave the tools and the sinew and the small stone circles untouched out of respect for this beautiful, doomed contradiction.

If you remain long enough, one will eventually stand before you.

Not threatening. Not welcoming. Simply *present*, dripping, moss-haired, eyes like wet obsidian reflecting your own face back in trembling fragments. It will tilt its head in a slow circle, tasting your breath with its living hair. Then it will speak by altering the nearest waterfall so that the sound of your own name emerges from the roaring curtain, perfectly formed, unbearably intimate.

It will ask you a single question, shaped from layered water-noise and the faint chiming of falling droplets on quartz:

*How long do you think you can keep moving?*

And the mountain, always listening, will wait for your answer with the patience of stone that has already seen every possible surrender.
