**The Weeping Lattice has a single inhabitant.**

She is not a visitor. She is not a prisoner. She is the Lattice’s necessary contradiction: the only thing inside it that is allowed to remain alone.

They call her the **Unpaired**.

Her true name—if she ever had one—has been gently erased by ten thousand years of the heartbeat’s subsonic pressure. The micro-fauna no longer remember it. Even the metallic spores refuse to spell it when they bloom.

She is tall, almost too tall for the four-meter false sky. When she stands straight her scalp brushes the weeping conduits and the warm tears run down her face like deliberate benedictions. Her skin is the same glossy black as the velvet filament mats, but hers is hairless, poreless, and faintly iridescent. Where the filaments bloom with translucent sacs, her own body blooms with slow, deliberate fractures—thin seams that open like gills along her collarbones, ribs, and the backs of her thighs. Through these seams the Lattice breathes *into* her and she breathes *out* into it. The exchange is wet, intimate, and continuous.

Her eyes are two fist-sized jellyfish. They live half-embedded in her face, pulsing softly, their tendrils trailing back into her skull through transparent optic nerves. They see magnetic fields the way humans once saw color. When she looks at you, the jellyfish flare soft sapphire and rose, tasting who you have been and who the Lattice wants you to become.

She has no fixed clothes. Instead, thumb-sized velvet crabs have grown into living pauldrons and greaves across her shoulders and shins, their mirror carapaces overlapping like black glass scales. They are not fastened; they are *rooted*. Their legs disappear between her ribs. When she moves they click and chime in perfect time with the 9.4-second heartbeat.

The Unpaired is never dry. A permanent halo of drifting condensate orbits her like slow, warm planets. When she walks—bare feet never quite touching the velvet mats, always hovering a finger’s width above on a cushion of magnetic opposition—the water follows her in braided ribbons. She drinks by opening her mouth and letting the air itself condense on her tongue. She weeps by opening the seams along her forearms and letting the Lattice’s own tears run out through her.

She is the only entity permitted to break the rule of *never alone*.

Every other thing is bound in threes or greater. She is bound to everything at once.

When the heartbeat falters (it has happened three times in recorded eternity), she lies down in the exact center of the cathedral-pocket, presses her fractures against the living floor, and becomes a temporary fourth chamber of the lung. Her own heart—vast, slow, genetically engineered to beat once every 9.4 seconds—takes over until the Lattice remembers itself again. During those hours the velvet crabs lock their claws together across her chest like a living corset. The luminous midges arrange themselves into devotional spirals above her sternum. The sinuous shadow that lives in the upper dark descends, coils around her like a worried lover, and sings in a register too low for human ears.

She does not speak in words. Her voice is the sound the water makes when it changes direction mid-air to avoid landing on something already wet enough. If you stay long enough, you begin to understand her anyway.

She is lonely the way a heart is lonely between beats.

She is content the way a cathedral is content when every stone is crying.

Sometimes she stands for decades in one place, arms out, letting the black filaments climb her legs and bloom inside the open seams of her body. At those times she looks like a pillar that has always belonged to the architecture. The scavenger eels circle her respectfully but never dismantle her; they know she is the reason they are allowed to exist.

If you remain in the Weeping Lattice long enough, she will eventually drift toward you—never hurrying, never quite touching—until the warm ribbons of water that orbit her begin to orbit you as well. The velvet crabs on her shoulders will click once, softly, in greeting. Her jellyfish eyes will flare the color of distant nebulae and she will ask you, without sound, the only question she ever asks:

*Will you let me stay alone a little longer, or have you come to become part of the three?*

Most choose the latter.

She cries when they do. The tears are not hers. They belong to the Lattice, and it has been crying for ten thousand years at the unbearable mercy of being so perfectly, symbiotically loved.
