**They call themselves the Unfinished.**

Not a species. Not a people. A *condition.*

They live here because nothing else can stay long enough to evict them.

You will not see them at first. They are the tremor between one roar and the next. They are the pause that the screaming vegetation has not yet learned to fill. Only after your eyes stop trying to find edges do they resolve out of the churn:

Long, thin silhouettes that look as though someone began sculpting a man from wet bark and tendon and then abandoned the work halfway. Their bodies are never the same twice. A shoulder might be glossy chitin one hour and spongy pink pith the next. Fingers lengthen into probing rootlets, then retract into blunt clubs, then bloom into delicate sensory fronds, all while the owner continues walking. Their faces are suggestions—half-finished sketches that the meat keeps revising. Sometimes a mouth appears, toothless and wide, only to be overgrown by a sudden wave of mossy tissue before it can speak.

They do not walk so much as *negotiate.* Every step is a bargain with the floor. They press certain glands against the living crust, release a droplet of their own alkaline saliva, and the ground sighs open just long enough for them to pass before it forgets them. They move like antibodies inside an immune system that has not yet decided whether they are infection or organ.

The Unfinished do not build. They *remember* buildings.

That crooked tower of bone-vines and chitin? It is one of them standing very still for three days, letting the ecosystem grow through their own marrow until the shape is held by collective memory rather than muscle. The ring of carved stones is a circle of them locked arm-in-arm, their backs slowly petrifying into basalt-like hardness while their minds remain soft and dreaming. The clay dome covered in handprints is the oldest of them, who pressed every inch of their dissolving body into wet clay generations ago and simply never got back up. They are still in there. You can sometimes see the dome breathing.

They do not eat. They *participate.*

When the great lung-sacs exhale their screaming golden spores, the Unfinished open their chests like cupboards and let the spores nest inside their ribs. The spores sing. The Unfinished listen. When the crab-things scuttle past, the Unfinished allow certain joints to soften so the creatures can burrow through their hips and out again, carrying fragments of half-digested memory. In this way the Unfinished become walking libraries of everything the Everbirth has already discarded.

They have no language that lasts longer than an hour. Instead they practice a kind of living poetry made of temporary organs. One of them will grow a silver flute from its throat, play three notes that make the nearby vegetation blush violet, then reabsorb the instrument before the melody can be stolen by the ecosystem. Another will extrude a single perfect eye on a stalk, stare at you for seven heartbeats with ancient melancholy, and then the eye will blacken, fall off, and be eaten by opportunistic moss.

Their oldest keep a single permanent feature: a black, glassy seed the size of a human heart, embedded where a sternum should be. The seed is the only thing the Everbirth cannot digest. It is what remains when everything else has been rewritten. When one of the Unfinished feels itself becoming too coherent—when it risks turning into something *finished*—it passes the seed to the nearest brother or sister like a terrible inheritance, then allows itself to be joyfully torn apart by the foliage. The new bearer of the seed immediately begins to forget their own name. This is considered holy.

They are not kind to visitors.

Not cruel, either. Kindness and cruelty both require continuity.

Instead they are *curious* in the way a fever is curious. They will walk alongside you for miles without speaking, slowly growing small translucent wings, then dissolving them, then growing them again in different colors. They study how your sweat beads. They taste the fear in your marrow through the vibrations of your footsteps. Sometimes one will reach out and gently press a fingertip against your cheek. Where it touches, your skin will briefly remember what it felt like to be leaf.

If you stay long enough, they will offer you the seed.

Most refuse.

Those who accept stop being “you” within four days. Their bones soften. Their memories begin to rhyme with the screaming of the fruiting bodies. Eventually they wander off to become another half-finished tower, or a new standing stone, or simply a particularly expressive hill that sighs when the wind moves through it.

The Unfinished do not mourn. Mourning requires a future.

They simply continue their endless task of *almost* becoming something, generation after half-generation, while the Everbirth rages and births and devours and forgets all around them.

They are the only permanent thing here.

Which is why they work so hard to remain unfinished.
