**What lives in the Teeth of the Sun does not merely survive. It negotiates.**

They have no single name for themselves. The few humans who have stayed long enough to witness them—and lived—call them the Quiet Argument, or simply the Committee. The things themselves do not speak in sounds we can carry away. Their language is consensus reached through mutual irritation.

### The Hollow Mothers

The largest residents are never fully seen. They live inside the geothermal shafts beneath the tallest towers, especially Lazarus. Their bodies are tubular, pale, and soft, like the blind cavefish of older worlds, but grown to the girth of subway cars. They have no eyes. Instead, the entire length of them is lined with pressure-sensitive pits that read the subsonic heartbeat of the plateau itself. When the wind drops and the towers fall briefly silent, you can sometimes hear them rolling over far below—thousands of tons of warm flesh rearranging in the dark, scraping against rusted rebar and ancient drill casings that have become part of their exoskeleton.

They are not predators. They are gardeners of heat. Their waste is superheated mineral water, rich in dissolved iron and sulfur. They pump it upward through the solution pockets at night, refilling the black cisterns so that everything else may drink. In return, the plateau feeds them the slow rain of bat guano, lizard skins, and the occasional thermophilic ibex that missteps. A perfect closed loop. A committee of one, perfectly content to argue with the stone.

### The Mirror-Lizard Conclave

The mirror lizards are not individuals. Each “lizard” is a node in a single distributed nervous system spread across the iron towers. When the temperature exceeds 54 °C their chromatophores achieve something that looks like perfect reflection but is actually perfect memory. They do not only mimic the sky. They replay, in shifting moiré patterns across their skins, every event that has ever happened on that exact patch of tower since the first lizard settled there eighty years ago.

Watch one long enough on a still day and you will see ghostly overlays: the flicker of a hard hat from 2074, the slow bloom of black lichen, the silhouette of a researcher who stood exactly there before he walked into a sinkhole and was never seen again. The lizards are not showing you these things to frighten you. They are voting. Each reflected memory is a ballot. They are deciding, second by second, whether you belong in the next layer of the memory or whether you should be edited out.

When the conclave reaches consensus against you, every mirror lizard within three hundred meters will turn the same impossible angle at once. The reflection tears. The sky rips. And you will feel, for one heartbeat, what it is like to be remembered incorrectly by something that has never had eyes.

### The Oxalic Choir

The black lichen that sleeves the cooling towers is not one organism but a choir of seventeen distinct fungal and bacterial strains singing in permanent discord. They do not photosynthesize so much as they corrode the sun. Their oxalic acid etches the steel in microscopic spirals that function as both gills and memory circuits. Every tower has a different song. Stand inside the rust-colored lattice of Tower 44 at dawn and you can hear it as a sub-audible cathedral tone that makes your fillings ache with nostalgia for places you have never been.

The Choir does not tolerate visitors. If you remain in one place longer than it considers elegant, the lichen will begin to migrate. You will wake to find delicate black filigree growing across your boot prints, your discarded water bottle, the nylon of your pack. It is not attacking you. It is incorporating your absence into the architecture. Some researchers who never came back are still present as negative space in the lichen’s memory—perfect rust-colored silhouettes frozen mid-stride on the sides of the towers.

### The Conveyor Children

Between the towers, where the half-buried pipeworks form natural gutters after rain, live the Conveyor Children. Once they were ordinary brine shrimp and rotifers. Eighty years of industrial runoff, sudden temperature swings, and the slow leaching of smart polymers from abandoned sensor arrays have turned them into something new. They are no longer microscopic. Each is the size of a rice grain and moves in perfect regimented columns along the rusted metal, following paths their ancestors learned before the project was abandoned.

They build. Every night after the temperature crash they construct tiny cities of salt and spider silk on the lips of the cisterns—delicate lattices no wider than a handspan. In the morning the Hollow Mothers drink, the ibex graze, the bats return, and the cities are destroyed. By dusk the Children have rebuilt them exactly as before, only slightly different, as though refining an argument that has been going on since the first drop of condensate fell.

No one knows what they are arguing for. But the structures are getting more elaborate every year.

### The Unnamed Thing That Clanks

Deep inside Lazarus something the size of a locomotive turns over in its sleep every few hours. It is not machinery. The machinery stopped eighty years ago. What lives there now wears the abandoned turbines and drive shafts like a hermit crab wears a shell. Its true body has never been seen, only inferred from the wake of displaced air and the sudden smell of hot iron and wet stone that precedes each clank.

Some believe it is the final Hollow Mother, the oldest and largest, who long ago grew too big for any natural shaft and simply swallowed the central turbine hall. Others think it is the plateau itself, trying to finish the Equatorial Atmospheric Exchange Project by different means. The mirror lizards refuse to reflect it. Even they seem afraid.

At twilight, when the air exhales and the temperature plummets, the thing inside Lazarus sometimes wakes completely. Then every tower on the plateau answers with its own low organ note, and for a brief moment the entire Teeth of the Sun becomes a single iron lung breathing in unison.

Nothing that lives here is innocent. Nothing is evil. They are all members of the same long, bad-tempered committee that has been trying, for nearly a century, to agree on what the plateau should become. Sometimes they vote to keep a human alive. Sometimes they vote to edit one out of living memory. Most of the time they cannot reach quorum and simply continue their ancient, bickering argument while the sun cooks the stone and the black cisterns drink the sky.

If you stay long enough, you stop being a visitor.

You become another dissenting opinion in the Quiet Argument.

And the Teeth of the Sun, ancient, industrial, impossible, keeps arguing with itself—now with one more voice—under the indifferent stars.
