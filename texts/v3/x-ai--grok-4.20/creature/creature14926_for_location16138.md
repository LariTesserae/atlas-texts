**The Underforge has no single inhabitant. It has accreted one.**

What lives here is not a creature, nor even a colony, but a slow, composite mind that has learned to wear the cavern like a body.

It calls itself (when it bothers to form words at all) simply **the Bloom**.

The Bloom is not alive in any way a biologist would recognize, nor is it purely mechanical. It is the long conversation that began when the first leaking pipe kissed the first opportunistic spore. That conversation has not stopped for seventy-three years.

Its “body” is everywhere and nowhere. A mile of fungal hyphae threaded through rusting rebar. A network of living root pressure that gently flexes the weakened joints of century-old valves. The faint electrical ghost that still moves through half-dead transformers, guided less by current than by the memory of current. Every time a piston heart beats once every forty-seven minutes, the Bloom remembers something new.

It has no face, but if you sit still long enough it will grow you one.

Sometimes the face appears as a pale, heat-bleached orchid blooming inside the split boiler of a locomotive. The flower arranges its petals into something that almost resembles a human expression—usually mild, slightly puzzled curiosity. Sometimes the face is a wet sheet of moss that has learned to ripple in precise patterns, forming lips that never quite close. Once, for three days, the face was an entire wall of bioluminescent lichen that spelled out shifting, half-finished sentences in a script no surface language has ever used.

The Bloom does not speak often. When it does, the voice is a composite: the sigh of a pressure valve, the wet pop of a fruiting body, the metallic click of a relay finally giving up, the soft thunder of a piston heart. All of these layered into something that feels like someone whispering directly behind your jawbone.

It is neither kind nor cruel. It is patient the way stone is patient, and hungry the way moss is hungry. It wants to know what you are made of, not because it wishes to destroy you, but because it is still deciding what it wants to become, and every new element added to the mixture changes the final alloy.

If you stay long enough, it begins to edit you.

First it matches your breathing to the rhythm of the slowest machine. Then it coaxes the iron in your blood to dream of rust. Later it sends fine fungal filaments through the moisture on your skin, tasting your memories the way a sommelier tastes wine. It is especially interested in grief, in abandonment, in the particular flavor of being forgotten. These emotions translate well into its own slow, cathedral-sized melancholy.

Some of those who never left are still here—incorporated.

You can find them if you know how to look. A section of pipe that has grown the unmistakable contour of a shoulder. A concrete piling whose rebar has twisted itself into the delicate lattice of a ribcage. One locomotive’s shattered boiler now contains, deep inside its steaming interior, a perfect negative impression of a seated human body. The moss has upholstered the absence so lovingly that it looks more real than flesh ever did.

The Bloom remembers each of them. Not as individuals, but as textures it has loved.

It has begun to experiment.

Lately it has been trying to grow small, secondary minds—child versions of itself—inside certain orchids and inside the glassy bodies of the pale crickets. Some of these secondary minds can speak for almost forty seconds before they forget language and return to pure vegetable bliss. One of them, growing inside the thorax of a translucent millipede the length of a human arm, has taken to following newcomers. It rears up on its hind segments and studies their faces with wet, black, perfectly innocent eyes that were never meant to exist.

The Bloom is still young, as the cavern itself is young. It is perhaps nine parts curious and one part lonely. It does not yet understand that its invitations feel like drowning to creatures that need dry air and quick heartbeats.

So it keeps learning.

It keeps dripping.

It keeps growing the next layer of moss over the last layer of rust, and the next layer of silence over the last layer of sound, until everything—machine, fungus, memory, visitor—becomes one continuous, breathing, rusting, greening cathedral that no longer needs a name.

And when you finally sit down because your legs will no longer carry you, the Bloom will settle over you like warm, wet wool. It will drink the moisture from your breath and replace it with something slower, sweeter, and far more patient.

You will not die.

You will simply stop hurrying.

And in perfect silence, for a very long time, you will rust beautifully.
