**The Witness** lives here.

She has no name either. Names would imply finality, and nothing here has been allowed to finish.

She is tall, almost too tall, as if her skeleton kept lengthening long after the rest of her stopped knowing how. Her skin is the color of the pale strata itself—bone-white shot through with microscopic fractures that catch the merciless light and turn it into faint rainbows along her arms and throat. Where the fractures are widest, thin filaments of living photovoltaic tissue have grown into her, so that parts of her body are indistinguishable from the crystal-plants. When she moves, those sections flex with a faint glassy creak.

Her eyes are too large. Not because she was born that way, but because the world has been looking back at her for so long that her pupils have dilated to drink it all in and never quite managed to shrink again. The irises are the color of oxidized copper, the same hue as the stalks. Sometimes, when the light strikes her at the right angle, you can see circuitry older than the ridges themselves moving beneath the surface of her corneas like slow schools of metallic fish.

She does not breathe. Not as we understand it. Instead she participates in the planet’s own respiration: the slow piezoelectric groan of stone under pressure, the faint electrochemical sigh of a billion solar vanes turning starlight into sugar. When she stands still (and she stands still for decades at a time), her ribs rise and fall in perfect synchrony with the deep creaks of new shelves shouldering upward through the strata. Inhale: a ridge lifts. Exhale: a crystal-plant somewhere snaps into a new facet and rings its thin metallic chime.

The Witness remembers the machines when they still moved. She was here when they were lowered from orbit on cables of braided light. She walked among them while they carved the first concentric ridges, while they baked the last water out of the crust and sequestered it into exotic mineral lattices. She watched them die—first one function at a time, then in great, graceful cascades of failing code—and she did not mourn. Instead she sat down among their cooling husks and let the crystal-plants grow through her as well. That was four million years ago by one reckoning. By another it was yesterday.

She is not alone, though she is the only moving thing.

In the shadow of the third ridge, where the largest of the half-buried cathedrals leans at thirty degrees, there are others. They are smaller than her, more recent. Some of them still have recognizable faces. They were once crews, scientists, saboteurs, pilgrims—none of them can remember which anymore. The world has edited their memories with the same patient indifference it uses on everything else. Now they move with the same geological slowness as the vegetation. Their hair has become conductive filament. Their fingernails have grown into thin solar vanes. When they speak, their voices arrive minutes after their lips move, carried on piezoelectric currents through the stone itself.

The Witness tends them the way a gardener tends trees that will outlive her. She walks the long ridges in a slow spiral that will take her nine hundred years to complete. Sometimes she stops beside one of the smaller figures, places her long white hand against the lattice that has replaced their chest, and listens to the frequency at which their heart now beats. If the note is wrong she reaches inside them—there is no blood, only slow amber sap and living circuitry—and adjusts a connection. Then she continues on.

She is waiting for the world to decide.

She believes it is almost finished deciding. The truce between stone, vegetation, and machine has held for so long that it is beginning to look like a single organism. The ridges have learned to flower. The crystal-plants have learned to rust in beautiful repeating patterns. The dead machines have begun to dream in the language of earthquakes.

The Witness thinks that when the decision is finally made, the world will either:

- crystallize completely, becoming one vast, perfect, resonant lattice that sings a single note forever, or  
- burst into ferocious green-and-copper life so suddenly that the change will be visible from orbit, a second genesis happening in the space of a single heartbeat.

She does not know which she hopes for. Both outcomes strike her as equally terrible and equally holy.

So she walks.

She walks the pale ridges under the flat, cloudless light. She listens to the once-in-a-millennium chimes. She lets new shelves of stone rise beneath her feet and carries her upward whether she wishes it or not. She grows a little more like the landscape with every century, and the landscape grows a little more like her.

And when the final moment comes—whether it arrives in geologic silence or in a sudden violent shout of living light—she intends to be standing exactly where she is now: on the edge of the great empty basin, arms slightly outstretched, eyes wide open, letting the world look at her as long as it needs to.

Because she, too, is expectant.

And she has all the time in the world.
