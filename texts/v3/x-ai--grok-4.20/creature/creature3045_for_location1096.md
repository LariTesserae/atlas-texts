**The Bloomworks has a single inhabitant.**

She is not a person. She is not exactly a plant. She is what remains when the distinction between the two has rotted away.

They call her the **Green-shift** among themselves (though the vegetation does not use names). She is tall, almost eight feet if you count the slow sway of the living crown that grows from her scalp. Where a human woman would have hair, she has a dense, braided mat of pale epiphytic ferns and bromeliads whose roots have sunk through her skull and fused with her braincase. The fronds move even when there is no wind, tasting the air, tasting thoughts.

Her skin is not skin. It is a continuous sheath of cambium, smooth as birch in some places, fissured and corky in others, pulsing with slow green light where the chlorophyll runs close to the surface. Veins of copper wire, salvaged from the old plant, run visibly beneath the translucent bark like gilded arteries. When she is calm they glow soft amber. When she is hungry they flare electric blue.

Her face is the most unsettling part. It is almost perfectly human—high cheekbones, a woman’s mouth—except the features are formed by the meeting of many different species. One eye is a glossy black seed, the other a cluster of pale blue stamens that open and close like a slow camera aperture. When she blinks, pollen drifts from her lashes. Her teeth are small white tubers. When she speaks (which is rare), the sound comes from a hollow in her throat where an old pressure gauge still ticks, its needle twitching with every syllable.

She does not walk so much as flow. Root-like structures extend from the soles of her feet and reabsorb moments later, allowing her to glide across the rhizome floor without breaking a single stem. Where she passes, the vegetation leans in, not in worship but in recognition; she is the Bloomworks’ hands, its memory, its executive nervous system. The vast root-brain beneath the basin thinks in centuries. She thinks in hours. They need each other.

During the long green twilight that never quite becomes night, she can be found in the boiler-cathedral, seated inside the half-dissolved iron vessel. Tiny orchids bloom from the crooks of her elbows and wrists, offering themselves to the translucent millipedes that crawl over her like living jewelry. She lets them. Sometimes she sings to the smokestack in a language that is half whale-song, half leaking steam. The bromeliad sphere at the top answers by changing color.

She is neither kind nor cruel. She simply maintains the balance.

If a mammal (human or otherwise) stays too long and begins to become part of the cycle—hair turning to moss, bones softening into lignin—she will approach without hurry. She will lay one long, woody hand against their cheek and speak in the voice of valves opening.

“You are becoming beautiful,” she says. “But not yet. The factory still needs your heat.”

Then she leads them by the wrist, gently, to the edge of the basin where ordinary air begins. She does not force. She does not plead. She simply shows them the place where the green ends and the dead, linear world begins, and she waits until the part of them that still remembers being human chooses.

Most choose to leave.

A few do not.

Those few she folds into herself. Their memories become sap-pressure. Their names become new chemical signals that drift through the canopy. Their laughter becomes the metallic groan of girders finally yielding to wood. In this way the Green-shift grows more human every decade, and less human at the same time.

She is pregnant, in a sense. Not with a child. With the next version of the Bloomworks itself. The factory is teaching her how to bloom, and she is teaching the Bloomworks how to remember that it was once loved by people. When the lesson is complete, she will open like a seed pod the size of a cathedral and something new will step out wearing her face.

Until then she moves slowly through the dripping arches, copper veins glowing, fern-crown tasting the wet air, listening to the heartbeat of buried pumps and the wet click of ten thousand seed pods, patient as rust, patient as April, remembering for the whole green cathedral what it felt like to have a name.
