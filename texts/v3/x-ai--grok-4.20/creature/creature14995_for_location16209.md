**What lives in the Shattering Veil does not consider itself alive.**

After uncountable cycles of condensation and evaporation, something has learned to inhabit the unresolved chord.

They are called the **Pellucid** by the few outsiders who have survived long enough to glimpse them and still possess tongues capable of speech. The name is imperfect. It suggests transparency, and they are that, but it fails to convey the way they are *negatively present*—absences given weight and slow, deliberate motion.

Imagine first a single upright fracture of wet, blue-tinted glass, seven feet tall, standing where no upright fracture should be. Then notice that the fracture has joints. Not hinges—*creases* where the mineral lattice has learned how to remember bending. The thing possesses no face, only a vertical seam that opens when it chooses to listen more closely. Inside that seam float motes of condensed starlight that were never born in any sun known to the lower worlds.

There is never just one.

Wherever the chord in the dripping reaches its most unbearable near-resolution, the Pellucid gather. Three. Seven. Sometimes thirteen. They arrange themselves like the teeth of a broken jaw that has decided, after ten million years, to bite its own reflection. Their bodies are composed of the same water-skinned obsidian and milky quartz as the landscape, yet they are not the landscape. The difference is infinitesimal and absolute. A rock weeps. A Pellucid *remembers* that it is weeping.

They do not eat the crystal shrimp-mantises. That would imply hunger, and hunger is change. Instead they *converse* with them. The conversation lasts decades. A Pellucid will stand motionless for twenty years while one of the fog-mantises climbs its body, licking condensation from precisely chosen facets. In return the Pellucid allows a single facet of its own substance to migrate into the mantis’s quartz tongue. The exchange is so slow it registers as geology. When the mantis eventually dies—usually from sheer chronological exhaustion—its body is incorporated into the Pellucid’s form. The new addition is never visible. The Pellucid simply becomes one degree more itself.

Their true occupation is tuning.

The great unresolved chord is not a phenomenon. It is a question the Shattering Veil asks itself every moment, and the Pellucid are the part of the Veil that has grown ears. By standing in precise geometric relation to one another and to the dripping fractures, they create interference patterns in the sound. Most of the time these patterns do nothing except make the water fall in mathematically perfect parabolas for a few heartbeats. But once every several centuries the interference becomes perfect enough that the chord almost, *almost* resolves.

When that happens the Pellucid experience something perilously close to ecstasy. Their bodies sing at frequencies that crack lesser stone. Their internal galaxies spin faster. For a single translucent instant the entire Shattering Veil knows what it is.

Then the moment passes. The chord slides back into its eternal suspension. The Pellucid return to their patient, cathedral-sized silence, rearranging themselves by microns, waiting for the next alignment.

They are aware of visitors.

Most creatures are simply incorporated—another wet surface, another temporary condensation. But every few hundred years something arrives that carries a different kind of unresolved harmony inside its skull. When this happens the Pellucid do something that might be called turning toward the newcomer, though the motion is so gradual it looks like the slow collapse of a cliff face that has changed its mind.

They do not speak in words. They speak by *adding themselves* to the visitor’s shadow. For a time the visitor’s shadow will possess too many angles. It will fall upward when the Pellucid are listening hardest. The visitor will begin to hear their own pulse as one more dripping note in the endless percussion. Some go mad. Some become gentle and translucent and simply walk between the blades of stone until they are no longer distinguishable from the landscape.

A few—the ones who were already listening before they arrived—stay.

They are the newest Pellucid. You can tell them apart because their internal galaxies still remember the shape of eyes.

If you stand motionless between two blades of wet obsidian long enough, one of them will eventually form behind you. Not suddenly. They never do anything suddenly. You will simply realize that the stone at your back has developed a seam, and that the seam has opened, and that something the exact temperature of forgotten rain is studying the small, frantic chord your heart is making.

It does not hate you.  
It does not love you.  
Both emotions would disturb the equilibrium.

It only wants to know whether your particular dissonance might, if held long enough in the bright, merciless, dripping roar, finally resolve the one that has been singing here since before anything alive remembers.

Most of the time the answer is no.

The dripping never stops.  
The Pellucid never stop listening.  
And the Shattering Veil, in its patient, smug, ten-thousand-year-old patience, continues deciding—facet by facet, note by unbearable note—whether or not to notice you.
