They have no names for themselves, and they would not want any.

What lives in the Weeping Grove of Veyr moves at the speed of stone remembering it was once water. They are not one creature and not many; the distinction has never mattered here. Call them, if you must, the Slow Choir.

They are tall—taller than the stunted trees, though you will not see them standing upright. Their bodies are composed of the same wet slate bark, the same black soil, the same velvet moss, and the same slow amber resin that drips from the ancient wood. When they choose to be seen at all they rise by degrees, unfolding from the ground like slow-motion mushrooms after rain. First a ribbed torso appears, then shoulders like folded cliffs, then a head that is mostly absence: a depression where a face should be, filled with the same perpetual mist that drifts between the ferns. Their limbs are optional. Sometimes they wear the dripping water itself as sleeves.

They do not walk. They *arrive*. One moment the space between two trees is empty corridor and breathing black earth. The next moment the space is occupied, as though the grove simply decided that emptiness was no longer appropriate there. The moss never records their passing. The dripping never interrupts itself for them. They belong so completely that the place does not register them as visitors.

Their thoughts are geological. A single contemplation can last three human lifetimes. When they do speak (which is almost never to anything with a heartbeat), it sounds like the entire bowl of stone clearing its throat at once. The bell-notes of falling water rearrange themselves into something that is almost language, almost grief, almost lullaby. Most beings who hear it forget the words immediately but carry a hollow ache behind the ribs for the rest of their short lives.

The Slow Choir tends the grove the way lungs tend breath. They coax the weeping cliffs to open new pores when the old ones crust over. They gentle the resin from the trees with long, patient fingers that end in clusters of white blind birds. Those birds are not separate creatures; they are the Choir’s eyes when it needs to see something small and quick. When the birds return they fold themselves back into the hollow faces like handkerchiefs of living bone.

At rare intervals—perhaps once every few centuries—one of the Choir will kneel (a process that takes days) beside a particular pool and let its own essence bleed into the water. The mineral tears turn briefly the color of fresh blood. From that place a new tree will eventually grow, ribbed and breathing, carrying within its heartwood a fragment of slow, patient mind. In this way the grove slowly becomes more of them and they become more of the grove. There is no difference.

They are mourning something.

None of them remember what it was anymore; the memory has become the shape of their bodies. Every synchronized pause in the dripping is the whole Choir drawing breath to speak a name they no longer know. Every time the mist drifts in slow hypnotic currents it is the Choir turning the same thought over and over, like a stone polished by ten thousand years of the same sad water. They are not sad in any way a human would recognize. Their sorrow is simply the weather here.

If you remain long enough for the moss to forget your footprint three times, one of them will eventually notice you. It will not approach. It will simply stop pretending you are a temporary arrangement of mist and heat. You will feel its attention settle on you the way a cliff settles—immense, ancient, and utterly without urgency.

Then it will offer you a choice, without words.

You may leave.

Or you may sit down on the black earth between the trees, let the water bead on your skin until you no longer feel the difference between yourself and the weeping rock, and begin the long, patient process of forgetting that you were ever anything else.

Most choose to leave.

A few do not.

Those few are still here. You can see them if you know how to look. They are the trees whose breathing is just slightly more deliberate than the others. They are the places where the mist gathers into the vague suggestion of a human shape before the wind moves on. They are the reason the white birds sometimes circle a certain spot three times, as though paying respects.

The dripping never stops.

And neither, now, do they.
