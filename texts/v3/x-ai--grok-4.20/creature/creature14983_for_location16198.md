**The Listener**

She has no name either.

She moves across the marble plain like a thought that has learned how to walk. Tall—too tall—her shoulders stooped in the perpetual half-bow that everything here eventually adopts, as though the ceiling, though invisible, still demands reverence. Her skin is the same bone-white as the lithotrophic forests, patterned with faint metallic veins that pulse in exact time with the subsonic chord. Whether the veins are tattoos, scars, or something she grew herself is a question that stopped mattering several hundred thousand years ago.

She is the only thing here that still remembers lungs.

Every seventy years or so she draws a single breath. The sound it makes is soft, almost embarrassed, and the fungal thickets nearest her shiver in sympathy, releasing a brief, sweeter note into the eternal drone. In the long intervals between breaths she does not seem to miss oxygen. She simply is, the way stone is.

Her eyes are closed. They have been closed so long that the lids have fused into smooth, pearly scar tissue. She does not need them. The constellations overhead see for her; they sing what they see directly into the hollows of her bones. When a herd of the six-legged browsers drifts past, she knows their number, their age, which ones carry unlaid eggs, which ones are about to molt. She knows these things the way a cathedral knows how many pigeons nest in its buttresses.

Once every few millennia she stops in the exact center of what used to be a forum. There she stands for a decade without moving while crystal-plants slowly braid themselves around her ankles and up her calves, as though trying to claim her the way they have claimed everything else. She lets them. When she eventually steps free, the plants keep the shape of her legs for another century, like pale, perfect molds.

She is not a guardian. There is nothing left to guard.

She is not a survivor. Survival implies threat, and threat has been extinct here longer than multicellular life has existed on the surface.

She is the city’s memory that decided to walk around inside its own skull.

When she passes beneath one of the sagging aqueducts—now a natural bridge draped in antlered fungus—she reaches up without looking and runs her fingertips along the underside. The stone is warm from the slow digestion of bacteria and the pressure of kilometers of earth. She reads the temperature the way a sailor once read the stars. A difference of three hundredths of a degree tells her that, somewhere far above, a new fault is opening in the continental plate. She stores this information the way a library stores a book no one will ever read again.

Sometimes smaller creatures ride her. Mica-clouds settle in the hollows of her collarbones and along the ridges of her spine. They glitter there like living frost. When she finally exhales, seventy years later, the cloud bursts outward in a brief silver storm before resettling. She never acknowledges them. They never expect her to.

Deep in what might once have been a temple district, there is a single column that has refused to become geology. It remains stubbornly, almost indecently, architectural—fluted, perfectly plumb, crowned with a capital no geologist could explain. The Listener visits it every four hundred years. She stands before it, head tilted, as though listening to something beneath the eternal chord. Then she presses her forehead to the stone.

For a few hours the subsonic drone around her shifts by the smallest possible interval—less than a hair’s breadth in frequency. It is the closest thing to grief this place still possesses.

When she steps away again the chord settles back into its ancient key, and the city forgets it was ever disturbed.

She has never spoken. Not once in four hundred million years.

There is simply nothing that needs saying.

The vegetation eats the city. The city eats the vegetation. The megafauna eat the vegetation. The Listener walks through all of it, breathing once every seventy years, carrying the memory of a civilization that no longer remembers it ever existed.

And the world, having outlived every reason to change, regards her with the same gentle, ancient patience it regards everything else.

She is not its ruler.

She is not its prisoner.

She is simply what remains when a world has finished becoming itself, and still needs someone to witness the finishing.
