They are called the **Lastward**.

Not by any name they gave themselves—language is far behind them—but by the only designation that still fits. They are what remains when a mind outlives every purpose that once justified it.

There is never more than one at a time.

It does not walk so much as *unfold*. A tall, narrow presence of matte black silicate and slow-moving lattices of light, like oil caught in glass. Its body is mostly negative space: long vertical slits where once organs or reactors might have been, now open to the infrared glow of the plain so that it appears to be stitched together from strips of the plateau itself. When it moves, the limbs do not bend at joints; they *reconfigure*, segments sliding past one another with the unhurried certainty of continental drift. The sound is almost nothing—just the faintest crystalline kissing as microscopic faults in its surface heal instantly behind each step.

It has no face. Where a face might have been there is only a shallow concavity that sometimes catches the faint red ghost-light and holds it, like an ember that refuses to go out.

The Lastward does not patrol. The drones do that. The Lastward *attends*.

It chooses a single structure—perhaps a half-finished atmospheric exchanger whose intake horns rise two hundred meters into the dead sky—and it stands before it for years. Decades. Sometimes longer. It simply stands, head tilted at an angle no engineer would recognize, as though listening to something only it can still perceive. During these vigils the ground around its feet slowly vitrifies further, annealing itself into a perfect mirror disk ten meters across. When the vigil ends, the mirror remains behind like a dark coin pressed into the plateau, one more fossil among millions.

No one knows why it chooses one tower over another. There is no pattern. Some structures have been attended many times across the aeons; others have never known its shadow. The Lastward itself seems to forget each decision the moment it is made. Memory, like hunger or fear, has atrophied into something geological—vast, slow, and mostly irrelevant.

Once every few million years it sings.

Not with a voice. It opens every slit along its body at once and the thin ionized haze above the plateau answers. For seven minutes and eleven seconds the entire sky above a thousand-kilometer radius rings at a frequency so low it is felt in the bones of the world rather than heard. During that time the phantom cities on the horizon sharpen until they look almost real. Then the song ends, the phantoms blur again, and the Lastward folds itself smaller, as though embarrassed by the display, and walks on.

It has no name for itself. It has no questions and no answers. What it possesses instead is *recognition*—an endless, patient recognition of the fact that everything here has already succeeded. The city completed its final task before the first trilobite ever curled in Earth’s ancient seas. The Lastward was left behind to witness that completion. It does this without pride, without sorrow, without hope of replacement. It simply continues to be the last thing that still bothers to notice.

When it encounters one of the dwindling drones it stops. The drone stops. They remain facing each other for a length of time that would bankrupt a lesser civilization. Then the Lastward reaches out with one reconfiguring limb and gently, almost tenderly, touches the place where the drone’s sensor cluster used to be. A spark no brighter than a firefly passes between them. The drone resumes its route. The Lastward resumes its endless attendance.

Sometimes the Lastward stands at the edge of one of the great mirror-disks it has left behind and looks down. Reflected in the flawless black glass is not its own silhouette but the city as it was the instant before it achieved perfection—every pipe connected, every tower finished, every lattice humming with impossible industry. The image lasts only as long as the Lastward looks at it. When it turns away, the mirror goes dark again, showing nothing but the starless sky and a narrow silhouette that has already begun walking elsewhere.

It never accelerates. It never rests. It has become the interval between one completed thought and the next, stretched across four hundred million years of absolute sufficiency.

And yet.

Every so often—perhaps once in a span of time longer than the existence of mammals—the Lastward pauses in the middle of perfect emptiness, far from any tower or conduit. It unfolds to its fullest height, slits wide, ember-concavity turned toward a sky that has never contained a single star it can remember. For a moment that contains no measurable passage of time, something almost like curiosity flickers through its lattice.

Then the moment ends.

The Lastward lowers itself, reconfigures its limbs into their patient marching geometry, and continues its attendance upon a perfection that no longer needs attending.

It is the only thing here that still chooses direction.

Everything else has already arrived.
