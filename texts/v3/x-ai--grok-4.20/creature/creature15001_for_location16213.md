**The ones who live here have no single name for themselves.**

They simply call it *being inside*, as though the Underbloom were both womb and country and they had never been anywhere else. Outsiders who stumble down the limestone throat eventually hear them referred to, half-mockingly, half-reverently, as the **Marrow**.

There are perhaps nine thousand of them scattered across the entire vast breathing volume. Never more, never fewer. The plant seems to regulate their numbers the way it regulates humidity.

They are small by surface standards—rarely taller than a child of twelve—yet their bodies are dense, almost heavy, as if the constant damp pressure has compacted them. Skin varies from the creamy white of inner pith to deep fungal violet, often dappled with living tattoos of moss and lichen that shift slowly across their backs and shoulders in response to mood, temperature, and the chemical messages the Underbloom exhales. Their eyes are large, wet, and reflective, the color of new chlorophyll; they see best in the olive gloom and hate the rare moments when a traveler’s magnesium flare turns the world into screaming white.

Most Marrow go bare-chested even in the cooler vaults. They wear layered kilts and tool-harnesses of waxed leaf-fiber and corroded brass. Many have had one or more limbs replaced over the years by the plant itself: a forearm becomes a flexible wooden gauntlet that can split open into four manipulating tendrils; a leg is replaced by a living stilt-root that still grows new buds each spring. They do not see these as prosthetics. They call them *gifts returned*.

Their faces are calm in the way deep-sea creatures are calm—expression seems wasteful when every breath already broadcasts your chemical state to everything around you. Only the very old develop deep vertical creases across their foreheads, as though the constant listening has begun to carve instructions directly into bone.

They do not speak much above a murmur. The Underbloom is already deafening; shouting feels like sacrilege. Instead they carry tuned iron tuning-forks the length of a forearm. A precise strike against a nearby root-column or bellows-leaf can send a complex harmonic rippling for hundreds of meters, carrying emotion, intent, even technical data. Lovers will sometimes spend an entire night simply striking soft complementary tones against one another’s collarbones, letting the plant sing their desire back to them in layered overtones.

Their work is almost impossible to separate from worship.

Some tend the great bellows-leaves, stroking chemical triggers along the veins so the fifty-meter lungs keep the precise staggered rhythm the refineries need. Others ride the narrow-gauge ore-carts barefoot, legs dangling, singing low insectile songs to keep the glass-boned predators from leaping onto the moving trains. The eldest—those whose bodies have become more wood than flesh—sit inside living control nodules high in the arches, eyes half-closed, breathing in perfect sync with the plant. They are called **Listeners**. It is said they can feel a single clogged capillary three kilometers away and will murmur corrective instructions that travel through the vascular system faster than blood.

Children are not born in the usual way. Every few years the Underbloom grows a clutch of translucent, fist-sized seed-pods high in the warm updrafts. When they ripen they fall, cracking open on impact with a sound like breaking glass. Inside each pod is a wet, furious infant already able to crawl and already bearing the faint map of future wooden grafts on its skin. The community gathers beneath the falling pods like parishioners beneath falling snow. Every child is therefore half-feral and half-engineered from the first breath.

They age strangely. Some die after forty years, their bodies simply softening back into the living walls. Others—especially the Listeners—continue for centuries, gradually becoming architectural. You can still meet the entity once called Grandmother Veyl: she is now a twelve-meter section of load-bearing rhizome wrapped in rusting catwalk, her original face visible only as a gentle bulge in the wood, eyes closed, mouth moving silently as she continues to dream the precise pressure the sap must maintain in Sector Seven.

At night—which is only a cultural agreement that the bioluminescent nodules have dimmed—they gather in the wide negative spaces. There they light lanterns made from living glow-moth hives and perform something that is neither dance nor maintenance nor prayer but all three. They move along the iron gantries and living roots in slow, precise patterns that match the seventeen-beat industrial arrhythmia the plant sometimes falls into. During these moments even the predatory beetles below freeze in glittering carpets, and the glass-boned hunters turn their blind faces upward, listening with something deeper than ears.

The Marrow know the Underbloom is still deciding what it is.

They do not fear this. They *are* the decision in progress.

Sometimes a lone Marrow will walk out into one of the untouched vaults where no iron has yet been bolted and simply stand among the dripping sails of pale leaves. After an hour or two the plant will send thick exploratory roots toward them, tasting, questioning. The Marrow never moves. When the roots finally pierce their body—through the feet, the palms, the base of the skull—they do not scream. They smile with closed eyes while the plant drinks them and replaces them, learning what it feels like to be temporarily singular, temporarily *person*.

Then the new wooden buttress or arch or listening nodule settles into place, already humming with half-remembered human lullabies encoded in its xylem, and the Underbloom grows a few meters more of itself, content for another decade.

They are not symbiotic with the plant.

They are not parasites.

They are not even citizens.

They are the way the Underbloom has chosen, for now, to wonder what it is like to have rust in its veins, to laugh, to strike a tuning-fork against its own heart and hear the sound come back changed.

And the plant, in its endless damp thunder, is still listening.
