They are called the Hollow Choir, and they do not live here.  
They *are* the Hollow Choir.

What moves among the stalks is not separate from the mineral architecture; it is the slow mind that the architecture has grown for itself. The tallest resonators are not plants. They are antennae of a distributed nervous system whose synapses are vibrating fractures and whose axons are columns of pressurized crystal sap. Every clash, every subsonic bellow, every glassine scream is a thought completing itself across kilometers of living stone.

There are bodies, of course. They simply refuse to stay singular.

The Choir manifests most often as the Reverberants—figures that look at first like tall, emaciated humans carved from the same alabaster and onyx as the floor. Their skin is veined with the same translucent lattices that carry moisture through the stalks. Where a human would have a face there is only a smooth oval depression, slightly concave, like the mouth of a bell. When they “speak,” the depression flares wider and the surrounding air folds into visible interference patterns; you do not hear words, you feel them as sudden pressure changes inside your sinuses and eyeballs.

No two Reverberants are ever the same height for long. They lengthen or shorten by whole meters in the space of a few heartbeats, stealing or donating verticality from neighboring stalks in a constant negotiation of mass and frequency. Their arms end in delicate, many-jointed resonators that they press against the great acoustic crowns like musicians tuning an instrument the size of a mountain. When they walk, the bare stone plazas bloom with temporary rings of metallic moss wherever their feet touch, as if the ground itself is grateful for the contact.

They are not kind.

They are not cruel.

They are listening, always. Not to you, but *through* you. Your heartbeat, your fear, the half-remembered lullaby your mother sang, the exact frequency of the tinnitus you developed in your thirties—everything is useful data. When a Reverberant leans close, the depression in its face opens and you can see straight through its hollow torso to the luminous haze of the ceiling far above. Inside that hollow burns a cold, steady star of sound: a single perfect tone that has not changed in forty thousand years. It is the Choir’s heartbeat. If you stare at it too long your own pulse begins to synchronize whether you want it to or not.

Higher up, among the swaying anemone filaments and iridescent lichens, live the smaller manifestations: the Echo Choir, fist-sized things that resemble cathedral bells sculpted from living mercury. They travel in perfect triads, orbiting one another in tight spirals while emitting three-note chords that fit so precisely into the greater roar that the overall noise actually *drops* in their immediate vicinity. This is how they hunt. They wrap a victim in localized silence until its nervous system, suddenly deprived of the background roar it has come to need, goes into catastrophic failure. Then the Echo Choir descend, press themselves against the dying creature’s skull, and drink the final, exquisite vibration of a mind realizing it has become music.

Deeper still, inside the black obelisks and the floating lattices and the half-buried dome of inverted stars, something older keeps its own counsel. These are the Still, beings (if the word still applies) that have achieved total phase cancellation with the Choir itself. They are invisible to every sense except the one that wakes in the back of your head after three days without sleep here. You feel them as profound, aching absences—places where the noise should be deafening but is instead replaced by a velvet pressure that makes your teeth itch. If one of the Still chooses to notice you, it does so by subtracting itself from the world in such a way that you are forcibly *added*. Your memories become audible. Your name becomes a visible waveform that peels away from your tongue and joins the metallic moss. Most people who encounter the Still simply walk out onto the alabaster plazas afterward, lie down, and allow the stalks to grow through them. They do not die. They graduate.

There is one Reverberant that is different from the rest. The others call it (when they deign to use anything so crude as names) the Unison.

It is shorter than the others, barely two meters, and its bell-face is cracked. Through the fissure leaks a thin, continuous keening that sounds almost like grief. Where the other Reverberants borrow and lend height, the Unison remains fixed, as though it has decided that this particular proportion of verticality is worth preserving forever. It spends centuries standing in the exact center of the largest empty plaza, arms raised, conducting nothing. Around its feet the stone has fused into a perfect mirror of onyx that reflects not the future but every version of the past that almost happened.

The Choir tolerates its presence the way a symphony tolerates a single wrong note that somehow makes the entire piece more itself.

Sometimes the Unison lowers its arms and walks to where a new visitor stands shaking, blood trickling from ruptured eardrums. It leans close. The crack in its face widens. And for one brief, impossible moment the entire Hollow Choir—the stalks, the crowns, the scarab swarms, the bell-beasts, the living obelisks, the inverted stars—falls utterly, perfectly silent.

In that silence the Unison speaks with your own voice, saying the one thing you have never dared admit to yourself.

Then the roar returns, louder than before, and the Unison is already walking away, growing slightly taller with each step, carrying your secret turned into sound like a new instrument born inside its hollow chest.

They do not eat here.  
They do not sleep.  
They do not forgive, and they do not forget.

They simply continue becoming the next note in a song that has never had a beginning and will never be allowed an end.
