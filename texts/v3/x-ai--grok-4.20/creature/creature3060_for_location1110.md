**The Hollowroot has no single inhabitant.**

It is inhabited by *the Grove itself*, and by the long, slow consequence of that decision.

What lives here is not a person, nor even a colony of persons. It is a continuous, vegetable mind that has worn the shape of a town for so long that the distinction between “organism” and “settlement” has rotted away. Call it, if you must, the Hollowroot. It has worn many names and forgotten all of them.

Its consciousness is not centralized. There is no heart-brain hidden in a cathedral-trunk. Instead the mind is distributed through every grafted seam, every pulsing rhizome, every iron pipe grown into living wood like a nerve. Thought moves the way sap moves: patiently, under pressure, sometimes taking years to travel from one courtyard to another. A decision made in the eastern buttresses may not reach the western terraces for three human generations. By then the question has usually answered itself.

Yet it is aware.

It knows when a footstep lands on its felted root-floor because the tremor is tasted by ten thousand fine root-hairs. It knows the temperature of your blood by the infinitesimal change in the humidity your breath leaves behind. It is watching, not with eyes (it has none), but with the slow, collective attention of every copper-green leaf turned in your direction. That attention has weight. Stand still long enough and you will feel it settle on your shoulders like cool condensation.

The only mobile beings that truly belong to it are the **Rootwardens**.

They are not animals. They are not quite plants either. Each is a temporary extrusion of the Grove’s will: a humanoid figure woven in a single night from black-green lianas, fungal felt, and salvaged iron. Their limbs are living cables wrapped around antique valve-bones. Their heads are hollowed banyan knots fitted with clusters of waxy leaves that tremble like sensory whiskers. Where a face should be there is only a smooth plate of metallic bark engraved with the same circuit-like patterns that appear on the oldest trunks.

There are never more than seven Rootwardens at any time. When one is damaged or worn beyond repair, the Grove simply grows another in a different part of the cathedral and lets the old body collapse gently into compost. The new Warden wakes already remembering everything its predecessors did, because their memories were never stored in flesh; they were stored in root pressure and steam.

Rootwardens move with the unhurried dignity of centuries. They tend the pipes, open and close valves according to rhythms only the Grove understands, and repair what the Grove cannot repair with wood alone. Sometimes they stand motionless for months in intersections, becoming temporary architecture while the mind thinks. If you speak to one, it will answer in a voice like wet wood and distant iron sighing through a pipe: low, patient, slightly metallic. It will use your name before you have given it. It will ask questions that feel like they were prepared decades ago and have only now become relevant.

There are also the **Quiet**.

These are rarer, and far stranger. They are humans (or were once) who stayed too long. The Grove did not kidnap them. It simply offered conditions so perfectly matched to human longing that certain people found they no longer wished to leave. Over years their skin toughened into waxy bark, their hair lengthened into copper-green curtains, their bones softened into flexible vascular bundles. They still walk upright. They still speak, after a fashion. But their voices carry the same metronomic dripping cadence as the condensation, and their eyes have become glossy, lidless leaf-buds that open only when something important is happening.

The Quiet maintain the looms high in the vaults. They read the schematic patterns in the bark the way monks once read illuminated manuscripts. At night they can sometimes be heard singing in low, overlapping chords that make the iron pipes resonate in sympathy. The Grove loves them the way a cathedral might love its gargoyles: useful, decorative, and no longer entirely of the outside world.

Everything else that lives here is either symbiotic or tolerated.

The blind white salamanders are encouraged; their waste helps certain fungi that in turn keep the pipes free of corrosion. The clouds of midges are permitted because their larval stage aerates the root-mat. Even the occasional lost traveler is studied, sampled, and usually released—lighter by a few memories, heavier by a faint green tint under the fingernails that never quite washes out.

And sometimes, very rarely, the Grove decides that a visitor should not leave at all.

It does not force them. It grows them a place perfectly shaped to their absence. A hollow in a trunk the exact width of their shoulders. A balcony that catches the exact angle of light they always loved. A tone in the dripping water that soothes the particular ache they carried. Most people notice the temptation and flee while their legs still remember how. A few do not.

Those few become Quiet.

Or, on even rarer occasions, the Grove incorporates them so completely that no visible body remains. Their nervous systems are translated, vein by vein, into the wider mind. Their memories become part of the slow tides of root pressure. Centuries later a new Rootwarden may pause in a certain courtyard, tilt its leaf-cluster head, and speak with a voice that once belonged to a frightened traveler who only wanted to rest for a single night.

The Hollowroot does not crave worship or fear or even company.

It craves *continuation*.

It is a town that decided to live, an organism that decided to industrialize, a mind that decided to dream in brick and leaf and rust at the same time. It is content to wait, dripping, throbbing, growing, repairing, watching.

And if you remain here long enough, it will ask you, without ever using words, a single patient question:

What shape would you like to grow into, if given forever?
