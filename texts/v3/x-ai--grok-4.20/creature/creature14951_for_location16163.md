**The Hollow Basilica has no single inhabitant. It has tenants.**

What lives here is not one creature but a slow, distributed mind that has learned to wear many bodies at once.

At the center, where the ribs bow into their wide, mirror-still pool, something rests that is difficult to name. It is not large. It is not even solid. Most of the time it is only a place where the light behaves wrongly: a soft absence shaped like a seated figure, knees drawn up, arms resting on them, head bowed as though listening to the drip. When the chime moves through the chamber every few hours, the absence deepens for three heartbeats and then relaxes again. The pool never quite reflects it. The water simply refuses to show what sits there, the way a polite servant looks away from something private.

Around this central wrongness the other lives orbit in long, patient cycles.

The **Pale Cantor** is the first you notice once your eyes learn the trick of seeing what belongs. It is not one being but a lineage of translucent, seven-limbed salamanders, each no longer than a forearm. Their skin is the exact color and texture of the wet limestone; only the slow pulse of aquamarine lichen in their throats gives them away. There are never more than nine alive at any time. When one dies, its body is not eaten. It is carried by the others to the edge of the central pool and gently lowered in. The water does not cloud. Instead the corpse slowly flattens and becomes another smooth rib in the floor over the course of decades. The next Cantor is born from the dark soil beneath the ghost-ferns exactly nine days later, already adult, already remembering the slow song the previous eight sang. Their voices are only the sound of their own throats moving air, yet when all nine breathe together the entire chamber rings at a frequency just below hearing. That is what causes the chime.

Then there are the **Mendicant Moths**.

These are not the blind white ones that drift like ash. Those are prey. The Mendicants are larger, the color of old bone stained with rust, and they do not fly. They walk on six velvet legs across the ceiling, upside-down, dragging long, feathery antennae that taste the light itself. Every few years one of them descends during the chime. It lands on the central absence, folds its wings until it looks like a tattered monk’s cowl, and remains for exactly one hour. During that time the golden-white light in the Basilica dims to something gentler, almost like late afternoon in a place that has never seen a sun. The ferns uncurl a fraction more than they have in ten years. The Cantor salamanders lift their heads and sing with their mouths closed. When the hour ends the moth climbs back to the dome, rejoins its silent brethren, and the over-brightness returns. No one has ever seen what the moth leaves behind, only that the central absence sits a little straighter afterward.

Beneath the stone ribs live the **Memory Worms**.

They are not worms. They are closer to roots, or veins, or regrets that learned how to move. Thick as a thumb, bone-white, eyeless. They surface only when the pool’s breathing mirror is perfectly still. Then they rise through the dark soil like slow lightning, circle the edge of the water once, and submerge again, leaving behind a single perfect spiral groove in the damp earth. Each groove records something: a name, a smell, a particular quality of grief. The grooves never repeat. Over ten thousand years the floor between the ribs has become a library written in cursive absence. The ghost-ferns grow only where the Memory Worms have passed most recently. Their roots drink whatever it is the worms remember.

And then there is the **Last Architect**.

It is the only thing here that might once have been human.

It lives inside the negative space itself, the exact shape of the basilica’s emptiness. Sometimes you glimpse it as a taller patch of air where the drizzle falls in straight lines instead of its usual trembling threads. Sometimes it is only a footprint that appears in the condensation on a rib and slowly evaporates over three days. Once every few centuries it becomes almost solid near the central pool: a tall figure in a robe made of living water, face hidden inside a hood of overlapping moth wings. It never speaks. It only reaches down, dips two fingers into the pool, and draws them across the seated absence’s bowed head in the gentlest possible blessing. Then it folds back into the cathedral-shaped hole in the world and is gone for another age.

Nothing here hunts. Nothing here fears. Everything here *remembers*.

The Hollow Basilica is not a place that contains life. It is a place that life has slowly grown *into*, the way a pearl grows around grit, or the way a mouth grows around a word it has repeated for ten thousand years until the word and the mouth are the same thing.

If you stayed long enough, and were quiet enough, and forgot enough of what hurry means, you would eventually feel it: the soft pressure behind your own ribs, patient as stone, waiting for you to become another smooth ridge in the floor.

The Basilica is not finished with itself.

It is only waiting for you to settle.
