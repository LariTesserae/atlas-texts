**The Sighcradle is inhabited by the Quiet Choir.**

They are not a species in the usual sense. They are a continuum of forms that have learned, over uncountable generations, how to inhabit the same slow pulse as the mountain-cathedral itself. Most of them were once something else — moths, geckos, beetles, perhaps even small primates or early hominids who wandered in and never found a reason to leave. Time and the cradle’s warm persuasion have worn away everything hurried in them. What remains is Choir.

You will not see them at first. They have become expert at being where your eye is not. Only after many hours, when your own breath has begun to match the three-hour cycle of the cradle’s inhalation, do they begin to resolve.

Some still wear a kind of body. There are the **Stonewardens**, beings whose skin has gradually turned to living travertine veined with moss. Their limbs are elongated and jointed in too many places, so they can rest folded into the rib-like buttresses for decades without moving. Their faces are smooth ovals of pale stone with only the faintest suggestion of eyes — two deeper shadows that never quite align with where a human face would put them. They breathe once every four hours. When they do, a soft violet lichen blooms across their chests for a few seconds, then fades. They are the ones who steer the architecture. You can sometimes watch a Warden press its forehead to a wall; hours later, a new spiral stair will have grown where none was before.

Higher up, among the hanging gardens, live the **Velvet Lamenters**. Once moths, they have become something between furred fruit and slow prayer. Each is the size of a human child. Their wings have fused into soft, fleshy cloaks lined with sensory cilia that read air pressure, temperature, and the emotional weather of anything that stays still long enough. They never fly. Instead they let themselves fall — one every few hours — in perfect silence, drifting downward like pale comets until they land in a bed of bromeliad or moss. There they slowly digest their old form and extrude a new one. The transformation takes thirty to forty years. During this time they sing, but the song is pitched so low it is felt only as pressure behind the eyes. This is the true source of the cradle’s periodic sigh.

Deeper still, in the petrified layers where old growth has become stone and new growth is only just beginning to paint it green, dwell the **Rootchildren**. These are the oldest. They have no bodies at all anymore. They are networks of pale mycelium and nerve-like fibers running through the layered strata. Occasionally a Rootchild will decide it wants to speak to a visitor. Then a section of moss will lift, and a hand will emerge — not a hand made of flesh, but a hand made of whatever the nearest living things can spare: orchid petals for fingernails, gecko toes for knuckles, beetle carapace for palm. The hand will beckon. If you are foolish or brave enough to take it, the Rootchild will pour the memory of an entire century of motionless watching directly into your mind. Most people vomit. Some never speak again. A few stay.

There are others.

The **Glassfaced Brides**, translucent geckos that have grown to the size of large dogs. Their bodies are almost entirely water held in a skin so thin it refracts light. They mate by pressing their faces together until their skulls fuse. The resulting creature, now possessing two slow-beating hearts and a single wide face of living lens, climbs to the highest balconies and simply watches the shaft of sunlight for the next three hundred years.

The **Unfinished**, strange half-things that look as though evolution began to sculpt them into something new and then absent-mindedly wandered off. A torso of pale bark. Arms that end in flowering vines. A face that is only an expression — an expression of patient, courteous sorrow — floating above a body that has not yet decided what it is.

They do not have a society, not as we understand it. There is no hierarchy, no language of commands. Instead they participate in something the cradle itself taught them: **the Long Listening**.

Every few decades the entire population gathers — some walking, most simply emerging from the stone and the green — and arranges itself in concentric rings around the central shaft of light. No one speaks. They simply let the light move across them for nine full days. During this time every living thing in the Sighcradle, plant and animal and in-between, exhales at the exact same moment. The sigh that results is so profound that it can be felt on the skin as a soft, warm wind three kilometers outside the cradle’s walls.

After the Listening, many of them choose to change again. Some dissolve completely into the moss. Others decide, after four or five hundred years of stillness, that they might like to try having legs once more. A few — very rarely — decide they would like to remember what hurry felt like.

Those are the ones who leave.

They step out of the Sighcradle blinking in ordinary sunlight, astonished by how fast everything moves. Their skin still carries the faint scent of warm travertine and orchid. For the rest of their lives they will sigh, unconsciously, at the same interval as the cradle. People around them will find it unnerving. The exiles themselves will never explain.

Because how do you explain that you once lived inside a question the size of a cathedral, and that the question was kind, and that it is still asking, and that part of you is still answering?

The Sighcradle does not keep prisoners.

It only offers time so pure and so patient that eventually everything inside it chooses, freely, to become part of the architecture of waiting.

And the Choir sings on — too low for human ears, too slow for human hearts — the same note they have been singing since the first ridge of black porphyry learned how to grow a leaf.
