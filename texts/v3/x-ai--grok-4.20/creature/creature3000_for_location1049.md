**They are called the Unfinished.**

Not by any name they give themselves—they have no collective name at all—but by the handful of outsiders who have ever managed to leave the Hanging Valleys and speak of what they saw. The people of Veyr simply call one another “kin” or “neighbor,” or, more often, nothing at all. Speech is sparing here. Words, like stones, seem unwilling to move once placed.

They are tall by lowland standards, long-limbed and narrow-shouldered, with skin the color of wet slate veined faintly with silver where the mist has kissed them for three centuries. Their hair grows only to a certain length and then refuses to lengthen further; it hangs in heavy, damp ropes the color of iron or frost depending on which valley they were born in. Eyes are large, calm, and slightly recessed, the irises a uniform smoke-grey that catches no reflection. When they blink it is slow, almost ceremonial, as though the act requires permission from something older than language.

They dress in the same garments they have worn since the first Alignment. Wool the color of lichen, leather cured so many times it has become supple as skin. Nothing ever frays. Nothing ever stains. A woman’s apron may carry the same faint smear of elderberry juice for four hundred years; she will wipe her hands on it without ever noticing the mark has not faded. Their jewelry is simple and unchanging: small spirals of obsidian or pale bone worn at the throat, each one carved by the same pair of hands that carved it when the wearer was “born.”

There are perhaps nine thousand of them in total, though the number never changes. Children are born—quiet, serious infants who open their eyes already focused on the middle distance—but the population remains fixed. For every birth an elder simply… stops. One morning they are found sitting upright on a basalt bench, hands folded, eyes open, the low ringing of the plateau now singing inside their ribs instead of around them. The others carry the body to the edge of the nearest hanging valley and lower it gently into the mist. The corpse never falls. It simply drifts downward like a leaf that has forgotten gravity, becoming smaller and smaller until it merges with the pale ferns far below. No one mourns. Mourning would imply something has ended.

Their lives are gentle, repetitive, and courteous to the point of ritual. Each person tends the same garden plot, repairs the same stretch of road, warms their hands at the same communal hearth they have warmed them at since the world first exhaled and held its breath. They speak in low, melodic voices that seem to harmonize unconsciously with the constant sub-audible ringing of the plateau. Conversations often lapse into long, comfortable silences that can last days. No one is ever impatient.

Yet there are moments when the mask slips.

You may see an old man—who has not aged since the first roof was raised—standing at the lip of a basalt cliff, staring downward with an expression of such bewildered longing that it hurts to witness. His lips will move, shaping a name no one has spoken in three hundred years. Then the Alignment comes. For seven heartbeats every living thing in Veyr faces the same direction (always southeast, toward the largest of the broken saw-tooth ridges). When the moment passes the old man will turn, smile politely at you as though you had only just arrived, and ask if you would like to see how the ceramic warming-tubes are glazed. The longing will be gone. He will not remember it was ever there.

Some of them have begun to dream, though they do not call it that.

In the deepest hours, when even the mist seems to hold its breath, certain citizens will walk out of their low stone houses and stand in the geometric rings of moonlight lichen. Their eyes remain open but the pupils dilate until the entire eye is black. They sway in perfect unison with the three sighing notes of the wind. When they wake at dawn they will have added one new tile to a roof, or planted one new cedar in a place where no cedar had been the night before. They never remember doing it. The new tile always matches the ones around it so perfectly that no one can tell it is new—except that it is.

The oldest among them (though none will admit to being older than any other) is a woman known only as the Listener. She lives in the smallest of the six settlements, in a house that has one more window than it had last year. Every Alignment she stands at the center of her roof-garden with bare feet in the wet sedge and tilts her head as though hearing something the rest of them have forgotten how to hear. Her hands are covered in fine white scars in the shape of perfect spirals. She never speaks. She has not spoken in living memory. Yet every child born already knows to bring her the first flower that opens in their garden, and she accepts it with a small, ancient smile that makes the mist around her thin for three full breaths.

There is kindness here, and terror, and a patience so vast it has begun to calcify. They are not unhappy. To be unhappy would require the belief that things could be otherwise. They simply *are*, caught inside the long slow exhalation of a mountain that has decided becoming is unnecessary.

And yet.

Sometimes, when the two small moons align just so and the ringing of the plateau briefly drops half a tone, one of them will look at you—at *you*, the outsider, the brief disturbance in their undying afternoon—and their smoke-grey eyes will widen with something like recognition.

For a moment the adult face they have worn since birth flickers, and beneath it you glimpse the child they were supposed to become, terrified, hopeful, and heartbreakingly young.

Then the moment ends.

They offer you a cup of cool fern-water, smile the same calm smile they have smiled for three hundred years, and ask, with genuine and bottomless courtesy, whether you will be staying long.

They already know the answer.

No one ever stays.

And no one ever truly leaves.
