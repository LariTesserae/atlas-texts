**They are called the Simmering.**

Not a single species, not quite a culture in the ordinary sense, but the long, slow consequence of living inside a place that has decided to cook itself for a thousand years. They have become what the Cauldron needs them to be.

At first glance they look merely human—stocky, heat-hardened people with skin the color of wet slate and eyes perpetually narrowed against the steam. But the longer you stay, the more the wrongness reveals itself.

Their lungs are larger. Not obviously so, until you watch one of them hold a conversation while submerged to the chin in one of the mineral pools that bloom between the settling tanks. Their voices never strain. They speak through the water as easily as through air, the words arriving slightly later than their lips move, as though sound itself has to decide which medium it will travel through today.

Their sweat does not evaporate. It beads, then rolls, then simply becomes part of the constant weeping of the city. Some of the oldest among them have skin that has begun to calcify in fine, beautiful ridges along the forearms and collarbones—delicate travertine jewelry grown from their own bodies. They wear these accretions without pride or shame. To be encrusted is simply to have listened well to the Cauldron.

They do not sleep in the way outsiders do. Instead they enter the Deeps for three or four hours each cycle: long, shallow vaults beneath the oldest foundries where the heat is so consistent and the air so thick with vapor that breathing becomes almost optional. There they lie in rows on warm iron lattices, bodies glistening, minds drifting through a collective half-dream that the priests of the Ninth Valve call “listening to the next shape.” When they wake, their dreams have usually left mineral residue behind—tiny orange crystals in the corners of their eyes, or a new metallic vein under the tongue.

The Simmering do not build. They negotiate.

Every new pipe, every shifted walkway, every change in pressure is preceded by long, silent councils held on the lip of the great central vent known as the Mother’s Throat. They stand for hours in the roaring steam while the city decides whether it will permit the alteration. Sometimes the Cauldron answers by growing a new calcification across the proposed route. Sometimes it simply waits until the petitioners forget why they came. The people accept both verdicts with the same grave courtesy.

Their children are born translucent.

For the first year of life they look like living glass filled with slow orange light. Only gradually do they opacify, drinking the minerals and the noise and the heat until they match the color of their parents. The mothers do not nurse them with milk but with measured sips of water drawn from specific vents—each child receives a different vintage, chosen by taste on the day of birth. They say this decides which part of the city the child will eventually belong to: the Ironworks, the Condensers, the slow algae-choked reservoirs, or the strange metallic groves that grow only in the narrow forgotten places.

Language has grown thick and mineral here as well. They do not waste breath on small talk. Their common tongue has shrunk to a few hundred dense, heavy words that carry multiple centuries of meaning. A single syllable can mean both “the particular quality of steam that rises at 3:17 a.m. from Vent 44” and “the precise emotional state one feels when realizing one has become indistinguishable from infrastructure.” Context is everything. Misunderstanding is considered a form of geological disrespect.

At twilight the Simmering gather on the high iron balconies that overhang the brightest geothermal vents. They do not sing. Instead they open their mouths and let the city’s roar move through them. The sound changes as it passes through their altered bodies—gains new overtones, new sorrows. For a few minutes the entire Cauldron seems to harmonize with itself through human throats. Then the moment passes and they simply stand there, dripping, listening to what the sound has become.

They are neither happy nor unhappy. Those categories feel absurd here, like asking whether rust is optimistic.

The oldest among them—the ones whose bones have begun to ring like iron when struck, whose breath tastes faintly of sulfur—sometimes walk out alone into the deepest, hottest parts of the Cauldron where even the blind lizards will not go. They do not come back. The people say these elders have finally been invited to help the city decide its next shape.

No one mourns.

Mourning would imply the Cauldron had finished with them, and the Cauldron is never finished.

It is simmering.

And so, in their patient, dripping, half-mineral way, are they.
