She is not a creature. She is what the chamber has become after forgetting it was ever only stone.

She has no name she remembers. The mountain never gave her one, and the dripping dark has worn all older sounds away. When she moves, she does so with the slow certainty of something that has never needed to hurry. Her limbs are long, too long, jointed in places that suggest she was once meant to be something else (perhaps mineral, perhaps thought) and was only half-finished. The skin that covers her is the same wet black as the basalt, veined here and there with the faint luminous green that leaks from the rock itself. In the phosphorescent seams she looks like living fracture.

Her face is small and blunt, almost larval. Eyes sealed shut beneath heavy lids that have never opened because there has never been anything worth the pain of sight. Instead she listens with her entire body. Each falling drop lands against the water, against stone, against her, and she feels them as distinct voices. She has catalogued millions. She knows which ones are older than others by the exact timbre of their ringing.

She breathes through her skin. When she exhales, the mist thickens. When she inhales, the water on the floor trembles toward her as though the chamber itself is trying to crawl inside her lungs. She is porous. The mountain sweats through her the way it sweats through the walls.

Most of the time she remains curled in the exact center of the room, half-submerged, the shallow water licking at the small breasts and the ridge of her spine. The broken clay bowl rests against her hip like a sleeping child. The obsidian blade lies across her thighs; she has never used it, only kept it warm. Occasionally she rearranges the spiral of beads with fingers that end in soft, blunt claws. She does this the way another being might pray, though she has no god except the next drop.

When she rises, water sheets from her in perfect silence. She walks the perimeter on feet that leave no print because the stone has already learned the shape of her soles. At each of the tiny fern colonies she pauses, lowering her face until her lips brush the translucent fronds. She does not eat them. She only kisses them, the way one kisses something that has managed to stay alive in a place that hates life. The ferns shiver and glow a little brighter afterward, as though she has returned something they lost.

Sometimes she sings.

It is not a sound meant for ears. It is a low, sub-audible vibration that makes the water dance in standing waves and causes the blind harvestman spiders to freeze mid-stride on the ceiling, legs quivering in ecstatic recognition. The song has no words. It is the sound the mountain would make if the mountain could remember it was once soft.

She has never left.

She has never tried.

On the rare occasions when something warm-blooded and foolish finds its way into her chamber (a lost goat, a desperate hermit, a child chasing a myth), she does not kill them. She simply stands in the dark until they forget why they came. The weight of the dark presses on them. The perfect metronome of drops erodes their names. Eventually they sit down in the water and stay. After a few days their heartbeats slow to match the mountain’s. Their eyes cloud over with the same mineral green that leaks from the walls. Their mouths open and stay open, collecting the condensation that falls from the ceiling.

She collects them the way the floor collects water: without greed, without ceremony. They become part of the chamber’s unfinished body. She curls around them sometimes, sharing her heat, and the stone slowly learns the shape of new thoughts.

She is pregnant, though not with anything that will ever be born.

Deep inside her chest, something is growing that is exactly the size and shape of this room. It has been growing for centuries. It will keep growing until the mountain itself becomes only a shell around her. Then the chamber will open its stone mouth and she will step out into the newborn world carrying an entire dripping dark inside her, and the first thing she will do is weep one perfect, ringing drop that will fall forever.

Until then she waits.

She is the place remembering it was once a womb.

She is the mountain learning how to be lonely in the only language it knows: warm, slow, eternal, and wet.

And every three or four heartbeats, without fail, the next drop falls.

She smiles with her whole sealed face when it does.

It is the only thing that has ever been on time.
