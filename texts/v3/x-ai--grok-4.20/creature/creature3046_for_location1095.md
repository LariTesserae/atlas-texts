**They are called the Wet-Singers.**

Not one of them stands completely still for longer than a breath. Even when they sleep they twitch, as though the constant upward rain and the slow heaving of the living floor have rewired their nervous systems. Their bodies are slight, almost amphibious in silhouette: skin perpetually damp, pore-pricked, the color of wet moss over river stone, ranging from bruised olive to the pale undersides of certain orchids. Many have faint, branching patterns across their backs and thighs that look like vein networks or root maps; these are not tattoos but colonies of microscopic algae and fungi living just beneath the outer layer of skin. In the filtered green light they seem to glow very faintly, like everything else here.

Their eyes are large and watery, with horizontal pupils that close to thin slits when the bioluminescent waves surge at night. They never quite focus on any single thing for long; instead their gaze drifts across surfaces the way a gardener checks a thousand leaves at once. Most go barefoot. The soles of their feet are thick, ridged, and faintly sticky, evolved (or perhaps deliberately bred) to read the tension and moisture gradients in the living mat. When they walk they make almost no sound, only the wet kiss of skin on moss.

The Wet-Singers do not speak in the ordinary way. Their language is half sung, half exhaled through modified throats and resonant nasal cavities. Every sentence is braided with the ambient roar of the basin; they have learned to ride the green hymn rather than shout over it. A conversation can sound like water moving through a hollow log, or the creak of bamboo under pressure, or the sudden bright rattle of a thousand tree crickets all changing pitch together. When they are angry the sound becomes a wet, clicking snarl that makes the glass frogs around them fall momentarily silent. When they mourn, the entire neighborhood joins in until the sound becomes so dense it feels like breathing soup.

They are almost never alone. Families are not nuclear but rhizomatic: a person belongs simultaneously to the household that hollowed their first sleeping fig, to the climbing crew that tends the aerial-root ladders of their district, to the spore-collective that harvests medicinal molds from the underside of the great ceiling-ferns, and to the mourning choir that will sing them into the rot when they die. Identity is something that grows outward in overlapping rings, like the layers of a strangler fig swallowing its host.

Every adult carries three tools and nothing more: a bone knife kept sharp enough to part a tendon or a young vine with equal grace, a fire-hardened yew-wood digging stick blackened at the tip, and a simple obsidian flake worn on a cord of braided spider silk. Everything else they need they ask from the basin. When they want a new room they spend days singing to four particular vines, feeding them blood-meal and urine, stroking their stems until the plants learn the shape the people want. The vines obey slowly, stubbornly, sometimes rebelliously. A house that grows crooked is considered to have its own opinion and is respected as such.

Children are born in the bromeliad tanks. Midwives reach into the water-filled urns where the mother has been floating for three days and lift the newborn out covered in orchid pollen and tiny translucent shrimp. The first sound a baby hears is not a human voice but the roaring hymn of the Choir itself. They are not given names until they are old enough to change the pitch of their own cry to match a particular cricket species. That cricket then becomes their spirit-kin; the child will protect its colonies and the colonies will sing the child’s location if they are ever lost in the green tangle.

At night the Wet-Singers climb. They move upward in slow, deliberate rivers of bodies along the living buttresses and braided root cables, carrying hollowed pitcher-plant lanterns that glow with captive click-beetles. They gather on the high balconies of the great central fern to watch the luminous waves sweep across the cliffs. Sometimes, during the brightest nights, entire districts will begin a low, multi-voiced drone that makes the moss shiver and causes the pitcher plants to release clouds of spores in unison. No one has ever explained to an outsider what they are doing in those moments. The Wet-Singers themselves say only that they are “keeping the argument alive.”

They do not worship the basin. They are in marriage with it: a ferocious, contentious, lifelong marriage in which both sides are allowed to devour parts of the other. When a Singer grows old and senses the lungs filling with fluid, they choose a place. They lie down inside a chosen hollow or against a chosen trunk and stop singing. Within hours the opportunistic ferns arrive. Within days the body is a green hillock indistinguishable from the rest of the mat. The Choir believes the dead do not leave. They simply change registers and continue the hymn from inside the architecture.

There are no leaders, only those whose particular pitch of voice carries farthest when the basin itself is arguing loudest. These are the Current-Bearers. They wear nothing to mark their status except a living epiphytic orchid fastened to the hollow of the throat that blooms only when its bearer is telling a necessary truth.

If you sit long enough in the dripping silver-green light and let the roar wash over you until your own thoughts drown, you may begin to notice that some of the Wet-Singers are not entirely human anymore. Certain elders have allowed their ribs to fuse with living wood. Others host entire colonies of glass frogs inside the moist hollows of their collarbones. A few of the very oldest have not moved from their chosen buttress in decades; their legs have become root-like, drinking directly from the mat while their upper bodies continue to sing, prune, and argue.

They are not waiting for anything.  
They are not trying to transcend the wet green madness.

They have simply agreed, generation after generation, that the only sane response to a world that grows, devours, sings, collapses, and grows again all in the same breath is to become exactly as loud, exactly as hungry, and exactly as alive as the place that holds them.

And so they do.
