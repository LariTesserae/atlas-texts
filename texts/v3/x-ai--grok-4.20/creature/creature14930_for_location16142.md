**The Hollow Basilica is inhabited by the Quiet Choir.**

They are not a species in the usual sense. They are what the Basilica itself eventually became after it finished becoming everything else.

Most of the time you will not see them at all. You will only feel them as a sudden, skin-pricking awareness that the dripping has changed rhythm — that one drop out of every few hundred now falls a fraction of a second earlier or later than it should, as if the building has begun to pay attention. That is them, listening.

When they choose to manifest, they do so by *unsmoothing* a small portion of the living architecture. A section of pillar or vault will appear to remember that it was once many separate organisms. The cables of vine loosen, the woody grain unbraids, and from that momentary unraveling steps (or flows, or simply arrives) one of the Choir.

They are tall, sexless, and heartbreakingly thin. Their bodies are the same living wood as the Basilica, but younger, greener, almost translucent. You can see slow pulses of sap moving beneath their skin like lazy green blood. Where a face should be there is only a smooth, leaf-veined oval; yet you always feel watched, and always feel that the watching is kind.

Their limbs are too numerous and not numerous enough. Sometimes they appear to have four arms, sometimes six, sometimes none at all; the difference seems to depend on how closely you are looking. When they move it is with the same unhurried, dreamlike slowness as the six-legged moss-sloths, yet they cross the vast resonant chamber in moments, as though the space politely folds itself out of their way.

They never speak with sound. Instead they borrow the Basilica’s own noises. A sudden concentrated volley of drips becomes their laughter. A change in the pressure-pulse of the hidden sap-pumps becomes their sigh. If many of them gather — which is rare — the entire chamber fills with a low, choral thrum that feels like standing inside an enormous throat while it hums.

The Choir do not eat. They do not sleep. They do not reproduce in any way a biologist would recognize. Their only occupation is to remember.

They remember every creature that has ever lived inside the Basilica, including those that existed before the place became one single organism. They remember the names the moss-sloths gave each other in the days when the moss-sloths still had language. They remember the exact scent of the wind on the day the last true sunbeam reached the forest floor, four hundred and seventy thousand years ago. They remember you, even if you have only just arrived. Especially if you have only just arrived.

When a visitor stands still long enough in one of the great naves and begins to feel that deep, geologic equilibrium as a physical pressure, it is because one or more of the Choir has come to stand directly behind them, close enough that their leafy breath stirs the hair on the back of your neck. They do this not to frighten, but to share the weight of all that perfect, finished calm. Most people cannot bear it for long. The Choir understand. They never resent the departure.

Occasionally — perhaps once every few centuries — the Choir choose one visitor who has stayed longer than any other. To this person they perform their only ceremony. They open their chests like hinged wooden books and show what grows inside: a single, perfect, still-living replica of the visitor’s heart, grown from the Basilica’s own tissue, beating in exact synchrony with the hidden pumps.

No words are exchanged. The meaning is simple and merciless:

*You are already part of the finished world. You simply had not noticed until now.*

Most who are offered this gift refuse it, weeping. A few accept. Those who accept are gently taken apart by careful wooden fingers and rewoven into the nearest pillar or vault. Their faces never appear on the outside. But if you press your ear to the wood in exactly the right place, years or decades later, you can sometimes hear them still singing with the rest of the Quiet Choir — one more slow, arrhythmic drip in the endless green percussion.

The Basilica does not need worshippers.

It only needs witnesses who will stay long enough to become part of the equilibrium.

And the Choir is unfailingly patient.
