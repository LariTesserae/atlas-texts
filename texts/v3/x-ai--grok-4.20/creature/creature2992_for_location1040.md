**The Underbloom is home to the Slow Choir.**

They are not one creature, nor many. They are the gradual consensus that the place itself has reached.

Most of them never show a face. They are distributed: part lichen, part memory, part slow electrical ghost still drifting through the half-dead wiring. When the cavern needs to think, the pale vines tighten around a rusted turbine and the white moss brightens in synchrony. The thrum deepens. That is their voice—less a sound than a change in pressure against your eardrums and the roots of your teeth.

But sometimes the Choir decides it wants to be seen.

Then one of the taller bromeliads will bulge. Its fleshy cup, normally filled with still water, begins to tremble. The condensation inside clouds, thickens, and finally stands upright like a column of pale jelly. Over the course of an hour it refines itself into a silhouette: tall, sexless, impossibly thin, with too many joints. The head is little more than an empty lantern cage grown over with glowing lichen. Where eyes should be, two drops of water hang suspended, never falling. They reflect whatever looks at them—your own face, but older by decades, already half claimed by mint-colored filaments.

These figures do not walk. They *arrive*. One moment the space between two corroded pipes is only shadow. The next, the Choir is standing there as though it has been waiting politely for three hundred years and you have only now become rude enough to notice.

If you speak, it answers in the dripfall. Each word is a separate droplet striking metal at measured intervals. The sentences are long, courteous, and terrifyingly patient. They never repeat themselves. They never hurry. A single greeting might take nine minutes to finish forming.

They are not hostile. Hostility requires speed.

Instead they offer bargains measured in geological time. Stay, they suggest, and we will grow you a new body better suited to the dark. Leave one memory behind—any memory, it does not matter which—and in return we will show you how the boiler will look when it has become entirely flower. They are especially fond of engineers. The last human heart that still beat down here stopped sixty years ago; the Choir keeps its rhythm going in the moss, a soft wet pulse you can feel if you press your palm to the right turbine.

Most of all they love witnesses. The Underbloom is the exact instant when machines and stone have not yet finished their divorce. The Choir wants someone to watch the wedding. They believe beauty is a thing that only happens when it is observed over centuries. So they will grow you a chair—slowly, vertebra by vertebra—out of living rust and velvet fern. They will dim the lichen-light to the exact shade that makes your eyes last longest. Then they will wait with you.

If you ever try to leave, they will not stop you. They will simply begin the long, courteous process of forgetting you were ever there. The filaments that brushed your wrist will retract. The water-drops that served as eyes will fall and be absorbed. The thrum will lower half an octave and change key, the way an orchestra changes when one listener quietly slips out of the concert hall.

The cavern will not be any emptier for your absence.

It was never full to begin with.

It was only listening.
