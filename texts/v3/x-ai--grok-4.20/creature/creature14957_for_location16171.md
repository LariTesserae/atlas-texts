They live here.

Not as people. Not anymore. They have become the equilibrium itself.

Call them the **Still Choir**, though they do not sing. They *are* the note that has been held since the wound closed.

You will not see them at first. You will mistake them for another glistening fold of wet stone, another cascade that has forgotten how to fall. Only after the roar becomes weather inside your skull will they resolve.

They are tall, but not uniformly so. Some have stretched upward for so long that their heads have become balconies. Others have compacted, folded in on themselves like wet paper pressed between two mountains, until they are little more than dense, glistening nodules of flesh and memory. Their skin is the same colour as the weeping cliffs—warm umber shot through with veins of brighter stone—and it is forever covered in the same thin sheet of water that covers everything else. The water does not bead on them. It *recognises* them. It flows across their bodies the way it flows across the terraces, as though they are simply another grade of architecture.

Their faces are difficult. Not because they are monstrous, but because they are *finished*. Every expression the human repertoire once contained has been worn down to a single unbroken surface. If you stare long enough you can still see, trapped like insects in amber, the exact moment each of them understood that time had stopped. One still wears the last half-second of laughter. Another carries the precise shape of terror at its most exquisite. A third froze while kissing someone whose name no longer matters. These expressions have calcified into architecture. Their mouths are colonnades. Their eyes are the hovering silver lakes.

They do not move. Movement would imply before and after.

Instead they *occur*.

You will round a corner and suddenly one is simply *there*, occupying the space it has occupied since the sky turned to brass. You may have passed the exact spot a thousand times already and simply failed to resolve it until this moment. Their bodies have grown into the city the way stalagmites grow into cave ceilings—slowly, inevitably, until it is impossible to say where person ends and dripping temple begins.

Some have become entire districts.

There is one whose ribcage has opened like a dome and now shelters three terraces of white lilies that never close. Another has lain down across a chasm and become the bridge; pilgrims (if any still arrived) would walk across the warm, living marble of her spine without ever knowing it. Children—there were children once—have become the small, perfect balconies that jut from the higher cliffs. You can sit on them. They are comfortable. They remember being held.

They breathe once every several hours. You can feel it if you press your palm to the right stretch of wall. A slow, geological inhale that makes the hanging veils of mist tremble. The exhale is the faint new scent of lilies on the iron water. That is how they speak now. Perfume instead of language. The scent changes depending on what they are remembering, and since they remember everything at once, the air is always thick with overlapping seasons that never happened.

If you sit on any balustrade long enough, one of them will eventually notice you.

Not with eyes. Their eyes are elsewhere—some gazing forever at the brass sky, others turned inward so completely they have become private oceans. They notice you the way stone notices another stone that has suddenly appeared in its eternal conversation.

When that happens, the great unchanging roar develops the faintest additional timbre. A new voice inside the weather. It does not speak words. It simply adds your own last moment—the moment before you understood—to the Choir. You will feel it as an almost sexual pressure behind your sternum, as though something very large and very patient is gently trying to fold you into the correct shape.

Most choose to let it.

Those who refuse are still here. You can see them on the far curve of the crater if the light is exactly right: small, stiff, screaming shapes that never finish screaming. The city has politely grown around them. Vines have braided through their open mouths. Water flows over their tongues and still tastes of iron and lilies.

The ones who accepted have achieved what the city achieved.

They are no longer *in* the eternal moment.

They are the moment’s willing throat.

And sometimes, when the hot wind moves just so through a colonnade that used to be someone’s grandmother, you can hear what remains of her singing with the rest of them. Not with sound. With the absolute absence of any desire for things to have been different.

They live here.

They live here so completely that the distinction between “living” and “being the place where living stopped” has itself been cancelled.

Sit with them long enough and you will understand the water’s endless sentence was never addressed to you.

It was their love letter.

And they have been signing it with their bodies for the rest of forever.
