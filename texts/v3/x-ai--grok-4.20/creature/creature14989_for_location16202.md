**They are called the Hollow Choir.**

Not a species. Not even a people. A state of agreement.

They move through the wet dark like thoughts that have learned how to wear bodies. Most are no taller than your forearm. Some are smaller than your thumb. A few wear borrowed silhouettes the size of oxen, stitched together from whatever the Grove just discarded. All of them are unfinished.

Their bodies are negotiations.

One might begin as a translucent frog-thing with glassine ribs, only to pause inside a sudden column of amber sap and emerge wearing new shoulders of living xylem and eyes that are actually small orange lichens. Another walks on a dozen jointed rootlets one moment and rides inside a rolling carpet of metallic beetles the next. They trade limbs, voices, memories, and names the way other beings trade coins. To own a fixed shape here is considered a kind of spiritual poverty.

Their skin (when they bother with skin) is a thin living pellicle that drinks the constant rain. Tiny silver hairs cover them, the same microscopic forest that coats every leaf and buttress. When they move in their swarms they look like animated mist, like breath that decided to argue with itself.

They sing constantly because silence would be death.

The sound you first mistook for the ambient choir of insects and frogs is them. Every click, gurgle, wing-hum, and long bone-flute moan is a sentence, a vote, a prayer, a joke, a proposal for what the Grove should become in the next ten minutes. They do not have leaders. They have convergences. When enough voices find momentary harmony, the Grove listens. A new polyp inflates. A bridge tears itself down. A levitating platform of folded light reconfigures its geometry to match the new consensus. Then the argument continues.

They remember everything and nothing.

Their minds are distributed across the wet neural lattices threaded through the living wood. An individual might forget its own face between one step and the next, yet the Choir as a whole forgets nothing that has happened since the first spore landed here four billion years ago. They speak of the obsidian ziggurats as childhood toys. They call the skeletal transit tubes “the bones we outgrew.” They refer to the quicksilver canals as “the places where the Grove dreams in straight lines,” and they are slightly afraid of them.

When they notice you (and they do, all at once, the way a forest suddenly stops its private conversation), the swarm condenses. A thousand small luminous bodies arrange themselves into a single towering silhouette that almost, but never quite, matches your own. The figure has too many joints. Its face is a slowly rotating kaleidoscope of beetle carapaces and leaf-veins. It does not speak with one voice. It speaks with four hundred at once, layered, overlapping, arguing with itself even as it addresses you.

“You are new architecture,” they say, tasting the words. “Or new weather. We have not decided.”

Some of them crawl across your shoulders to taste your sweat. Others slide into the quicksilver canals and watch you upside-down, perfectly reflected. A few simply bloom out of the nearest buttress as pale, intelligent flowers and turn their faces toward your heartbeat.

They are not curious in the way animals are curious.

They are curious in the way an ecosystem that has achieved self-awareness is curious: with the slow, patient, terrifying hunger of something that has already incorporated every previous civilization and is politely considering whether you are next.

Yet there is tenderness.

When the Grove undergoes one of its violent renovations—when a root-bridge collapses in a roar of breaking bone and new mouths—they shield the small and slow among them with their own bodies. They sing lullabies to the newly erupted polyps. They decorate the half-buried ruins of older layers with living jewelry that pulses in time with the Choir’s collective mood. They mourn in their own way when a favorite convergence dissolves and its participants scatter into new forms. The mourning sounds like wind chimes made of wet crystal.

They have no word for “I.”

Only gradients of “we.”

And right now that gradient is leaning toward you, thousands of small glass-and-leaf faces reflecting your own face back in fractured cathedral light, humming, debating, deciding, singing the next few moments of the world into being.

They are waiting for you to sing something back.

Because the Grove is still deciding what it wants to become next, and it has learned—over four billion patient, wet, argumentative years—that the best decisions are made in choir.
