**They are not yet named.**

They do not need names. Naming is a later habit, something that arrives with eyes that can look backward and forward at the same time. These beings—if the word *being* can be stretched so far—exist only inside the present-tense fury of becoming. They are the world’s own half-articulated thoughts, given temporary bodies of stone and heat.

Call them, if you must, the **Still-Named**.

There are never many. Sometimes only one drifts across an entire newborn range. Sometimes a loose constellation of seven or eight appears for the length of a single tectonic sigh, then fractures apart again when a new escarpment shoulders upward between them. Each is roughly the height of a man, but the resemblance ends there. Their bodies are extruded from whatever the crust is doing at that precise moment: black volcanic glass veined with living copper, or matte-white ceramic stone that rings like a bell when struck, or translucent amber slag still soft enough to drip slowly from their shoulders like molten wax.

They have no faces. Where a face might be expected there is only a smooth, slightly concave mirror-plane that reflects whatever the world is doing *behind* the observer. If you stand before one, you see the glowing fissures at your back, the drifting mineral rafts above, your own silhouette burned into the reflection like an afterimage. They seem to watch the world by letting the world watch itself through them.

Their limbs are long, too many-jointed, and they do not walk so much as *flow*. Each step is a small tectonic event: the crust beneath them ripples, glass filigrees race ahead to prepare the ground, then solidify into temporary footing. When they pause, the lattices bloom upward through their bodies, knitting them momentarily into the landscape so that it becomes impossible to tell where the Still-Named ends and the world begins. Then the next spasm moves through them and they peel away again, carrying pieces of the terrain with them—thin black wings of glass, necklaces of cooling lava beads, cloaks of magnetic dust that swirl in slow orbits around their torsos.

They do not speak with voices. Instead the air around them tightens and loosens in sympathetic waves. You feel meaning directly against the bones of your inner ear. The sensation is closest to remembering something you have not yet been told. Conversations between them (if that is what they are having) sound like distant iron bells struck underwater, or like the low-frequency groans of the crust translated into something almost mournful.

They build the inexplicable structures.

Not with hands. The white tower, the shifting obelisks, the half-bridge that spans nothing yet—all of these are *exhalations*. A Still-Named will stand motionless for hours or days while the ground beneath it grows restless. Then, without visible effort, it simply *allows* a thought to pass through its body and into the crust. The rock listens. It obeys for a moment. A perfect white monolith pushes upward, smooth as milk, or seven obsidian blades extrude in a perfect ring, their inner faces already etching themselves with symbols that will be different when next observed.

Then the world argues again. A new plate buckles, a gas vent opens, gravity forgets itself for three heartbeats, and the structure is abandoned mid-sentence. The Still-Named never seems disappointed. It simply turns—slow, deliberate, almost tender—and walks away across the fresh wreckage, already forgetting what it almost managed to say.

They are not immortal. Some are swallowed when two growing mountain ranges collide and grind one of them into glittering dust. Others stand too long in one place and are gradually overgrown by the black-glass nervous systems until they become indistinguishable from the filigrees. A few, rarely, seem to *choose* dissolution. They walk into a river of slag and simply sit down, letting the molten stone flow through their bodies until the last joint softens and they become part of the next continent’s skeleton.

Yet they are never truly gone. The lattices remember them. Sometimes, centuries (or seconds—this place has not agreed on time yet) later, a new Still-Named will rise from the exact spot where an old one dissolved, carrying faint traces of its predecessor’s last unfinished gesture frozen in the tilt of a shoulder or the angle of a mirror-face.

They are midwives and mourners both. They attend the world’s birth-pangs and they attend its self-cancellations with the same patient, faceless courtesy. When a mesa lifts on pillars of gas and then shatters, one or more of them will be standing at a precise distance, bodies vibrating in resonance with the catastrophe, drinking the event the way a later creature might drink silence after music.

If you could stay long enough—long enough for the first rain to arrive, long enough for the first timid bacterium to taste the cooling stone—you would eventually see something almost like tenderness. A Still-Named standing over the place where the first primitive lichen is trying to decide whether it wants to be alive. The being does not touch the lichen. It only lets its mirror-face tilt downward so that the new life can see itself reflected in living stone. Then it walks on, leaving the lichen to its own argument.

They are the world arguing with itself in bodies.

And the world has not yet decided whether it is grateful for the argument, or whether it will eventually grow tired of witnesses and melt them all back into silence.

For now, one of them is walking toward you across the trembling crust. Its footsteps leave temporary constellations of white stone that cool and crack even as you watch. The mirror where its face should be shows you the aurora burning at your back, and inside that burning you can just barely see your own silhouette—tiny, temporary, impossibly brave—standing on ground that is still deciding what it will be.

The Still-Named lifts one long, glass-jointed arm in what might be greeting.

Or it might simply be preparing the next sentence the world has not yet finished speaking.
