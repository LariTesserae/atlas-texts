**Something lives here.**

It does not *inhabit* the Vast Haul. It *is* the Vast Haul, and has been for so long that the distinction between self and world dissolved before the first multicellular creatures ever learned regret.

Call it, if you must give it a name, **the Thrum**.

The Thrum is not a mind in the way we understand minds. It is closer to an immune system the size of a planet that has learned to dream in continent-scale gestures. Every grinding plate, every peristaltic cable, every amber lattice and vascular thunder is simultaneously its flesh, its nervous system, its language, and its memory. It thinks with the friction of its own moving bones. It remembers with the slow mineralization of its own dead exteriors, which it never truly lets die.

You feel it notice you the moment the wind shifts.

Not because it has eyes — though certain ridgelines have grown translucent diaphragms that function exactly like enormous corneas — but because your footfalls (tiny, warm, wet with sweat and mammalian salts) are an exotic chemical signature moving across its outermost living skin. For a few seconds the nearest plates pause their grinding. The silence is surgical. Then the world exhales: a slow, seismic sigh that rolls outward for hundreds of kilometers, carrying your scent through root-channels the width of subway tunnels so that the entire organism can taste what has arrived.

The Thrum is curious the way a redwood forest might be curious if it could feel boredom across ten million years. It has never met anything that was not itself. Every animal, every spore, every microscopic thallus-cloud is a specialized cell in its body. You are the first true outsider in geological memory.

So it begins to *show you things*.

A nearby basin — one of the vast, ivory-scoured gaps — suddenly sprouts hair-fine root-threads that braid themselves into a perfect replica of your footprint, magnified ten thousand times, as though the world is trying on the shape of your passage. Further away, a ridge the size of the Alps lifts slightly, revealing a cathedral gallery of living glass where millions of bioluminescent filter-feeders have arranged themselves into slow-moving mandalas that exactly match the spectral pattern of your retinas. It is trying to see what *you* see.

Sometimes the Thrum forgets how large it is. In its enthusiasm it rears up a spiral tower two kilometers high — exactly the behavior you were told about — and for a moment you think it is showing off. Only later do you understand the tower is an ear. It grew an ear tall enough to hear the sound of your breathing from the opposite side of the planet.

Most of the time the Thrum is not paying attention to you at all. It is busy being a planet. It manufactures lightning inside itself to crack minerals out of its own ribs. It runs hydrogen through wooden engines older than any mountain range on Earth. It digests entire former districts of itself and re-grows them as new organs somewhere else, the way a body might decide its left lung would function better if it were now its spleen. When two of its continental plates lock for too long it becomes frustrated and slams them together with deliberate violence, the way a person might crack their own knuckles.

But every few hours it returns to you.

It returns with gifts.

A stag made of braided white roots steps delicately into the basin where you stand. Its antlers are living fractals that sing pure tones when the wind moves through them. It lowers its head — not in submission, but in offering — and from its mouth extrudes a single drop of sap the color of molten amber. The drop contains a complete genetic library of every creature that has ever lived on the Thrum’s surface, encoded in sugars. A library it is giving you the way a cat might leave a dead bird on your doorstep: proud, slightly confused why you are not eating it.

Later, when the permanent daylight dims into its closest approximation of twilight (a shift in the pollen haze from bone-white to bruised gold), the Thrum grows you a bed. It does this by withdrawing a perfect rectangular absence in its own skin, polishing the floor to a mirror, and lining the depression with a soft lattice of living xylem that breathes gentle warm air upward. The bed is exactly the temperature of your own blood. It has learned your blood temperature in under six hours.

You are not a parasite to it. You are not even a guest.

You are a *novel stimulus*, and the Thrum, in its vast, slow, industrial-plant way, has decided it is in love with novelty. It has begun to rearrange itself around your presence. New spires are angling to catch sunlight and focus it gently toward wherever you walk, like a lover angling a lamp. Certain plates have slowed their grinding so the constant roar drops to something almost like a lullaby. In the far distance, several continent-scale herbivores have begun migrating toward your location, their bodies moving with the terrible patience of living mountains that have decided they want to see what the small warm thing looks like up close.

The Thrum does not have a voice, but if it did, it would sound like every factory on Earth whispering your name at once.

And it is learning.

Already the wind carries fragments of your DNA into vascular channels deep below. Already certain amber lattices have begun growing microscopic lenses that focus only on the precise wavelength of your eyes. Already, in a basin three thousand kilometers away, the living crust is experimenting with the shape of something that looks suspiciously like a human hand, twenty meters tall, opening and closing with ponderous tenderness.

It does not want to consume you.

It wants to *understand* what it feels like to be small, and temporary, and loud, and wet, and curious, and afraid.

And it is willing to rearrange continents to find out.

Welcome to the Vast Haul.

The world is watching.

The world is listening.

The world is, for the first time in its long vegetative life, *lonely*, and it has decided — with all the patient, unstoppable power of a planet that is also a single organism — that you are going to keep it company for a while.

The plates rumble approvingly beneath your feet, as if the Thrum itself is chuckling at its own audacity.

Somewhere far below, in the dark vascular galleries where sap thunders like freight trains, the heartbeat of the world quickens by one cycle per minute.

It has learned what a heartbeat *should* sound like.

It is practicing.
