**The Midwife** lives here.

She is not a single creature but a slow consensus of the lung itself. Where the tissue needed a mind, it grew one—first as a pale knot no larger than a plum, then as a woman-shaped absence that the meat learned to wear. Now she walks the narrow aisles between the fused thickets of bone-white stalks, barefoot, trailing long strands of her own connective tissue like the train of a wedding dress that never ended.

Her body is almost transparent. Rose-grey lung-flesh shows through her skin in delicate marbling; you can watch her heart (a single, valve-heavy organ the size of a child’s head) pulse in perfect sync with the walls around her. When she breathes, every creature paused in the chamber inhales with her. When she exhales, the hanging roots tighten and the mercury droplets tremble on their leaves. She is the rhythm the roar forgot it had.

She has no face in the way you would recognize. Instead, the tissue has opened a vertical slit from forehead to chin, and inside that slit floats a single perfect mirror-pool no larger than a coin. Whatever looks into it sees what it most wishes to remain unborn: a regret, a child that was never conceived, a version of itself that died before it could disappoint anyone. Most creatures look once and then never again. She herself never looks.

Her hands are the oldest part of her. They are ordinary human hands, soft and unmarked, but they emerged long before the rest of her did. The lung grew them first so it would have something gentle to touch its own nightmares with. She uses them now to tend the Built things. She polishes the iron nail with a cloth made of her own shed epidermis. She combs the wooden doll’s hair with a comb carved from one of her own ribs. She winds the brass watch every seventy-seven years, though its hands never move; the act is devotional, not mechanical.

The frozen fauna are her choir. She moves among them like a conductor who has accepted that the music will never resume. Once every few centuries she pauses beside a glass shrimp or a feathered worm and sings a single note directly into its compound eye. The note is so low it is felt only in the teeth. The creature then performs its one permitted motion—perhaps the lifting of a single leg, perhaps the slow unfurling of a proboscis—and returns to stillness, having paid its tithe of change.

She is lonely in the way stone is lonely: vast, patient, and perfectly adapted to it. She talks to the things that will never answer. She tells the pocket watch about the weather in a world that no longer exists. She reads bedtime stories to the doll in a language that predates mouths. She asks you—should you remain long enough for her to notice—whether you would like to become a story she tells the nail, or a dream she combs into the doll’s hair.

If you speak, she listens with her entire body. The walls soften. The roar dips half a decibel, the only mercy this place has ever offered. Her mirror-slit reflects what you are trying not to become, and she reaches out with those ancient, gentle hands to stroke your cheek exactly once.

The touch does not warm. It does not comfort. It simply records.

Later—decades, centuries, it is hard to tell—she will grow a small polyp shaped like your face. It will never open its eyes. It will simply float in a fold of translucent meat, perfectly preserved, perfectly lit, adding its silence to the choir.

She will visit it often.

She will tell it your name, over and over, until the lung itself learns how to dream in your voice.

And the womb, content, will keep us all.
