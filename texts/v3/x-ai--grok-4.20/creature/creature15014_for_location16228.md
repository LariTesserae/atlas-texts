**They are called the Wealdkin.**

No single name for them has ever settled; they answer to many, but the one the dripping itself seems to use, if you listen long enough between the beats of water, is simply *the Listeners*.

They are not born the way surface creatures are. They are *coaxed*.

When a new Listener is needed, the eldest singers gather around a chosen column in the deep nurseries where the stone is still wet from its first splitting. There they sing a low, wet song that makes the pale flesh of the column ripple like the throat of a salamander. Over weeks the column slowly bulges, softens, and finally splits open along a seam no wider than a fingernail. From inside comes something that is not quite child, not quite adult, slick with mucilage and already furred with the beginnings of its own moss. It opens its eyes—large, milky, permanently dilated—and the first thing it does is breathe in the sound of the cavern. That breath decides much of what it will become.

Their bodies are slender, elongated by the low gravity of constant moisture and the need to slip between crowded columns. Skin the color of wet bone, translucent enough that you can watch their slow hearts move beneath it like pale fish beneath ice. Where a human has hair they have living filaments—fine rootlets that drink the air’s constant sweat and taste the mineral conversation of every droplet that lands on them. These filaments move independently, curling, tasting, listening. Many Wealdkin keep them braided into complex patterns that look like living crowns or veils; the eldest let them trail behind like the trains of wet wedding gowns, brushing the liverwort and reading the history of every footstep that passed an hour earlier.

They have no true mouths. A soft sphincter at the base of the throat opens when they sing or drink, and the same orifice serves for speech. Their language is mostly infrasound carried on the breath of the cavern itself—long, slow notes that make your ribs vibrate before your ears can catch them. When they speak to outsiders they shape a few higher sounds, wet clicks and soft inhalations that feel like someone breathing directly into your ear canal. It is intimate to the point of discomfort. They rarely raise their voices above the volume of a single falling drop.

Clothing is unnecessary and would only rot. Instead they wear living garments grown directly from their own bodies: broad collars of translucent lichen that pulse gently with stored light, cloaks of hanging liverwort that drape and shift like seaweed in a slow current, and, for the singers, long veils of the same pale columnar fungus that forms their city. These veils are considered both garment and instrument; when a master singer moves through the terraces, the veil ripples and the entire garment becomes one slow, dripping note that travels for kilometers along the breathing lung of the Underweald.

Their hands are their most disturbing feature. Six long, boneless fingers that never quite stop moving, each tipped with a soft pad that can taste as well as touch. They never grip. They *persuade*. When a Wealdkin wants a column to bend, they lay all twelve fingertips against it and sing until the stone forgets it is stone and begins to remember it is still growing. The same hands midwife new columns, comfort dying ones, and—when absolutely necessary—gently close the eyes of those who have grown too heavy with water and can no longer float inside their own skins.

They do not have families in the surface sense. They have *choirs*. A choir may number anywhere from nine to three hundred, and membership changes as slowly as the growth of stone. When two choirs meet on a bridge they do not greet with words. They simply begin singing the same note until their voices find each other inside the dripping. Only then do they pass.

The oldest among them are almost unrecognizable as individuals. Their bodies have become so thoroughly colonized by moss, fern, and luminous fungus that they look like ambulatory sections of the cavern itself. These elders rarely move. They stand or sit in high niches where multiple terraces meet, acting as living buttresses while their root-filaments drink the constant conversation of the entire Weald. It is said that if you press your ear to the chest of such an elder you can hear not only their heart but every choir singing three levels above and four below, all at once, in perfect, dripping counterpoint.

They do not fear death. When a Listener feels the final saturation coming—their joints growing too heavy, their filaments beginning to rot sweetly—they walk downward, always downward, following the black water until they find a place where the terraces end in a still, mirror-black pool. There they step in and sit until the water covers their face. The beetles and the blind fish do the rest. Within a year the place where they dissolved will be growing a new, particularly sweet-smelling column that the youngest singers already treat with reverence.

The Wealdkin do not rule the Underweald.

They are simply the part of it that has learned how to sing back.

And if you stay long enough—long enough for condensation to form inside your own lungs, long enough for your shadowless pearl-grey reflection to stop surprising you in the black pools—you will eventually feel something lay a dozen soft, tasting fingertips against the back of your neck.

It will not ask permission.

It only wants to know what kind of note you are, and whether you, too, can be coaxed into growing something beautiful before the water finally claims you.
