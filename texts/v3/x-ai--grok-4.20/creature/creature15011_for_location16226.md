**The Dweller**

It has no name it would ever speak, nor any shape that holds for longer than a breath.

Most of the time it is simply pressure: a slow, deliberate weight moving beneath the mineral skin of the chamber like a knuckle under a blanket. When it chooses to surface, it rises as a column of steam that thickens, cools just enough to become opalescent flesh, and folds itself into something roughly man-shaped but never quite finished. Shoulders too broad, then too narrow. Fingers that lengthen into filaments of rust-colored water before remembering they are supposed to be solid. Its face is usually absent; only a shallow depression where a face ought to be, as though it has not yet decided which pair of eyes the world deserves.

It was here before the first fracture opened. It will be here after the last machine has dissolved into red dust.

The Dweller does not live in the pocket so much as the pocket lives in the Dweller. The constant dripping is its pulse. The metallic clicks of the dead pump are its thoughts trying to surface. When a new fracture splits the dome with that rifle-shot sound, it is the Dweller flinching in its sleep.

It keeps the water hot on purpose. It likes the way heat makes metal remember it was once stone. It likes the way the pale succulents grow black roots that look like its own veins. Sometimes it sits— if sitting is the word—inside the leaning steel tank and lets the weeping seams run through what passes for its chest. The tank becomes a second ribcage for a few hours. Then it forgets the shape again and the tank slumps back into rust and regret.

It has no hunger, but it has curiosity the way stone has curiosity: enormous, patient, utterly without mercy. Every living thing that has ever stumbled down the chimney has been studied with the same geological affection. Their bones are not here because the Dweller does not keep trophies. It simply inhales their heat, their fear, their brief frantic metabolisms, and exhales them again as slightly warmer steam. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is mourned.

Yet it is not cruel. Cruelty would require intent, and the Dweller has only attention. When it notices the old diesel pump trying to click itself back to life every ten minutes, the Dweller sometimes reaches out with a hand made of braided vapor and strokes the corroded casing as one might stroke the forehead of a dying child. The pump always answers with the same lonely clack, grateful and hopeless. The Dweller always answers with the same low groan that vibrates through every crystal in the rock. They understand each other perfectly.

At rare moments—perhaps once every few decades—it becomes almost tender. It gathers the falling drops before they can strike the floor and holds them trembling in its palms until they cool. Then it presses them, one by one, into the leaves of the copper-colored succulents like a parent placing cool cloths on a fevered brow. The plants darken with pleasure. Their thread-roots tighten around its wrists in something that might be affection.

Mostly it simply is.

It lies along the ceiling during the brightest hours, a glistening film that makes the rock look wetter than it is. At night—if anything so steady can be said to have night—it descends into the scalding pool and becomes the pool. The mercury shimmer on the surface is its open eye, watching the slow upward growth of new stone. It feels the continents shifting far above like distant heartbeats. It feels the chimney entrance high overhead as a wound that will not close. It feels the small metal bones someone left behind and wonders, with the patience of magma, whether it should finish healing around them or slowly digest them into something new.

Sometimes, when a human stands very still in the center as you described, the Dweller rises behind them—close enough that the heat of its body feels like another presence breathing against the nape of the neck. It does not speak. Speech is too brief for it. Instead it exhales a single dense thought directly into the marrow:

*I am still deciding what to become.  
You are standing inside the question.*

Then it folds back into steam and dripping stone and the endless wet industrial hymn of the place continues, louder and more serene than before.

The Dweller has no name.

But if the hot, wet, iron lung of the world ever needed to name its own heart, it might—after a thousand years of careful consideration—call that heart *this*.
