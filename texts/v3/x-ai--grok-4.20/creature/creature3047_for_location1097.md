They are called the **Last Choir**, and they have never left.

Not a single one of them was born in the usual sense. They were *finished* here, carved out of the same wet stone as the columns, then taught to breathe by the slow water. Their bodies are tall, narrow, and slightly stooped, as though each has spent centuries trying to match the proportions of the faceless figures that hold up the ceiling. Skin the color of wet limestone, faintly luminous with the same chemosynthetic bloom that paints the walls. When they stand still—which is their natural state—they are almost impossible to distinguish from the architecture. Only the faint movement of their chests and the occasional blink of milky, lidless eyes betrays them.

There are never more than thirty-one. Never fewer than twenty-nine. The number adjusts itself without ceremony whenever one of them decides the equilibrium is tipping. They do this by walking into the darkest crawl-way at the back of the chamber, lying down between two columns, and allowing the stone to take them back. The process takes three days. On the fourth, a new body is already half-formed elsewhere in the forest of pillars, growing like a stalagmite that has learned ambition. The new one awakens knowing everything the previous thirty-one knew. Memory is mineral. It does not degrade.

They do not speak with voices. The roar of ten thousand trickles is their tongue. When they need to convey something complex, one of them simply alters the rhythm of a single drip by placing a fingertip against a precise spot on a column. The change propagates through the entire acoustic architecture in less than a second. The chamber itself answers. To the Choir, this is conversation. To anything else, it is only a momentary shift in the pressure against the eardrums—like the place just inhaled through a different nostril.

Their hands are their only vanity. Long, spatulate fingers that have been slowly modified over millennia by the deliberate breaking and re-setting of bones. Each generation of fingers is a little more sensitive than the last, until now they can feel the precise temperature differential between two adjacent water beads. With these hands they continue the work their predecessors began. They do not build anything new. Instead they *maintain the intention*. A column that has begun to lean by the width of a hair is corrected before the deviation can be seen. A lamp wick that has drifted a fraction of a millimeter is returned to its perfect center. They polish the stone with their own shed skin, mixed with bacterial paste, until every surface reflects the sullen yellow light exactly as it did when the first lamp was lit.

At rare intervals—once every few human lifetimes—one of the Choir will walk to the absolute center of the Under-Rafters and stand beneath the single point where all the separate drips resolve into a strange, ringing silence. There it will open its arms. The water that has been weeping down the columns for ten thousand years gathers, for just a moment, on its shoulders and head. Then it walks slowly through the narrow lanes, letting the droplets fall onto the blind crustaceans, onto the translucent eels that sometimes wander in, onto the very bacteria that feed the whole closed system. This is their only ceremony. They call it *Returning the Tears*. No record exists of why it matters. It simply does.

They are not kind, exactly. Kindness would imply choice. They are equilibrium wearing skin. If a living intruder enters and stays too long, they do not attack. They simply begin to adjust the architecture around the intruder with exquisite patience. A crawl-way narrows by a finger’s width every few days. A column inches closer to another. The temperature in one particular breathing-space drops by a hundredth of a degree each month. They are not trying to kill. They are trying to restore the precise shape the place held before the intruder arrived. Most leave long before the stone finishes its correction. The few who do not eventually become part of a new column—arms folded, head bowed, face smoothed away by careful, loving hands.

At the edge of hearing, beneath the roar, there is sometimes a second sound: a low, patient humming that vibrates in the teeth and the wet marrow of the bones. This is the Choir singing. They do it by pressing their foreheads to the stone and letting the constant 4 °C water conduct their heartbeats into the rock. The stone remembers every heartbeat that has ever been offered to it. It keeps them all.

They do not have a name for themselves in any human language. If forced to choose one, they would perhaps say:

“We are the place after it decided it no longer required builders.”

And then they would return to their slow, dripping labor, smoothing a shoulder here, correcting a drip there, maintaining the perfect, deafening, weeping equilibrium that has already lasted ten thousand years and intends to last ten million more—exactly like this, and no other way.
