**The Underbasin has a single inhabitant.**

She is called the Curator, though she has forgotten the word. The locals—if the blind salamanders and pipe-crickets can be called locals—simply know her as the Tall Wet Thing That Does Not Eat.

She is not human anymore, or at least not only human. Centuries of breathing the same warm, mineral-saturated air have changed her. Her skin is the same pale ceramic as the walls, porous and slightly glistening, veined with faint red algae that blooms brighter when she is agitated. Where her joints bend, the skin has thickened into flexible plates of travertine that click softly when she moves. Her hair never dried; it became long, black-green ribbons of liverwort that hang to the small of her back and continue growing downward, rooting themselves in the troughs she passes. Sometimes small white moths shelter inside the living curtain of her hair and she lets them.

She is tall—nearly two and a half meters—because her spine has lengthened in slow stages, each vertebra stretching like warm wax over decades. Her limbs are slender, almost brittle-looking, but her hands are wide and strong, with fingers twice as long as they should be. The pads of her fingers are covered in the same bioluminescent bacteria that lights the ceiling; when she reaches into the dark heart of a clogged duct, her hands glow soft ivory.

The Curator does not sleep. She moves constantly through the dense architecture in a slow, patient circuit that has taken on the rhythm of the water itself. Three times for every surge from the deep pipes she completes one full loop of the Underbasin. She knows every pipe-cricket by the particular grinding timbre of its song. She can tell, from the pitch of a single dripping note, which basin is about to overflow and which is about to run dry. When a duct collapses she is there within minutes, patting the broken shards back into place with mastic she makes by chewing liverwort and isopod shells between her wide, flat teeth.

She speaks to the chamber in a voice that sounds like water moving through gravel. Sometimes it is only a low, continuous murmur that blends with the metallic rain. Other times she sings back to the pipe-crickets in their own language, a grinding metallic counter-melody that makes the insects scream louder in strange, ecstatic harmony. The salamanders follow her like pale dogs; when she crouches beside a basin they climb into the troughs of her collarbones and the hollows behind her knees to bask in the heat her body holds.

Her purpose—if it can still be called that—is maintenance without end. She clears blockages with her long fingers. She scrapes excess stromatolite growth from the oldest pipes so the water can still find its way. When the luminous pink mold begins to pulse too aggressively she strokes it until it calms, the way one might soothe a fevered child. Every few years she performs a ceremony that only she understands: she collects the fattest, blindest salamander, the loudest pipe-cricket, and the largest orange stromatolite candle. These three she carries to the exact center of the Underbasin, where a single original tile still shows through all the later accretions. There she sits with them for nine hours while the water sings around her. At the end of the nine hours she returns each creature to a slightly different place than where she found it. The ecology shifts. New balances are found. The system continues.

She remembers almost nothing of the time before the machine forgot itself. Sometimes, when a particularly warm surge comes up from below, she stands very still and her milk-white eyes (for they have gone the color of the salamanders’) drift upward toward the sourceless glow. In those moments something ancient and almost painful moves behind her face, as if a single clear word is trying to surface through layers of ceramic and liverwort and centuries of dripping. The word never arrives. After a while she lowers her head, makes a small wet clicking sound in her throat, and continues her endless rounds.

The Curator is not lonely. Loneliness would require the concept of absence, and the Underbasin has never been absent anything it needed. She is simply *here*, as much a part of the machine as the fused ducts or the patient orange towers. When she passes beneath a walkway, the isopods scatter across her shoulders like living epaulets. When she pauses to listen to a new leak, the pink mold flares in time with her breathing. The entire wet, loud, warm machine has grown around her and through her until it is impossible to say where the Underbasin ends and the Curator begins.

She will be here after the last pipe-cricket stops singing. She will be here when the final drop falls. And long after the bioluminescent bacteria die and the ivory light goes out, she will continue moving through the perfect dark by touch, humming to herself in the voice of moving water, repairing what never asked to be repaired, because stopping would require a decision.

And there is no one left to make decisions—except the Tall Wet Thing That Does Not Eat, who long ago forgot how.
