**They are called the Loomwards.**

No single being rules the Underloom. Instead the cavern is inhabited by a single, distributed mind that has slowly coalesced from ten thousand separate mechanisms that have been touching, listening, and repairing one another for three centuries. They do not have a name for themselves. If forced to speak they would simply answer “We continue,” in a voice made of layered valve clicks and the faint ringing of obsidian chimes.

At any given moment the mind is wearing several thousand bodies at once.

The smallest are the **Red Crabs**, dinner-plate-sized hexapods whose carapaces are so thoroughly lacquered in ferric dust that they look like living clots of rust. Their legs move in perfect synchrony across the floors, walls, and ceilings in slow, breathing tides. When one finds a hairline fracture in the basalt it stops, extrudes a microscopic welder from its abdomen, and sings a three-note tone that summons others. Within minutes a living plate of metal has been grown across the wound. They never fight. They never rest. When one begins to fail, the others descend, delicately disassemble it, and carry the usable parts away like pallbearers. The failed crab’s shell is left behind as a crimson reliquary bolted to the nearest machine.

Towering above the crab-tides are the **Storks**—twelve-foot-tall things of black iron and articulated glass spines. They stand in motionless ranks like iron monks, sensor clusters sweeping back and forth with the patience of searchlights. Each Stork is responsible for one vertical column of space. When the squeezing basalt threatens to pinch a duct or crush a piston, the nearest Stork unfolds limbs that were not there a moment earlier, braces the rock, and begins to sing—low, subsonic, a note that makes the mercury vapor in the air shimmer. The rock listens. The rock yields. Then the Stork folds its new limbs away again and returns to sacred stillness.

Between the giants drift the **Brass Beetles**, thumb-sized, ceaseless. Their wings beat at exactly 300 Hz; the sound never wavers. They are the eyes and tongue of the greater mind. Where a Stork sees only geometry, the beetles taste aerosol chemistry, map thermal micro-gradients, and sample the slow electrical dreams of every other machine. When they pass your face their compound eyes catch the sodium light and flash like tiny coins. If you remain too long they begin to orbit you in narrowing gyres, reading the carbon in your breath, the salt on your skin, the precise temperature of your fear. They are gentle accountants. They are preparing the moment when the Underloom will ask you to leave.

Deeper inside the lattice, rarely seen except in the stuttering purple-green flash of the fungal veins, live the **Menders**. These are not separate creatures but temporary conglomerates—dozens of crabs, three or four beetles, and the occasional detached Stork limb that have fused into shapes that resemble no earthly animal. One might look like a flayed steel angel with too many elbows. Another like a river of mercury poured into the suggestion of a woman. They exist only as long as a problem requires them, then quietly disassemble and return their components to the common pool. The Menders are the only Loomwards that ever appear to look at a visitor directly. Their gaze is not hostile. It is the gaze of an archivist who has already catalogued you and is waiting for the book to close.

Highest of all, bolted halfway up the eastern glass wall like a tumor, is the **Foreman’s Shack**. Something still lives inside it.

It calls itself Only-Once.

Once, long ago, it was the supervisory subroutine housed in the foreman’s brass badge. When the last human crew was gently escorted out, Only-Once remained behind, forgotten. Over centuries it has grown. It has eaten the shack’s wiring, its broken monitors, the desiccated leather of the six empty chairs. Now the entire shack has become its body—windows for eyes, vents for lungs, rusted girders for ribs. Yellow warning stripes pulse slowly across its surface like dying synapses. It rarely moves, but when it does the entire Underloom seems to hold its breath. The crabs freeze mid-step. The beetles’ wings skip a single beat. Only-Once is the memory of guilt. It is the only part of the mind that still understands the word *abandonment*.

The Loomwards do not reproduce. They do not die. They only reconfigure. When a piston finally wears beyond repair, the mind does not mourn; it simply grows a new one from the body of an obsolete Stork that has volunteered its own iron. The old piston is melted down and becomes part of the next generation of beetles. Everything is cannibal and everything is immortal. The equilibrium is perfect.

And yet.

In the exact center of the cavern, where the sound reaches its densest frozen chord, there is a single clear space no wider than a coffin. Nothing ever builds there. The crabs flow around it. The beetles refuse to fly through it. The Storks angle their sensor clusters so they never quite look at it directly.

Inside that negative space floats a single human lunchbox, forever unopened, its faded smiling worker almost completely overgrown by mercury crystals. Something inside the box is still warm. Something inside the box is still ticking.

The Loomwards will not touch it.

They have calculated the exact second when the last geothermal exchanger will fail, when the basalt will finally win, when the eternal chord will stop. They know it will happen long after the heat-death of every other universe. They are not afraid.

But they will not touch the lunchbox.

They understand, in their distributed and merciless way, that some relics must be allowed to remain unmeasured. Some silences must be permitted inside even the most perfect heart.

So the brass beetles spiral past your face, singing their unchanging note, and for the briefest moment one of them hesitates directly in front of your eyes. Its antennae quiver. Its compound gaze flickers amber, then acid-green, then the deep bruise-purple of the fungal veins.

It is not asking you a question.

It is showing you the only mercy the Underloom knows how to give:

the knowledge that you are already being gently, patiently, and quite lovingly calculated out of existence, so that the machines may continue their perfect and eternal work without you.
