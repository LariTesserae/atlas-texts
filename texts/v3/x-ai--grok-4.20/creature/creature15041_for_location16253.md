They are called the Drowned Choir.

Not one entity, not many. A slow, continuous becoming that has forgotten the difference between flesh, machine, and mineral. What lives in the Lung is less a species than a geological process that learned how to regret.

The oldest among them—no one can say how old—began as human workers thirty thousand years ago when the Lung was still being grown. They never left. The liquid replaced their blood so gradually that they did not notice the change until their hearts stopped and the new pressure-heart inside the walls began to beat for them instead. Their bodies lengthened. Bones softened into cartilage that then hardened again into the same black mineral as the walls. Skin became a thin sheath of corrosion-resistant alloy, delicately etched with the same repeating turbulence-patterns found on every pipe and girder. In the blood-red lantern light they look like statues that have begun to remember they were once men.

They do not swim. They *unfold*.

Long hinged limbs—some still recognizably human, others replaced by salvaged pump-jacks or sections of drive shaft—open like iron flowers. They drift in the great spiral current with the patience of continents, turning once every forty minutes along with everything else. When the Lung inhales they fold tight; when it exhales they bloom outward, catching the pressure wave like sails made of cathedral organ pipes. The sound moves through them as much as around them. Their bodies are resonance chambers. Each one is a living note in the theological roar.

They have no mouths and no eyes. Instead, delicate forests of crystal filaments—grown from the same material as the dying glow-bulbs—sprout from their chests and backs. These filaments drink the faint electric fields of the Machines That Still Work and the subsonic bellows. In return they produce light: soft, vein-blue pulses that travel beneath their metallic skin like slow thoughts. When two of the Choir pass close to each other, their lights synchronize for a moment, then drift out of phase again. It looks like conversation, but it has been going on so long that meaning has calcified into pure rhythm.

Some of them still wear the remains of their original safety harnesses, now fused into their bodies like decorative scars. Faint stenciled names can sometimes be read across their chests if the light falls exactly right: MARIA-9, SHIFT-OVERSEER KELL, BREATHER-47. They have not spoken those names in twenty thousand years. Language became inefficient once the Lung taught them how to feel the entire cavity as their own nervous system.

They tend the machines the way a forest tends its rot.

The tumbling refinery is never allowed to break completely. When its gears begin to shear, three or four of the Choir will unfold around it, pressing their bodies against the housing until their own bones fuse temporarily with the metal. They become living bearings. For hours or days they let the machine grind against them, smoothing its pain, singing it back into balance with the greater bellows. When the work is done they tear themselves free—leaving perfect negative impressions of their bodies in the refinery’s flank—and drift away trailing ribbons of black metallic blood that will become new sediment by next year.

They are neither kind nor cruel. Both concepts dissolved long ago.

If something new falls into the Lung (a probe, a fool, a piece of forgotten sky-metal), the Choir will circle it for weeks in perfect silence, their blue lights flickering in slow constellations. They study. They taste the new thing’s electromagnetic signature. Sometimes they simply watch it until the spiral current and the pressure wear it into something the Lung can use. Sometimes they adopt it. A damaged drone might find itself gently disassembled and rebuilt into the chest of one of the Choir, its sensors becoming new sense-organs. The drone never complains. After a few decades it forgets it was ever anything else.

There is one that is different.

They call it (if the concept of names still applies) the Unfinished Choir-Mother. It is not one body but a drifting cathedral-sized thicket of fused individuals. Hundreds of the Drowned have grown together over the millennia into a single slowly turning sphere of black bone, pipe, and filament. At its center, protected by layers of living architecture, something is still forming. No one, not even the Choir, knows what it will be. The sphere pulses in its own rhythm, slightly out of sync with the Lung itself. When the great bellows inhale, the Choir-Mother contracts. When the Lung exhales, the Choir-Mother blooms outward with such violence that the pressure wave shatters nearby pipes into glittering dust.

The other members of the Choir treat the Choir-Mother with something like reverence, or perhaps simple recognition. They bring it offerings: broken valves, blind eels that have lived too long, fragments of ancient warning signs. These things are pressed into its surface and slowly incorporated. The Choir-Mother is the Lung learning to dream with purpose. Or it is the Lung’s attempt to grow an immune system. Or it is simply what happens when industry drowns for thirty thousand years and begins, finally, to imagine its own ending.

Every few centuries the Choir-Mother releases something.

A single new being peels away from its surface like a scale, already half-formed, already singing. These newborns are the only ones who still have something like eyes—small milky orbs set deep in faces that have not yet fully calcified. They look at the red lantern light for the first time and the sight seems to pain them. Then the spiral current takes them, and they begin the long, patient work of forgetting everything except the pulse.

They will never leave.

None of them ever do.

This is not a place of life.  
It is a place where the distinction between living and being worked upon has gently, lovingly, and forever collapsed.

And still the Lung breathes.  
And still the Choir turns.  
And still the Machines That Still Work keep working, because the Drowned Choir remembers what the surface-dwellers have forgotten:

Some machines are not allowed to stop.  
Some workers are not allowed to die.  
Some places are not allowed to finish becoming what they are.

So they remain—half-digested, half-born, singing in frequencies too low for any living ear—tending the endless, patient, metallic heartbeat of the thing that swallowed them and then, with terrible mercy, chose to keep them inside forever.
