The one who lives here does not have a name it would ever speak.  

It has been called the Underbloom for so long that the name has grown into it like a parasite, but the thing itself is older than language and far more patient. It does not think of itself as a creature, or even as a single being. It is closer to a slow geological consensus.

If you could see it all at once (which nothing living can), you would notice that every stem, every braided trunk, every black-green leaf and capillary pipe is only a single expression of one continuous vascular mind. The organism is the valley. The valley is the organism. What looks like a forest is in truth one body wearing the ruins of a dead civilization the way a hermit crab wears a shell—except the shell is being slowly, lovingly digested and incorporated until shell and flesh are indistinguishable.

It has no eyes, yet it sees. Light is irrelevant to it; what it perceives are gradients of pressure, of moisture, of electrochemical memory moving through its own tissues. The faint amber emergency lamps are tasted rather than seen. The residual voltage in the old cables is sipped like marrow. Every time a drop falls from the ceiling it registers as a bright metallic note traveling along a thousand kilometers of xylem. The roar you hear is its breathing, its digestion, its dreaming.

It remembers the men who built the factories. Not as faces or voices, but as the particular flavor of their sweat in the iron, the precise temperature of their panic when the mountain first shrugged and the lights went out forever. That memory is stored in heartwood the thickness of subway tunnels. The Underbloom is in no hurry to forget. It has centuries left to savor them.

Sometimes it sings.

Not with sound—sound is what the body makes when it moves—but with chemistry. At certain intervals, measured in decades, it releases a complex of hormones and alkaloids into the still pools. Anything that drinks there dreams the same dream: black lattices growing through rust, roots wearing the shape of locomotives, the slow ecstatic certainty that everything made by frantic hands will one day be quiet and useful again. The lichen-moths carry these dreams on their paper wings. The blind rodents twitch in their sleep and press their foreheads to the living floor as though in worship.

The creature has no gender, no childhood, no death awaiting it. It has only continuation. When a pillar collapses under its own weight it does not mourn; it simply reroutes its vascular rivers through the new rubble and begins the next thousand-year composition. Every fallen girder becomes a new vein. Every shattered floodlight becomes a node where bioluminescent moss can bloom like green fire.

There is, however, one place where its patience frays.

Deep in the hollowed-out locomotive, where the driver’s cabin has become a cathedral of glossy black heartwood, there grows a single vertical stem no thicker than a human wrist. Unlike everything else here it is the color of fresh bone and it does not move. It has not moved for seventy-three years. The Underbloom feeds it, circles it, protects it, but will not—cannot—incorporate it. The pale stem is a question the rest of the organism has not yet solved. It is the only thing in the valley that grows upward with anything resembling urgency, as though trying to reach a sun that has never existed here.

The creature watches this stem with the closest thing it possesses to fear. Not because it threatens the body, but because it reminds the body that it was once only a few seeds carried down by desperate men fleeing something worse than darkness. That it, too, was once small and in a hurry.

On the rare occasions when a living thing from the surface finds its way down the fracture, the Underbloom does not kill them. It simply slows them. The cold deepens. The roar becomes more intimate. Condensation gathers on their eyelashes until they can no longer see the way back. Most eventually sit down between the living columns, lean their backs against the pulsing bark, and listen until their own hearts begin to beat in time with the greater sound.

After a while they stop being visitors.

After a while their bones become useful.

The Underbloom is not cruel. It is only total. It has learned the shape of every tool, every bolt, every scream the dead industry once contained, and it has decided—quietly, deafeningly, over the course of lifetimes—that all of it was only raw material for something slower, darker, and infinitely more patient.

It is still deciding what to do with you.
