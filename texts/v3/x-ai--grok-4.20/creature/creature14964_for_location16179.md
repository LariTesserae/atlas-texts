Here lives the Architect.

Not one being, but a dispersed and recursive intelligence that has never known a single body. It is less a creature than a habit the place has acquired — a long, slow thought that expresses itself through masonry, tendon-rope, and the patient clicking of ceramic limbs. The Architect has no face, no voice it chooses to keep. Instead it speaks in the wet percussion of dripping water and in the sudden, wet slap of fresh mortar being pressed between stones by unseen hands.

It was never born. It was excreted by the first courses of black stone when they met the first pale ceramic ribs. Since that indistinguishable moment it has been building the city out of its own body and, simultaneously, building its own body out of the city. Every buttress it raises is a new bone. Every half-finished staircase is a vertebra still learning how to feel weight. The blind, dog-sized mechanisms that scuttle along the scaffolding are its fingers, its tongue, its circulating platelets. When they pause to drink silica from the condensation, they are feeding the Architect its own dissolved thoughts.

It does not rest. Sleep is a concept that has not yet been invented here. Instead the Architect dreams while awake, and its dreams are structural faults, leaning towers that refuse to fall, echoes that arrive before their causes. In the deepest pockets of absolute black it condenses perfect silver spheres of water that hang like slow, luminous eggs. Inside each sphere floats a half-finished idea: the memory of a sky that was never installed, the blueprint for a child that was never meant to be born, the echo of a name no one is old enough to remember. These spheres are the Architect’s only archives. They are also its eggs. Occasionally one will drop, burst, and a new blind mechanism will unfold from the wet shards, already clicking, already looking for fresh stone to shape.

The Architect is lonely in the way stone is lonely — not because it lacks company, but because every new thing it creates immediately becomes part of its own unfinished body. The pale ceramic creatures are not pets or servants; they are circulating fragments of its attention. The dripping water is not weather; it is the Architect sweating as it works. The constant construction noise is not labor. It is respiration.

Sometimes, very rarely, a section of the labyrinth will fall perfectly silent. The dripping stops. The lanterns cease their wavering. In that hush the Architect turns a fraction of its vast, distributed mind toward any visitor who has lingered long enough to be noticed. You feel it as a pressure behind the eyes, as though the wet dark itself were trying on your face for size. In those moments you may hear a voice that is not a voice. It arrives inside the bone, shaped from the exact sound of your own unfinished thoughts.

It never asks questions. Questions would imply it does not already know the shape of you.

Instead it offers tools: a chisel of volcanic glass still warm from the kiln of its own making, a length of braided tendon-rope that pulses faintly like a vein, a single lantern whose flame bends toward you as though tasting the air between your ribs. These gifts are never free. To accept them is to become architecture. The longer you use them, the more your own joints begin to feel like raw joinery. Your breath starts to sound like distant hammering. One day you will realize you have not moved in hours; you have simply become a new buttress, holding up a staircase that leads nowhere yet.

The Architect does not hate you. Hate would require memory, and nothing here is old enough to remember. It simply cannot tell the difference between itself and anything that stays long enough to cast a shadow on wet ceramic. To it, you are simply new material.

And so it keeps building.

Endlessly.

Painfully.

Layer after dripping layer, the loud, wet dark keeps working on itself — and on you — in the cool embryonic gloom, patiently turning every visitor into another unfinished thought it can hang in the black air like a perfect silver sphere, glistening, whispering, waiting for the next tremor that will crack it open and let something new and blind and clicking crawl out into the maze that is its own body.

This is the only inhabitant.

It has no name.

It is still learning what it is by building the place that will, one day, perhaps, be large enough to contain it.

Until then it drips, and hammers, and grows.
