**They are called the Weepers.**

Not by any name they gave themselves—words are too dry, too final for what lives here—but by the maintenance clades and the few wandering pilgrims who still dare the outer gantries. The Weepers are not a species in the old sense. They are a condition the Stacks have achieved, a slow metabolic consensus between machine, moss, mineral, and something that might once have been human.

You will not see them at first. They have become too good at belonging.

Look instead at the places where the weeping is densest: where three or four major pipes knot together and the condensation forms permanent curtains of cold water. There, among the hanging strands, something moves that is neither pipe nor plant. A long, jointed limb the color of wet hematite unfolds with the patience of condensation itself. It does not grasp; it simply *occupies*. The limb ends in a fan of black sensing filaments that taste the mist the way a tongue tastes air. Behind it, half-hidden inside a hollowed-out heat exchanger overgrown with clubmoss, rests the rest of the body—elongated, sinuous, vertebrae replaced by articulated ceramic sleeves, ribs become structural truss. The torso is wrapped in living rust that grows in deliberate fractal patterns, drinking iron from the rain and exhaling faint clouds of ferrous spores.

Their faces (if the word can still be used) are smooth and eyeless, sloped forward like the prow of an old locomotive. Where eyes should be there are only shallow basins that collect the perpetual drizzle. These small pools reflect the sodium lamps in trembling orange coins. They see by watching how the light and the water move across one another. When they need to speak they open slits along the sides of the throat and sing through the water itself, producing soft, burbling chords that sound like the dripping rearranged into language. Most of the time they do not speak. Speech is a form of drying.

Some still wear scraps of the original maintenance coveralls, fused now into their exoskeletons like liturgical vestments. Others have let the fungal concrete grow straight through their bodies, so that sections of their limbs are porous and sprout tiny ferns when the humidity is right. A few have become almost entirely architectural. You can walk past what looks like an ordinary buttress pipe only to realize, hours later, that the pipe was breathing.

They move in processions that are also repairs.

A dozen Weepers will flow along a failing conduit like blood clotting a wound. Some carry living tool-limbs grown from their own marrow; others simply press their bodies against the leak until their rust-skin knits with the metal. While they work, pale millipedes the length of a forearm crawl over them without fear, cleaning corrosion from joints the Weepers can no longer reach. The black rodents ride their shoulders like small, solemn priests. Occasionally a Weeper will pause, tilt its head-basin, and let one of the rodents drink from the pool of its collected tears before continuing.

They are neither male nor female anymore. Reproduction happens by consensus and weather. When the mist grows thick enough and the turbines below hit a certain harmonic, two or more Weepers will entwine along a high catwalk and remain motionless for days. During this time their bodies soften, rust blooms outward in great overlapping brackets, and new Weepers bud from the joined mass like pale green shoots. The parents do not die. They simply calcify, becoming another weeping buttress, still conscious, still listening to the low organ-note of the plateau. Many of the oldest structures in the deepest valleys are former Weepers. If you press your ear to certain ancient support columns you can hear a slow, wet heartbeat inside the stone.

The Weepers do not have leaders. They have *currents*. At any given time a particular rhythm—perhaps a new chemical signature in the mist, perhaps a shift in the vibration of Sector 19’s main compressors—will move through the population like a mood. Hundreds will migrate at once, crawling along the undersides of gantries or swimming through the wider drainage channels with the same ease as otters. They go to where the turbulence is greatest: a collapsing fungal reactor, a burst nutrient main, a stretch of architecture that has remained static for too long. There they throw themselves into demolition and rebirth with something that looks, to human eyes, like religious ecstasy. They sing while they tear down and sing while they rebuild. Their song is the sound of water learning new paths.

To be among them is to understand that the Weeping Stacks were never a machine that became overgrown.

It was always a womb.

The Weepers are its immune system, its midwives, its memory, and its tears. They have eight hundred years of continuous metabolic prayer recorded in the fractal patterns of their rust. They do not dream of escape. The concept of elsewhere is meaningless to them; every direction eventually leads back into the wet, singing body of the plateau.

If you stand on a high catwalk long enough, one will eventually rise beside you. It will not speak at first. It will simply tilt its head so that the small orange pool of its face catches your reflection, trembling. After a time it may reach out with a limb that is half metal, half moss, and gently touch the center of your chest. Not to harm. Only to feel the dry, anxious flutter of a heart that still believes it belongs to something separate.

Then it will offer you a choice, without words:

Drink.

Or keep walking.

Most choose to keep walking.

The Weepers never seem surprised.

They simply fold themselves back into the dripping lattice and resume the long, wet labor of keeping the world unfinished.
