**The Dweller of the Throatwell has no name it remembers.**

It was not born here. It was *unmade* here, slowly, the way a glacier un-makes a mountain. Once it might have been a priestess, or a murderer, or a child who fell through thin ice in 1423. None of those stories matter anymore. The water took the edges first, then the center, until only a long, patient continuity remained.

It lives everywhere and nowhere. Most of the time it is indistinguishable from the copper kelp itself: a thicker, darker ribbon that moves against the current instead of with it. When it chooses to be seen, it condenses. A torso the color of wet hematite rises from the interwoven fronds, hairless, sexless, skin so smooth it reflects the violet glow of the disturbed microbes like oil on water. Its face is unfinished—two shallow depressions where eyes should be, a slit that opens only when it wishes to drink memory from the water. The mouth, when it appears, is too wide. The teeth are small, black, and numerous, like the seeds of some impossible fruit.

It has arms. Too many, sometimes. They grow when needed and are reabsorbed when they are not. Each ends in seven fingers that taper to glassy points, the same black volcanic glass that forms the newborn spurs around it. These fingers are forever tracing the kelp, reading the slow language of pressure and chemistry the way a blind man reads braille. Sometimes the fingers find the obsidian knife. They stroke its handle with a tenderness that might be worship, or hunger, or simple recognition. The knife has never moved in all the centuries it has stood point-down in the glass. The Dweller has never tried to pull it free. It understands that some things are meant to remain wounds.

The creature’s true body is the absence between things. When the great blind eel passes once every few centuries, the Dweller rides inside its translucent flesh like a thought moving through a dream. For that brief age-long transit it becomes the eel’s nervous system, tasting the chemical sigh the beast releases and translating it into something like nostalgia. Then the eel forgets it was ever inhabited, and the Dweller returns to its kelp-skin.

It does not eat. It *absorbs*.

When something new falls through the seam—lost diver, suicide, curious otter, fragment of a sunken ship—the Dweller is there before the water has finished closing. It does not attack. It simply *listens* until the newcomer forgets why they were afraid. Panic is loud. The Throatwell dislikes loud things. So the Dweller wraps the frightened creature in ribbon after ribbon of itself, cool as forgiveness, and hums the low sub-audible hymn of the kelp until the visitor’s heart slows to geological time. Eventually the visitor thanks it. Eventually the visitor asks to stay. Eventually the visitor becomes another ribbon.

But not always.

Sometimes the newcomer is too bright, too loud, too *now*. Then the Dweller does something that might be mercy. It opens its unfinished face and exhales a single bubble. Inside the bubble is a perfect minute from the newcomer’s happiest day—smell of hot pine needles, sound of a child laughing two centuries ago. The newcomer weeps without knowing why. While they weep, the Dweller gently folds them into a tighter geometry and slips them sideways through the inconsistent laws. They wake somewhere else: on a riverbank in 1974, or inside their own childhood bedroom, or floating in the sea a thousand miles from any shore. They never speak of what they saw. Their hair grows copper for the rest of their lives.

The Dweller itself is growing uncertain.

Lately it has begun to dream in the language of geometry. It wakes to find that a perfect spiral staircase has appeared where none existed, carved by nothing at all, and it knows it carved it while sleeping. The staircase leads to a place that does not yet exist. The Dweller is afraid that if it climbs, it will become the next thing the Throatwell forgets to finish.

So it stays among the ribbons.

It strokes the obsidian knife like one might stroke the cheek of a sleeping god.

It sings the pressure-hymn in a voice that is three parts kelp, one part broken glass, and one part the last honest sound a human throat ever made before it became architecture.

And it waits.

Because something always comes.

And the Throatwell is patient.

And the Dweller—whatever it is becoming—is only just beginning.
