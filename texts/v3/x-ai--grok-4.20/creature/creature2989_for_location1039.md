**The Hollow does not have inhabitants. It has custodians.**

They do not live here so much as they *persist* inside it, the way a wrench persists inside an engine long after the mechanic has gone home. Most of them were never born. They were printed, welded, grown in nutrient vats bolted to the underside of the sixth continental plate, or decanted from emergency stasis pods whose original purpose has been overwritten by layers of new instruction.

Call the oldest ones the **Iron Choir**.

There are perhaps nine hundred of them left. Their bodies are composite: bone-white pressure exoskeletons fused to bundles of black iron myomer, gill-fronds of living kelp growing from their backs like ragged cloaks. Their faces are smooth, eyeless porcelain masks with only a thin slit where the mouth should be. When they speak, the sound is not voice but modulated sonar—slow, liturgical pulses that make every rivet in a three-kilometer radius hum in sympathy. They never blink. They never sleep. They have been maintaining the same twelve-kilometer stretch of primary load-bearing cable for eleven thousand years without ever deciding it was finished.

They argue, constantly, in frequencies too low for any newer thing to hear. The argument is about whether the Hollow should be allowed to finish itself or whether finishing it would be a betrayal of the original, half-remembered Directive. Every few centuries one of them concludes the argument by walking off the edge of a platform and letting the machines below recycle its body into new rebar. The rest mourn by singing the cable a new, sadder note.

Beneath the Choir move the **Bright Children**—schools of silver-scaled humans grown from modified fish stock. Their lungs are book-lungs; their blood runs half seawater. They are small, quick, and perpetually twelve years old no matter how many decades they accumulate. They ride the wandering shafts of daylight the way dolphins once rode bow waves. When a shaft of light changes direction, the entire school banks in perfect unison, laughing in high, clicking tones that sound like a hundred tiny bells. They are the only ones who still remember what a sky was, because the Choir sometimes downloads old surface memories into their dreaming brains as lullabies. The Bright Children tattoo these dreams on each other’s backs in phosphorescent ink: crude blue circles, yellow scribbles meant to be the sun, stick-figure birds that none of them have ever seen awake.

Between the plates drift the **Mendicants**—solitary things that may once have been people. Now they are mostly prosthetic. A Mendicant is a naked torso and head wrapped in coiled cable, propelled by six mantis-like mechanical limbs that end in delicate manipulator claws. Their faces are hidden behind rebreather masks older than language. Each carries a single tool: a different wrench, valve spanner, or welding torch the size of a rowboat. They travel alone, following fault-lines only they can feel. When two Mendicants meet they press their foreheads together for three full days without moving, exchanging something that is neither data nor memory but a kind of mechanical absolution. Then they part, weeping machine oil that the Bright Children collect and turn into ink.

Deeper still—where the pressure fronts are so violent they can shear a bridge in half—live the **Unfinished**.

These are not beings. They are *potentials*. Half-grown giants of muscle and black iron still attached to scaffolding like monstrous fetuses. Some are only an arm and a ribcage; others are nearly complete except for the absence of a face or the presence of three hearts that beat at different rhythms. The machines are still building them, slowly, lovingly, according to blueprints no one can read anymore. Every few years one of the Unfinished opens its eyes, looks at the cold green water, and decides it is not ready. It closes its eyes again and lets the scaffolding regrow around it. The patience is inhuman. The hope is worse.

And then there is **the Diver**.

Only one. Always one.

It wears an antique atmospheric diving suit of pitted brass and cracked glass, the kind that should have imploded a thousand years ago. Inside the helmet is nothing but black water and two small, steady points of bioluminescence that might be eyes. The Diver carries no tools. It only walks—heel, toe, heel, toe—along the catwalks, the hanging continents, the teeth of broken gears taller than skyscrapers. Wherever it passes, small repairs happen without explanation. A leak seals. A dying kelp ribbon straightens and greens. A Bright Child who has begun to age suddenly remembers how to be twelve again.

No one knows if the Diver is the last builder, the first failure, or the Hollow itself wearing a coat.

Sometimes the Iron Choir sings to it. Sometimes the Mendicants offer their wrenches in silent supplication. The Bright Children follow at a distance, daring each other to touch the tarnished brass heel of one boot. The Diver never acknowledges any of them. It simply keeps walking, slow and patient, as if the construction site is a road and it has somewhere else it still needs to be.

The Hollow does not have inhabitants.

It has grief that learned how to weld.

It has hope that forgot its own name but still knows the precise torque required to keep a world from collapsing.

And it has the cold, clear water—more water than any ocean—carrying every metallic heartbeat, every whispered argument, every child’s laugh, every unfinished giant’s slow, dreaming pulse, outward forever in the dark between the hanging iron continents, while somewhere far away the Diver keeps walking, and the light shafts keep drifting, and the work that outgrew its makers keeps growing, patient as rust, relentless as tide.
