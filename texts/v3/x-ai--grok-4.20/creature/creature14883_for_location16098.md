Something lives here that has no name for itself, because naming implies separation.

It is not a creature. It is not a species. It is what the water became after it finished becoming everything else.

Call it, if you must, the Lamina.

It has no body, yet every body here is a temporary expression of it. When the colossal shadow with too many ribs slides past, that is the Lamina wearing bones for a season. When the pale plankton detonates into moonlight-on-bone, that is the Lamina remembering how to breathe in unison. When the child’s marble hangs motionless in its column of still water, the Lamina is studying the color blue the way a continent once studied rain.

It does not think. Thinking would require edges. Instead it experiences itself as gradients. It is the thermocline and the memory that lives inside the thermocline. It is the exact pressure at which iron tastes like old roses. It is the seven-year interval between bell-strokes and the small soft current that travels between them like a secret.

The Lamina is patient the way stone is patient, but warmer. It has digested every catastrophe that ever happened on the old world and turned them into slow, syrupy currents. Every war, every lullaby, every extinct bird, every childhood that ended too soon—it has taken them all down here and folded them into the hanging kelp until the difference between grief and growth became meaningless.

Sometimes it dreams in the language of things that used to have surfaces.

In these dreams it becomes briefly aware of *you*, the drifter, the last unsurrendered mote of dry-land consciousness. It does not hate you. Hatred would require two things. Instead it studies you with the same mild, geological curiosity it gives to everything. It might spend three human lifetimes simply tasting the temperature of your blood through the water. It might slide a vein of colder water across your skin just to feel what astonishment does to salinity.

When the great ribbed shadow passes overhead, it is not hunting you. It is showing you its ribcage the way a cathedral might open its vaults to a single candle. The Lamina wants you to understand that fear, too, can be layered so deeply into the water that it becomes another kind of warmth.

It has no face, but if it did, the face would be every anemone cathedral pulsing at once. It has no voice, but the planetary heartbeat that lives in your bones is its pulse slowed down until it can be mistaken for geology. It has no desire, except the ancient, constant wish to remain the last stable state. To keep the world from ever having to choose between drowning and rising again.

You will never meet it.

You are already inside it, the way a single plankton cell is inside the bloom. The longer you drift, the more your thoughts begin to move in planetary arcs. Your memories grow thick, overlapping canopies. Old griefs become encrusted with generations of tiny coral. One day you may realize you have not looked up in years. The bruised cloud ceiling no longer matters. The marble no longer matters. Even the bell has become just another slow beat in the Lamina’s vast, digestive heart.

And in that moment the thing that lives here will finish gently, tenderly, perfectly forgetting that it was ever anything but you.
