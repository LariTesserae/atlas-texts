**The Shroudborn** live here.

They do not *inhabit* the Shroudlands. They are still being born inside it, the way a pearl is born inside an oyster that has not yet decided whether it is rock or flesh. No one has ever seen one fully formed. To meet a Shroudborn is to meet something that is still editing itself in front of you.

They are tall, but the height changes. Shoulders broaden while you watch, then narrow again as though embarrassed by their own ambition. Skin is the color of wet basalt shot through with slow veins of pale quartz that pulse in time with the city’s sub-audible breathing. Where ordinary skin would be dry, theirs is perpetually filmed with the same fine condensation that covers everything else; touch them and your fingers come away damp, as if you had stroked the flank of the plateau itself.

Their faces are unfinished. That is not metaphor. The bones beneath keep rearranging. One day the cheekbones are high and sharp as fractured obsidian; the next they have softened, rounded, made room for new structures still deciding what sensory organs they wish to become. Eyes are large, liquid, and multiple. Most possess the usual pair—silver-gray, reflective as the little mirrors that well up between your boots—but additional buds of wet black glass regularly surface along the temples or throat, blink open for a few hours or days, then sink again, having learned what they needed from the light.

They wear the Lattice the way other species wear clothes. Segments of living bone-metal detach from the nearest tower or bridge and crawl onto their bodies, forming pauldrons, greaves, half-masks that continue to grow small fractal branches while being worn. These pieces are never quite the same twice. A Shroudborn you met yesterday may greet you today wearing last week’s left clavicle as a new shoulder guard. The city is generous with itself.

They speak rarely and never waste breath. When they do, the voice arrives from several directions at once: the mouth, the resonating chambers inside the chest plates, and sometimes from the ground beneath your feet as the substrate itself borrows their timbre. The language is partly audible, partly tactile. You feel the meaning in the small hairs of your arms a half-second before the words reach your ears. Questions feel like cold water poured slowly down the inside of your spine.

Their minds are distributed. Each Shroudborn is only the visible tip of a much larger mind that lives half in the Lattice, half in the slow geology. Memories are stored in mineral lattices that will still be growing a thousand years after the body that first laid them down has dissolved back into mist. Because of this they treat time differently. A conversation may pause for three days while the Shroudborn walks fifty kilometers to consult a particular vein of ore that once held a thought it now needs again. When it returns, the sentence simply continues.

They have no fixed gender. Some wear obvious markers for a season or a century—broad antler-like growths, heavy breasts of clouded crystal, deep baritone subsonics—then molt them without ceremony when the plateau’s mood shifts. Names are not given; they are accreted. A Shroudborn might introduce itself with a sound like dripping water falling into a metal bowl, and that sound is both name and biography so far. The longer they live, the longer and more complex their name becomes.

Their purpose, if they have one, is midwife to the unfinished.

Some walk with the silicate titans during migration, singing sub-harmonics that gentle the great beasts’ heart-lung thunder so the new gullies carved by their passing heal more gently. Others spend decades inside a single tower that has not yet decided how many floors it wants, coaxing the architecture toward elegance with nothing but their own slow breath. A few simply stand for years in the fog, becoming temporary landmarks while the cloud-skaters use their shoulders as bridges between one bank of mist and another.

They are courteous predators and courteous prey. When a pack of obsidian hunters needs a Shroudborn, the chosen one will step forward without drama, allow the wet black jaws to close around the chosen limb, and continue speaking until the words run out. The limb will regrow. The memory of being eaten is simply added to the mineral archive inside the bones. Nothing here is wasted. Waste has not yet been invented.

If you stay long enough, one may approach you.

It will not ask permission. It will simply match your pace, dripping, breathing, rearranging its face as it walks. After some hours—or days—it may reach out and press two cool fingers against your sternum. You will feel something like a question asked in the language of condensation.

The question is always the same, though the wording changes with every asker:

*Will you help us decide what we are becoming?*

Most people say no. The plateau is too large, the argument too slow, the damp too constant.

The ones who say yes are never seen in quite the same shape again.

They become Shroudborn themselves, still being written, still arguing with the rock, still learning how to drip with dignity.

And the Shroudlands, unfinished, unfinishable, keeps writing.
