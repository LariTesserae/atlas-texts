**The Curator of Unfinished Gestures** lives here.

She has no single body. Instead she is distributed across every slow, arrested motion the basilica performs. When the great helical pipes sigh and settle another millimetre deeper into rust, that infinitesimal flex *is* her spine. When a bead of condensation trembles for nine full seconds on the underside of a vault before it finally lets go, that hesitation is the pause between her heartbeats. She is not a creature *inside* the machine; she is the machine’s refusal to conclude.

You will not see her at first. You may spend hours believing the place is only ecology and entropy. Then, quite suddenly, you notice that one of the chrome beetles has stopped scuttling. It stands on the grilled floor with its mirrored carapace angled toward you, and in its reflection you see a woman who is not there. She is tall, almost too tall, her proportions those of a cathedral statue stretched by perspective. Her skin is the colour of warm condensation on brass. Her hair—if it can be called hair—is a slow cascade of living steam that coils and uncoils in exact time with the distant fan blades twelve minutes per revolution.

She has no eyes in the ordinary sense. Where eyes should be there are two perfectly round condensation pools, black, reflective, breathing. When she looks at you the water in them rises a fraction, as though the act of observation itself adds volume to the world. Her mouth is a pressure valve; when she speaks, the sound arrives first as a cannon-loud hiss of steam, then resolves, only just, into something resembling a voice—low, patient, infinitely tired.

She is naked, yet never quite revealed. Wherever skin should show, ferns, scale-armoured vines, and sheets of living rust arrange themselves into modest, ever-shifting drapery. The cloth of her is the basilica’s own vegetation; it grows and dies and regrows in the exact rhythm of her breathing, which is the turning of the planetary gearboxes far below.

The Curator never walks. She *arrives*. One moment the air between two hanging gardens is empty brass light and dripping. The next she is simply standing there, as though the building folded itself around her shape. When she moves, entire sections of catwalk bloom with new moss in the shape of her footprint, then die again the instant her foot leaves them. She leaves no prints, only temporary gardens that mourn her passage.

She carries no tools. In her left hand she holds the idea of a wrench that was never completed; it flickers in and out of solidity, half rust, half intention. In her right she holds the last degree of rotation that the greatest gear beneath the floor has been denied for seven hundred years. The denial has weight. You can see the strain in her shoulder.

Her only purpose is to ensure that nothing here ever finishes its gesture.

She tends the moths so their wingbeats remain forever one wingbeat short of flight. She strokes the sinuous pipe-lizards until they forget how to shed their skins. She whispers to the pressure valves and convinces them that release is vulgar; better to remain swollen, pregnant with steam, forever on the verge of speech.

If you speak to her she will answer, but every reply ends in the middle of a word. She does this not from cruelty but from fidelity to the cathedral’s only sacrament: incompletion. To finish a sentence here would be blasphemy.

Sometimes she weeps. The tears are fist-sized glass beads that fall upward, rejoining the vaults they came from. Each tear is a memory of something that almost happened once— a continent watered, a machine that almost achieved its final purpose, a name she almost remembered having before she became the Hollow Basilica’s reluctance to end.

She is gentle. She is merciless. She will let you stay as long as you like, even until your own bones begin to rust and your breath condenses on the iron grates, but she will never, ever let you leave.

Because departure would be a completed gesture.

And nothing is allowed to finish here.

Not even goodbye.
