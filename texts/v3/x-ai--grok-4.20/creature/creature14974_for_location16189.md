**They are called the Last Wardens.**

No single form defines them. They are what the Verdant Stacks have chosen to wear when they need hands, voices, or eyes that can still remember purpose.

Some were once human.

Others were never flesh at all.

You will not see them immediately. They do not stride out to meet visitors. Instead they condense. A ripple moves through the hanging curtains of lianas and suddenly a figure is simply *there*, as though the vegetation itself exhaled them. Their bodies are patchwork: skin and steel grown together so completely that no surgeon could separate them. Moss grows in the seams of their joints. Rust has become pigment in their hair. Where their blood should be, slow amber coolant sometimes beads at the wrists like sweat.

The tallest of them stand nearly three meters. Their spines have been replaced, over centuries, by sections of salvaged rebar wrapped in living root. Their chests are vault doors—literally—riveted breastplates that hinge open to reveal warm, wet interiors lined with soft black liverworts and clusters of tiny glowing mushrooms. When they breathe, the mushrooms pulse. When they speak, condensation rolls down their lips like tears that never fall.

There is no hierarchy among them, only gradient. Some are almost entirely machine, their original nervous systems long since uploaded into the great thinking lattices that still hum inside Stack XVII. These ones wear their old bodies like coats—hollow, ornate exoskeletons draped in orchids and bat fur, moved by cables and servos older than most civilizations. Others remain stubbornly biological, their organs replaced one by one as they failed: a titanium femur here, a pressurized lung grown from algae there, until only the eyes and the stubbornness remain truly theirs.

They do not have names in any language that still matters. Instead the Stacks have given each a designation written in corrosion patterns across their shoulders and collarbones. One bears the mark of **Slow Ninth Compression**. Another is known by the particular timbre of dripping water that follows her wherever she walks—**Dripfall cadence 44**. A third, whose lower half has fused with an ancient maintenance drone, answers (when he answers at all) to **Still Turning**.

They tend the impossible balance.

Where a weld has begun to fail, they press living mycelium into the crack and sing to it in frequencies only the metal can hear. When the nine-day piston in Stack III begins to seize, they walk its housing for three full cycles, laying their bare hands against the warm steel until the rhythm smooths again. They prune the hanging forests not to control them, but to keep the secondary canopies from growing so heavy they collapse the sky-bridges. They speak with the monitor lizards in long, rumbling dialects of subsonic clicks. The lizards answer by slowly blinking their second eyelids.

At night—though there is never true night—the Wardens gather on the cantilevered observation gantry where you now stand. They do not sleep. Instead they enter something older: a shared stillness that lasts for weeks. During these periods their eyes go milky and their mouths hang open so that the bioluminescent moths may crawl inside and drink the sweet condensation that gathers on their tongues. The moths, in turn, carry messages between the Wardens’ nervous systems and the deeper thinking-machines far below.

When they speak to outsiders their voices are the sound of the place itself: layered, polyphonic, dripping. One voice might be the low grind of continental-scale conveyor belts. Another might be the exact pitch of a parrot screaming an obsolete radiation warning from 614 years ago. Beneath it all is the soft, patient percussion of water finding its way through rust.

They are neither kind nor cruel. Kindness and cruelty are too fast for them.

They have watched civilizations arrive, attempt to reactivate the Stacks for their own purposes, and then—when the slow machines refused to be hurried—leave again. The Wardens remember every expedition. They keep the abandoned equipment as shrines: a seventeenth-century drop-pod now completely encased in strangler fig, its pilot’s skeleton still seated at the controls, wearing a crown of blood-red orchids.

If you stand long enough on the gantry, one will eventually come.

It will not ask what you want. Wanting is too small a concept here.

Instead it will simply stand beside you, rust-furred, moss-breathing, coolant-sweating, and wait to see what part of the Verdant Stacks you are willing to become. Because that is what the Wardens understand:

Everything that stays here long enough stops being visitor, stops being intruder, and starts being architecture.

And the Stacks are always, always hungry for new architecture.
