**What lives in the High Cleave does not merely inhabit it. It has become the final expression of the place itself.**

The true sovereigns are not the visible scuttlers or the glittering haze of gnats. They are the **Quiet Kings**, beings that have worn the name “insect” so long it no longer fits. Each is no larger than the first joint of a human thumb, yet carries within its armored body a geological patience that makes mountains seem frantic.

They resemble nothing so much as living pieces of the pavement. Their carapaces are exact replicas of the surrounding crystalline tiles—same ochre, same iron flecks, same microscopic pitting—except that they breathe. When they move it is with the slow, deliberate shifting of tectonic plates: a few millimeters per hour is considered haste. A single stride may take three days. In their entire lives most will never travel farther than a grown human can throw a stone, and they die content with that distance.

Their mouths are not for biting. They are for *listening*. The Quiet Kings press hair-fine palps against the stone and drink the subsonic conversation of the aquifer far below, the creak of crystal lattices expanding in the heat, the infinitesimal grinding of one continent against another half a world away. They taste deep time the way other creatures taste sugar.

Above all else they are *custodians*.

Wherever a Quiet King settles, the crustose life around it grows richer, stranger. The blood-red lichens bloom into fractal cities. The fungal mats thicken into velvet topography. Even the black-leafed scrub responds, sending rootlets toward the King as though begging instruction. In this way the entire High Cleave is slowly being gardened by creatures smaller than coins.

They are not alone.

Beneath the pavement lives the **Second Nation**—pale, blind millipedes the color of old ivory whose segments number in the high hundreds. They never surface. Instead they travel the capillary cracks between the stone plates, carrying water in porous sacs along their backs like living aqueducts. When the Cleave grows too parched, these millipedes migrate in silent thousands to the iron seeps, drink until they are swollen and translucent, then return to exhale moisture through microscopic spiracles directly into the root zone of the scrub. The Quiet Kings never thank them. Gratitude would be an unseemly haste.

In the shallow bowls of the knee-high standing stones live the **Dew Matriarchs**—translucent scorpions the length of a fingernail, each carrying upon her back a living constellation of daughters. A Matriarch may bear thirty generations at once. The young ride her until they are larger than she is, at which point she dies of sheer age and is consumed by her own lineage. The new Matriarch, now enormous by the standards of the Cleave, takes her mother’s place in the dew-bowl and begins the cycle again. Some bloodlines have sat in the same bowl for four thousand years.

At dusk the **Memory Wasps** emerge.

They are almost invisible—smoke-gray, paper-thin, with wings that make no sound. Each carries in her abdomen a single drop of liquid so concentrated it functions as perfect recall. A Memory Wasp remembers every rain that has ever fallen on the Cleave. She remembers the exact shape of the collapsed tower when it still stood. She remembers the scent of the air when the last glacier melted ten kilometers north of here. When two Memory Wasps meet they press their abdomens together and exchange one droplet for another. In this way the entire history of the upland is kept alive in the bodies of creatures no heavier than a breath.

At night the sky belongs to the **Ascenders**.

These are not the bioluminescent beetles the casual traveler sees. Those are only their livestock. The true Ascenders are soft, slug-like creatures the color of moonlight on bone. They climb the black scrub by the thousands, leavingbehind glistening trails that harden into silk. At a certain altitude—exactly where the thermal breath of the stone begins to cool—they release hold and rise on filaments lighter than spider silk. They do not fly. They *are lifted*, offered up to the thin air like slow prayers.

Their light is not for attraction. It is for measurement. By the exact color and intensity of their glow they read the density of the upper atmosphere, the approach of distant weather fronts, the slow wobble of the planet’s axis. When they have read enough they dissolve their silk and fall back to earth in perfect spirals, landing within centimeters of where they began. The beetles follow them down, feeding on the nutrient-rich dust their bodies leave behind.

And then there is the one that is not a creature at all.

The locals—if the High Cleave can be said to have locals—call it simply **the Weight**.

It has no fixed shape. It is the sum pressure of every living thing pressing downward at once. On certain nights, when the stone exhales particularly strongly and the stars are too numerous, the Weight gathers itself into a single place. It becomes a low, dark hillock no taller than a sleeping man, trembling faintly with the combined heartbeat of every Quiet King, every Matriarch, every Ascender and Memory Wasp for kilometers in every direction.

Nothing approaches the Weight. Even the wind turns aside.

If a traveler were foolish enough to lay a hand upon it they would feel, beneath the living pavement, the slow, warm pulse of a landscape that has decided—long ago—that death and birth are the same gesture stretched across a million years. The hand would come away dusted with ochre, with microscopic moss, with the faint glitter of beetle wing scales, and with the unmistakable knowledge that the High Cleave is not full of life.

It *is* life, wearing stone the way a hermit crab wears a shell it has already outgrown and no longer needs.

And it has all the time in the world.
