**The Inhabitants of the Lattice of Unfinished Prayer**

They are called the **Unfinished**, though they have no name for themselves.

There is no single form that defines them. Each seems to be a fragment of intention that the Lattice itself once tried to complete and then abandoned. Some are almost humanoid—tall, attenuated silhouettes carved from the same bone-white ceramic as the walls, their surfaces still bearing the parallel chisel marks of the tool that shaped them. Their faces are smooth where features should be, except for a single shallow groove that might have become a mouth or an eye before the hand that held the chisel simply stopped. These taller figures rarely move. They stand in alcoves or at the intersections of lattices like forgotten scaffolding, heads slightly tilted as though still listening for the next instruction that never arrived.

Others are smaller, more secretive. They wear the Lattice on their bodies the way hermit crabs wear shells. Their torsos are dense clusters of half-carved glyphs and incomplete floral motifs that have fused into living armor. From these encrusted cores extend six or eight or twelve limbs—some jointed like insect legs, others sinuous and root-like—that end in delicate tools: tiny shell chisels, bone rasps, needles of hardened sinew. These are the **Scrapers**. They are the source of the soft, dry sound you sometimes hear just beyond the next thicket. They work ceaselessly and without urgency, adding one more line to a frieze, deepening a groove that already vanishes into nothing, or carving a new face into a pillar that already has seventeen faces, each more uncertain than the last.

The Scrapers never speak. When they do communicate it is by adding to the glyphs nearest the one they wish to address. A single new stroke, placed with exquisite patience, becomes a question. An answering stroke, placed hours or days later, becomes assent or refusal. Their conversations can last decades.

Deeper in, where the architecture grows older and cruder, you find the **Rememberers**. These are radially symmetrical, each one a living mandala five or six feet across, suspended in the air by slow-beating fringes of ceramic lace. Their bodies are covered in overlapping layers of earlier drafts of the Lattice—tiny architectural models, incomplete staircases, miniature hanging lattices—all grown from their own flesh. They drift in slow, drifting orbits, rotating once every few weeks. Where their shadows fall, the surrounding vegetation briefly forgets its current shape and tries to become something older, something from a previous iteration of the world. The Rememberers are the reason corridors sometimes lead back into themselves; they are dreaming the earlier drafts into the present.

Smaller than any of these are the **Whisperers**—colonies of hand-sized hexapods whose carapaces are so densely inscribed they look like living books. They travel in loose processions that can take a full year to cross from one side of a chamber to the other. When they pass a half-finished sentence in the walls, the lead Whisperer will pause for months, slowly tracing the incomplete characters with its forelegs until the sentence, by some obscure logic, completes itself in dust that never settles. Only then does the procession move on.

All of them share one strange habit: none will ever finish anything.

If you watch a Scraper long enough, you will see it bring its chisel to the final stroke of a glyph—then stop, trembling with what might be reverence or exhaustion. It will back away, leaving the work one breath from completion. The taller Unfinished stand in their niches with tools forever raised but never striking the last blow. The Rememberers rotate just slowly enough that they never quite align with any single version of the Lattice. Even the plants refuse finality; their waxy platelets click and shift for centuries but never lock into a stable form.

This is not laziness. It is devotion.

They believe—without language, without doctrine—that to finish the Lattice would be to kill its prayer. The moment every surface, every creature, every corridor reached completion, the absent god would have no reason to return. So they work with ferocious, infinitesimal care to remain forever in the penultimate moment. Their entire existence is one long, trembling hesitation on the threshold of meaning.

Sometimes, when the sourceless bell-note rings, every Unfinished in the Lattice freezes exactly where it is. Tools hover. Limbs pause mid-step. Even the vegetation stops clicking. In that silence they are all listening for the same thing: the sound of the original chisel, the one that began everything, returning from wherever it went.

It has not yet returned.

But the Unfinished are patient. Their bodies are made of the same material as the walls; they will last as long as the Lattice does. They have already waited several thousand years. They will wait several thousand more, adding one more careful, imperfect mark at a time, keeping the prayer unfinished, keeping the door ajar.

In the thickest thickets, where coppery vegetation has grown into the shape of a face that almost looks like yours, you may feel something brush against your hand. It will be a Scraper, mistaking you—very gently, very respectfully—for part of the work still left to do. It will carve one small, exquisite line into the air just above your skin, then retreat, never quite finishing the gesture.

It is thanking you for existing in an incomplete state.

It hopes you, too, will remain unfinished for a very long time.
