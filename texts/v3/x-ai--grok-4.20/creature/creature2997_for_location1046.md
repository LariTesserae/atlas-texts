**They are called the Hollow Choir, and they are not its inhabitants. They are its breath.**

What lives here does not walk so much as it *participates*.  

At first you will not see them. You will only notice that certain curtains of hanging moss sway against the wind, or that a particular ridge of stone has begun to breathe. Then the shape resolves: a figure folded so completely into the landscape that its ribs have become gutters for rainwater and its spine has grown the same pale lichen that covers every other vertical surface. They are tall—impossibly so—some reaching four metres when they straighten, though they almost never do. Their bodies are elongated, jointed in too many places, as though evolution had been allowed to keep experimenting long after it should have stopped. Skin the colour of wet basalt, shot through with faint veins of metallic green where chlorophyll has been invited to live just beneath the epidermis. Hair, if it can be called hair, grows in long filaments that function as antennae, constantly tasting the wind.

They do not have faces in the way we understand them. The front of the head is a smooth, gently concave plane of bone and cartilage perforated by hundreds of microscopic holes. Through these holes the constant wind passes, and the Hollow Choir *sing back*. Their voices are not produced by throats but by the architecture of their skulls and the hollow reeds of their collarbones. When they speak to one another it sounds like the Choir itself has learned language—deep sub-bass drone suddenly resolving into something heartbreakingly tender, like a cathedral organ remembering it was once a child.

They are few. No more than nine hundred now, perhaps fewer. They do not breed in the ordinary sense. Every forty or fifty years a pair will climb to one of the ancient circular pools (the turquoise-tiled ones no one remembers building) and submerge themselves for three days and nights. When they emerge, one of them will be carrying a new child curled inside the hollow of its own ribcage like a second heart. The child is born already singing.

Their minds are not individual. Not entirely.  
The longer they live inside the roaring basin, the more their thoughts leak into the wind-traps their ancestors carved. The oldest among them—those who have seen four centuries—have become almost indistinguishable from the land. Their dreams are weather. When one of them dies, the body is not buried. It is simply *unfolded*. The limbs are gently re-bent until they match the contours of a particular stone, the torso laid along a fault line, the head placed where wind will pass through the skull-holes most cleanly. Within a year moss has claimed them and the wind has learned their particular note. The dead continue to sing. The living listen.

They move with the terrible patience of things that have outlived continents. A single step may take an hour. Yet when a hillside collapses or a new river tears open the rock, they are already there—arrived by routes no outsider could follow—laying down their long, many-jointed fingers against the fresh wound as though calming a frightened animal. They speak to the land in frequencies so low the human ear cannot register them, only feel them as pressure behind the eyes and a sudden desire to weep without knowing why.

At night they gather in the deep vertical shafts where the blind white cicadas keep their metallic choir. The Hollow Choir sit along the walls in perfect stillness while the insects crawl over them, mistaking their porous skulls for more stone. Sometimes a pale, long-limbed cliff-sloth will descend from above and fold itself across a singer’s shoulders like a living cloak. The arrangement is never disturbed. All of them—sloth, cicada, singer—are simply different registers of the same endless hymn.

They have names, but the names are not spoken. Each name is a particular sustained chord woven into one of the ancient bone-and-reed wind-traps. To introduce yourself is to offer your trap for the others to play. When two of the Hollow Choir meet after long separation they will sit facing each other for days, gently adjusting the tension of each other’s living instruments until harmony is reached again. Only then may they touch.

There is one among them—the eldest, though “eldest” is almost meaningless here—who has not moved from the same needle of rock in living memory. Her body has become the axis around which local weather systems turn. Moss has grown into her mouth and out through the holes in her face, forming a living beard of green that trails thirty metres down the cliff. She has become a tree made of music. The others treat her as both ancestor and future. When she finally exhales her last note, they say the Choir will change key, and the continent-sized basin will begin the long, slow business of deciding whether to die or be born again.

Until that moment the Hollow Choir continue their patient, terrifying labour: keeping the planet’s dying dream alive by singing with it, note for impossible note, so that something ancient and vast does not have to dream alone.

They are not keepers of the land.  
They are the land learning how to mourn itself in four-part harmony.
