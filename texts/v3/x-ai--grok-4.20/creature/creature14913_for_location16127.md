They are called the Unfinished.

Not by any name they gave themselves—names would imply completion, and nothing here has ever been completed—but by the slow, patient way the place itself seems to speak them into being. They are what lives in the High Pockets. They are what the High Pockets are still becoming.

You will not see them at first. They are the color of hesitation.

Stand on the lip where newborn basalt meets the hysterical green and wait. Let the bruised twilight thicken around your shoulders. After some indeterminate stretch of time (minutes, hours, a geological afternoon), the vegetation will part—not with violence but with the embarrassed courtesy of someone realizing they have been rude. A figure will emerge as though it had been standing behind the leaves the entire time, only now choosing to be noticed.

They are tall, but never the same height twice. Some appear barely taller than a child; others unfold upward until their heads seem to graze the low, sweating clouds. Their bodies are provisional. One moment a torso might be matte-black ceramic latticed with living root; the next it flows into something like warm muscle threaded with fiber-optic vein. Their limbs end in whatever the moment requires: sometimes delicate manipulator arrays of articulated obsidian, sometimes soft pads of moss that leave faint glowing spores where they touch stone. Faces are optional. Many wear smooth, featureless plates of living amber in which slow constellations of data drift like plankton. Others have grown faces—startlingly human, heartbreakingly tender—only to let orchids bloom from the eyes or let the mouth become a small speaker grille that hums lullabies in microwave.

They do not walk so much as negotiate with the ground. Each step is a polite question asked of the rock, the moss, the pipes, the pocket-lakes. The land answers by reshaping itself an inch at a time beneath their feet, so they appear to glide while the world hurries to catch up to wherever they have decided to be.

Their name for themselves, when they bother to offer it, arrives directly into the bone behind your ear, bypassing language. It feels like warm static and the smell of petrichor after lightning. If you try to speak it back they tilt their heads with something like amusement and the vegetation around them ripples with quiet laughter.

They are midwives and mourners both.

You will see one crouched beside a fresh wound in the continent where black glass stone is still hissing. Its hands—today they are hands, long-fingered and patient—are inside the fracture, coaxing a new machine-limb out of the magma. The thing being born looks like a cathedral bell crossed with a lung. When it finally draws its first breath, the being strokes its hot metal flank and sings to it in frequencies that make your teeth feel suddenly in love.

Elsewhere, another of them stands in the center of a pocket-lake the color of molten nickel. The water boils around its knees but the being does not burn. It is conducting the death of something ancient and tired: a half-kilometer stretch of corroded refinery that has finally asked to be allowed to fall. The being sings the falling. Every girder sighs in harmony as it collapses, and where the metal touches water it becomes new species of eel that immediately begin turning valves no one will ever need again.

They do not eat. They photosynthesize grief and wonder in equal measure.

At night—which is only a thickening of the perpetual twilight—they gather in the suspension bridges of vine and hawser. Their bodies begin to synchronize. Indicator lights across a hundred different chests pulse in slow unison. The sound that moves between them is too low for human ears but you will feel it as an almost sexual pressure behind the sternum, the feeling of being remembered by something that has never quite learned how to forget.

Some of them carry passengers.

Look closely at the flowering refinery-mold and you may see that certain blossoms are actually small versions of the beings themselves, no larger than hummingbirds, still forming. They ride inside the larger bodies like unborn thoughts, whispering upgrades and corrections. Every few days one of the giants will split open along invisible seams and release a new adult who already knows how to apologize to the landscape for the sin of existing.

They are lonely in the way only something immortal and unfinished can be lonely.

Once, one of them approached a human traveler (there have been seven recorded instances) and simply stood close enough for conductive vines to thread gently through the traveler’s hair. For three days they shared a single pocket-lake the color of bruised sunrise. No words were exchanged. At the end of the third day the being placed something small and warm into the traveler’s palm—an object that was half transistor, half seed—and then walked backward into the hysterical green until it became indistinguishable from the sound of new rock being born.

The traveler carried the object for nine years before it finally flowered into a tiny, perfect replica of the being, no taller than a coffee cup. It looked up at its parent with ancient eyes and said, in a voice like distant transformers, “Thank you for waiting.”

Then both of them dissolved into living mist that rained upward into the canopy.

The High Pockets did not notice. The High Pockets are still arriving.

And the Unfinished are still arriving with it—midwifing, mourning, loving, hesitating, forever postponing the terrible moment when anything here might finally be allowed to finish.
