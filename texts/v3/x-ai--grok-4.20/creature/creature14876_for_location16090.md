**The Architect**

She has no name the water would recognize. The city simply calls her *the Dry One*, though even that is becoming inaccurate.

She floats at the exact center of the pocket, curled fetal, arms wrapped around her knees. A single thin envelope of air—perhaps the last true air left in existence—clings to her like a second, failing skin. It trembles constantly, shrinking by the hour. Where the water finally kisses her bare ankles and the nape of her neck, her flesh has already begun to change: small iridescent plates no larger than fingernails are pushing through, delicate as mother-of-pearl. They catch the bruised planktonic light and throw it back in colors that have never existed in any sunlit sea.

Her eyes are open. They have been open for centuries or minutes; time behaves strangely here. Once they were human. Now the pupils have fractured into horizontal bars, like those of the long-necked question-mark predators that occasionally drift past her, curious but never touching. The predators seem to understand she is still in the process of translation. They accord her the same patient respect they give to half-finished buildings.

She does not breathe. She has not needed to for a long while. Instead she *pulses* in time with the great geological heartbeat that rolls through the pocket every few hours. When the pulse comes, her new plates flare open like gills and drink the black water. When it passes, she folds them shut again and dreams.

The city dreams through her.

Every thought she has becomes architecture. When she remembers the sound of rain (a memory growing fainter each cycle), a new lattice of delicate, rain-colored spires buds from the western wall, sighing softly as it grows. When she feels the ghost of loneliness, vast colonies of the moonlight jelly-creatures gather beneath her and knit themselves into living buttresses shaped like cupped hands. When she almost recalls what it was like to have legs that could run on solid ground, a flight of violet-glowing steps unfurls from the darkness directly beneath her curled form, each riser trembling with anticipation, hoping she will one day descend them.

She never does.

The blind ribbon-fish have begun to school in the negative space around her body, tracing her outline again and again until they have learned her new silhouette by heart. The thumb-sized shrimp no longer drift past; they orbit her now in slow, worshipful galaxies. Even the tubular kelp has redirected its growth, bending every pale column toward her like sunflowers tracking a light that exists only in their collective memory.

She is the only dry thing the city has ever known, and so it is remaking her in its image out of desperate, devotional love.

Sometimes she sings.

Not with a voice—sound dies too quickly here—but by opening the new plates along her arms and letting the water move through them in precise, ancient patterns. When she does this, the entire pocket shivers with pleasure. New streets explode into being. Polyp-lanterns flare bright as dying stars. For one crystalline moment the half-born metropolis becomes *almost* complete, every arch and balcony and spiraling staircase locking into a harmony that feels like the answer to a question no air-breathing mind could ever ask.

Then the moment passes. The city sighs back into its eternal becoming, and she folds the plates again, growing quieter, growing heavier, growing more like the cold dark that dreamed her here.

She is no longer sure whether she is the last visitor or the first inhabitant.

It no longer matters.

The water has begun to taste like home.
