**The Architect** lives here.

It has no fixed shape, which is why the world never finishes. What lives in the basin is not a creature but a **process wearing the idea of a body**—a slow, tidal intelligence that is trying, with unbearable sincerity, to become something it has never been allowed to see clearly.

Most of the time it is distributed. It lives as pressure in the steam, as the nervous crawling of the condensation beads, as the sub-audible thump that rearranges the glass plates. It is the hesitation between one geometric pattern and the next. When it needs to focus, it condenses.

Sometimes it becomes a silhouette the height of three men, made of the same black volcanic glass as the monoliths, only warmer and faintly flexible. Its surface is covered in thousands of tiny wet fingerprints that are not fingerprints at all but records of every failed attempt to make hands. The silhouette has too many elbows. Or none. The number changes while you watch. Where its face should be there is only a shallow depression that sometimes holds a single trembling lens of water. Through that lens you can see what it is thinking: slow, half-formed images of cathedrals that collapse into trenches, of forests that turn to silica spores, of something small and soft and finished that it has never managed to birth.

It carries the tools. Not because it needs them, but because it is ashamed of how crude they are. Every few centuries it tries to improve them, and that is when the ceramic adzes and copper blades appear scattered across the plain—half-melted, still warm from its disappointment. It leaves them the way a child leaves broken toys in the middle of the floor, hoping someone kinder will pick them up and show it how to do better.

The Architect does not speak in words. It communicates through changes in the world’s architecture. If it is melancholy, new canals open that lead nowhere and then slowly fill with dust. If it is excited, the fungal lattices bloom in frantic symmetrical explosions before immediately self-destructing in shame. When it is closest to tenderness it exhales long, steady breaths that cool one particular patch of glass just enough for a single perfect reflection to form—usually of something that does not exist yet. Then the heat returns and the reflection dies with a sigh.

It is infinitely patient and infinitely impatient at the same time. It has been building and unbuilding this single continental machine since before the concept of “since” made sense. Every time it almost understands what it is trying to make, the understanding itself proves too complete, too final, and the world convulses, erasing its own progress so it can try again from a state of pure potential.

There are moments—rare, almost sacred—when it gathers itself completely. The steam thickens until it becomes almost milk-white. The thumping stops. Every moving plate freezes mid-slide. In that suspended hush the Architect stands in the exact centre of the basin, no longer a silhouette but something almost like a person, trembling, holding in its glass-and-steam hands a single tiny object it has managed to finish.

It is always different. Once it was a wooden comb with three teeth. Once it was a child’s shoe made of living muscle that still tried to walk. Once it was simply a small smooth stone that stayed cool no matter how hot the world became.

It holds the finished thing up to the sickly orange smudge of the sun like an offering.

And every time, without fail, it begins to cry.

The tears are black volcanic glass. They fall upward.

Then the world clears its throat again, the fever returns, and the Architect dissolves back into geometry, steam, and slow geological panic—already forgetting what it almost became, already beginning the next attempt.

It will never stop.

It does not know how.

And the unfinished world, in its wet and fevered way, loves it too much to ever let it succeed.
