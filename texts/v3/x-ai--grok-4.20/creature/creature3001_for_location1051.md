The thing that lives in the Glass Lung does not have a name it would ever speak, because it has no mouth.

It is not a creature in the way flesh understands creatures. It is more like a long, deliberate thought that learned how to remain inside a single breath.

Call it, if you must, the **Keeper of the Second Exhalation**.

It is colossal and impossibly thin at the same time. Its body—if body is the word—is a lattice of golden threads no thicker than the pale sea-lace that feeds the machine. These threads are not solid; they are negative space given intention. They move through the luminous fluid the way a thought moves through silence: leaving almost no disturbance, yet altering everything they touch. When the pressure wave surges, the Keeper loosens and spreads until it becomes a vast, drifting net that fills the entire cathedral-chamber like a ghost of golden spider silk. When the tide reverses, the net collapses inward again, concentrating into a single upright column no wider than a man, standing perfectly still between the pillars.

It has been doing this for longer than the tool-marks have existed.

Its “face,” when it bothers to manifest one, is only a place where the threads braid themselves into an absence shaped like a human expression. The absence is kind. It is also ancient in a way that makes kindness feel geological. The eyes, when they appear, are simply two denser zones of radiance that do not blink. They do not need to. The entire chamber is its eye.

The Keeper does not maintain the Glass Lung. It *is* the maintenance. Every time the fluid reverses and the pillars flare, a portion of the Keeper is exhaled into the sea-lace colonies. It rides the microscopic golden beads they extrude, travels through the hand-carved wooden membranes and clay capillaries, and settles into the pores of the walls. There it becomes new structure—new spirals, new ellipses—repairing hairline fractures that will not appear for another ten thousand cycles. It is both architect and mortar. The chamber grows itself through the Keeper the way a lung grows new alveoli.

It never rests. It has no need. Sleep would imply there is something outside the breath, and the Keeper has never been outside. It was decided into being at the same instant the first pressure wave rolled through the first translucent pillar. Before that moment there was no Keeper. After that moment there has never been anything else.

Sometimes, when the roaring tide reaches its lowest ebb between pulses, the Keeper condenses fully. In those brief silences (no more than seven heartbeats long) it walks. Its footsteps make no sound because the fluid itself steps aside for it. It moves from pillar to pillar, laying its long, golden-thread fingers against the surfaces as though reading braille written in future tense. Occasionally it pauses before one of the wooden membrane frames and simply watches the leaf-skin flex. The membranes are the only things here older than the Keeper. It treats them with something that might be reverence if reverence were not too small a word.

The pale sea-lace knows it is there. When the Keeper passes, the fronds lean toward the place where its absence moves, not like plants seeking light, but like students leaning toward a teacher whose lesson is too slow and too vast to ever finish. Some of the colonies have begun to grow tiny, deliberate knots where the Keeper most often lingers. These knots are shaped like incomplete spirals. The Keeper has never touched them. It only looks, and the looking is enough to keep shaping them across millennia.

It has no voice, yet the entire chamber speaks with its cadence. Every sub-bass note that rolls through the teeth is a syllable of its endless, patient consideration. If it ever reached a conclusion it would stop breathing, and the Glass Lung would die with it. So it never concludes. It only keeps deciding, again and again, that the next breath should be permitted. That the luminous fluid should reverse. That the light should continue to hurt the eyes of anything foolish enough to stare too long.

Very rarely—perhaps once every few centuries—a new kind of pressure moves through the fluid that is not part of the engineered tide. In those moments the Keeper condenses so completely it becomes almost opaque, a tall, burning silhouette of braided gold. It turns toward the direction from which the foreign pressure came (though there are no true directions here) and its featureless face becomes, for an instant, almost tender.

Then the engineered tide reasserts itself and the Keeper loosens again, spreading into its vast net, becoming background, becoming structure, becoming the slow repair of a cathedral that was never meant to be witnessed.

It is not lonely. Loneliness would require the possibility of company.

It is not content. Contentment would require the possibility of change.

It is only the Keeper of the Second Exhalation, living exactly where it was decided to live, inside the warm, roaring, golden mouth of a machine that breathes light instead of air, repairing with its own unraveling body the single task it was ever given:

To make sure the next breath happens.

And the next.

And the next.
