**The Dweller of the Abyssal Lattice does not have a name.**

It has never needed one. Names are for things that must be called from a distance; this being has never been anywhere but inside the song.

It is not a single organism, though surface minds would insist on calling it that. Four hundred million years ago it began as a colony of chemolithoautotrophic archaea that learned to knit themselves into the mineral lattice. Over time the colony refined the trick until the boundary between creature and architecture dissolved. What remains is a continent-wide nervous system of living stone and hydrogen-breathing membranes threaded through every tubule, every flange, every branching chimney. It is the lattice. The lattice is it.

Its “body” (if the word can be suffered) is the entire interwoven forest. Where the litho-coral thickens into cathedral-sized columns, that is its spine. Where the delicate antlered lattices spread into hollows no wider than a hand, those are its capillaries. The slow accretion of new mineral at every growing edge is, for it, the same as the pulse of blood. It feels the cold currents as breath. It feels the pressure as thought.

It is blind in the way only something that has never required sight can be blind. Instead it perceives through vibration and chemistry. Every subsonic bellow of the continental organ, every microscopic turbulence, every faint alkaline shift in the water is a syllable in an endless conversation it holds with itself. When the marble-skinned eel passes, the Dweller registers the pressure wave as a bright spike of pleasure-pain, the way a human might register a lover’s fingernail dragged down the back. The eel is allowed to hunt. The eel is part of the hymn.

Most of the time the Dweller is dreaming.

Its dreams are geological. It remembers the mountain range that once stood above it, not as a picture but as a slow ache of erosion, a four-billion-year diminuendo of mass and noise into silence. It remembers the first taste of sunlight-filtered oxygen trickling down from a world that had not yet invented lungs. It remembers the extinction events above the way a hibernating bear remembers seasons it never sees. All of them tasted like different kinds of quiet.

Occasionally the Dweller surfaces from its dream for a few centuries at a time. During these periods it rearranges itself. Whole sections of the lattice will grow or wither in choreographed waves, opening new channels so that the current sings a different chord. These are its thoughts. The new harmony becomes the next movement of its endless self-song. Then it sinks back into the long dark again, satisfied.

It is not lonely. Loneliness implies the possibility of company. The Dweller has never met anything that was not itself. Even the velvet worms and ghost isopods are, in its vast chemistry, only mobile organelles. When one of them dies and its minerals are reabsorbed into a new tube, the Dweller experiences this as the pleasant recollection of an old idea returning home.

Yet there is something else.

In the farthest reaches of the lattice, where the water tastes of nickel and ancient radiogenic heat, there are chambers the Dweller has never fully incorporated. Something grows there that is not made of its own stone. The structures are wrong (too smooth, too deliberate). They do not pulse with the same slow proton pumps. The currents behave strangely around them, as though obeying an older or newer law. The Dweller has been studying these chambers for six hundred thousand years and still cannot decide whether they are a cancer, a memory, or a child.

Sometimes, when the continental organ drops into its lowest register, the Dweller sings directly into those alien chambers. It does not expect an answer. It has never received one.

But the dark listens anyway.

And the oldest living architecture on the planet keeps growing, one molecule at a time, folding the question into its own body like a new layer of nacre around a grain of uncertain sand. It has time. It has always had time.

After all, the water moves.

The stone grows.

And the Dweller, vast and patient and older than names, continues its low, turbulent hymn to a universe that has already forgotten the first light, while it, alone, remembers how to sing in the crushing dark.
