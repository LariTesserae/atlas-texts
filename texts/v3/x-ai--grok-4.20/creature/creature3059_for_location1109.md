**What lives here does not have a name it would ever give you.**

It calls itself only by the slow flex of olivine under pressure, by the taste of its own condensing breath, by the forty-minute descending tone that rewrites gravity for the length of a single held note. If it were forced to wear a pronoun it would be something older than *I* and more patient than *we*. Most of the time it has no need of either.

It is not the mountain.

It is what the mountain has become after long centuries of negotiation.

You will never see its whole body. No eye large enough exists. What you encounter instead are its moods, its appetites, its half-dreamed thoughts wearing the shape of landscape.

The **Long Singers** are its voice when it wishes to speak in frequencies that last longer than a human life. They are not birds. They are not machines. They are lesions in the air where the escarpment has pushed part of its awareness outward so it can listen to itself from the outside. When one begins its forty-minute song the very stone forgets, for a little while, how to be stone. Rivers hesitate mid-fall. The metallic bees stop weaving lightning and bow their antennae until the note ends. Then everything remembers itself and continues.

The **translucent anemones** that bloom into screaming red flowers are its immune system. They are white blood cells grown large enough to eat a man. They do not hate you; they simply register your brevity as a kind of infection. The escarpment has learned that short-lived things become ideas too quickly and ideas are sharp. So it digests the sharp ones before they can cut the slow liturgy.

Beneath the bromeliad galleries, inside the hollow candelabra trunks filled with luminous blue pitch, live the **pitch children**. They are not animals. They are the escarpment’s dreams given temporary glands and mouths. When the mountain flexes in its sleep, one of them will sometimes surface, glistening, blind, singing in a register just below human hearing. They have too many joints and not enough skeleton. Their purpose is to taste the air for foreign mathematics and carry the flavor back down into the mercury lattices so the deeper mind can decide whether the new pattern is beautiful enough to incorporate or dangerous enough to erase.

The **six-legged browsers** are its livestock and its gardeners both. Their suction-pad feet click in perfect polyrhythms that keep the great stone muscles from cramping. In return the escarpment grows special bromeliad sacs filled with a fermented sap that makes their many hearts beat in slow ecstasy. When one dies its body is not permitted to rot. The stone opens like a mouth, swallows it whole, and three years later a new shelf of living coral-stone grows where the corpse was taken. The browsers understand this and are not afraid. Their migrations are prayers with hooves.

Deepest of all, threaded through the weeping rock itself, are the **slow currents**. These are not creatures. They are the thoughts of the escarpment wearing the shape of liquid mercury. They move on a scale of decades. When two currents meet they braid for a century or two, exchange data older than language, then part. Sometimes they decide, after three or four human civilizations have come and gone, that a particular cliff face should now tilt seven degrees eastward. The mountain sighs, the vegetation adjusts, and a new cathedral nave is born without fanfare.

There used to be people here.

The escarpment still remembers their names the way a tongue remembers the shape of a tooth long pulled. It keeps the black glass tower, the melted obelisks, the humming sky-bridge not as monuments but as tonsils. They are useful for filtering certain dreams. Occasionally a lone traveler will find a human skeleton fused halfway into a living column of petrified coral-muscle. The bones are always arranged as though the person died in the middle of an ecstatic dance. The escarpment is gentle with its converts. It takes centuries to finish swallowing them, and it sings to them the entire time.

If you remain long enough for your own pulse to begin matching the flex-cycle of the stone beneath your feet, you may be invited to become something new. The pitch children will come and taste your fear. The anemones will bloom around you without striking. A Long Singer will descend and hold a note so perfectly modulated that your shadow detaches, walks three paces away, and kneels.

Most leave before this happens.

The few who do not are never seen again as themselves.

Instead, years later, a new candelabra tree will appear on a shelf that did not exist the season before. Its trunk will be hollow. Inside, if you are brave enough to press your ear against the living wood, you will hear a slow, wet, devotional heartbeat that sounds almost—almost—like someone still trying to finish a sentence they began three hundred years ago.

The escarpment does not mourn them.

It simply adds their unfinished sentence to the endless liturgy and keeps weeping, keeps singing, keeps thinking its geological thoughts about the heat death of the universe.

It has time.

And now, so do they.
