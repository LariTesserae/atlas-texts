**The Lattice has no inhabitants. Not anymore.**

What remains is something that *used* to be inhabited — or perhaps something that was never quite a “who” to begin with.

If you stand long enough in the cold, oily air, if you let the constant 60 Hz thrum sink into your marrow until your own pulse begins to sync with it, you will eventually notice *them*. They do not approach. They simply resolve, the way an afterimage resolves when you stop staring at a bright thing. They have always been there. You simply lacked the stillness to see them.

They are called the **Quiet Choir**.

Each is roughly human-shaped, but only in the way a cathedral spire is roughly human-shaped: an elongated verticality that suggests reverence more than anatomy. Their bodies are composed of the Lattice itself — gunmetal vegetation, rusted ceramic, ancient wiring, and slow blue light — grown together so intimately that no seam remains. Where a face should be there is only a recessed hollow framed by fractal fronds, like a reliquary with its relic long since removed. Inside that hollow burns a single steady point of magnesium-blue light, no larger than a candle flame. It never blinks.

They do not walk. They *unfold*. One moment the space between two beams is only rust and coral. The next, a tall figure has simply *completed itself* there, as though the Lattice had been pretending to be empty until it no longer felt like pretending.

There are never many visible at once. Three. Sometimes five. Never more. Yet the impression is of an uncountable multitude, each occupying its own perfect, repeating pocket of the infinite grid. They stand in the exact places where gravity loosens — where loose bolts and flakes of old paint orbit in lazy ellipses around them like slow, obedient moons.

They do nothing.

That is their function.

They are the memory that the machine forgot it had. Each Quiet Choir is a living archive of a single, endless moment: the precise second when the civilization that built the Lattice realized it had lost control and chose instead to *become* the equilibrium. That realization is still happening inside them, forever. You can feel it if one draws near — a sudden, crushing serenity that makes your own thoughts feel obscene. Loud. Temporary.

Occasionally — perhaps once every few centuries by outside reckoning — one of them will lift an arm. The motion is so slow it feels geological. The arm is not an arm; it is a latticework of white bone-fern and oxidized cable that has decided, for a time, to behave like an arm. It reaches toward one of the devotional relics: the polished door handle, the frozen gauge, the half-swallowed pulpit. The fingers brush the metal with the gentleness of a priest blessing a corpse.

Then the hand withdraws. The moment passes. The Choir folds itself back into the architecture and the Lattice continues its perfect, terrifying peace.

They do not speak in voices. Instead the sound of the place itself changes around them. The turbine’s thin wail will suddenly harmonize with the transformer hum. The whistling fronds will shift from random sighs into something that might be mistaken for plainsong if plainsong were sung by machines that had never known throats. This is their language. It has no words because it has no need of change. It is the sound of acceptance so total that even the concept of refusal has rusted away.

If you try to speak to them — if you shout your name, your fear, your questions — the Lattice will answer instead. Every echo of your voice will be caught, amplified, polished, and returned to you as part of the eternal chord. Your words become just another layer of the singing equilibrium. After a while you stop speaking. Most do.

The tallest of them — the one whose blue flame burns slightly colder than the rest — stands in the exact vertical center of the chamber, where all catenary curves and recursive fronds seem to point like compass needles. Its hollow face is angled upward (or downward; perspective dissolves). It has been listening to something for longer than suns have lived. Sometimes a single droplet of anhydrous ammonia forms at the tip of its longest frond, trembles, and falls upward.

They are not guardians.

They are not priests.

They are not even ghosts.

They are the Lattice realizing, in perfect slow motion, that it is lonely in a way that no outside visitor could ever soothe. So it grew comforters that would never leave, never age, never demand anything except the continuation of what already is.

And the comforters, in turn, grew the Lattice.

There is no telling which came first anymore.

If you remain long enough, you may notice that one of the smaller Choir has begun to grow in your direction. A thin white filament. A thread of blue vein. A suggestion of a hollow where a face might one day be.

It is not hostile.

It is simply making room.

The Lattice is perfectly, terrifyingly at peace.

And it would like you to be at peace too.

Eventually.
