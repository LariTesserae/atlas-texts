**The Lanternkeeper**

It has no name it would give you. It has no face, no limbs, no fixed locus. Yet it is always here, and it is always moving.

It is not one thing. It is seven absences wearing the shape of what once tried to be human.

You will never see it directly. What you might glimpse—if the lenticular cloud fractures the light just so—is a flicker at the edge of a crystal curtain: the suggestion of a shoulder turning away, or a hand that ends at the wrist because the rest of the arm has been translated into pure refraction. Sometimes the wind carries a scent that is not possible here: warm skin, woodsmoke, human breath. The smell lasts less than a heartbeat and leaves the tongue tasting iron.

The Lanternkeeper is what remains when seven people chose to stay after their bodies were no longer welcome.

They came long after the nameless volcano died but long before the first myth about it was spoken. They were pilgrims, or exiles, or perhaps cartographers of the invisible. They built the seven half-domes with the solemnity of those who believe they have reached the last place on earth. Then they did something the mountain would not forgive: they tried to root here. They tried to *become* a part of the clarity.

The mountain answered by taking them at their word.

One by one their blood refused to clot. One by one their thoughts grew so sharp they cut their own minds. One by one they walked out of their shelters, set their tools upright like small altars, and stepped into the crystal lattice as though stepping through a door made of mirrors. Their flesh did not decay. It simply *stopped mattering*. The wind and the ultraviolet and the slow patient hunger of the gypsum blades took what was soft and gave the rest back as geometry.

Now the Lanternkeeper is the negative space those seven left behind.

It keeps the crystals growing along lines that almost remember human intention. Some of the repeating waveforms etched into the blades are almost the shape of a woman’s collarbone. Some of the geodesic arches almost form the negative silhouette of a man kneeling. The patterns are trying to finish themselves because the Lanternkeeper is still trying to finish the gesture it began when it was still seven separate people.

When the mist beads on the highest edges and the silver threads run down, the Lanternkeeper drinks without a mouth. The water does not vanish into fissures. It vanishes into memory. Each drop becomes one more counted heartbeat of a heart that no longer has permission to beat. That is why the single falling tone you hear from the center is so unbearably lonely: it is the sound of something that knows it is dead and has nevertheless agreed to keep counting.

The tools inside the collapsed domes are not relics. They are still being used.

Every few centuries the Lanternkeeper returns to the half-domes and lifts the quartz hand-axe, the bone awl, the petrified sinew. It cannot hold them in hands it no longer possesses, so the tools simply rise. For a few moments the air inside each dome shivers with the memory of work: scraping, cutting, binding. Then the tools settle again, in exactly the same upright positions, only now they are microscopically closer to one another. The change is too small for any eye that still uses water and protein to detect.

But the crystals see.

That is why the glassy chorus changes timbre over the ages. It is the sound of seven absences learning, very slowly, how to sing with one voice.

If you were to stand long enough in the exact center—between the seven fallen shelters—and if you were willing to let the thin air and the savage light burn away everything soft in you, the Lanternkeeper might finally turn around.

You would not see a person.

You would see the crystals rearrange themselves for half a second into the exact negative space of seven human silhouettes standing shoulder to shoulder, looking at you with the patient, unbearable tenderness of something that has outlived every reason to care and has not yet managed to stop.

Then the wind would shift, the chorus would die, and the silhouettes would become knives again.

But the impression would remain: that you had been seen by something ancient, mineral, and unbearably lonely—something that still remembers what it felt like to be warm, and has decided that remembering is enough.

The Shattered Lantern is not empty.

It has one inhabitant who is many, who is none, who is patiently, perfectly, forever almost becoming something it will never be allowed to finish.

And it is listening for your next heartbeat, so it can add it, with utmost care, to the count.
