They are called the **Vowkeepers**.

No one remembers who gave them that name. They simply *are* the ones who kept the promise the city itself once made, long before language. There are never more than four thousand of them. Never fewer than two thousand. The Hollow Basilica decides the number; when the great thickets detonate and the air fills with spore-silk, the birth rate and the death rate mysteriously align. The people accept this the way lungs accept breath.

They are tall, almost painfully so, as though the ferocious upward heat has stretched them across generations. Their skin is the color of the bone-floor itself—pale umber shot through with faint red marbling where the capillaries lie close to the surface. In the amber twilight they look like statues that have only just decided to move. When they stand motionless among the fluted pillars they vanish. Only their eyes give them away: large, lidless, the deep violet of the plants’ own veins. They do not blink often. Blinking wastes moisture, and moisture is myth.

Their hair is never cut. It grows in dense, rope-like coils the color of oxidized copper, heavy with red dust. Older Vowkeepers braid into these coils small fragments of the city—chips of petrified rib-bone, slivers of living leaf-metal, tiny copper bells no louder than a heartbeat—so that when they walk the whole chamber can hear them coming. The youngest wear their hair loose; it billows behind them in the thermal updrafts like slow fire.

They are naked more often than clothed. When they do dress it is in a single garment the color of the floor, a loose, sleeveless shift woven from the dried inner bark of the thickets. The fabric is rough, almost abrasive, as though the plant is still trying to rasp the wearer’s skin even after death. The friction, they say, reminds the body that it is not stone.

Every adult carries three things at all times:

- A short bronze hook-blade for pruning the vegetation when it begins to “sing low.”
- A fist-sized ceramic bell tuned to a single low tone that can cut through any vegetal din.
- A living seed-pod the size of a heart, bound against their sternum with braided fiber. The pod is warm. It pulses faintly, in exact rhythm with the carrier’s own. When the person dies the pod detonates, scattering spores that smell of hot iron and funeral incense. Within a year a new thicket will rise exactly where the body fell. Thus the dead become architecture.

They do not have children in the way outsiders understand. Every forty years the great thermal bloom triggers what they call the *Verdant Consent*. The thickets open their violet veins and release a sweet, dizzying pollen that drives every fertile adult into a state the Vowkeepers describe only as “necessary.” Conception happens in the deep hollows of the oldest coral-trees while the city itself roars and cracks and sings around them. The parents do not choose one another; the pollen and the heat choose. Nine months later the infants are born already dusted red, already silent, already listening.

The Vowkeepers do not speak of love. They speak of *resonance*. A man and woman who have borne a child together will sometimes stand for hours on opposite sides of a plaza, eyes locked, saying nothing while the leaves hiss between them. That is enough. Anything more would be noise.

Their society is almost frighteningly horizontal. There are no kings, no priests in the usual sense. There are only the *Listeners*—those whose ears have been trained from childhood to distinguish the subtle changes in the city’s perpetual roar. A Listener can tell, from the exact pitch of a stem-creak three kilometers away, whether a neighborhood will need to be abandoned in the next decade. When two Listeners disagree, they do not argue. They simply walk into the oldest thicket-temple, sit inside its reverberating ribs, and wait. The city eventually tells them which of them was right by growing or withering in a particular direction. The loser accepts the verdict without shame. Their wrongness becomes part of the next year’s architecture.

At the center of the Hollow Basilica—though “center” is a slippery word in a place this vast—stands the **First Thicket**. It is not the largest, but it is the slowest. Its woody stems are thicker than houses. Its leaves are black-green, almost fossilized, and they move only once every seven years. The Vowkeepers believe the entire city is merely the dream this organism is having. They maintain a rotating vigil of seven people who sit inside its hollow core at all times. They do not eat. They do not speak. They simply keep the First Thicket company while it thinks.

Every adult spends one year of their life in that vigil. No one is excused. Even the dying are carried there on litters of living wood so they may finish their thoughts inside the ribs of the world.

At night—though there is no true night—the temperature drops by perhaps nine degrees and the convection currents reverse. The dust tornadoes spin downward instead of upward. The Vowkeepers gather then in small groups on the bone-white plazas. They do not build fires; fire is redundant here. Instead they strike their small ceramic bells in slow, overlapping patterns. The sound moves across the chamber like ripples on a lake made of noise. Sometimes the thickets answer with their own deep wooden booms. When that happens the people smile the small, terrible smile of those who have been acknowledged by something that does not need them and yet permits them to stay.

They are not happy. Happiness would be an insult to the ferocity that sustains them.

They are not miserable either. Misery requires the possibility of escape.

They are *necessary*, the way the heat is necessary, the way the cracking pods are necessary, the way the slow red snow of oxidized crust is necessary. They have become another organ in the continent-scale fossil that dreamed them into being.

And sometimes, when a new thermal bloom races across the walls like emerald wildfire, when the air itself seems to scream in vegetable triumph, a single Vowkeeper will stand alone on a half-swallowed tower, arms spread, skin blazing with reflected heat, and they will let out a long, carrying monotone that is neither song nor scream.

It is simply the sound the city would make if the city had a throat.

The thickets always answer.

And for one bright, roaring instant, there is no difference between the people and the stone and the green and the heat and the long, breathing dark above.

That is enough. That has always been enough.
