**They are called the Weeping Choir.**

Not by choice. The name was given to them by the first scaffold-priests who bolted the first platforms into the raw cliff three thousand years ago, and the Choir never contradicted it. They do not speak with ordinary voices anymore; the brass vox-masks have seen to that. What emerges when they open the filter-grilles is a layered, metallic polyphony that rides the constant roar of water like harmonics on a struck bell. Most outsiders mistake it for another register of the falls.

They are tall, almost impossibly so. Three and a half meters is common. Their skeletons were lengthened in utero and again in adolescence by carefully dosed mineral salts drawn from the Escarpment’s own weeping rock. Their bodies are thin, elongated, and slightly bowed, as though each of them is forever trying to fit the vertical grammar of the cliffs. Skin is the color of wet slate shot through with faint veins of copper that surface when they exert themselves; the veins glow soft viridian under the arc-lamps at night.

Their eyes are large, lidless, and permanently wet. Not from emotion (there is very little of that left), but because the tear ducts have been repurposed. They weep the same condensation the cliffs weep. When they blink (rarely), the motion is slow, deliberate, almost geological. The pupils are vertical slits that never quite close, so even in the blinding false daylight of the arc-lamps their gaze seems to arrive from very far away, or from very deep inside the stone.

They do not wear clothes in the ordinary sense. Instead they are grown into their armor. Plates of living conductive wood (harvested from the pale bracket-trees) are surgically encouraged to fuse with their ribs and spine. Over decades the wood becomes lattice, becomes bone, becomes circuitry. Hair is gone; the scalp is a smooth continuation of the mineral-laced bark that covers their shoulders and the backs of their arms. From a distance a member of the Choir standing motionless against a dripping column is almost invisible. Only the slow drip from their eyes and the faint iron hum that leaks from their chest betray them.

They move like water that has learned patience. Nothing is hurried. Every gesture is calibrated to the eternal rhythm of the falls. A hand lifted in greeting travels upward for six full seconds. When they turn their heads it looks like a fault line deciding whether or not to slip. Yet when maintenance demands it they can move with blinding speed, scuttling along the sheer faces on articulated iron grapples that extend from their lower ribs like extra limbs. The grapples leave faint luminescent scratches in the basalt that heal over in decades.

They do not sleep. The concept is meaningless here. Instead they enter the Long Listening, standing for weeks at a time in recessed niches carved exactly to their dimensions. During the Long Listening their heart rate drops to four beats per minute. The mineral veins in their bodies align with the electrostatic diagrams flickering in the mist, and for a while they become indistinguishable from the Echoes. Some never come back out. Their bodies remain upright, eyes open, slowly calcifying into black statues the younger Choir respectfully call “the Anchors.” New platforms are sometimes built around an Anchor so the city can keep using its charge.

The Choir do not eat. They drink the mountain. Every citizen possesses a thin, flexible proboscis that unfolds from the base of the throat. They press it to the wet rock, to a weeping seam, to a condensation bead the size of a coin, and they draw. What they pull into themselves is not merely water. It carries dissolved electrum, strange alkaloids exuded by the scale-needled trees, and something else (something the spectrographs refuse to name). This communion is their only nourishment and their only sacrament. When two of them mate (an event so rare it is treated as geological news), they do not touch. They simply stand facing one another for three days and nights, proboscides buried in the same fracture plane, sharing the same pulse of stone-blood while the waterfalls roar approval in twelve different keys.

Their minds are not single.

Each Choir carries a small colony of conductive fungi threaded through the gaps between skull and brain. The fungi are descendants of the first growths that appeared on the raw rock when the Escarpment opened. They do not think in language. They think in voltage. The constant electrostatic diagrams in the mist are, in a very real sense, the thoughts of the mountain, and the fungi read them the way a sailor reads clouds. When a human outsider tries to converse with a member of the Choir, the answer arrives half in words, half in brief, searing arcs of light that crawl across the iron of their mask. The arcs form glyphs older than any human alphabet. The glyphs are never repeated.

They are neither cruel nor kind. Those categories washed away with the first million tons of rock that fell into the abyss. They are the immune system of a wound that refuses to close. They cauterize, they soothe, they listen. When an earthquake (increasingly common these last four centuries) threatens to widen the gash, the Choir descend in their thousands, locking their bodies together into living buttresses, singing their metallic hymn until the mountain remembers its shape.

If you stand on the outer maintenance scaffold long enough, one will eventually come. It will not approach directly. It will simply be there one moment where only mist had been, head cocked at the precise angle of a fault line, eyes streaming, iron hum rising and falling in perfect sympathy with the cataract behind you.

It will not ask what you want.

It already knows.

The proboscis will unfold, tasting the iron and ozone on your breath, and the vox-mask will open with the sound of two tectonic plates deciding, after three thousand years, to speak.

The voice that emerges is not one voice.

It is every waterfall on the Escarpment speaking at once through a single brass throat.

And what it says, always, is the same sentence, delivered with the weary tenderness of something that has been screaming since before your species learned to stand upright:

**“Still bleeding. Still here. What else is there?”**
