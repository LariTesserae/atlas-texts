**The place has no name, and it never needed one. It has only ever needed eaters.**

They are not creatures first. They are prolongations of the green.

The ones who stayed have been here so long that their original bodies are remembered only as texture. You will not see them at once. They are distributed. A certain thickening of a vine here, a knot of root that catches the light like a collarbone there, a cluster of pale orchids whose eye-patterns follow you with the exact slow blink you used to make when you were still entirely human. The pocket digested them by degrees, kindly, the way a stomach digests without hatred. What remained was allowed to keep moving.

They call themselves (when they still use voices) the Lattice-Born.

There are never more than thirteen at any moment. When one finally dissolves into pure architecture, another visitor is already halfway to becoming the replacement, though they do not yet know it. The number stays constant the way a forest keeps the same number of apex predators. The green requires balance.

They do not walk. They negotiate. One moment the moss is only moss; the next it is lifting a figure upward on a thousand soft palps. Their skin is photosynthetic. In full light their limbs glow the color of young limes; in shadow they go the deep bruised green of rainforest floor. Veins of pale xylem run visibly beneath the surface, carrying water and whatever is left of their blood. Their hair did not fall out. It became aerial roots that drift behind them in living dreadlocks, tasting the mist, drinking the internal rain.

Their faces are the hardest to look at.

Each has kept two original eyes, but these now sit in nests of petal and stamen. The rest of the face has been colonised by friendly epiphytes so that expression happens in layers: a slow unfurling of lip-leaves when they are pleased, a tightening of thorn-brows when the architecture is arguing with itself. When they speak, the words come from everywhere. The rushing water in the pillars briefly organises into syllables. The white moths orbit their heads and beat their wings in complicated code. Their own throats have become hollow flutes; breath pushed through them produces a wet, choral music that feels like being understood in bone rather than mind.

They are not kind. Kindness is a dry-land concept.

They are attentive. When a new visitor descends the stone throat, three or four of the Lattice-Born will detach from whatever pillar or ceiling they have grown into and drift downward through the beams of self-generated light. They move like sea-plants in a current that only they can feel. They circle the newcomer without urgency. They study the exact rhythm between the visitor’s lungs and the moss’s lungs. Most are sent away while the boundary is still repairable. A few are offered the long bargain.

The bargain is never spoken outright. It is grown.

A particular orchid will open beside the visitor’s sleeping place. Inside its throat is a single drop of nectar that tastes like every childhood memory of being safe. If the visitor drinks, the process begins. Roots first—thin, exploratory—sliding painlessly between tendon and bone. Then the softening of edges. Then the slow, ecstatic realisation that solitude was an illusion invented by creatures who had never been properly inside a stomach that loved them.

Those who accept become exquisite.

One of them was once a woman who sang. Now she is the entire western wall. Her ribs have become spiraling pillars. Her voice still lives in the water that runs inside them; on certain nights the roar resolves into lullabies older than any human tongue. Another was a soldier. He is the ceiling now. When the pocket needs to rearrange itself, his shoulders shift by centimeters per hour and the stone above groans in sympathy.

The current thirteen have titles they gave themselves, half in joke, half in terror:

- The Knitter of Instant Bridges
- She Who Remembers Being Lungs
- The One Who Is Mostly Throat
- The Cartographer of Shadows That Walk Ahead
- He Who Is Becoming Ceiling, But Not Yet

They tend the conversation between species that has lasted centuries. When two pillars need to fuse, the Lattice-Born climb inside the wound and sing the cells into new agreement. When the enormous fern-winged thing far above grows restless, they send glass frogs upward in glittering clouds to soothe it. They prune what must be pruned. They rejoice when something beautiful dies, because death here is only translation.

At the center of the pocket—exactly where the breathing is loudest—there grows a single tree that is not a tree. It is the memory of every creature that ever chose to stay. Its bark is smooth as foreheads. Its leaves are tiny, perfectly shaped human ears. If you press your own ear against the trunk you can hear every voice that was ever digested here, layered into one vast, wet, loving roar.

The Lattice-Born will let you listen.

But only once.

Afterward they watch to see whether you walk back toward the narrow throat of stone, or whether you sit down among the sphagnum, take off your boots, and let your feet sink to the ankle.

If you do the latter, one of them will drift close. Perhaps the One Who Is Mostly Throat. She will open the hollow flute of her neck and ask, without words, whether you are ready to add your own small note to the endless performance.

Most visitors still leave.

Some do not.

And the green, patient and enormous and intimate, opens another pore to receive them.
