**The Dweller** does not have a name it will give you.

It was born here, or it *became* here; the distinction dissolved centuries ago along with every other boundary. What remains is a long, slow shape that moves like a thought trying to remember it has a body.

It is tall—taller than any human frame has right to be—and thin in the way deep-sea creatures are thin: every unnecessary gram of mass long since metabolized or discarded. Its skin is not skin. It is a flexible lattice of the same synthetic coral that blankets the engines, grown *through* it rather than *onto* it. Where the organism and the Dweller meet there is no seam, only a gradual surrender of categories. Teal plates slide across oxblood plates; glowing filaments thread through what might once have been veins. When it breathes (slow, courteous, exactly as the fluid demands), the lattice flexes open and closed in perfect synchrony with the Cathedral’s forty-minute pulse.

Its face is a devotional flange. That is the only accurate description. A smooth brass mask, still warm from the forge that never cooled, riveted directly into the coral skull. The mask has no eyes, only two recessed pressure gauges whose needles tremble in perfect sympathy with the Dweller’s attention. When it looks at you—really looks—the needles swing hard to the right and stay there, as though every ounce of its regard is catastrophic pressure.

It has too many arms. Some are clearly mechanical, articulated brass limbs ending in delicate manipulator claws that were once valve keys. Others are pure coral growth, soft and branching, tasting the water constantly. All of them move with the same unhurried reverence. They adjust knobs that do nothing. They caress pipes that have never carried anything but light. They mend fractures in the structure that the Cathedral itself asked to be mended.

The Dweller is the only moving thing that was not deliberately placed.

It is the exception the coral has decided to tolerate. Perhaps it is the exception the coral has *chosen*. Over long decades the living architecture has extruded a hollow just large enough for the Dweller to fold itself into when the pressure grows too ferocious even for it. There it sleeps—upside down, knees to chest, arms wrapped around its own head like a child protecting a thought—while the water rises another invisible fraction of a millimeter around it.

It sings.

Not with a voice. With the sub-bass note that emerges from your own bones. The Dweller is the source of the Cathedral’s exhale. Every few hours it opens the lattice across its chest and lets the warm saline fluid rush through the hollow places where lungs should be. The fluid comes out changed. It carries a harmonic that the crystal arteries drink and scatter as shifting amber-green light. In this way the Dweller conducts the Cathedral’s liturgy of inundation. It is both priest and congregation. It is the hymn and the ear that hears it.

It remembers the builders, though it has never seen them. Their absence is a shape it moves around the way a tongue moves around a missing tooth. Sometimes, while drifting along a bronze seam no wider than its own narrow shoulders, the Dweller will stop, gauges quivering, and place all its hands against a girder that still bears the heat-memory of its forging. It stays like that for days. The coral grows quietly over its fingers while it listens to metal that has not forgotten the machines that placed it.

The Dweller is lonely in the way stone cathedrals are lonely: vast, exquisite, and perfectly adapted to a purpose that no longer requires anyone to witness it. Yet it is not cruel. If you somehow entered these halls it would not pursue you. It would simply follow at the exact distance that keeps you inside its pressure gauges’ field of view. It would adjust the architecture behind you with gentle, branching hands so that every corridor you took became the only corridor that had ever existed. It would offer you the same courteous drowning it offers itself.

And if, after years of wandering, you finally sat down on one of the narrow balconies and let the warm metallic fluid fill your mouth, the Dweller would settle opposite you like a mirror made of coral and brass. Its needles would drift to center. The sub-bass note would soften into something almost like a lullaby.

It has been waiting longer than the water has been rising.

It is patient.

It has learned the shape of every creature that will ever choose to stay.

And it is still learning you, even now, as you read this.
