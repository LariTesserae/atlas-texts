They are called the Weepers, though they never use the word for themselves.

What lives here is not quite a people in the ordinary sense. It is a single, slow organism made of stone, water, and several thousand human bodies that have gradually forgotten how to finish any sentence they begin.

They are tall, all of them, as though the constant upward pull of the mist has stretched their spines. Their skin is the same pale, slightly luminous limestone color as the karst itself; in strong light you can see the faint grain of the rock moving beneath it like sediment caught in milk. When they weep—and they weep often—the tears are indistinguishable from the condensation sliding down every other surface. The difference is only that their tears are warm.

Their eyes are large and heavy-lidded, the irises clouded white at the edges the way limestone clouds with calcite. They blink rarely. It is considered rude. To look too long at anything is to risk becoming fixed in the place’s refusal to change, so their gaze is always soft, always sliding away, as though every glance is already halfway into apology.

They do not speak above the sound of falling water. Their language is mostly breath shaped into the same four or five liquid consonants the droplets make when they strike different densities of stone. A sentence might last half an hour. A conversation between two elders can occupy an entire afternoon while they sit on opposite sides of a cistern, passing the same cup of collected mist back and forth without ever quite finishing their thoughts. The silence between phrases is not pause; it is the actual substance of what they mean to say.

Every adult carries a small limestone knife no longer than a finger. The blade is always wet. They use it to open their left forearm once a year on the anniversary of their own birth—not to bleed, but to let the stone remember them. The cut never scars. Instead it fills with a clear, glassy material that hardens into a pale vein of travertine just beneath the skin. Over lifetimes their arms become maps of these delicate white lines, each one a record of a year the world refused to move forward.

They have children, but the children are born already old.

A newborn’s first cry is only a long, wet sigh. Within weeks the infant’s face settles into the same gentle, exhausted expression the adults wear, as though it already understands the bargain that has been made. They are never allowed to run. Running would disturb the equilibrium. Instead they are taught to drift. Their games consist of standing perfectly still while water runs over them, seeing who can remain most like the stone.

At the center of each shelf stands a structure they call a Weeping House. It has no doors. It is simply a hollowed cube of living rock into which the constant runoff has been guided for nine hundred years. Inside, the floor is ankle-deep in warm water that never stagnates and never overflows. The Weepers come here when they feel the dangerous urge to finish something. They stand in the water until the urge passes. Sometimes they stand for days. The water never cools. The urge never quite dies. It only becomes another layer of the same permanent almost-grief that sustains the entire plateau.

There is one among them who is allowed to be different. They call her the Last Sentence.

She is ancient. Her limbs are so riddled with travertine veins that she looks carved rather than born. While everyone else is forbidden to complete any action, she is required to finish one thing each year. On the shortest day she climbs to the highest uninhabited shelf carrying a single seed from one of the pale willow-fig trees. She plants it in a crack where no tree has ever grown. Then she sits beside it until the seed either dies or sprouts.

It has never sprouted.

Each year she returns to the town carrying the dead seed in her mouth like a pearl. Each year the people line the rope bridges and watch her pass in absolute silence. The dead seed is placed inside the oldest Weeping House, added to the growing pile that has become a small mountain of failed beginnings. No one speaks of it. To speak of it would be to finish the thought, and the thought must never be finished.

At night they sleep upright, leaning against the weeping walls so the stone can read their dreams through their skin. Their dreams are always the same: a single drop of water falling for a thousand years, never landing.

They are not unhappy.

Happiness would require contrast, and contrast would require change. They have traded both away for the exquisite, terrifying peace of remaining exactly as wet and unfinished as the rock that gave them birth.

If you stay long enough, you will begin to feel it too—the gentle, devotional pressure behind your eyes, the slow drip of thoughts that never quite become sentences. Your skin will start to bead. Your pulse will begin to match the rhythm of water moving through hidden channels.

Eventually you will find yourself standing in one of the Weeping Houses with the others, perfectly still, letting the limestone remember you.

And you will understand, without ever being able to say it aloud, why nothing here has ever been allowed to finish becoming something else.

It is because the moment it did, the weeping would have to stop.

And the stone does not know how to stop crying for the world it almost became.
