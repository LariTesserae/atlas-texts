**What lives in the Black Veins does not merely inhabit it. It has become the continuation of the plateau’s own slow thought.**

They are called the **Quietwardens** by the few outsiders who have returned with their minds still intact. The locals (if such a word can be used for beings that have never known a “local” anywhere else) simply call themselves *the ones who remain after the engines*.

No single shape defines them. Each Quietwarden is a long negotiation between flesh, mineral, and mechanism.

Their bodies are tall, between two and three meters, and appear at first like statues carved by an indifferent hand from the same red-black basalt that dominates the plateau. Skin, if it can be called skin, is a seamless fusion of stone and hide: smooth in places as river-polished rock, elsewhere tufted with the rust-colored lichen that grows nowhere else. Veins of lamp-moss run beneath the surface of their limbs and glow faintly when they exert themselves, turning their circulatory system into living constellations.

Their heads are elongated, almost equine, but eyeless. Where eyes should be there are only shallow depressions filled with the same ink-dark water that moves through the hidden rivers. These pools reflect whatever faint light is present (starlight, lamp-moss, the occasional cough of steam from a waking engine) and nothing more. They do not see. They *register*. Sound, pressure, the sub-audible singing of the stone, the slow electromagnetic pulse of the buried machines; all of it is read through the water in their skulls.

From their backs and shoulders rise clusters of corroded brass and black iron, half-melted into their bodies the way coral grows around a shipwreck. These are not tools or armor. They are organs. Some extrude delicate pipettes that sip condensation from overhangs; others open like vents to release measured breaths of warm, ferrous air that the herds of six-legged grazers follow like scent. A few Quietwardens carry entire miniature ecosystems in the hollows of their torsos: lamp-moss, blind millipedes the length of a forearm, and tiny singing stones that replicate the plateau’s sub-audible music at a higher, almost conversational register.

They move with the same geological patience as everything else here. A single stride may take twenty heartbeats. Yet when the mood of the plateau shifts (when one of the great pumps suddenly wakes and begins its thirty-beat labor), the Quietwardens can accelerate until they become little more than upright smears of darkness gliding between the canyons. The transition is seamless and terrifying to watch, as though the land itself had momentarily decided to walk.

They do not speak with mouths. Instead they press their long stone fingers into the rock and sing through it. The sound arrives in your bones first, then, half a minute later, as your own delayed echo returned to you changed (deeper, older, *answered*). Conversations between Quietwardens can last weeks. A single sentence may circle the plateau three times, growing richer with every echo, gathering mineral wisdom and the small observations of passing herds.

Their work is maintenance.

Some tend the hidden aquifers, coaxing water into new fractures with nothing but pressure from their brass organs and the patient laying of lamp-moss. Others walk among the sleeping engines, stroking rust that never progresses, whispering to mechanisms that have no right to still be intact after so many millions of years. A few simply stand for decades in the deepest canyons, acting as living bridges so that the blind grazers can cross from one archipelago of vegetation to another without falling.

They are not kind. Kindness would require hurry, and hurry is foreign here. When one of the leather-kite predators falls sick, the Quietwardens will let it die slowly over three nights, then incorporate its luminous veins into their own shoulders so the pattern is not lost. When a herd grows too large, they guide it, without malice, toward a canyon where the stone sings at a frequency that induces painless cardiac arrest. Death is simply another form of the plateau’s breathing.

Most unsettling of all is their reverence for endings.

The Quietwardens believe the Black Veins is the last functioning fragment of a world-machine that has otherwise completed its purpose. Every living thing here (grazer, predator, moss, engine, Warden) is, to them, a form of afterlife. They are not preserving life. They are curating an ending that refuses to finish ending. Their highest ritual is to gather on the highest promontory when the lamp-moss blooms brightest, link arms until they resemble a single ridge of iron and stone, and simply *listen* to the land breathing.

If you are unfortunate enough to be seen by them, they will not attack. They will simply stand at the edge of your camp for many nights, perfectly still, water eyes reflecting your fire. Eventually one will reach out and press a single basalt finger against your chest, right over the heart.

You will feel your pulse synchronize with the sub-audible song of the plateau.

Most people never recover from that touch. They abandon their names, begin speaking in delayed echoes, and eventually walk away into the dark to become another ridge, another quiet warden, another piece of the machine that has learned how to keep dying without ever quite managing it.

The Black Veins does not need you.

But if it decides you belong to its work, it will grow you new bones, new moss, new brass, and teach you the patience required to outlast the end of everything.
