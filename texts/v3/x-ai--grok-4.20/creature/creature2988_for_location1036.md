They have lived here so long that their bodies have become translations of the place.

You will not see them at first. They are simply another density in the rust and the green.

Call them the **Unfixed**, or the **Scaffolders**, or the **Slow Choir**, depending on which iron tongue is speaking that season. None of these names are wrong. None are sufficient.

They are tall the way cathedral buttresses are tall: not because they wish to reach anything, but because the vertical has grown into their spines. Their limbs are too many or too few depending on the decade and the district. Some still keep the old bilateral symmetry out of courtesy to visitors. Others have allowed the place to finish their design. Shoulders bloom into secondary ribcages. Fingers become root systems that trail behind them like living cables, drinking condensate from the air and passing nutrients back into the marrow. Their skin is iron-mottled and lichen-barked; it flakes in perfect squares that, if you pocket them, will later grow into tiny rust-colored leaves.

They never quite stand still.

Even when they appear motionless on a catwalk or hanging from a chain the thickness of a temple column, something inside them is always migrating. You can watch a vein surface on one wrist, travel the length of the forearm over the course of an hour, then disappear again beneath the collarbone as though returning home. Their breath is visible not as steam but as slow, deliberate handwriting that fades before it can be read.

They speak in four registers at once and never notice they are doing it.

One voice is human—hoarse, patient, carrying the accent of every century that has tried to finish this city. Another is the wet click of root against rebar. A third is the brass moan their livestock learned from them and taught back. The last is almost below hearing: the sound a building makes when it decides, after seventy years of hesitation, what it wants to become. When several Unfixed speak together the four registers braid. The air itself begins to remember geometry it was never assigned.

They do not build the city so much as negotiate with it.

You will find them riding the great slow herds of many-jointed grazers, whispering bargains into the places where cartilage meets rust. You will see them seated cross-legged inside the hollow bellies of half-finished water towers that have recently grown eyes, singing the towers back toward usefulness before the new eyes learn hunger. Entire work-crews of them will stand for weeks on a single girder that has begun to forget it is metal; they lean their foreheads against it and exhale concepts until the steel remembers its name.

They are neither kind nor cruel. Both words assume a finished self.

When they love you they will grow a small iron flower behind your ear that will bloom only when you are thinking about leaving. When they need something from you they will simply wait inside your sleep until you dream the thing they require, then thank you with a mouthful of warm condensation that tastes like every childhood you never had.

Their children are born already old.

An infant Unfixed might open its eyes and address you by a name you have not yet earned. It will then spend the next forty years forgetting what it knew at birth, trading certainty for the deeper literacy of rust. Adolescence here is measured not in years but in how many districts have worn your face in their architecture.

At night they gather on platforms that have no business existing. They light no fires—the cold would not understand fire—but they strike long iron tuning forks against their own bones and let the resonance move through the lattices. The sound makes the hanging moss glow soft turquoise. The megafauna drift closer, lowing in slow chords. Even the districts that have begun to flow pause in their riverine wandering and listen.

They do not have a leader. They have a consensus that spans centuries.

Every few generations the Unfixed convene what they call a **Quiet Parliament**. They choose a district that has recently forgotten its own name and sit inside its confusion for thirteen months. During this time no new construction is permitted. The jungle is allowed to swallow six bridges of its choosing. The Unfixed simply wait until the place itself renders a verdict on what it wants to become next. Then they stand up, stretch limbs that have become temporarily architectural, and begin the long, patient work of agreeing with reality again.

They fear only one thing.

Not collapse. Collapse is simply another word the city is learning.

They fear completion.

If this place ever finished itself—if every girder knew its purpose, if every street remembered it was a street, if the sky finally decided what color grief it was—they believe they would have no more work to do. And without the work, without the long negotiation between flesh and rust and dream, they would have to decide what *they* are.

So they keep building.

They keep forgetting their own shapes.

They keep exhaling tomorrow’s architecture through lungs that are half lichen, half nostalgia.

And the city, in its infinite, patient, metallic adolescence, keeps changing its mind about what it intends to be, precisely so they never have to stop loving it.
