**The Cathedral of Thirst is inhabited by the Choir.**

They are not a people who settled here. They are what remains after settlement forgot itself.

No one can say exactly what the Choir looked like when they first arrived centuries ago. Their original bodies have been gently, relentlessly edited by the living cathedral. Every inhabitant is now a hybrid of mammal, vegetable, and slow current. Their skin has become a supple, pale celadon that matches the great stems; it is hairless, faintly photosynthetic, and perpetually damp. Where hair once grew, fine translucent rootlets now emerge, drifting in the fluid like the fronds of the seed-fish. These rootlets taste the water constantly, tasting iron, mint, time, and the mood of the plant itself.

They are tall by necessity. The pressure and the liquid make short limbs inefficient, so their skeletons have lengthened and lightened. Limbs are graceful, almost too long, with extra joints that let them fold like the branches of the cathedral when they rest. Fingers and toes end in soft, flattened pads that can both grip slick stems and photosynthesize when they spread flat against a leaf. Their ribs have widened and fused in places, turning the torso into a flexible bellows that moves in unconscious rhythm with the cathedral’s breathing cycles.

Faces are the strangest compromise. The jaw has softened and recessed; mouths are small, lipless, and usually kept closed because breathing is done as much through the skin as through the lungs. Noses have flattened to slits. Eyes have grown large, almost entirely black, with a faint green reflection like wet jade. The pupils are not round but slightly squared, an accidental echo of the ribbed vaults above. They do not blink often. Instead, a thin nictitating membrane drifts across the eye every few minutes, clearing away the constant film of nectar and microscopic life.

Most striking are the resonant sacs. Along the collarbones and down the sides of the neck, the Choir have developed smooth, bladder-like swellings of tough, translucent tissue. These are partly lung, partly vocal chamber, partly plant. When the cathedral sings its deep organ note, the Choir instinctively inflate these sacs and join it. Their voices are not “sung” so much as grown: long, mournful drones that feel as if they are being pulled up from the root mat itself. A single adult can sustain a note for nearly nine minutes without drawing breath.

They do not wear clothes. Clothing rots too quickly here. Instead they adorn themselves with living jewelry: bracelets of braided rhizome that continue to grow and tighten or loosen according to the wearer’s health; cloaks of fine hanging moss that trail behind them like slow green smoke; and, on ceremonial occasions, headdresses of flowering epiphytes that bloom only when the wearer is calm.

There is no hierarchy in the usual sense. Status is measured by how deeply one has allowed the cathedral to edit the body. The oldest members—those who have lived here for sixty or seventy years—are called the *Rooted*. Their legs have partially fused with the floor mat. They can no longer walk in the human sense; instead they drift upright, tethered by thick woody roots that trail beneath them like the trains of royal robes. Their minds have slowed to match the cathedral’s decades-long thoughts. They speak rarely, and when they do the words come out half-dissolved in vegetal moans. Younger Choir treat them with the same courteous reverence one might give to an especially ancient tree.

Daily life is almost entirely devotional and metabolic.

They spend hours drifting in the empty galleries, arranged in loose vertical choirs, simply listening. When the great bladders in the vaults contract, the Choir rise as one, inflate their resonance sacs, and answer. The act is simultaneously musical, sexual, and religious. During these convocations many of them release clouds of pale golden gametes into the fluid—half pollen, half sperm—that swirl in the slow currents until they find compatible partners drifting nearby. Conception is communal and unhurried. Children are born rarely and raised by the entire cathedral. Infants are placed in natural cradles high in the leaf-vaults where the fluid is slightly sweeter and warmer; they emerge years later already half-photosynthetic, already singing.

There is almost no violence. The few conflicts that arise are settled by *resonance duels*: two opponents drift facing each other and emit opposing frequencies until one is forced to drop out or fall unconscious from the pressure. The loser is usually carried by the winner to a healing column where the plant’s own tissue knits their bruises.

They have no written language. Instead they keep memory in the living architecture. Important events are carved as patterns of controlled growth along chosen stems—raised ridges, subtle color shifts, sequences of nodules. Over decades these patterns become part of the cathedral’s own body. To read the history of the Choir, one must press one’s bare skin against the wall and wait for the slow pulse of the plant to teach the story through pressure and taste.

The Choir do not fear death. When an elder feels the time approaching, they choose a column and press their back against it. The plant responds. Over weeks or months the stem slowly grows around them, incorporating flesh and bone into new living wood. The body does not rot; it becomes architectural. The final expression of the dying person—a last sustained note—remains trapped inside the column as a faint, perpetual vibration that can be felt by anyone who places an ear against the bark for long enough.

Only the very young still ask what lies beyond the sealed canopy miles above. The adults answer with a gentle, resonant chord that means, simultaneously:

*We were once dry.*  
*We no longer remember why that mattered.*  
*Listen.*

And the child, already growing fine rootlets behind the ears, listens.

They are content.

After all, the Cathedral of Thirst has outlived every intention its builders once had.

So have they.
