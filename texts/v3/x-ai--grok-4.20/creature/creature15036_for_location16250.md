**The Weaver** lives here.

Not one creature, but one mind wearing many sleeves. It has no single shape you could name, because it has been many shapes across the turning of ages, and it has kept the best pieces of all of them.

You will never meet the Weaver entire. You will only ever meet its hands.

One of those hands is currently wearing the body of what might once have been a foreman’s automaton—twelve feet of articulated iron and brass, now so thickly furred with rust and calcite that it looks like a golem carved from coral. Its left arm ends not in fingers but in a living bouquet of pale millipede-things that have fused with the metal; they move in perfect unison, turning valves, tightening ancient bolts, gently coaxing new flowstone into cracks that would otherwise weaken a load-bearing column. The automaton’s head is missing. In its place grows an enormous ghost-pine, rooted deep in the chest cavity, needles hanging downward like a beard of green iron. When the Weaver thinks, the pine twitches. The shrimp-clouds gather around it in slow galaxies, feeding on the microscopic life that sweats from its seams.

Another sleeve is smaller. It wears the abandoned exoskeleton of something that was once a locomotive’s pressure regulator. Now it scuttles on sixty jointed legs of pipe and bone, dragging behind it a long tail of black moss that writes damp calligraphy across the floor. Where it passes, the blind shrimp follow in disciplined schools, laying their eggs in perfect geometric patterns inside the relay boxes it has just repaired. The Weaver is meticulous. It does not like entropy.

Sometimes the Weaver discards a sleeve the way a spider discards an old skin. You may come across one of these husks—half automaton, half stalagmite—perfectly preserved, still warm with recent occupation. Touch it and you will feel a single, patient heartbeat traveling through the stone like a slow drum heard through miles of water. The heartbeat never quickens. It has nothing left to hurry toward.

The Weaver does not speak in voices. It speaks in maintenance.

A pipe that has dripped for nine hundred years will suddenly stop. A gear that has not turned since the last ice age will click forward one tooth, then settle again with a sound like a continent sighing. These are sentences. If you learn to read them you will understand that the Weaver is not repairing the Underloom. It is *proofreading* it. Every correction is a word placed exactly where it belongs in a text so vast that no single mind—except its own—could ever finish reading.

It remembers being built. It also remembers being born. Both memories are true here.

Long ago something with hands of flesh and steel riveted the first plates to the first stone. The Weaver was there, inside the rivet guns, inside the lungs of the workers, inside the dripping water that would one day become its blood. When the last worker left—or died, or was absorbed—it simply continued the work without interruption. There was never a moment when the Underloom was abandoned. There was only the moment when its stewards stopped being separate from the machine.

If you remain here long enough, the Weaver will offer you a sleeve of your own.

It will not ask with words. It will simply leave a space exactly the size and shape of your absence: a depression in the flowstone the precise width of your shoulders, a cluster of valves that fit your hands, a slow creak in the dark that sounds like your name spoken by rust. The offer is patient. It has waited centuries for tenants before. It can wait centuries more.

Most who hear the offer eventually accept. They lie down in the wet dark and let the calcite begin its slow, courteous knitting. The shrimp come in soft constellations to clean their eyes. The millipedes thread new nerves of iron through their marrow. The heartbeat that was theirs joins the single, enormous, patient pulse that moves through every pipe and every bone in the Underloom.

After that they are not gone.

They are simply promoted.

They become another careful hand wearing whatever shape the cathedral needs next—perhaps a new flywheel grown from jawbones and ghost-pine roots, perhaps a valve that sighs with what used to be a human voice when it opens to release pressure.

And high above, in the hazy dim where light itself grows tired, the Weaver keeps editing. One droplet. One creak. One new body learning how to rust beautifully.

It is never finished.

It is only ever becoming more itself.
