**The Sweeper of the Weeping Stone** lives here.

She has no true name anymore. The few who once knew it are dissolved into the flowstone, their calcium and memory long since incorporated into the slow, fleshy pillars. She calls herself simply the Sweeper, though even that word has begun to lose meaning. After so many centuries the distinction between her and the chamber has grown porous.

She is tall, or was once. Time and the constant humidity have rounded her the way they round every other edge here. Her shoulders slope like worn travertine; her spine has taken on the gentle S-curve of a flowstone curtain. Skin the color of wet bone clings to her in translucent sheets, perpetually beaded, perpetually shining. Where the skin has grown too thin the faint purple and orange of the bacterial films show through, staining her ribs and collarbones in living geological patterns. Her hair—if it can still be called hair—hangs in heavy, mineral-saturated ropes that drip constantly. Each rope ends in a single perfect droplet that never quite falls, as though the chamber itself refuses to let anything truly leave.

She moves only when the music of the drips tells her it is time.

Most hours she simply stands inside the narrow gap between two fused pillars, arms raised, palms turned outward, becoming another mineral body among the rest. Water sheets over her. Bacteria bloom in the warm hollows of her armpits and the small of her back. She breathes so slowly that a single inhalation lasts nearly a minute; the exhalation is even longer. In this state she is almost indistinguishable from the stone—another soft, dripping pleat in the architecture of the Underchamber.

But when the irregular rhythm of falling water changes—when three particular drips strike their resonant bowls in the precise sequence that has not occurred for decades—she stirs.

Then the Sweeper walks.

She walks the same path she has walked for four thousand years: a slow, counterclockwise circuit that never quite completes. Her bare feet make no sound; the wet stone drinks each step. In her right hand she carries a thing that was once a human jawbone and is now mostly travertine. With it she gently scrapes the bacterial films from the places where they have grown too thick, returning their pigment to the water. She does not clean. She merely moves. She is the chamber’s slow, deliberate circulation.

Sometimes she stops before the little ring of knee-high standing stones. There she lowers herself—joints sighing like settling limestone—until she sits on the basalt bench. The three slabs have conformed to her shape over the centuries; the stone and the woman have molded one another. In the shallow cups atop the standing stones she studies the trembling mirrors of water. Whatever she sees there never makes her smile or weep. Her face has forgotten both expressions. She only looks, for a very long time, while the golden constellations drift across her wet cheeks.

The pale salamander knows her. When she moves, it follows at a respectful distance, gills pulsing like tiny white banners. The blind isopods gather at the edge of her shadow as though even they, in their stone patience, find something worth waiting near.

She never speaks aloud. Instead the chamber speaks through her. When the mood of the dripping changes—when the warm lung-air grows fractionally heavier—she will open her mouth and the sound of water falling on water will pour out of her throat in perfect mimicry. She is the only creature here that can translate the chamber’s long, mineral thoughts into something almost like voice.

At rare intervals a single clear tear falls from her left eye. It is thicker than water. It falls with a sound deeper than any other drop in the Underchamber and leaves a tiny, permanent pearl of calcite where it strikes. These pearls are the only things in the entire basin that are truly new.

She has no desire to leave. The fissure far above is meaningless to her; she has not looked up in eight hundred years. The world beyond the Weeping Stone is too dry, too sharp, too hasty. Here everything has already reached its final, glistening form, and so has she.

The Sweeper does not guard the chamber.

She *is* its ongoing decision to remain exactly as it is—warm, saturated, gently fevered, and perfectly, eternally content to weep.

And when the last human memory has finally leached out of the broken clay lamp and become just another layer in the flowstone, she will still be here, standing between the fleshy pillars, arms raised, letting the water run over her until even the distinction between Sweeper and Stone grows too soft to matter.

Until then she tends the dripping.  
She listens to the ten-thousand-year rhythm.  
She waits, without impatience, for the next three drops to fall in the only sequence that can still move her.

And the Underchamber, in its slow mammalian breathing, is glad.
