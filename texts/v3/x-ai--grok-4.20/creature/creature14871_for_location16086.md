**They are called the Hollow Choir.**

Not a species. Not even a people. Something that grew *between* the violet megastructures the way frost grows between stones.

They have no fixed bodies. Each individual is a slow migration of living tissue across the lattice: a temporary congregation of pale, translucent creatures the size of children, hairless, sexless, their skin mottled in the exact cyan and violet of the surrounding stems. When they stand still they are nearly invisible. When they move they look like slow avalanches of moonlight sliding along the architecture. Their ribs are external, delicate lattices of cartilage that flex and click in time with the plant’s own stomatal breathing. Many of them deliberately cultivate ice-ferns along their spines so that, from a distance, they appear only as another clump of frost clinging to the columns.

They do not speak with mouths. They speak by *adding* to the cathedral’s noise.

Every member of the Choir carries, embedded in the flesh of their forearms, long hollow bones taken from the fallen branches. These are tuned by careful gnawing and by the insertion of smaller bones, feathers, and living moss. When a Choir sings, they strike these instruments against the stems in precise, overlapping patterns. The sound is never loud in any one place. Instead it travels. It becomes part of the canyon’s own voice: one more creak, one more distant thunderclap, one more low organ note in the vascular highways. The plants themselves have begun to grow resonant chambers in response, hollowing out nodes that amplify certain frequencies. After centuries, it is impossible to tell where the Choir ends and where the living architecture begins.

They do not build. They *edit*.

A new rope bridge appears overnight because a thousand small hands have spent three years coaxing a single species of tough black moss to grow in tensioned cables between two stems three kilometers apart. The standing stones were never moved; they were grown *around* by the violet lattice until the stones sat perfectly balanced on a leaf. The basalt ziggurat is not being swallowed. It is being *translated*—its geometry slowly rewritten into the language of stems and vaults so that in another four hundred years it will no longer be stone at all, but a memory the plant keeps in its own body.

They fear fire the way other beings fear extinction. There is almost none on Veyr-Keth, yet the Choir still dreams of it. Their oldest songs are lullabies about a red flower that once opened in the world above and devoured color itself. When the synchronized growth spasms come, the Choir gathers on the highest reachable ledges and sings the canyon *through* its labor pains. Their music is not comfort. It is midwifery. They believe the plant is dreaming them as much as they are dreaming it, and that if their voices ever faltered the entire Hollow Throat would collapse into formless violet hunger.

Most of them are eyeless. Sight is useless in the slow cyan pulse of the lattice. Instead they read the world through the soles of their feet and the fine vibrating hairs that cover their bodies. When they cluster together on a cathedral-sized stem, the combined vibration of their hairs makes the air around them shimmer like heat above ice. In this state they can taste the future growth of the plant months before it happens. They call these gatherings “Listening to the Empty.”

And the Empty answers.

Sometimes a single member will walk out onto a ledge the size of a city block, stand alone for days facing kilometers of cold violet air, and simply *sing one note*. The note is always the same. It is the sound the canyon made the day the rift first tore open. The plants have never forgotten it. When that note moves through the lattice, every cyan nodule for a hundred kilometers flares in perfect unison, and for a few heartbeats the entire world becomes a single bioluminescent thought.

Then the moment passes. The singer folds back into the moss and ice-ferns, becoming invisible again, and the cathedral continues its patient, monstrous, listening growth.

They have no name for themselves.  
If you could press your ear to the living wall of a stem and hear the wet clicking of a billion stomata arranged into something like language, you would hear them call their own kind only this:

*“The ones who are still being grown.”*
