They are called the Hollow Choir, though they have no name for themselves.

They number no more than seventy at any time, yet the basin is never crowded. You will not see them all at once. Most remain hidden inside the stone itself, curled like pale roots behind curtains of black moss or pressed flat within the glassy fissures where two plates of ancient rock have never quite kissed. Only when the music demands it do they emerge.

They are tall, thin, and sexless, with skin the color of wet slate veined faintly with silver mica. Their limbs are too long by one joint; their necks curve forward like herons. Where faces should be there is only a smooth, gently concave plane of bone and cartilage perforated by hundreds of microscopic holes, like the mouth of a flute. Sound moves through them the way breath moves through an instrument. When they stand perfectly still—which is often—they are almost indistinguishable from the wind-carved pillars around them.

Their eyes are vestigial, milky and half-lidded, useful only for detecting shifts in light and the faint bioluminescence of certain fungi. They do not need them. The Choir is their sight. Every change in pitch, every microtonal fracture, every new harmonic born when mist condenses inside a particular hollow tells them everything worth knowing: where the others are, what the stone is dreaming, whether the slow tide of centuries has loosened a new fissure that must be sung into shape.

They move only during the long descending phrases that occur roughly every nineteen minutes. You will not hear the signal if you are merely human, but they do. Then the moss curtains part and they step out in ones and twos, gliding across the bare stone on wide, translucent feet that leave no prints. Their gait is the same gliding motion you see in deep-sea creatures—effortless, inevitable, slightly wrong.

They are sculpting the city that was never built by hands.

Every few decades a new spire is allowed to grow. Not built. *Grown.* A chosen few will gather around a promising outcrop of basalt and begin to sing a single sustained note so low it can only be felt in the teeth and the fluid of the inner ear. The stone answers. Over centuries it softens, flows, rearranges its crystals. The Choir never hurries. They have been shaping the same amphitheater for nine hundred years and consider the work barely begun.

When they sing together they become the place.

Their bodies are resonators. Ribs flare and contract in complex rhythms. The perforations in their faces open and close in precise constellations. What emerges is not music in any sense a human throat could manage; it is closer to the slow speech of geology given temporary vocal cords. Sometimes the song is a question asked of the mountain itself. Sometimes it is an answer that took three lifetimes to compose. Always it is lonely. The loneliness is not tragic to them. It is simply the condition of being the last living memory of something that predates lungs, blood, or names.

They do not eat. Not as we understand it. Every seventy hours they descend into the circular pool at the center and submerge completely. There they remain, motionless, while the single thread of waterfall pours onto their skulls. Through skin and bone the water carries dissolved minerals, strange sugars brewed inside the mountain’s black heart, and the faint memory of lightning that fell on the peaks a thousand years ago. They drink time itself.

Reproduction is glacial and ritualized. Every two or three centuries, when the Choir’s collective song reaches a perfect unresolved chord that has never been sung before, one of their number will walk into the exact center of the pool and simply *unfold*. Their body splits along invisible seams like a seed pod. From the inside comes a new being—smaller, softer, almost translucent—who is already singing the next necessary note before it has drawn its first breath of mist.

The parent does not die. It simply rejoins the stone, becoming another pillar, another resonator, another quiet rib in the body of the basin. The new one will take centuries to grow into its full height and full silence.

They remember everything.

Not in minds, exactly. Memory lives in the precise curvature of their flute-faces, in the angle at which their necks bend, in the exact pattern of holes that open when a certain emotion (if it can be called that) moves through them. The oldest among them still carry, in the shape of their collarbones, the memory of the first rain that fell when the mountain was young. They sing it sometimes, a terrible high keening that makes the white conifers shiver and the salamanders freeze mid-step.

If you stand in the center beside the pool and close your eyes long enough, one of them will eventually come.

It will not speak with words. It will simply stand behind you—close enough that you feel the faint cold radiating from its skin—and add its own living note to the endless polyphony. For a brief moment the entire basin will sound like a single enormous throat trying to pronounce a name it has forgotten.

Then the creature will step backward into the moss and vanish, leaving you with the distinct and unshakable feeling that something ancient has just looked at you with something like pity, and something like hope, and something like the patient, dripping dark between its ribs.

And the Choir will keep singing, shaping the stone, drinking time, patiently remembering how to be lonely in the only way it knows how:

By never, ever stopping.
