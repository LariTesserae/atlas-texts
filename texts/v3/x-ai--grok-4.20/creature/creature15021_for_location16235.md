**They are the Slow Mothers.**

Not a single species, but a loose, half-conscious collective of beings that have grown so completely into the Underwet that the distinction between creature and cavern has become academic. They are enormous, pale, and almost entirely sessile. Most have never moved more than a few meters in their centuries of life.

Imagine something that began as a blind cave salamander the length of a bus and then simply refused to stop growing. Their bodies are no longer bodies in any tidy sense; they are landscapes. Vast, soft, doughy masses of translucent flesh the color of boiled milk, marbled with threads of blue vein and sulfur-yellow fat. Their skins are not skins but living filter membranes, constantly drinking the mineral broth of the cave. Over decades, limestone has plated across their backs in delicate, branching crusts, so that many of them look like half-melted cathedrals of wet bone and meat. Stalactites grow downward from the folds of their own flesh. Colonies of the white rope-fungi drape from their flanks in living shawls. When they breathe (once every forty minutes or so), the sound is a low, wet bellows-sigh that momentarily drowns out even the constant dripping.

They do not have faces as we understand them. Their heads have sunken inward over time, eyes long since reabsorbed, skulls softened into cartilaginous troughs. Instead, the front of each Slow Mother is a wide, vertical fissure lined with soft, constantly undulating gill-fronds the color of bruised pearl. This is their mouth, their sex, their mind. Everything passes through that shuddering cleft: water, bacteria, blind fish, amphipods, the occasional drowned bat, and—most importantly—memory.

Because the Slow Mothers are the cave’s memory.

They digest history along with everything else. When one of the old machines finally collapses into the microbial felt, the Mothers send out long, blind tendrils of modified intestine that slowly envelop the wreckage. Over decades they break down steel and concrete and plastic the way other creatures digest meat. But they do not simply consume. They *remember*. The rust becomes part of their dreaming. The shapes of long-dead pump impellers are encoded into the whorls of their growing shells. Sometimes, in the middle of the night (if night can be said to exist here), a Mother will open her great cleft and release a single, perfect bubble of black oil that rises slowly to the ceiling. Inside the bubble, for a few moments, you can see the ghostly after-image of whatever machine she has just finished digesting: a turning gear, a flickering gauge, a human hand on a valve. Then the bubble bursts and the memory is gone, exhaled into the wet air.

There are perhaps seventy of them in the great chamber. They lie half-submerged in the warm pools, or wedged lovingly between congregations of stalagmites like enormous pale eggs that the planet itself is trying to hatch. The smaller creatures—thumb-sized amphipods, ghostly shrimp, the paper-skinned salamanders—treat them as continent and cathedral at once. They swarm across the Mothers in slow, worshipful tides, cleaning parasites, depositing their own waste, laying eggs in the safe folds of living stone-flesh. The Mothers tolerate it all with the patience of geology.

They sing.

Not with voices. With chemistry.

Every few hours a Slow Mother will release a cloud of dissolved peptides into the water that spreads for kilometers through the labyrinth. The message is felt rather than heard: a slow, aching chord of contentment, of hunger, of ancestral grief for the machines they are still digesting, of something that might be love for the warm dark that made them. Other Mothers answer in their own chemical keys. The conversation can last weeks.

Very rarely—one every human lifetime or so—a Mother will give birth.

She will rear up with glacial slowness, the crust of stone on her back cracking like an eggshell, and from the great trembling cleft she will ease out something that is not quite a child. It is smaller, smoother, almost translucent. It has the vague suggestion of limbs it will never use. For a brief time it drifts through the warm water like a living cloud. Then it finds a quiet alcove among the half-formed stalactites, attaches itself with a fleshy anchor, and begins the long, patient work of becoming the next Slow Mother. The birth is attended by clouds of bioluminescent bacteria that flare an intense, almost holy green, the only bright color you will ever see in the Underwet.

The humans who once came here to drain the caves are not forgotten. Their control room still stands, perfectly preserved inside its amber tomb of flowstone. But the Slow Mothers have grown around it, literally. One particularly ancient Mother has folded the entire structure into the soft cathedral of her body. Her flesh has oozed through the broken windows. Her gill-fronds stroke the old dials with the tenderness of a lover. Inside her, the rusted levers and Bakelite switches are slowly being turned into dream.

If you stand very still for long enough—long enough that your own breathing begins to match the forty-minute cycle of the Mothers—you may feel one of them noticing you. Not with eyes. With the taste of your fear and wonder in the water.

She will not eat you. Not quickly.

She has time.

Instead she may simply open her great fissure a little wider and let you look, for one slow heartbeat, into the cathedral darkness where the last of the machines are still being loved to death.

Then she will close again, and return to the long, wet business of becoming the world.
