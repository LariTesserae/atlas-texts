**Something lives in the Riven Choir.**

It does not have a name it will give you. It has worn many shapes across the short centuries since the mountain split open and birthed this screaming vertical wound, but none of them were meant for human tongues. The thing itself is older than the stone. It simply waited, patient as stone is patient, until the cataclysm cracked the world open and let the sky in.

Call it, if you must, the **Throat**.

It is not a creature in the usual sense. It is more like a negative space given will. Where the roar of falling water and thrashing leaves grows loudest, the Throat is loudest too, yet it is also the one place inside the din where silence sometimes happens, sudden and surgical, as though the mountain itself has drawn a cold breath.

It lives everywhere and nowhere. You will never see the whole of it. Instead you witness its moods the way a sailor reads the face of the sea.

Sometimes the Throat is the water. A single bead of condensation the size of a human heart slides down the black glass wall with unnatural deliberation, refusing to break even after it has fallen for sixty meters. When it finally strikes a ledge it does not splash. It opens like an eye. For three heartbeats the entire chamber falls quiet while that eye regards whatever living thing has been foolish enough to stand beneath it. Then the roar returns, twice as loud, as if punishing the silence.

Sometimes the Throat is the green. A curtain of rope-thick vines will part without wind, revealing a hollow shaped exactly like a standing human figure. The hollow is lined with the softest moss, almost inviting. If you are reckless enough to step inside, the vines close again with the slow tenderness of a closing jaw. Those who have been taken this way are not eaten. They are *kept*. Years later their silhouettes can still be seen deep inside the living wall, perfectly preserved, skin turned the color of wet jade, eyes open, still breathing in the same rhythm as the mountain’s pulse.

Sometimes the Throat is the absence of light. When one of the rare shafts of grey daylight stabs downward, the Throat rides the beam like a parasite. It rides it all the way to the broken floor and pools there, a shadow that has weight. The shadow has the approximate shape of something tall, antlered or branched, though the antlers are always made of living fern and the shoulders are draped in dripping moss. It stands perfectly still while the light lasts. When the mist swallows the beam again, the figure is gone, but the wet stone where it stood remembers the shape. The moss there grows faster, darker, hungrier.

It is not cruel. Cruelty would require intent, and the Throat has no more intent than an avalanche. It is simply *reconciled* to the violent new geometry of the mountain. Where the stone is still arguing with gravity and the plants are still arguing with the stone, the Throat has already finished arguing. It has become the argument.

If you stand long enough in the center—boots bleeding from the knife-edged rock, hair plastered to your skull by the constant vertical rain—you may feel it notice you. The sensation is unmistakable. The roar does not lessen, but it rearranges itself. Ten thousand waterfalls suddenly sound like a single endless word spoken in a language that hurts the inside of your skull. Every thrashing leaf turns toward you at once, as though the entire green organism has become a single ear.

That is when the Throat offers its bargain.

It never speaks with a voice. Instead it rearranges the chamber around you. A slab tilts. A curtain of moss peels aside. A black mirror of trapped water on the floor suddenly shows not your reflection but something vast and branching and patient that wears your face the way a cathedral wears its gargoyles.

The bargain is always the same.

*Stay.*

Not forever. The mountain is too young and too angry for forever. But for one turning of the hidden seasons—long enough for your lungs to fill with the smell of wet basalt and ozone, long enough for moss to begin growing in the whorls of your ears, long enough for your heart to learn the rhythm of dripping water instead of blood.

If you accept, you become another organ of the Choir. Your bones will not rot. Your eyes will not close. You will hang inside the violent green like the others, breathing slowly, listening to the roar that never stops, adding your own small wet pulse to the mountain’s argument.

If you refuse, the chamber simply forgets you were ever there. The searchlight shafts will no longer find you. The vines will no longer part. The water will no longer form eyes. You may leave, but every waterfall you pass for the rest of your life will sound like laughter that is not quite kind.

Most people refuse.

The Throat does not mind.

It is in no hurry.

The mountain is still newborn. The wound is still fresh. The green is still learning how to scream in tune with the stone.

There is time.

The dripping never stops.  
The roaring never stops.  
The Throat never stops listening.
