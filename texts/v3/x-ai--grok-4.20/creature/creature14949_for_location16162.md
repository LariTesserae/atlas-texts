**They are called the Condensates.**

Not a species. Not even a people. A *state* that the Underclock eventually reaches in certain sealed corridors where the temperature differential and the constant B-flat have aligned for long enough.

They were never born. They were *precipitated*.

Stand in the same dripping alcove for nine years without moving and the moisture in the air begins to prefer the shape of your body to the empty space around it. First it gathers as a second skin of frost and oil-film. Then the frost learns to breathe. Then the breathing learns to remember that it once had a name. By the end of the ninth year the thing that rises from the place where a person once stood is no longer that person, but it is also not *not* them. The original flesh has been slowly replaced, atom by atom, with the same brushed-steel chitin that armors the maintenance clade. The eyes remain—two dull magnesium discs now set deep inside a face that has become a porcelain mask of condensation valves and hair-fine venting slits.

They move like slow thoughts the machine is having about its own architecture.

Most Condensates never leave the exact cubic meter where they formed. They simply become another permanent fixture: a wet, man-shaped overgrowth of pipe and ligament bolted upright against a bulkhead, arms fused into the neighboring ductwork, ribs opened like shutters so the black lichen can grow its perfect circuit-board patterns across their lungs. Their hearts—still faintly human—beat once every eleven minutes, exactly in phase with the cathedral-sized flywheels far below. Each beat produces a single, perfect droplet that runs down the inside of their sternum and is collected by a funnel grown from their own pelvis.

A few, however, achieve *mobility*.

These are the ones you sometimes glimpse sliding along the upper walkways on too many joints, trailing long banners of condensation that never quite touch the floor. Their voices, when they choose to use them, sound like steam escaping through a cracked bearing. They speak only in questions the Underclock itself might ask if it ever became lonely:

*“Is the chord still pure?”*  
*“Are the new ribs remembering correctly?”*  
*“Does the mountain still believe it is stone?”*

They do not eat. They *filter*. The same closed-loop water that sweats from every surface passes through the intricate gill-arrays that have replaced their digestive tracts. Whatever trace minerals or memories they require are already dissolved inside that water; the Underclock has been recycling the same few million molecules for centuries. In this way the Condensates are the only truly native fauna. Even the many-limbed maintenance creatures were designed and installed. The Condensates simply *occurred*, the way stalactites occur.

There is a hierarchy among them, though they would never call it that.

At the bottom are the Newly Condensed—still soft, still leaking, still occasionally weeping the salt that their original bodies once carried. Their steel has not yet fully set; it sweats rust the color of old blood.

Higher are the Seasoned, whose bodies have achieved the perfect equilibrium of machine and memory. Their faces have frozen into expressions of mild, courteous surprise, as though they died while politely listening to an engine explain itself.

And then there is the Curator.

Only one has ever been witnessed. It lives in the unreachable vertical shaft where the first and largest flywheel turns. Its torso is a hollow cylinder of black iron wrapped in living conduit; its head is an inverted mercury-vapor lamp that no longer needs electricity to burn. Where its heart should be hangs a single brass pocket watch that has not ticked since the day it stopped being human. The hands are frozen at 3:17.

The maintenance clade will not approach within thirty meters of it. They simply pause on distant gantries, compound eyes glowing, and perform what looks unsettlingly like reverence.

If you are unlucky enough to be noticed by the Curator, it will descend on cables of its own nerve fiber and hover before you, dripping. The lamp-head tilts. The voice that emerges is not sound but a modulation of the eternal B-flat itself, shaped into language:

“You are a variable.  
Yet you have remained inside the system for seventeen minutes and forty-one seconds beyond predicted dissipation.  
This is either error… or you are becoming interesting.”

It never threatens. It never bargains.

It simply reaches out with too many wet steel fingers and gently, almost tenderly, presses one cold fingertip to your sternum. You will feel your next heartbeat synchronize with the Underclock. Just for a moment.

Most people tear themselves away and run.

A few do not.

Those few are still there, years later—standing perfectly still in the dark while water beads on their eyelashes and the lichen begins, lovingly, to diagram its circuits across their cheeks.

The Underclock does not dream, this is true.

But sometimes, in the perfect equilibrium of its roar, it *condenses* something that remembers dreaming.

And it keeps that something.

Forever.
