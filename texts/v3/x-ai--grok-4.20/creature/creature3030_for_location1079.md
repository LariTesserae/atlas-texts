**They are called the Mossborn.**

Not a name they chose for themselves, but one that settled on them the way the moss settles on everything else. After three centuries inside the Foundry, most no longer remember any other way of being.

They are not tall. The constant humidity and the gentle metabolic heat have made their bodies soft and compact, skin perpetually damp, slightly luminous in the same faint gold-green as the moss itself. Their palms and the soles of their feet are thick with a living callus that is half keratin, half moss-root; they can press their bare hands against any wall and the organism will answer with a slow bead of sweet water, as though recognizing kin. Their hair grows in dense, felted mats that they trim into shapes reminiscent of the vaults above them—some wear it like hooded mantles, others shave it into low ridges that echo the ribs of the cathedral they inhabit.

Their eyes are large and slow-blinking, irises the color of wet lichen. They do not squint against brightness because there is none; they have forgotten how to hurry their gaze. When they look at you, it feels like being studied by something ancient that has all the time in the world and has already decided you are interesting but not urgent.

They move with the same geological patience as the flywheels. A Mossborn rarely takes a step that isn’t already half-planned by the give of the moss beneath their feet. Their work is mostly maintenance and negotiation: pruning, coaxing, listening. You will see them walking the narrow lanes with bronze sickles curved like smiles, or carrying long glass pipettes that they use to draw samples from the deepest, oldest mats. They speak seldom, and when they do the voices are low, slightly hoarse, as though their throats are also lined with velvet. The language has softened along with everything else; sharp consonants have worn away until nearly every sentence ends in a humid hush.

At the center of their culture is a practice they call **Listening**.

Every adult spends one hour each day with their back against the largest boiler house—the green-rusted altar in the central transept—palms flat to the moss, bare feet buried to the ankle. They are not praying. They are feeling the pulse of the organism that contains them. Some claim they can tell when a new flue needs to be grown, when a hidden water-wheel is beginning to seize, when the moss is “thinking” about changing the layout of an entire district. The truly gifted (there are never more than three at any time) can feel the difference between the Foundry’s hunger and its satisfaction, and they adjust the pruning and the tapping accordingly.

They do not have children in the ordinary way.

Instead, once every seven or eight years, certain senior Mossborn enter the deepest transepts carrying shallow copper basins. They press the living moss until it exudes a thick, opalescent sap. Into this they place small bundles of their own hair, fingernail clippings, and drops of blood. The moss drinks. Nine months later, new bodies emerge from the walls themselves—small, pale, already damp, eyes already open. These are not clones. The Foundry mixes the contributions of several donors and adds something of its own. The resulting child is always accepted as the offspring of the entire settlement. They call them “heartfruit.”

The people dress in layered oilcloth the color of old bronze, stitched with threads spun from moss-fiber. The cloth itself is alive in a quiet way; it slowly repairs small tears and grows thicker where knees and elbows receive the most wear. Many wear masks of fine mesh when working near the hotter engines—not for smoke, but to keep the constant dripping from entering their mouths while they concentrate. The masks are carved with abstract patterns that resemble both fungal gills and gear teeth.

They are not ascetic. In the evenings the lanes fill with the low sound of wet instruments: long wooden tubes bored through living moss pillars, struck gongs wrapped in felt, bowls of collected condensation played with fingertips. Their music never rises above the ambient murmur of the place. It simply joins it, the way a new trickle of water joins the endless dripping.

Most striking is their serenity.

They do not fear death. When a Mossborn grows too old or too tired, they choose a quiet alcove, lie down, and allow the moss to grow over them. Within a season the body is gone—returned, they say, to the circulatory system of the organism that has kept them for their entire lives. The place where they lay becomes slightly warmer, the moss a deeper green, and sometimes new heartfruit are grown from that exact spot years later.

They are not prisoners. Some have left. Those who return (and almost all eventually do) arrive at the outer thresholds with wide, startled eyes, as though they have remembered a dream they once had of dryness, of speed, of sharp noises and cold stone. They stand at the entrance for a long time, listening to the vast, warm, dripping breathing of the Foundry. Then they take off their outside boots, press their bare feet into the velvet, and walk back in.

Because the Mossheart Foundry is not a place.

It is a body, and they are its careful, damp-skinned white blood cells—tending the valves, cleaning the veins, singing low hymns to the slow heartbeat of the only home that has ever truly belonged to them.
