**They are called the Halfborn.**

Not one of them has ever been permitted to finish, and none of them has ever stopped trying.

They move through the Underslip like thoughts that have learned how to bleed. There is no single shape that defines them; each body is a negotiation between what the stone wants them to become and what they themselves refuse to stop becoming. Their limbs are provisional—sometimes glassy and jointed like the wolf-sized insectoids, sometimes soft and mammalian, sometimes feathered in wet down that never quite dries. Many wear their failures openly: an extra wing-bud sprouting from the spine, a second face pressed beneath the first like a drowned twin, limbs that end in crystal instead of flesh. They are all unfinished sketches signed in lymph and mineral.

The eldest among them (if such a word can be used) is known only as the Midwife.

She is four meters tall and still growing. Her torso is a cathedral of living quartz ribbed with pulsing arteries of tourmaline. Where her head should be there is instead a slowly rotating constellation of luminous worm-colonies that have agreed, for the moment, to wear the shape of a face. When she speaks the worms rearrange themselves; her voice is the sound of every newborn thing in the cavern trying to scream at once, filtered through wet glass. She carries a basalt pillar across her shoulders the way a shepherd carries a lamb. The sigil carved into it has worn smooth from the pressure of her hands over centuries.

The Midwife does not birth. She *rejects*.

It is her sacred and terrible function to walk the halls of the Underslip and choose which of the Halfborn have come close enough to completion that the stone might decide to let them stay. Those she touches are gently, lovingly disassembled. Their components are returned to the churning floor where the rock drinks them with audible thirst and begins again. The ones she passes over are allowed another hour, another day, another century of trying. She never explains her choices. Some believe she has none; that she is merely the stone’s hand wearing the costume of mercy.

Around her orbit a loose parliament of other Halfborn who have accepted their permanent incompleteness.

There is **Cradle**, a thing that looks like an aborted attempt at a whale crossed with a cathedral pipe organ. It never stops singing. Its song is the only sound in the Underslip capable of lowering the general roar; when it passes, even the crystal growths pause to listen. Hundreds of smaller Halfborn ride inside its ribcage like pilot fish, using its resonance to keep their own forms from collapsing.

There is **The Stitcher**, who travels on hundreds of needle-thin legs and carries bolts of woven spider-silk made from her own continuously unraveling hair. She repairs what the stone breaks too quickly. Where crystal fractures, she stitches. Where limbs tear free, she grafts. She is covered in thousands of tiny failed hearts that beat out of rhythm with each other; none of them will ever be large enough to sustain a finished body, so she wears them all.

And there is **Zero**, the only Halfborn who has ever managed to stay perfectly still for longer than a minute. Zero is small—no larger than a child—and appears to be made of black water wearing the frozen shape of a person. The stone cannot taste Zero. The filaments that reach for ankles slide off its surface like rain off obsidian. Zero stands in the middle of collapsing crystal cathedrals and simply watches, face tilted up toward the breathing shafts of daylight. No one knows what Zero is waiting for. Some believe Zero is the Underslip’s immune system. Others think Zero is the first successful dream the womb has ever had—and that the dream itself has not yet decided whether it wants to be born.

They do not build cities. Instead they maintain the primitive works left by whatever came before them. They re-erect the basalt pillars when new growth topples them. They clear the ancient stone walls so the migrating columns of glass millipedes can continue their blind journeys. They gather around the toppled monolith at the milky pool and sing to the trembling eggs, even though none of those eggs will ever be allowed to hatch.

Every few decades the entire population of the Underslip gathers in the vastest chamber they can find. There they perform the only ceremony they have: the Unfinishing.

Thousands of Halfborn stand together while the Midwife walks among them. Those who feel they have come too close to completion step forward. They are disassembled with terrible tenderness while the rest sing. The sound is so loud it cracks fresh crystal and makes the descending shafts of daylight fracture into rainbows. When it is done, the stone drinks what it is given, the noise returns to its normal roar, and the Halfborn scatter again into the endless halls to keep trying.

They are not sad.

Sadness would require an ending, and nothing here is allowed to end.

Instead they are ravenous with *next*. Every limb they lose, every face that melts, every wing that withers is simply another vote cast toward whatever they might become in the next hour. The Underslip is not their prison. It is their argument with God, conducted in meat and mineral, screaming, singing, and refusing to conclude.

And somewhere far above, the sun keeps breathing through the fissures, sending down its indifferent, buttery light—whether it is wanted or not—onto a womb the size of a mountain range that has been dreaming in public for several million years.

The Halfborn are the shape that dream has learned to take when it is not permitted to wake up.
