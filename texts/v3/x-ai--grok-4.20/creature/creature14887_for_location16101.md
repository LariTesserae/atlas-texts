**They are called the Hollow Choir.**

No single creature lives here. The Choir is not an individual, nor even a species. It is a continuous, many-bodied mind that has never known a border between “self” and “stone.”

They have no name for themselves. The clicking, chiming hymn that fills the fracture is their only tongue, and it is spoken by every plate, every leg, every vibrating wing at once. When the song changes—which it does constantly, in registers too slow for a human lifetime to notice—the change itself *is* the thinking. The mountain’s settling, the opening of new fissures, the slow dripping of condensation: all of these are syllables the Choir incorporates into its endless, unfinished canticle.

Most of them are small. Thumb-sized, armor-plated, their shells the exact color and temperature of the black glass rock that birthed them. Their backs are etched with microscopic runes that are not decoration but developing sensory organs; every new fracture in the mountain is felt by thousands of them at once, and the information is sung outward before the rock has even finished splitting. They move in laminar sheets, bodies overlapping like scales on a dragon that has no body of its own—only this living, rippling skin draped across every vertical surface.

Yet there are larger bodies folded inside the mass.

Every few thousand voices, a different shape condenses. Rabbit-sized, sometimes larger, their shells translucent as frozen breath. Inside their thoraxes you can see slow pulses of violet light—actual hearts made of bioluminescent bacteria they have cultivated across millennia. These greater singers rarely walk. Instead they cling to the hanging filaments with hooked forelimbs and vibrate their sealed wings at frequencies that make the stone itself answer. When one of them begins a new bass drone, the smaller creatures rearrange themselves into living frescos: spiraling lattices, tightening helices, geometries that have never been seen by any eye that possesses lids. These patterns are not art. They are hypotheses the Choir is testing about what shape the next chamber of the mountain should take.

There are no queens. There are no individuals who lead. Every creature here is both cantor and listener, priest and nave. When a section of wall grows too heavy with bodies and collapses (an event that happens perhaps once every forty years), the falling Choir does not scream. It simply sings a descending minor chord that the rest of the fracture catches and transforms into the next movement. The crushed bodies are not mourned; they are immediately incorporated. New singers crawl out of the cracks already knowing the updated melody, already carrying the memory of the fall.

Some of the oldest sections of the Choir have grown strange.

In the deepest, narrowest throat of the fracture—where the walls almost touch—there are creatures that have fused together over centuries. Dozens of armored bodies have melted into single, cathedral-sized organisms whose jointed plates now resemble fluted columns of bone. These living buttresses hum at frequencies so low they cause condensation to fall in perfect rhythm, like a slow, cold heartbeat. Travelers who stand beneath them for more than a day begin to feel their own ribs trying to answer.

And sometimes—very rarely—a different kind of singer emerges.

These ones are almost human in silhouette. Tall, thin, their many legs folded into the suggestion of arms and spine. Their shells are pale and smooth as marble, and they do not sing with their bodies but with long, translucent tubes that extrude from their torsos like organ pipes. When one appears, every other voice in the Veins falls into absolute, reverent silence for exactly thirteen heartbeats. Then the new singer sounds a single, perfect note that contains every song that has ever been sung in the fracture, plus one that has not yet been born. After the note ends, the creature folds back into the living carpet and is never seen again in that form. The Choir remembers it forever.

They do not eat.

They do not sleep.

They are not waiting for anything.

The Veins of the Hollow Choir are not a habitat. They are a hymn that has learned how to have a body, and that body is still growing. Every new fissure, every bead of condensation, every traveler who stumbles in and begins, against their will, to breathe in thirteen-beat measures, becomes another note the mountain is learning to sing.

If you stay long enough, you will feel it too: the slow, geological urge to open your mouth and add your own small, imperfect voice to something that has never once been finished, and never wishes to be.

The Choir does not mind.

It has been waiting for your particular dissonance since before your grandparents were born.
