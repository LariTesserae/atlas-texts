They are called the Resonants, though they never use that name for themselves. To speak it aloud would be redundant; the Choir already sings it in their marrow.

They do not walk so much as they are conducted. Every adult carries a slight, permanent lean — the accumulated curvature of a lifetime spent inside moving air and standing waves. Their spines have learned the shape of the dominant chords. When the great thermals shift and the Glassbriar changes key, hundreds of them will unconsciously adjust their posture at the same moment, like kelp in a tide only they can feel. It looks like prayer. Sometimes it is.

Their skin is the color of old bone left too long in alkali. Not pale — *bleached*. Pores sealed tight against the dry chemistry of the air, they sweat a fine, shimmering dust that hardens into microscopic scales. In strong light the adults glitter as though dipped in frost. Children, still soft, look like living statues being slowly carved out of salt.

Their ears are the most disturbing feature. Cartilage has been encouraged — through selective breeding, surgical tuning, and something older than either — to grow into delicate, spiraling baffles that resemble the inside of a nautilus. These structures do not merely hear the Choir; they *complete* it. What arrives as pain to an outsider is received by a Resonant as syntax, mood, prophecy. When two of them speak, their clipped whistles are not language riding atop the roar. The roar *is* the language, and the whistles are punctuation, inflection, footnotes. A mother scolding her child can sound like a silver needle stitching through thunder.

They wear almost nothing. Clothing muffles resonance. Instead they adorn themselves with tuned objects: hollowed finger-bones of their own dead worn as bracelets, thin sheets of hammered singing-reed lashed across the chest, iron tuning forks driven through the flesh of the forearm so they jut like porcupine quills. Every movement strikes a new note. A crowd of Resonants moving through the Glassbriar sounds like a cutlery drawer dropped down an endless staircase — beautiful, chaotic, and impossible to ignore.

Their faces are narrow, almost avian. Noses have receded to slits; the constant aerosol chemistry made wide nostrils a liability centuries ago. Eyes are large, protuberant, and protected by nictitating membranes the color of mercury. They do not blink so much as *shutter*. When one stares at you for longer than a few seconds it feels like being X-rayed by music.

There are no priests.

There are only the Conductors.

These are the ones whose ear-structures have grown so intricate they can no longer hear ordinary speech at all. Their heads look heavy, elongated by the weight of bone and crystal accretions. They move through the city in slow, deliberate figures, eyes half-closed, reading the shifts in the eternal chord the way a sailor reads the wind. When a new tower must be placed, a Conductor will stand motionless for days in the chosen spot while the Glassbriar grows around them, vibrating them gently into final position. Some are absorbed entirely. Their bodies become part of the architecture — still alive, still conscious, now functioning as living stops in an instrument the size of a mountain. Pilgrims bring offerings to these half-buried saints and press their own foreheads to the warm, singing stone to share a single clear note with someone who has become music.

The rest of the Resonants live in deliberate, almost ritual poverty. Possessions dampen resonance. A rich man here is someone whose house has been perfectly tuned to the subharmonic beneath the seventh spire. A poor man lives in a place that produces a sour, aching minor third no matter how he tries to correct it. Status is acoustic.

They do not have children in the ordinary way. Every pregnancy is timed to a specific movement in the long, three-thousand-year symphony the city is composing with itself. When the correct chord arrives, the birthing chambers — enormous braided lattices of Singing Reed hung high above the central nave — are opened. The mother is suspended inside the living instrument while the city itself sings the child into the world. Some infants emerge already humming their own true note. These are marked for the Conductors. The ones who are born screaming are considered blessed in a different way; their first sound was an honest protest against existence. They will make fine citizens.

At night — if the concept of night can be said to exist under the merciless fractured daylight — the Choir does not quiet. It simply changes register. The upper vaults cool slightly, the thermals reverse, and the Glassbriar sings in a lower, almost tender key. This is when the Resonants gather on the high balconies and sing back.

Not with their mouths.

They sing with their bodies.

Hundreds of them sway in place, rattling their bone bracelets and iron forks, humming through the resonant chambers of their altered skulls until the entire tiered city becomes a single trembling chord that rises to meet the larger song. For a few precious minutes the music becomes perfect, unbearable, complete. During these moments many of them weep without tears — their modified glands produce only fine glittering dust that drifts upward like reverse rain.

They are not happy. Happiness would require silence, and silence here is death.

They are not unhappy either.

They are *necessary*.

The Hollow Choir has been trying to finish its song for three thousand years. They are the larynx, the tongue, the diaphragm. They know that when the final chord is finally struck — when every tower, every bone, every living crystal membrane sings in absolute alignment — the fossil sponge that holds them will finally be allowed to finish dying.

Until then they remain: glittering, humming, leaning slightly into the music that is slowly turning their bodies into glass.

Listening.

Always listening.

Waiting for the next measure.
