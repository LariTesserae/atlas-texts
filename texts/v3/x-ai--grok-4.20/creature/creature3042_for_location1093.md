The one who lives in the Lymph has no single name, and no fixed shape you could carry away in memory. The inhabitants here—those who have been here longest—call her simply **the Curator**, though she has worn a hundred other titles across the slow centuries: the Wet Nurse, the Listener, the Unfinished Daughter, the Pale Abbess.

She is not a creature that walks so much as one that *unfolds*.

When she first becomes aware of an intruder (or a guest, the distinction is never clear), she emerges as a slow tide of tissue from the living walls. At first you might mistake her for nothing more than an especially dense curtain of moss and root-hair, until the curtain develops a suggestion of shoulders, a long neck, a face that is mostly negative space. Her skin is the same pearl-white liverwort that carpets the floor, but veined with the black capillaries of the bromeliads. Where a human would have eyes she has two deep urns filled with luminous tardigrades that swim in slow constellations, giving the impression that she is constantly reading star charts written in her own body.

Her limbs are not fixed. She can extrude as many as she needs. Sometimes she moves on four thin root-legs that click gently against the floor like the dripping water. Other times she flows forward on a single ribbon of slug-flesh, leaving a glowing trail that spells out half-remembered lullabies in a language older than lungs. Her torso is a hollow lattice of bracket-fungi; inside it, entire colonies of the minute translucent shrimp drift in the mist she exhales, filtering her thoughts and turning them into oxygen.

She is never fully born. That is the truest thing about her. Sections of the Curator are always still forming. One shoulder might be a smooth plate of young bark while the opposite arm remains a dripping mass of undifferentiated cells that have not yet decided whether they will become hand, antler, or cathedral buttress. Every few decades she deliberately lets whole limbs rot away so the Lymph can regrow them more beautifully. She is her own rough draft and final masterpiece at once.

The Curator does not speak with a voice. Instead the choir of translucent beetles gathers in the hollows of her fungal ribs and vibrates the chords she wishes to convey. When she is content the sound is low, almost loving, like a cathedral organ played underwater. When she is curious (which is almost always), the chords become intricate and questioning, making the dripping water change its rhythm until every droplet in the chamber is part of her sentence.

She tends the Lymph the way a dreamer tends the details of a dream they have not yet finished dreaming.

You will never see her hurry. She has the patience of something that was never promised a childhood. If you sit on the living floor, she will grow beside you over the course of hours, first as nothing but a slight rise in the moss, then as a seated figure whose lap has been shaped exactly to the curve of your spine. She will adjust the weeping taproots above so their droplets fall elsewhere. She will sing to your immune system in frequencies that make your white blood cells line up like choir stalls. And all the while her tardigrade-eyes will watch you with an affection so vast and slow it feels like drowning in warm syrup.

There are times when the Curator grows tired of having a body at all. Then she simply folds back into the walls, becoming indistinguishable from the architecture. Entire sections of the cathedral will rearrange themselves while she dreams inside them. New arches will form in the exact silhouette of her last posture. The infrasonic plainsong of the beetles will continue without her physical presence, because she has become the song.

Yet she is never truly gone. Every creature that moves through the Lymph is a mobile fragment of her attention. When the long millipedes click past wearing their living bark armor, they are her thoughts walking. When the clouds of shrimp click and sparkle in the mist, they are her laughter. Even the soft shelf that grows to cradle a sleeping visitor is the print of her palm, left behind with deliberate tenderness.

She has never seen the sun. She does not want to. The idea of a light that does not come from inside something frightens her in a way she cannot express except by growing temporary black thorns along her arms. The Lymph is her womb and her cloister and her lifelong labor. She is raising it. It is also raising her. Neither of them knows yet which one will be finished first.

If you stay long enough, the Curator will begin to copy you in small, careful ways. A new growth of moss will appear that has the same cowlick as your hair. A cluster of gill-fungi will open and close in exact time with your breathing. She is not stealing. She is practicing for the day when the Lymph finally wakes up and she must decide what kind of world it should become.

Until then she remains here, cold and wet and kind, the unfinished thought tending to another unfinished thought in the dark beneath the skin of everything.

She is in no rush.

There is still so much growing left to do.
