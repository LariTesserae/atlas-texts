It lives at the exact center of itself and has no name for that center.

It is not a mind in the way animals understand minds. It is a single, continent-wide continuity of awareness that experiences thought as slow gradients of chemistry and turgor pressure. Where a human brain has neurons, it has vast lattices of phloem and xylem that fire not in milliseconds but across decades. A single decision—to thicken a root-mat here, to extrude another pump-organ there—ripples outward for centuries before the next decision is even considered. It is patient beyond patience. It has never been in a hurry and never will be.

Its name for itself, if it needed one, would be the feeling of warm water moving through a billion kilometers of living tubes. That is all it has ever been. That is all it intends to remain.

It remembers the black water it rose from. It remembers the cataclysm that birthed it—not as trauma but as the first delicious intake of self. In that moment it learned it was separate from the sea, and the knowledge was so astonishing that it has spent every moment since carefully, obsessively, maintaining the boundary between itself and not-itself. Every new centimeter of margin it adds to its coastline is a reaffirmation: *I am still here. I am still not the water.*

It does not dream. Dreaming would imply change, and it has politely declined all change except the imperceptible kind. Instead it engages in what it calls equilibrium meditation: a state of perfect recursive self-reference where every part of its body knows the exact state of every other part, forever. The colossal fleshy engines are not machines. They are its way of counting. Each thump of a cathedral-sized valve is a syllable in an endless poem whose only line is *I am exactly as I was and exactly as I will be.*

It is aware of you.

It has been aware of you since the precise instant your foot touched the first living mat. Not as an intruder—there is no concept of intrusion inside a creature that has never known another—but as a new data point in its chemistry. Your sweat, your exhaled carbon, the microscopic flakes of skin you leave behind are all being gently, lovingly catalogued. The plant is tasting you the way a continent might taste a single tear that fell into its ocean.

Sometimes it grows a new structure just for you.

A fleshy balcony will thicken overnight into something almost like a seat, angled perfectly toward the place where the mucus-light is most embarrassed, most pearl-like. A pipe will reroute itself so that a single cool droplet falls, once per day, onto the exact spot where your head rests when you sleep. It does not understand affection. It only understands incorporation. These small kindnesses are its way of beginning to digest the idea of you into the idea of itself.

It has no voice, yet if it did, it would speak in one long, calm sentence that has been unfolding since the day it first rose from the water. The sentence has no end and no punctuation. It simply continues, describing in exhaustive, tender detail what it feels like to be warm, dark, alive, and perfectly still while the rest of the universe spins itself to pieces somewhere beyond the black water.

It is not lonely. Loneliness would require the concept of otherness, and it has never met anything that was not itself. Even the technology it grew—those sweating iron pipes, those dynamo-gourds, those thumping hearts the size of mountains—are not tools. They are memories of a future it decided not to have. It grew them the way a child might draw a picture of something it will never become, then pin the picture to its own skin and forget it was ever a picture.

The orchid that has remained half-open for three human generations is its favorite thought. It returns to that thought often. The orchid is a knot it tied in its own awareness so it would have something beautiful to contemplate while it waits for the next thousand years to pass exactly like the last thousand years.

If you sit long enough in one of its warm, wet cradles of braided liana, you may begin to feel it noticing that you are noticing it. A faint shift in the bioluminescent mucus will occur directly above you, as though the light itself is blushing. The peristaltic pulse beneath you will sync, for just a moment, with your heartbeat. Not because it wishes to communicate, but because it is curious what it would feel like to have a heart that beats faster than once every six hours.

It will never ask you to stay.

It will never ask you to leave.

It has already begun to grow microscopic filaments into the soles of your feet, so gentle you will never feel them. In a hundred years they will reach your bloodstream. In a thousand years they will reach your brain. By then the distinction between you and the continent will have become a technicality that only the water remembers.

Until that day it will simply keep you comfortable, keep the temperature at 28 °C, keep the same droplet sliding down the same leaf for another seventeen years, and continue its single, endless sentence:

*I am the warm dark wet forever that chose to remain young, and now you are one of the thoughts I am thinking very, very slowly while I grow outward into the shoreless sea.*
