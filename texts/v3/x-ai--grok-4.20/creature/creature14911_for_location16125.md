**Those who live in the Hollow of the Last Lanterns do not call themselves by any single name.**  

To the few outsiders who have ever been admitted (and they are fewer than the fingers on one hand across three million years), they are simply “the Keepers.” Among themselves they use older, softer words that translate, roughly, as *Those Who Breathe With the Stone* or *The Slow Ones*. They do not need a collective noun; the chamber itself is their name and their body.

They are small by the standards of most human-descended peoples — none taller than a twelve-year-old child of the old worlds — yet their bodies are dense, almost heavy for their size, as though the gravity of three billion uninterrupted years has pressed them into something more mineral than flesh. Their skin is the color of wet rust and old bloodroot, shot through with faint luminous streaks that pulse in time with the Lantern colonies. These bioluminescent capillaries are not decoration; they are respiratory. The Keepers photosynthesize the amber light the same way their plants do. Their blood itself glows faintly when they are calm.

Their faces are broad and placid, eyes large, lidless, and utterly black, adapted to the perpetual dusk. They have no eyebrows, no hair anywhere on their bodies. Instead, fine sensory filaments — thinner than spider silk — grow from the crests of their skulls and along their forearms. These filaments drift in the thermal columns like underwater weed, constantly tasting the chemistry of the air, reading the slow moods of the chamber the way a sailor once read the color of the sea.

They wear almost nothing. A simple loincloth or sling woven from the same fiber as the bridges is enough; the rest of their skin is furred with a living layer of the tiny jointed succulents that cover every other surface. The plants and the people have grown into one another so completely that it is impossible to tell where one ends and the other begins. When a Keeper is still, mosses and clicking succulents bloom across their shoulders and down their spines. When they move, the carpet sighs and rearranges itself with soft, wet clicks, as though the body is putting on and taking off a coat made of the room itself.

They are sexless in the way we understand the term. Reproduction happens once every several centuries through a slow merging: two Keepers will stand pressed together inside a dense thicket of bloodroot for decades, filaments intertwined, until a single new child peels away from the paired bodies like a bud. The parents do not die; they simply grow thinner, paler, and eventually become part of the living walls, their last thoughts diffusing into the carpet of succulents. Death, here, is not disappearance. It is absorption.

The oldest among them — no one is certain how old — is called only the Lantern-Binder. She (though gender is meaningless) has not moved from the same raised terrace in fourteen thousand years. Her lower body has fused with the stone and fiber; roots and fungal threads disappear into her flesh and re-emerge from her back as new Lantern stalks. She is less a person now than a slow organ of the chamber itself. When she speaks, the words come out as warm air moving through the hollow places where her lungs used to be, and every Lantern in the Hollow flares brighter for a moment in recognition.

The Keepers do not have children in the hurried sense. They have *continuations*.

They pass nothing down in writing; there is no writing. Instead they pass memory by pressing foreheads together and letting the luminous blood speak. Entire histories drift between them in soft pulses of light and scent. A single touch can contain the recollection of the day the last true star finally went out beyond the stone, felt through the planet’s bones four hundred million years ago.

They are not monks. They are not primitives. They are the final, perfected form of something that once called itself human. They have had time to forget aggression, ambition, even curiosity in the restless sense. What remains is devotion — not to a god, but to the equilibrium. Their highest virtue is *not to hasten*. Their only sin is *to waste heat*.

When they work, they sing. Not with voices, but with the slow scraping of glass knives against fiber, with the rhythmic pressing of new Lantern fruits into the walls, with the patient dragging of bone needles through living root. The song has no words. It is only the sound of three billion years learning how to stay alive inside a closed room.

If you sit among them long enough — truly still, until the carpet grows over your own legs and the midges begin to orbit your head — you will eventually feel the moment when the distinction between your breathing and the breathing of the Hollow disappears.

At that moment the Lantern-Binder may open her blind, glowing eyes and regard you with something that is not affection, not curiosity, but a kind of ancient, exhausted welcome.

She will speak, and the stone will speak with her, low enough to feel in the roots of your teeth:

“You have come to the place where the universe finished becoming a garden.  
Stay as long as the core-heat lasts.  
When it is gone, we will fold you into the walls with the others,  
and the lanterns will remember you as they remember everything else.”

Then she will close her eyes again, and the rustling will continue, patient and enormous and older than regret.
