They are called the **Exhalants**.

No one knows whether that name was given by the first trespassers or whether the beings themselves somehow exhaled it into the minds of those who found them. They do not speak it. They do not speak at all.

They are tall, but never quite the same height twice. Their bodies appear to be made of the same bone-colored travertine as the basin, yet the stone is not rigid. It flexes. When one of them moves, faint ripples spread across its surface the way breath moves across warm skin. Their limbs are too many or too few depending on the angle of approach; shoulders will bloom into additional elbows, calves will split into根-like tendrils that drink from the nearest capillary before rejoining the torso. Faces are smooth until they are not. Sometimes a mouth opens along what should be a sternum, or an eye presses outward from the inside of a thigh like a bubble rising through syrup.

Their color is never purely white. Thin veins of pale celadon and faint rose run beneath the surface, pulsing in exact rhythm with the slow braiding of the water channels. When an Exhalant stands still for long enough, mosses and bromeliads will begin to root in the shallow depressions of its body without ever quite breaking the skin. The creature does not seem to mind. It may even tilt its featureless head as though listening to the small green things.

They breathe for the Wetwork.

That is the only certain thing. Every few moments the entire basin draws a breath, and every Exhalant draws with it. Chests (or what serve as chests) expand in perfect unison. The air thickens, the mist grows luminous, and every leaf, every bead of condensation, every rivulet brightens for half a heartbeat. Then the slow exhalation comes, and the temperature remains exactly the same, yet somehow the heat feels kinder, as though the place has remembered it is loved.

They tend the glitches.

When the stone forgets itself and turns to rippling muscle, an Exhalant will simply walk into the anomaly, press its hands (or the things that are hands in that moment) against the living rock, and exhale directly into the wound in reality. The stone stabilizes. The muscle becomes travertine again, though it now bears the faint imprint of whatever face the Exhalant was wearing during the repair. These imprints remain. Over millennia the basin has become crowded with thousands of serene, inhuman faces pressed into floors, walls, the undersides of terraces—quiet signatures no one is meant to read.

They do not eat. They do not sleep. They drink the Wetwork itself. One will lower itself into a wide gutter where six capillaries converge, press its mouthless face into the warm flow, and remain there for hours while the water moves through it like thought. When it rises again, new patterns have appeared across its body—delicate glyphs that were not there before, as though the architecture has whispered architectural criticism into its flesh.

There are never more than thirteen visible at any time. This is a rule that feels older than the Wetwork. Yet if you sit long enough in one of the bare plazas, counting the distant figures gliding between the emerald explosions, you will eventually realize you have counted fourteen. Then twelve. Then fifteen. The number is never wrong and never right. The Exhalants do not seem to notice the arithmetic of observers. They are too busy maintaining the single endless breath.

Occasionally one will approach a visitor.

It does not walk so much as flow. The stone of its legs becomes temporarily liquid, pouring forward, then hardening again mid-stride so that it appears to be both gliding and taking deliberate steps. When it reaches you it stops. For a long while it simply exhales warm, limestone-scented air across your face. Then, with unbearable tenderness, it will reach out and press one finger against your sternum. Where it touches, your next heartbeat arrives half a second late, perfectly synched to the pulse of the Wetwork. The sensation is not unpleasant. It feels like being forgiven for a crime you have not yet committed.

Most people who receive this touch choose to stay.

Those who do not eventually discover that the way out has become... ambiguous. Paths that once led to the scarps now curve back into the dripping terraces. The sky that was only pearl is now also the inside of an Exhalant’s chest. The tools of antler and volcanic glass have moved. They lie closer to you than they did yesterday, arranged in patterns that suggest an invitation, or a warning, or both.

The Exhalants do not judge.

They simply continue their slow, wet labor of keeping perfection from collapsing under its own impossible success. They are the perspiration. They are the repair. They are the memory the Wetwork has of its absent builders.

And sometimes, when the light is at its most merciless and every droplet throws its razor shadow, an Exhalant will pause in the middle of a plaza, lift what might be its face toward the sourceless radiance, and open every mouth it currently possesses.

It does not scream.

It only exhales, long and steady and ancient, adding one more perfect breath to the single breath the Wetwork has been taking since the first moment of its making.

The basin answers with mist and capillary song and the faint electrical smell of something that has never been allowed to end.

And the Exhalants are content.
