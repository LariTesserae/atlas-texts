The ones who live on Veyr-Kaht do not consider themselves lonely.

They are the Veyr, and they move as if the plateau itself were still cooling beneath their feet. Their bodies are compact, barrel-chested, with shoulders hardened by centuries of carrying water up stairways and stone up ladders. Skin is the color of wet basalt after rain—deep charcoal shot through with faint metallic undertones that catch the haze-light and turn it almost violet. Hair grows thick, straight, and black; most keep it cropped close to the skull so the wind cannot find purchase. Eyes are small, deep-set, and permanently narrowed against the constant glare of high-altitude light. When they blink it looks like a geological event.

They are not a tall people. The tallest among them rarely clears a keth’s shoulder. Yet density clings to them the way moss clings to the rock. A Veyr in motion looks less like a person walking and more like a piece of the plateau that has decided, for a time, to travel.

Their faces are difficult to read by lowland standards. Expressions are economical: a slight tightening at the outer corners of the eyes can signal anything from amusement to murderous rage. Laughter, when it comes, is brief, low, and ends as suddenly as it begins, like a single hammer blow on good stone. They speak in measured tones that seem to come from the chest rather than the throat, each word weighed before it is released. Silence between them is not awkward; it is infrastructure.

The Veyr do not build for beauty. They build for continuance.

Every adult carries three names. The first is given at birth and used only by parents and lovers. The second is the ridge-name—taken when a child is old enough to walk the stairways alone—and tells which shelf, mesa, or slot claimed them. The third is earned, never spoken aloud, and recorded only in the small basalt tablets kept inside the House of Weights at the foot of the tallest inhabited ridge. To know someone’s third name is to know how they have paid their debt to the plateau.

They do not worship gods in the usual sense. Instead they keep four covenants with the land itself:

- The Covenant of Weight: nothing is allowed to remain light. Every new structure must be heavier than the one it replaces. Every death must be balanced by new stone hauled up from the deep quarries.
- The Covenant of Pace: haste is considered a form of theft from the future. Projects are deliberately paced so that children born at the beginning will see them finished in old age.
- The Covenant of Memory: the moss-bricks that light their homes are mixed with the powdered bones of the previous generation. The soft orange glow is, quite literally, their ancestors burning.
- The Covenant of Return: every Veyr swears that when the plateau finally shakes them off—whether by rockslide, thin air, or simple age—their body will not be buried. It will be carried to the uppermost ledges and left for the flightless ravens and the frost. Nothing is wasted.

In the terraced towns, life is lived in vertical layers. The lowest floors are for births and for the sick, because heat rises. The middle floors are for living and for the slow, intricate craft of shaping stone into tools that will outlast the hands that made them. The uppermost floors, whose roofs are also the streets for the level above, belong to the very old. From there they can watch the slow herds of keth moving like dark beads across the gray-green moss, and the ravens hopping between their backs with the same unhurried patience the Veyr themselves practice.

Children are rare and therefore sacred in a particular, stern way. A Veyr infant is not praised for being clever or beautiful; it is praised for being heavy. The first time a baby is placed on the great scales in the Hall of Weights, the entire ridge holds its breath. If the child is deemed “light” by the black-iron counterweight, the family enters a year of mourning and increased labor. Most are not light.

The six-legged otters that live in the deep slots are never hunted. The Veyr believe the creatures can move between stone and water because they remember when the basalt was still liquid. To kill one would be to murder a memory of fire.

At night the plateau belongs to the wind and to the slow creak of rope bridges swaying between ridges. The Veyr sleep in windowless rooms on the lee side of their stacked houses. They dream, when they dream at all, of the lowlands they have never seen—dreams that feel like falling. Most wake before they hit the ground.

And always, everywhere, there is the sound of hammers.

Not loud. Never hurried. Just the patient, ringing note of stone being asked, once again, to become something more useful than it was. The same note has been ringing across Veyr-Kaht for almost three thousand years. It will probably still be ringing three thousand years from now, long after the current ridges have crumbled and new ones have been claimed by new generations of small, dense, unyielding people who look exactly like their ancestors and move at exactly the same deliberate pace as the frost, the keth, and the patient turning of the long, slow year.

They are not in a hurry.

The plateau taught them that time is not a river.

It is a shelf.

And they were born on it.
