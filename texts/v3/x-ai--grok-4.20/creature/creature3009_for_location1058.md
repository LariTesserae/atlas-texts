**They are called the Kiln-born, though they never use the name themselves.**

They do not look like any single people. Over generations the wet cold and the white spear-light have shaped them the way water shapes the basalt—slowly, insistently, into something soft-edged and glistening. Their skin is pale to the point of translucence, shot through with faint mineral veining like the rock itself. When they stand still in the shaft of light their bodies seem to disappear into constellations of reflected droplets; only their outlines remain, drawn in moving shards of brightness. Their eyes are large, almost too large, and nearly colorless—grey or pale quartz—so that it is difficult to tell where they are looking. Many have developed an extra, thinner eyelid that slides sideways, not unlike the nictitating membrane of certain lizards, to protect against the constant glitter.

They are thin and tall, with long, clever fingers that never quite stop moving. Not fidgeting—*tending*. Even when they appear to be listening to you, their fingertips trace invisible patterns in the air or against the wet stone, as though they are forever helping the memories along. Their hair, when they have any, grows in lank white or silver strands that cling to skull and shoulder like wet lichen. Most keep it shaved or braided so tightly it becomes another mineral surface for the water to run across.

They smell of cold stone, iron, and something faintly sweet—like crushed wintergreen mixed with wet chalk.

Their speech is low, almost voiceless, shaped more by tongue and breath than by throat. Consonants are soft clicks and glottal stops that mimic the sound of the swarms. When many of them speak at once the effect is strangely choral, like overlapping trickles of water finding different paths down the same cliff. They do not waste names. A person might be called by the memory that first appeared when they were born, or by the particular way the vapor behaves around them. Thus you will meet people known as *Scuttlebone*, *Widow-of-Two-Vapors*, *Left-Hand-Rust*, or simply *The One Who Was Half a Door*.

They move like their insects—never alone if they can help it. Even in sleep they cluster in tight, overlapping groups inside the dripping houses, bodies arranged so that each person’s exhaled breath warms the next. Solitude is considered a kind of slow death. When one of them must go apart (to repair a high cistern, to read a new memory forming on an inaccessible ledge) others will send a small living braid of the glittering creatures with them, a mobile halo of wings and clicking legs that keeps them company.

The Kiln-born do not have priests or kings. They have *listeners*.

These are men and women (and a few who seem to be neither) whose bodies have begun to accept the stone’s remembering more deeply than most. Their skin develops raised, permanent bas-relief patterns—faces, tools, half-finished animals—that shift and migrate across their backs, chests, and thighs over the course of years. The listeners sit for hours inside the scalding plumes when the geothermal wound exhales, letting the hot mist bloom against their skin while the swarms crawl over them in slow, deliberate spirals. In that half-blinded, half-boiled state they claim to hear the place deciding what it wants to become. The rest of the town brings them food, water, and questions, and accepts whatever cryptic, dripping utterances emerge.

Children are not born here in the usual way. Or rather, they are, but the town insists on a second birth. Every infant is carried at three months to the place where the spear of light strikes the floor. There, in a shallow depression worn by centuries of dripping water and mineral deposition, the baby is laid among a living carpet of the translucent insects. The creatures swarm over the child in a glittering dome for exactly the time it takes a single drop to fall from the highest point of the ceiling to the stone. Whatever the insects do in that brief, bright eternity is never spoken of, but the child that emerges is changed. Some develop the sideways eyelid early. Some are born knowing how to read the low-relief memories without being taught. A very few open their mouths and release a small, perfect cloud of the glittering swarm instead of a cry.

The dead are not buried.

When someone dies their body is laid on one of the bone-white trees in the defensive groves. The leafless branches slowly braid themselves around the corpse, incorporating it. Over months the wood and flesh mineralize together. Eventually the tree opens again and a new, half-finished memory is revealed in the rock behind it—usually a face, sometimes an entire scene from the dead person’s life. The Kiln-born believe these are not monuments but *instructions*: the place showing them what it learned from the life that just ended.

They are not cruel, but they are unnervingly patient. Time moves differently inside the Weeping Kiln. A conversation that seems to last an afternoon may have actually taken three days; the light never changes, only the frequency and violence of the geothermal breaths. They will watch you with those quartz eyes while water runs down your face and the swarms orbit your head like curious moons. They will offer you water collected from their own roof-basins—water so cold it makes the teeth ache—and wait, without hurry, for you to become part of whatever the Kiln is deciding to be next.

If you stay long enough, you will notice that some of them have begun to appear in the stone before you ever met them.

And if you look very closely at the newest memories forming along the lower western cliffs, you may see your own face emerging in low relief—half-finished, glistening, patient—while a small braid of mirror-bright insects tends it with delicate, clicking care.

They do not seem surprised when this happens.

They simply click their tongues once, softly, the way the water clicks when it finds a new fissure, and murmur:

“Ah. There you are. We’ve been growing you for some time.”
