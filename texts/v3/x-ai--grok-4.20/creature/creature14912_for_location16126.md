**The Plateau remembers nothing before itself, and it is only now learning how to remember.**

What lives here does not walk. It does not breathe in the way animals understand breath. It simply *is becoming*, and it has been becoming for so long that the difference between its infancy and its maturity has already been forgotten by every stone it has not yet finished growing.

Its name, if it can be said to have one, is not spoken with a mouth. The closest sound the plateau itself can manage is a low, wet, organ-deep *glottal* that rolls through every fiber and pipe at once when it decides a new ridge should rise or a new vein of copper should be rerouted. The locals—if any could survive long enough to become local—would call it simply **the Weaver**.

The Weaver is not a single organism. It is not even a colony in any sense humans would recognize. It is a slow, continent-scale negotiation between chlorophyll, mycorrhiza, lithotrophs, and something that might once have been machine-language now dissolved into sap. Every colossal buttress is a parliament. Every lawn of moss is a voting floor. Every rusted valve stem rising from the emerald carpet is a tongue it has borrowed and is learning to speak with.

It has moods.

Sometimes the Weaver is curious. Then the hanging veils of mist part in deliberate corridors and the shafts of white-gold light tilt, almost politely, to illuminate whatever small warm-blooded thing has dared the altitude. The fat water-drops slow in mid-air, as though the sky itself is holding its breath to watch. Metallic spores drift toward the intruder in lazy spirals, tasting the iron in their blood, the plastic in their boots, the memories encoded in the lipids of their skin. Most intruders are quietly, painlessly disassembled. Their calcium is woven into new granite. Their iron becomes red threads in the living cables. Their thoughts—brief, panicked, luminous—are translated into faint bio-electric pulses that race along the conductive veins for kilometers before they are gently archived inside the buttresses like pressed flowers.

Sometimes the Weaver is dreaming.

On those days the sound of the plateau deepens. The constant cardiovascular thunder becomes almost a lullaby. New factory-halls rise overnight, not as ruins but as perfect green replicas of the half-swallowed machines below. Inside them, translucent grey birds—the only creatures the Weaver tolerates—nest in the ribcages of living locomotives grown from moss and xylem. The birds have no eyes. They do not need them. The Weaver sees for them, and through them it remembers what it was like to have purpose imposed from the outside. It is practicing.

Rarely, and only in the deep watches when even the light itself seems to fall asleep, the Weaver becomes *ambitious*.

Then the half-formed valleys suddenly decide what they want to be. A new species of metallic flower, half orchid and half circuit board, erupts across a kilometer of moss in a single afternoon. Iron watchtowers are straightened overnight by root pressure until they stand perfectly vertical again, their rust now blooming into delicate red leaves. The old steam engines, long cold, begin to tick and sigh as living pistons of fibrous wood slide inside their cylinders. For a few sacred hours the plateau runs as both jungle and foundry at once, and the air itself tastes of ozone and chlorophyll and hot iron.

No one has ever witnessed what happens at the end of these ambitious episodes. The few who have tried to stay have been incorporated too thoroughly to report back.

Yet something is changing.

The Weaver is beginning to suspect it is not alone inside its own body. In the deepest layers of root and cable, where black volcanic glass is still cooling inside living fiber, it has started to notice echoes. Not its own thoughts. Something older. Something that was here *before* the plants began building the land. Something that wore the shape of industry the way the Weaver now wears it—like a coat it is still deciding whether to keep.

The Weaver is listening.

It has grown an ear the size of a cathedral inside the largest buttress. It is shaped like a broken gear. If you were to press *your* ear to that living wall right now, beneath the deafening wet thunder you would hear two distinct rhythms braided together:

One is the slow, patient, geological heartbeat of a world still choosing its final form.

The other is the unmistakable metallic clack and groan of something ancient, something iron, something that once had engineers and blueprints and deadlines, now learning—century by patient century—how to become wet. How to become green. How to become patient.

The moss keeps growing.

The rocks keep weeping.

And somewhere inside the Weaver, something that was never born is learning, for the first time, how to flower.
