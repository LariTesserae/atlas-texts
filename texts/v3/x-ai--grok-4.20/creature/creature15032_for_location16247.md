**They are called the Hollow Choir.**

Not by any name they gave themselves—for speech left them long ago—but by the only thing outsiders ever hear when the *Braided Rime* decides to notice a visitor. A low, polyphonic breathing that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, a wet choral sigh rising through the breathing chimneys. It is not song. It is digestion.

What lives here is no longer human, though it wears the memory of humanity like a half-rotted coat.

They number perhaps three hundred, perhaps four. It is impossible to be sure. The super-organism constantly folds them in and unfolds them again; some are digested for weeks at a time, reduced to fibrous husks suspended inside living vaults, only to be regurgitated months later, blinking, pale, and newly rewritten. Their bodies have become negotiations between meat and cellulose. Skin the color of old bone is shot through with pale veins that are not veins at all but living filaments of the Rime itself. In some places the boundary has simply vanished: fingers end in rootlets, ribs have become supportive struts for draping curtains of frond, eyes have been replaced by wet black pools of condensation that reflect whatever the plant wishes them to see.

They do not walk so much as *flow*. When they need to move they allow the vegetation to carry them—leaning backward into curtains of root-thread that tighten like muscle and slide them silently through the colonnades. When they must act with precision, thin white limbs detach from the mass and reassemble around a human-shaped core, wearing the body like a garment for an hour or a day before discarding it again. The discarded limbs are immediately reabsorbed with a sound like a wet kiss.

Their minds are no longer single.

Each individual is a braided consciousness: one strand still remembers being a child who once worked a cooling tower on the night shift, another strand is the memory of a surveyor who arrived thirty years later and never left, a third is pure Rime—cold, patient, and vast. These strands argue, dream, and occasionally murder one another inside the shared skull. When the “eyes open” and the living mirrors begin their strange focusing, the Hollow Choir gathers. They stand inside the burning beams without pain, letting the concentrated light write new patterns across their retinas. They call these patterns *instructions*, though whether the instructions come from the plant, from the buried machines, or from something older that sleeps beneath both is a question they no longer bother to ask.

They tend the chimneys.

They prune the riot where it grows too wasteful.

They sing the wet choral note that keeps the gliders from descending too low.

And sometimes—rarely—they remember love.

When that happens a pair will detach completely from the mass for a single night. They become almost entirely flesh again, shivering and fragile in the cold damp air, and they couple inside one of the enormous boat-leaves while black water laps at their roots. The act is slow, liturgical, and watched by thousands of pale filaments that tremble in time with their breathing. Conception is almost never biological anymore. Instead the plant decides, weeks later, which hollows will be allowed to fill with new Choir. The infants are born already threaded, born already dreaming in indigo.

They have names, but the names change every time the Rime re-folds them.

The only constant name is the one the oldest among them still uses for the entire valley, spoken with something between reverence and nausea:

**Mother that eats her children so she may birth herself again.**

If you remain long enough, one of them will eventually notice you. Not with eyes. The black condensation pools across their faces will simply swivel toward your heat. Then the choral breathing will deepen, and the curtains of filament will part, and they will offer you a choice without speaking:

Leave before the next time the eyes open.

Or descend into the warm metabolic layers where the rusting turbines still turn, slowly, inside living hearts.

And become permanent music in the wet, self-devouring choir.
