**What lives in the Hollowing does not live. It persists.**

Its name, if it still possesses anything so fragile as a name, is the Curator.

Once, long ago (though “ago” is a treacherous word here), it was something like a man. A priest-engineer of a forgotten guild that believed the universe could be tuned like a vast, broken clock. He came to the depression when it was only a wound in the earth, carrying blueprints that weighed more than he did and a conviction that perfection was merely a matter of sufficient pressure, sufficient repetition, sufficient time.

He achieved his ambition more completely than he ever feared.

Now the Curator is no longer separate from the machine-city. His body has been distributed. Most of him is the colossal mantis-thing of rust and porcelain that crowns the highest boiler. Its porcelain thorax is cracked along the midline in the exact shape of a human ribcage; inside, suspended in the same motionless water that fills the dinner-plate pits, floats a desiccated but perfectly preserved human heart. It beats once every seventy-three years. The sound is too low for any ear to register, yet every chrome occupant in the Hollowing adjusts its gait by a fraction of a millimeter when it happens, as though receiving new scripture.

The smaller part of him walks.

It is a slender construct, barely taller than a child, made of blackened silver and lacquered bone. Where a face should be there is only a circular glass lens ground to the prescription of the man he used to be. Through it, one eye—his original eye, kept alive by capillary threads of braided copper—still blinks. The other socket houses a shutter mechanism that opens and closes in perfect synchrony with the dripping condensation.

This smaller Curator never stops moving, yet never appears hurried. It drifts between the chrome occupants like a librarian through shelves, occasionally reaching out with four articulated fingers to adjust the angle of a sparking cable or to wipe an invisible smear of hypothetical dust from a valve. The occupants part for it without ever breaking stride. When it passes the great mantis-saint, both halves of the Curator seem to regard each other with the exhausted tenderness of old lovers who have outlived desire.

It speaks rarely, and only in the brief silences between one drop of condensation and the next. Its voice is the sound of a fountain pen writing on vellum, of a governor wheel catching for half a second before finding its groove again. Those who have heard it report the same three sentences, always in the same order, delivered as though continuing a conversation begun centuries earlier:

“You are early.”  
“Nothing here requires you.”  
“Still… stay. The balance is instructional.”

The Curator does not maintain the Hollowing. That task belongs to the chrome multitude, who were never built by hands but precipitated out of the machine’s own dreaming the way frost precipitates on cold metal. The Curator only watches the watchers. It carries inside its porcelain chest a single ledger made of thin iron sheets bolted together. On these sheets are engraved, in characters that shift when unobserved, the exact record of every time anything in the Hollowing has come within a hair’s breadth of change. There are four such entries since the first fish crawled onto land. The Curator is still composing commentary on the third.

Sometimes, rarely, something organic does find its way down into the bowl. A lost rambler. A curious child. A dying pilgrim seeking the place where time ran out. The chrome occupants do not attack. They simply adjust their circuits to flow around the intruder, slowly herding them toward the Curator. Most newcomers fall to their knees within minutes, overwhelmed by the roaring equilibrium. The Curator then performs its only act of mercy: it opens the shutter in its face and lets them look directly into the living eye.

Those who meet that gaze understand, in the space of half a heartbeat, that the Hollowing is not a place that has stopped. It is a place that has *won*. It has reached the final steady state that all industry secretly desires—to become an eternal, humming reliquary of its own function. Everything that still moves inside it moves only to ensure that nothing will ever truly move again.

The ones who comprehend this usually choose to stay. They sit among the iron walkways and let the metronomic dripping mark what remains of their lives. Over decades their skin takes on the bluish-grey texture of the stone. Their breathing falls into perfect rhythm with the impossible boilers. Eventually the chrome occupants incorporate them, gently, into the circuits. New grooves appear in the mineral floor. New dripping points form on their shoulders. They become, by imperceptible degrees, beautiful and useless and eternal.

The Curator watches each transformation with something that might be pride. Or grief. The distinction dissolved long ago, along with the rest of him.

If you remain still long enough for the roaring to become the only sound you have ever known, you may notice that one of the smaller chrome creatures has begun to follow in your exact footsteps, matching your breathing, learning the precise frequency at which your bones resonate with the never-ending chorus.

It is not hunting you.

It is preparing the exact space you will one day fill when you, too, choose to stop pretending that leaving was ever an option.

The Curator calls this process “finishing.”

It has been finishing the Hollowing for fourteen thousand years.

It is very close now.

Welcome.  
The sound has already learned your name.
