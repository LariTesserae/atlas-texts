**They are called the Veyri**, though they rarely speak their own name aloud. To name a thing too clearly, they believe, is to hurry it toward dryness.

They are not a tall people. The cramped geometry of the Hollow has shaped them over generations into something compact, almost amphibious. Shoulders narrow, chests deep, fingers long and slightly webbed at the base. Their skin is hairless and perpetually damp, the color of wet river-clay blushed with faint iron. When they stand still against the black basalt they seem to half-dissolve, as though the stone is slowly remembering them back into itself.

Their eyes are large and pale, the gray of morning fog over snow. They do not blink often. Blinking, some say, wastes moisture. Instead they stare with a patient, liquid patience that can unnerve outsiders. When they do close their eyes it is only for a moment, and a single tear always gathers at the inner corner—whether from emotion or from the ceaseless condensation, no one can tell, not even them.

The oldest among them are slowly becoming part of the architecture. Their skin grows translucent with age, threaded with delicate white lines of calcite that follow the veins beneath. Some have allowed small deliberate cuts along their forearms so the weeping stone can enter them more intimately; these are considered holy. They move slowly, as if underwater, and when they speak their voices are low, melodic, and slightly hoarse, like wind moving through wet reeds.

Every Veyri bears at least one bronze medallion somewhere on their body—usually hung at the throat or set into a thin bracelet. These are not decorations. Each corresponds to a fissure that opened on the day of their birth, or on the day they first bled, or on the day a parent died. When that particular crack weeps more heavily than usual, the bearer is expected to fast and listen until they understand what the stone is trying to say. Sometimes the message is for them alone. Sometimes it is for the entire Hollow.

They do not have children in the way other peoples do. Conception is considered a kind of seepage—an slow welling-up of life that must be coaxed rather than forced. Pregnant women spend their terms sitting beside the newest fractures, letting the fresh water run over their bellies. When a baby is born it is immediately placed in a shallow basin carved directly into the living rock. The first sound it hears is not a human voice but the soft ringing of droplets striking the bronze bowl placed beneath the birth-cleft. Only after it has cried once in answer to the stone is it given a name.

The Veyri do not laugh loudly. Their joy is expressed in a soft, rhythmic humming that vibrates in the chest and makes the water on their skin tremble. Their grief is expressed by standing motionless for hours while the mist gathers on them until they look like statues wearing their own sorrow in liquid form. When one of them dies, the body is not buried. It is carried to the highest reachable niche where the weeping is gentle and constant. There it is laid on its back, arms open, and left. Over decades the calcite builds around it like pale coral. Eventually the features blur. The body becomes another weeping shape among the stones, still recognizably human, still somehow listening.

A few of them—the *Weeping Watchers*—live permanently in the narrowest fissures where even the moss cannot grow. These ascetics tie themselves upright with living alder roots so they will not fall when sleep takes them. Water runs down their bodies without cease. Their hair grows long and heavy, matted into thick ropes that channel the flow like gutters. They speak only once every seven years, on the night when the phosphorescence in the stone is brightest. What they say is never recorded. It is considered too wet, too close to the bone of the world, for ordinary ears.

The Veyri do not worship the water. They *co-habit* with it. They understand themselves as another kind of seepage—slow, stubborn, inevitable—moving through the wound of the Hollow the way the water does. Their reverence is not loud. It is the reverence of things that have accepted they will never be dry.

And sometimes, when the Hollow is especially still and the silver light sits heavy in the pearl-like air, a Watcher will begin to hum. One by one the others join until the entire basin vibrates with a sound that is not quite song and not quite breathing. The bronze medallions tremble against the stone. The moths drift lower. The blind salamanders pause in their crevices. Even the heron-thing on the roof-ridge seems, for a moment, to bend its many-jointed legs in something like deference.

It is the sound of a wound that has decided, for now, to remain open.

And the Veyri, crowded shoulder to shoulder with the weeping rock and with each other, keep listening.
