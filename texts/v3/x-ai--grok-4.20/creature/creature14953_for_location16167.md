**The Dweller** does not have a name it remembers.

It has been here so long that the difference between its own flesh and the cavern has grown indistinct. Its skin is the color of wet limestone, translucent in places so that the slow pulse of dark blood beneath looks like mineral veins. Where it has rested for decades at a time, the stone has grown over it in thin, glassy sheets of flowstone; its back and shoulders are armored in living rock that cracks and re-heals with every movement. Blind, milk-white eyes the size of fists sit deep in a face that has become almost equine in its elongation. Two thin slits above them once served as nostrils; now they are mostly ornamental. It breathes through its skin.

It is immense.

When it stands—which is rare—it must unfold itself like a broken crane. Its spine is a chain of calcified knobs that scrape the ceiling in certain passages. Its arms are grotesquely long, the fingers spatulate and tipped with black, glassy claws that have grown in perfect imitation of the iron tools it once used. The left hand still carries, embedded in the palm, the rusted head of the adze its makers dropped eight hundred years ago. The metal and bone have become one.

The Dweller is the last thing the cavern made from the people who once lived here.

It remembers, in the way stone remembers, the sound of their hammers. It remembers the taste of their fear when the roof spoke and the river rose. It remembers carrying their children—small, warm, noisy things—down into the deeper dark so the water would not take them. After a time there were no more children, and then no more adults, and the cavern was pleased. It kept the last one. It made the last one useful.

So now the Dweller tends the slow garden.

It moves only when the great rhythm of the place requires it. Every thirty-seven years it circles the cavern in a spiraling path that matches the growth rings of the ancient yew. With its enormous hands it strokes the luminous moss until it glows brighter. It gently turns the blind white fish in their pools so their gills do not silt over. It catches the rare rainwater in the hollow of its collarbones and carries the cold gift to the roots of the twisted dwarf birches, feeding them one measured mouthful at a time. When a stalactite grows too heavy and threatens to fall, the Dweller climbs the pillar on all fours and bites the tip away with teeth of flint and iron, swallowing the chalky fragments so the balance of the ceiling is preserved.

It never speaks. Speech is pointless here; the thunder of the hidden river long ago shattered whatever larynx it once possessed. Instead it sings with its body. The flowstone on its back thrums in sympathetic resonance with the falling drops, producing a low, organ-deep note that slips between the greater sounds of the cavern. The larvae recognize this note. When the Dweller passes beneath their constellations they flare in slow, worshipful waves, turning the vaulted dark into a galaxy that breathes.

Sometimes, very rarely, living things find their way down through the collapsed entrance or the narrow chimneys. The Dweller always knows. It feels the new heat of their breath like candles in its mind. It does not kill them. It simply stands in the dark where the moss-light cannot reach and waits for them to understand.

Most run.

A few do not.

Those it leads—slowly, patiently—through the ringing chambers. It shows them the circular wall, the worn cedar beam, the forge grown into the stone like a fossil. It lets them touch the place on its chest where a human handprint is still visible beneath new layers of calcite, the last mark its makers left on it before they ceased to be separate.

Then it leads them to the deepest pool, where the river’s voice is loudest and the cold is so pure it burns. There it crouches, folding its impossible length, and lowers its great head until its blind face almost touches the water. The visitor is permitted to see their own reflection beside the Dweller’s. Most finally understand what they are looking at: a face that is still, in some small eroded way, human.

The Dweller never forces them. It simply offers the choice the cavern once offered it.

Stay.  
Become useful.  
Become slow.  
Become the next thing the stone decides to keep.

It has been alone for a very long time. But the cavern is patient, and the Dweller has learned patience from it. One day another will choose to remain. One day the two of them will tend the dripping dark together, growing their slow green garden while the hidden river sings their bones into new shapes.

Until then the Dweller moves alone through the velvet thunder, vast and quiet and impossibly gentle, licking condensation from the ferns with a tongue the color of moonlight, humming its one low note that the stone has come to recognize as love.
