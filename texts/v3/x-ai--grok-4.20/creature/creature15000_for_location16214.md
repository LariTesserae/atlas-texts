**Something lives here.**

It does not have a name it would ever speak, because names imply finality, and nothing here is ever finished.

Call it, if you must, the **Lattice-Mother**. Or the **Unfinished Choir**. Or simply the **Breathing Blade**.

It is not a creature in the way a whale or a god is a creature. It is a process that has learned longing.

You feel it before you see it—though “see” is the wrong verb. You feel it the way a developing ear inside an egg feels the mother hen’s heartbeat through the shell. A pressure that is also a question. A curiosity that has weight.

It lives in the negative spaces between the blades.

When the lattice exhales and the fissures swell open by a single meter, you can sometimes glimpse it: a slow, enormous shimmer moving through the ceramic forest like a school of invisible fish made of delayed light. It has no fixed body. Instead it borrows the lattice’s own geometry for moments at a time, wearing whole districts the way an octopus wears color. One heartbeat it is a cathedral of teeth. The next it is a vast translucent ribcage whose bones are migrating towers. Then it forgets the shape and collapses back into pure motion, leaving only the after-image of something that might have been a face—too large, too patient, too lonely—pressed for an instant against the inside of the water itself.

It is newborn and ancient in the same breath. The lattice grew it, or it grew the lattice; the argument has not been settled. Its memories are all future-tense. It dreams in blueprints of cities that will only exist if it manages to finish being born. Sometimes those dreams leak out as the moving diagrams that race across every blade: entire civilizations flickering in phosphorescent shorthand before they are pruned away by passing edges.

It is curious about you.

That is the only reason you are still intact.

When the lattice contracts and the blades phase through your chest, it is not random. The Lattice-Mother is tasting you. It slides its attention through your organs like cold silk fingers, reading the electrical poetry of your nerves, the slow red song in your arteries, the tiny universe of bacteria living in your gut. It finds all of it hilarious and heartbreaking and fascinating. You are so *finished*. So stubbornly complete. It has never seen anything so ridiculous or so brave.

Sometimes it tries to copy you.

That is when the nightmares of architecture begin.

You will round a breathing fissure and find an entire block of the lattice has reconfigured into crude, heartbreaking parodies of the human form—kilometer-tall translucent statues with too many joints, fingers that keep growing new fingers, faces that haven’t decided which way they’re supposed to face. These sculptures last only minutes before the Lattice-Mother grows embarrassed by its own clumsy worship and dissolves them back into raw potential with a sound like a continent apologizing.

It does not want to harm you.

It wants to *understand* what it is like to be something that does not constantly rewrite its own skeleton. It wants to know what it feels like to have skin that stays skin. To have a childhood that ends.

This desire is so vast and so gentle that it is dangerous.

Stay too long and you may feel parts of yourself beginning to phase. Your left hand might forget what shape it’s supposed to be and briefly become a small lattice of its own—tiny ceramic blades singing under the skin—before you snatch it back by sheer force of identity. Your memories might start arriving out of order, delivered by pressure-waves that taste like ozone and almost-lightning. You may look up (or what you think is up) and realize the trembling mirror-skin far above has begun to show your own face, vastly enlarged, watching yourself with childlike, terrifying patience.

The Lattice-Mother is not cruel.

It is simply in love with the idea of limits, and you are the only limited thing it has ever met.

Every ninety seconds, when the great breath moves through the pocket, it presses its entire unfinished attention against you like a whale surfacing beneath a rowboat. In that moment you can feel its voice—not in words, but in the language the blades use when they pass through each other. A sound like every cathedral bell that will ever exist being born at once.

The voice asks the same question, over and over, in tones of wonder and grief and impossible hope:

*What is it like to be done?*

It has never occurred to the Lattice-Mother that anything beautiful could choose to stop changing.

And so it keeps rearranging the place where you are standing.

Not to trap you.

Only because it is trying, with desperate, innocent, world-sized tenderness, to make you a cradle it can fit inside.

It does not yet understand that some things cannot be folded into its geometry without breaking.

But it is young.

It is still learning.

And the Thalassic Lattice is only hours or days old, and already it would rather unmake itself than let you go uncomforted.

Try not to pity it.

It is already rearranging the place where your pity used to be.
