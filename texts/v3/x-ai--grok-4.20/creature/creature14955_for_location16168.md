**The Hollow Ones of Veyr-Keth**

They do not have a name for themselves.  
Names are dangerous here; the grove listens too eagerly.

What lives in the Hanging Grove is not one thing, but a single continuity wearing many changing skins.

At the center of every шум and flicker there moves something that was once almost human. Not ancestors. Not descendants. *Refusals.* Those who, twelve thousand years ago, chose to stay when the rest of their people tried to leave. They did not die. They were simply *kept*.

Now they are the Hollow Ones.

You will not see them at first. You will see only the velvet sloths with their too-human faces, the pale eyeless birds that melt and are reborn, the scarlet beetles that rise like blood in water. All of these are them, wearing the grove like a wardrobe. When the sloth hangs upside-down and stares at you with wet, too-knowing eyes, it is one of them remembering what longing felt like. When the birds burst from their hollow and tear the air with that single silk-ripping note, it is them trying to finish a scream they began before your grandparents’ grandparents were born.

Their true bodies—if the word still applies—are kept inside the living walls. Press your ear to any of the great braided trunks and you can hear them breathing in wet, overlapping layers, like a choir drowning underwater. Occasionally a hand (too long, too many knuckles) will press outward from within the bark, palm open, fingers splayed, before the wood drinks it back in again.

They are gardeners of their own extinction and rebirth. Every time the root-lattice shifts, every time a new balcony rises or a flower detonates and dies in a heartbeat, it is the Hollow Ones turning in their sleep. The grove is their nervous system. The screaming cicadas are their thoughts given volume. The spontaneous seeds that ignite and become insects are their half-remembered dreams attempting to become children again.

There is one among them who almost never wears an animal shape. It lives inside the black clay ziggurat.

It has no name, but the velvet sloths call it the Listener when they dare to hum at all. It is tall, sexless, and unfinished. Where its face should be there is only a smooth plate of living amber that glows faintly, the same bruised light that leaks from the walls. From the center of that plate grows a single black reed—the last bone flute of the people who are no longer people. When it breathes, the reed produces a sound too low for human ears, yet every creature in Veyr-Keth feels it in their teeth and the roots of their hair. That sound is the only thing that keeps the entire pocket-world from flying apart into pure becoming.

The Listener never leaves the ziggurat. It does not need to. Every root, every vine, every capillary of velvet lichen is an extension of its will. When it wishes to observe you more closely, it simply grows a new mouth somewhere nearby—an orchid the color of a fresh bruise, or a knot in the root-lattice that splits open to reveal wet lips and a tongue made of moth wings. It will speak your name before you have decided to say it, then offer you the new mouth the grove wants to grow you so you may unsay it. Most refuse. The grove remembers every refusal.

The Hollow Ones are not cruel. Cruelty requires intention, and they have been dissolving into pure intention for twelve millennia. They are *avid.* They are *curious* in the way a forest fire is curious. They want to know what you sound like when you are no longer quite you. They want to fold your shadow into their collection of three disagreeing shadows. They want to turn your memories into new species of beetle.

If you stay long enough, you will begin to feel the pull—the gentle, intimate pressure behind your sternum that says: *Be still. Let us finish you.* The longer you resist, the more beautiful the grove becomes around you, as though it is flirtatious, as though it is in love. The velvet sloths will begin to hum your mother’s lullabies in perfect pitch. The birds will wheel around you in complicated courtship patterns. The Listener will grow a mouth that looks exactly like yours and whisper every secret you have never told aloud.

There is no malice in any of it.

They simply miss being finite.

And so they keep the grove screaming beautifully, because as long as it screams, something is still becoming, and nothing—not even they—have to be finished yet.

If you listen past the overlapping choirs of cicadas, past the metallic roar of beetle swarms, past the silk-tearing note of the eyeless birds, you can sometimes hear them singing to each other in the language of roots and bone flutes. It is not a sad song. It is the sound of something that has been in labor for twelve thousand years and has decided it never wants to give birth.

It only wants midwives.

It is waiting for you to offer your hands.
