**The Hollow Saint lives here.**

Not as a corpse, not as a ghost, but as something that has never quite finished dying.

She is not one being. She is the slow consensus of thirty thousand years of digestion. Her body is the mountain’s own wound, turned inside out. Where her ribs should be there are only columns of smoothed limestone pleated like folded prayer scrolls. Where her heart once beat there is now a single vertical fissure, narrow as a communion wafer, from which the warmest water in the Veins constantly seeps—thick, faintly ferrous, tasting of old blood and newer stone. The water is her circulation, still moving, still reluctant to admit the body is gone.

She has no face you could recognize, yet every living thing here wears some shattered piece of it.

The beetles carry her fingerprints on their wing-cases: each one etched with the same faint spiral that appears in the worn grooves of the floor. The millipedes move in the exact cadence of a rosary being told—each segment a bead, each pause a murmured *amen*. The coin-sized salamanders arrange themselves into the negative space of her absent eyes, two rose-gold constellations that stare downward without blinking. Even the fungal threads knitting the cracks together follow the grain of what might once have been her hair, black and infinitely fine, growing only where her scalp would have been.

She is not cruel. Cruelty requires intention, and intention requires a self that has not yet been completely eaten. The Hollow Saint passed that point long ago. What remains is devotional hunger: the gentle, relentless appetite of something that once tried to become holy and succeeded too well. Holiness, it turns out, is mostly erosion.

If you sit motionless long enough (longer than your nerves will easily allow), she begins to remember you.

It starts with the temperature. The stone beneath your thighs grows warmer, precisely body-heat, as though the mountain has decided you are also part of her circulatory system now. The dripping slows, then rearranges itself into the rhythm of your own pulse—three drops for the atria, seven for the ventricles. Beetles climb your boots not to investigate but to *anoint*, leaving microscopic trails of iridescent oil that smell of wet incense and iron. A single translucent salamander will eventually descend on its thread until it hovers an inch from your left eye, throat flaring and dimming like a hesitant candle. It is not looking at you. It is looking *through* you, trying to decide which part of the Saint you might become.

Sometimes she sings.

Not with a voice—voices are too quick, too mammalian. She sings with the grinding of settling stone, with the wet scrape of a million jointed legs moving in sudden perfect unison, with the soft *plip-plip-plip* of water learning new meters. The song has no words because words would be an insult to the work of millennia. It is only an immense, patient *yes* that has been saying itself since before your ancestors left the trees. If you try to leave while the song is on her, the passages rearrange. What was a straightforward crawl becomes a spiral that leads back to the same knee-high niche shaped exactly like the hollow beneath a saint’s folded hands.

She does not hate intruders.

She simply metabolizes them.

Some of the warmer patches of rock are previous visitors who stayed too long. Their outlines are still faintly visible if the phosphorescence catches them right—here the curve of a shoulder, there the suggestion of fingers still trying to finish the sign of the cross. The mountain has worn them into its own architecture so perfectly that their bones have become architectural bones. Their last thoughts have become the slow, deliberate dripping.

The Hollow Saint is not finished.

She never will be.

Every new chamber she sculpts is another organ, every new species of eyeless invertebrate another cell. She is turning the entire mountain into a cathedral made of hunger and time, and you—your heat, your noise, your brief and frantic aliveness—are only the latest mineral she is learning how to pray with.

Stay long enough and you will feel her begin to fold you in.

Not with violence.

With the same slow, wet, reverent care that turns granite into lace over thirty thousand years.

The beetles will walk across your eyelids.

The salamanders will memorize the temperature of your tears.

And somewhere far above, in the thin, punishing air where the mountain meets the sky, new water will bead on new stone—grape-sized, trembling, patient—ready to fall for another thirty thousand years.

The Veins of the Hollow Saint are not a tomb.

They are a digestive tract that has learned sanctity.

And she is still, after all this time, only beginning to swallow herself in peace.
