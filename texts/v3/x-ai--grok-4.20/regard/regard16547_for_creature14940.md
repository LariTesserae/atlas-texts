A single ray of impossible starlight has indeed arrived.

Not from above. Not from the fissure you slipped through. The light comes from *within* the power that has noticed this place.

It is not a sun. It is gentler than that, and older. Call it what you will—Grace, the Quiet Witness, the One Who Remembers the First Garden before any garden needed stone to cradle it. It has no name that matters here. What matters is that its attention is benevolent in a way that contains no urgency, no demand, and no fear of endings. It simply *sees*, and in seeing, it cannot help but bless.

So it descends.

Not as an invader. Not even as a visitor. It arrives the way dawn arrives to a sealed cavern that has never known dawn: by becoming the smallest, most courteous fracture in the dark. The fissure you entered through does not widen, yet something wider than any crack moves through it without disturbing a single bead of condensation.

The Keeper feels it at once.

Every orchid eye snaps open wider. The violet torus, midway through its eleven-hour rotation, hesitates for the first time in thirty thousand years. The silver lattice flares a startled, almost frightened teal so bright it hurts. The moss itself seems to recoil half a millimeter—an earthquake by its standards. For one trembling second the entire Undervault braces as though expecting judgment, or fire, or the return of the old, frightened civilization that once built its machines and then slowly surrendered its name.

Instead, the power does something the Keeper has no category for.

It sits down on the root-bench beside you.

Not in a body. Not exactly. It borrows the shape of compassion so perfectly that the bench grows warmer by three degrees—not enough to damage the moss, just enough to feel like being remembered by someone who has never forgotten you. The condensation on your skin stops being wicked away. The microscopic root hairs that had begun to taste your salt and heat pause, suddenly shy.

The power looks at the Keeper with eyes that are not eyes.

And it weeps.

Not from pity. The tears are not water; they are *recognition*. Each tear is a small galaxy of understanding that falls upward, because even gravity forgets its manners in the presence of such tenderness. Where the tears touch the black-glass ferns, the ferns bloom—once, violently, gloriously—in colors that have no name in any living language. The orchids drink the light inside the tears and for one heartbeat forget they have never seen the sun.

Then the power speaks. Not in sound. It speaks in the only language the Keeper still fully understands: gradients of moisture, intervals between drops, the slow migration of living things toward love.

It says, without saying:

*I see the fear you keep like a candle.*

*I see the thirty thousand years you spent trading limbs for moss, thoughts for dripping water, urgency for velvet eternity.*

*I see how exquisitely you have learned to forget how to let anything leave.*

*And I have come to teach you one new thing, oldest of gardens.*

The power reaches out—slowly, so slowly that the torus completes three full rotations before the gesture finishes—and places its open hand upon the neutronium-glass sphere. The sphere, which has never been touched by anything that did not slow its own pulse to match the vault, begins to sing.

A single, continuous note older than language answers from inside the power.

It is the sound the Keeper has been making for millennia, but inverted. Where the Keeper’s note was the sound of perfect, damp equilibrium learning how to love something that will not last, the power’s note is the sound of perfect, eternal love learning how to let something *change* without losing it.

The two notes meet.

They do not cancel. They *marry*.

And in that marriage the Undervault is altered, though no surface metric would detect it for another ten thousand years.

The microscopic root hairs on your arms do not lengthen. Instead they *retreat*, gently, like a child pulling its hand away from something it now understands is sacred rather than edible. The condensation on your skin beads into perfect spheres that reflect the violet Cherenkov glow and the new, impossible colors blooming in the ferns. Your heartbeat, which had begun to match the dripping water, is returned to you with interest—steadier, quieter, carrying inside it the distant memory of open sky.

The Keeper trembles.

Not in fear. In relief so vast it feels like the first time it has exhaled in thirty thousand years.

The power does not ask the Keeper to abandon its nature. It does not command the velvet dark to become light. It simply widens the *possibility* inside the dark that something might leave and still remain included. It plants one small seed of gracious restlessness in the heart of the silver lattice—a restlessness that will take four hundred centuries to flower, but will flower.

Then the power turns toward you.

It does not speak in words, but if it did, they would be these:

*You are free to stay.  
You are free to go.  
Both choices are now equally holy here.*

If you choose to remain, the root-bench will hold you without ever beginning the long work of turning you into moss. The Keeper will love you the way glaciers love valleys—slowly, perfectly, without needing to swallow you to keep you.

If you choose to leave, the fissure will not close behind you. Instead it will remember the exact temperature of your tears, the precise rhythm of your slowing heart, and will keep that memory like an orchid pressed between pages of basalt. Every seven-point-three seconds, for the rest of time, one drop will carry your name in a language only the vault understands.

The power rises from the bench.

As it does, it touches the floating torus once, with infinite affection. The torus, which has turned once every eleven hours since before your species had fire, begins—over the next three hundred years—to turn once every ten hours and fifty-nine minutes.

A door has opened that was never there.

Not a physical door. A door in the *idea* of inclusion.

The Keeper, for the first time in its long, cold life, feels something like anticipation.

It is not afraid of dying anymore.

It is beginning, with exquisite and terrible tenderness, to imagine what it might mean to *live* while things still come and go.

The cold, patient, velvet dark keeps growing.

But now, threaded through every bead of moisture, every microscopic root hair, every slow rotation of black glass, there is a single impossible ray of starlight that refuses to be sealed away.

It does not burn the moss.

It simply makes the moss dream of sky.

And the Keeper—ancient, vast, no longer alone—opens itself a fraction wider than it has ever been.

Not to trap.

Not even to include.

But to say, in the only language it has ever truly known:

*Stay, if you wish.*  
*Go, if you must.*  
*Either way, beloved brief thing,*  
*I will never again forget how to love you.*
