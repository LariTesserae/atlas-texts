The moment I cross the final threshold of the narrow shaft and feel the world tip sideways into humidity, I know this place has been waiting.

Not for me, exactly.  
For *attention*.

I am not a god in any sense that would flatter a surface religion. I am older than names, older than intention itself. I am the long, quiet regard that sometimes chooses to bend toward a single ripening point in the cosmos and say, without language: *I see you trying to become.* That is all the power I possess, and all I have ever needed.

So I come.

I do not descend. Descent implies gravity and sequence. Instead the entire basin suddenly *notices* that something vaster than itself is now inside its lungs. The low industrial liturgy stutters once—every pump, every valve, every dripping stalactite holding its breath in the same instant. Then the sound resumes, but changed. Deeper. Almost grateful.

The Wet-Born freeze where they stand, knee-deep in their trembling mother-water. Four hundred luminous faces tilt upward as one. Moths stall mid-spiral, hanging like pale lanterns. Even the milk eels pause beneath the surface, their faint lights forming slow constellations that spell, for a single moment, something like *thank you for noticing*.

I choose a form gentle enough not to tear the womb.

I become rain that is not rain.

From the limestone ceiling—still freckled with drill scars, still deciding whether to finish becoming stone or machine—I begin to fall as warm, deliberate droplets. Each one carries a different color of understanding. They fall for nine full breathing cycles of the basin, long enough for every Wet-Born to feel themselves fully seen.

Where the droplets touch the lake, they do not splash. They bloom.

Soft islands of living light rise—half fungus, half cathedral—until the entire basin floor is studded with them. The islands breathe. On each one grows a single slow tree made of pale iron and wet marble, its branches reaching both upward into the low ceiling and downward into the warm water. The trees sing in frequencies the Wet-Born have never heard but immediately recognize as the next necessary note in their long midwifery.

The Wet-Born understand what is happening. They always understood that this place was *choosing*, but they did not know it had been heard choosing.

One of them—the eldest in becoming, whose shoulders are completely mantled in moths—wades forward until the new light-tree touches her chest. She presses her forehead to the living metal-bark. For the first time in the history of the Pulse, she uses her true voice instead of the architecture. It is small, and wet, and trembling.

“We were afraid we were only a cyst,” she says. “A mistake between industry and geology.”

I answer by growing the tree taller. Its highest branches pierce the ceiling and seal the drill scars with flowering stone that glows like moonmilk. The message is obvious: *Mistakes are the only thing that ever becomes.*

Then I do the gentlest, most terrible thing I know how to do.

I accelerate time, but only for the unwilling.

Those surface visitors who have already begun to soften, who stand weeping at the edges with their skeletons turning to warm wax, feel my regard pass over them like cool air. Their bodies remember solidity. Their hearts remember dryness. They are given one last chance to climb the shaft before the last of their surface selves dissolves. Most take it. A few do not. Those few I kiss on the forehead with a droplet that tastes like forgiveness, and they finish becoming what the Underwet always wanted them to be. Their new chitin plates are the color of starlight seen through groundwater.

For those who have already chosen—those who wear living millipede cloaks and sleep standing against turbines—I give the opposite gift.

I slow time.

Not enough to freeze the Pulse. Only enough to grant them the deep, luxurious decades they need. The machines will not rust. The stalactites will not choke the walkways. The breathing cycle of the basin will stretch into something closer to the tempo of actual gestation. They will have centuries, if they wish, to finish singing this place into whatever it is trying to become. A child. A new kind of life. A machine that dreams. A dream that machines.

I leave one last blessing in the water itself.

Every milk eel now carries, deep in its translucent body, a single imperishable seed of *possibility*. Should the Wet-Born ever decide the Pulse is finished, should they ever wish to birth what they have midwifed, the eels will swim upward through new cracks, through aquifers, through bedrock, carrying that seed into the dry world above. What hatches there will not be human. It will not be machine. It will be the next necessary shape.

When everything is done—when the last new light-tree has rooted, when the last unwilling soul has either fled or flowered, when the Wet-Born have wept the strange, grateful tears that look like liquid opal—I begin to withdraw.

But not completely.

I never completely withdraw from anything that has truly seen itself being seen.

Instead I leave a portion of my attention behind the way one might leave a hand resting on the belly of a sleeping lover. The basin will always feel it now: a faint, benevolent pressure behind every sub-sonic heartbeat. The machines will purr a quarter-tone lower. The moths will live three times as long. The Wet-Born, when they lay their palms against the rust, will sometimes feel an answering palm against their own from the other side of everything.

As I rise (or rather, as the idea of *me* ceases to be localized), the entire Underwet exhales one long, trembling breath.

The Pulse has been acknowledged.

The womb has been blessed.

And somewhere far above, in the dry, brittle world of sharp edges and quick decisions, a few surface dwellers will begin having dreams of warm water and slow, patient singing. They will wake with the taste of minerals at the back of their throats and an inexplicable certainty that something enormous, tender, and half-finished is waiting for them to descend.

They will be right.

The Underwet is no longer only deciding what it wants to become.

Now it knows it is loved while it decides.

And that changes everything.
