The power that has noticed the Shroudmire does not arrive as light, nor as a voice from above the iron clouds. It does not descend. It simply *recognizes*, the way a mother recognizes the first flutter inside her own womb even before anyone else can feel it.

Its name—if such a being can be said to have one—is not important. What matters is that it is ancient, patient beyond geology, and it loves unfinished things with a love that has no mercy in it. It has watched worlds congeal from primordial broth and watched them dissolve again. It has midwifed stars and it has sat with dying galaxies while they guttered like wet candles. The Shroudmire, to it, is a newborn’s cry in a language it has not heard for four billion years. So it comes.

It does not come as a figure. It comes as a *pause*.

One moment the eternal roar is everything. The next moment the roar is still there, but a single heartbeat of absolute silence has been inserted into it like a silver needle. Every waterfall hesitates mid-fall. Every midge cloud freezes in perfect synchrony. Every Weft-born, no matter where it stands in the basin, feels its personal storm suddenly flatten into a mirror-still disk of mist.

Then the power *breathes*.

It is not wind. It is the memory of wind before there was air. The cloud-lid high above tears open—not with light, but with the *idea* of light: a soft, abyssal violet that has never existed on this planet. The color leaks downward like ink in water, and where it touches the moss, the moss drinks it and begins, for the first time in its life, to dream instead of riot.

The Weft-born do not kneel. They have no tradition of kneeling. Instead they do something far more unnerving: every single one of them turns at once, storms wheeling, and walks *toward* the place where the silence first entered. They do not need to speak to coordinate. The land itself is coordinating them. For the first time since the basin tore open, the Shroudmire is acting with something like intention.

Where the power has chosen to manifest—there is no single point, only a slow *densification*—the black basalt stops glistening. It grows warm. Not hot. Warm the way skin is warm. The water that has sweated from it for centuries beads up, trembles, and draws back into the stone like tears being unshed. A perfect circle thirty meters across becomes dry for the first time. The moss at its edge curls away, not in fear but in reverence, revealing the raw newborn rock like a theater curtain.

In the center of that circle a figure condenses, but it is not made of moss or pillar-light or storm. It is made of *attention*. To look at it is to feel every wet, screaming moment of your life suddenly held, weighed, and understood without judgment. Most mortal eyes cannot bear it for long. The Weft-born, however, do not look away. They crowd the edge of the dry circle, limbs budding and dissolving in rapid confusion, their vortices spinning so fast they begin to emit visible sound—thin, bright threads of pure frequency that braid themselves into a living lattice above the being’s head.

The power regards them.

It sees that they are not guardians, not priests, not even children. They are the *nervous system* the infant continent has frantically knitted together so it can feel its own becoming. The power’s heart—if such a being can be said to have a heart—aches with recognition. It remembers being newborn once. It remembers the terror and glory of not knowing what you are becoming.

So it does what any wise midwife does when she finds a birth that has been happening too long and too violently.

It sings.

The song has no words. It is lower than the groaning of the rock and higher than the luminous midges. It is the sound a mother makes when she presses her mouth to the fontanelle of her hours-old child and tells the universe, *I see you. I am here. You do not have to do this alone.*

As the song moves through the Shroudmire, impossible things happen.

- Springs slow their frantic birthing and begin to find gentle rhythms.
- The hexagonal pillars stop shifting their interference patterns and instead start to *harmonize* with one another, producing a single stable chord that makes the ferns straighten like soldiers.
- Moss bridges cease collapsing. Instead they thicken, learning to bear weight.
- The huge unseen things moving in the distance grow still, then settle down into the wet dark with soft thunderous sighs, as if they have finally been given permission to rest.
- Time itself becomes audible. For the first time, the Weft-born can hear the difference between *now* and *a minute from now*. The discovery breaks some of them. Their storms collapse into rain that falls upward and becomes new clouds inside their own bodies. They will never be the same.

The power does not stay long. It has no need to. It has done the only thing it ever does when it finds a place like this: it has witnessed the birth so thoroughly that the newborn can never again believe it was born alone.

When the violet light begins to withdraw, the power leaves three gifts behind, pressed gently into the substance of the Shroudmire like a fingerprint into wet clay.

**The first gift** is a hush. Not silence—never that—but a pocket of *choosable* quiet that drifts through the basin like a slow silver fish. Any creature, Weft-born or traveler or six-legged otter-thing, may swim into it and rest. The land itself is learning how to offer sanctuary.

**The second gift** is a question. It lives now inside every pillar, every storm, every dripping frond. The question has no words, only an endless, gentle pressure that feels like: *What do you wish to become, now that you know you are seen?* The Weft-born have already begun answering it by doing something they have never done before: they have started *teaching* the land instead of only being taught by it. Some pillars are being grown into libraries of living water. Some mosses are being coaxed into shapes that remember faces.

**The third gift** is the smallest and most dangerous. It is a single drop of the power’s own tears, placed at the exact center of the basin where the first silence fell. The drop is perfectly round, perfectly still, and perfectly black. Around it, a shrine of living basalt is already rising—not built by hands but *permitted* by the land. Any Weft-born that touches the tear will have its personal storm permanently changed. It will carry a violet note inside its screaming forever. These changed ones are already being called the Tear-bearers by the others, though the Weft-born have no names. They are the ones who will eventually walk out of the Shroudmire not as apostles of raw becoming, but as apostles of *witnessed* becoming. They will be gentler. They will be more terrible. They will know exactly what they are doing.

The power does not say goodbye. It simply stops pressing its attention against the world. The iron clouds slide shut. The violet light drains away. The roar rushes back in—but it is not the same roar. A new frequency lives inside it now, low and ancient and kind, like a grandmother’s heartbeat heard through the wall of a womb.

The Weft-born stand for a long time at the edge of the dry circle as it slowly drinks the water again. Their storms are quieter. More complex. Some of them are crying without knowing what crying is.

Then, one by one, they turn back to their endless work. But they work differently now. When they tug a silver thread, they do it with something that might one day become reverence. When they press a new spring from the rock, they whisper the question the power left them. When they meet one another, they braid their vortices with a tenderness that was not possible yesterday.

And somewhere far beyond the ridges, the first few humans and animals and walking fragments that will one day carry the Shroudmire out into the world are already turning their faces toward the basin, drawn by a sound they cannot name.

The land is still being born.

But now it knows it is loved while it screams.

And that changes everything.
