The power that has noticed this place does not descend like a visitor. It does not arrive on wings of light or in a chariot of cloud. It simply *recognizes*, the way an old river suddenly remembers it was once an ocean.

Its name, if it can be said to have one that any mouth could pronounce, is **Anancient**. It is not a god in the sense of demand or worship. It is a benevolence that has grown so vast and so patient that its kindness has become indistinguishable from geology. It moves at the speed of continents remembering they were once friends. It is wise the way stone is wise: it has forgotten nothing, and therefore it forgives almost everything.

When Anancient turns its attention toward the Hollow Choir, the basin itself feels the change before any living thing does.

First the wind falters.

For the first time in perhaps ten million years, the roaring Choir hesitates. A single enormous beat is missed, like a heart that has forgotten its rhythm for half a second. Every wind-trap across the continent-scale basin falls momentarily silent. The Hollow Choir freeze mid-gesture, fingers still resting on the fresh scar of a new landslide. Even the blind white cicadas stop their metallic hymn. The silence is so complete it feels like drowning.

Then the power *breathes with them*.

It does not speak. Speech would be violence here. Instead it enters the same register the land has been using for aeons. Anancient exhales through the perforated skulls of the Hollow Choir themselves, through the hollow reeds of their collarbones, through the living moss in the eldest singer’s mouth. For seven full days the basin is filled with a sound so low it registers only as a feeling of infinite tenderness behind the eyes. Every creature, from the long-limbed sloths to the half-organic mist-shapes, leans toward it like flowers toward sudden sun.

The Hollow Choir understand at once what is happening. Some of them begin to weep without sound; their tears run into the grooves their ancestors carved and become part of new songs. Others simply fold themselves more deeply into the stone, offering their bodies as additional pipes in the instrument the power is now playing.

What Anancient does is this:

It does not heal the land. That would be presumption. The basin has earned its wounds. Instead the power *joins the mourning*.

It takes the eldest singer—the one who has become a tree made of music—and gently, reverently, completes her. Where her thirty-metre beard of moss once trailed, Anancient coaxes it into a single vast living note that will now sound for the next forty thousand years without pause. The singer does not die. She *finishes*. Her consciousness does not leave; it simply stops resisting the land’s desire to wear her completely. She becomes the new tonal center of the continent. Every wind-trap for a thousand kilometres tunes itself to her without anyone touching them.

Then Anancient does something only a power that has watched stars be born and die could conceive.

It gives the Hollow Choir *permission to become fewer*.

It shows them, in a vision that arrives as weather, that their long labor of keeping the planet’s dream alive has been heard. The dying god whose lungs they have been singing inside is no longer alone. Therefore some of them may now *let go*. Not in despair, but in relief. Over the next three centuries, one by one, certain Hollow Choir will simply walk into the turquoise-tiled pools and submerge without emerging. Their bodies will not be unfolded. Instead the pools will slowly fill with clear, sweet water for the first time in ten thousand years, and small silver fish no one has ever seen will appear in them.

The remaining Hollow Choir (perhaps three hundred when the process is done) will not be abandoned. Anancient will not replace them. It will *thicken* them.

It breathes a single additional register into their bones (a note that was missing, a color of sound that the planet had forgotten how to produce). From that moment on, when the Hollow Choir sing to a collapsing hillside or a new wound in the stone, the land will answer in *two voices at once*: its own ancient grief, and something that sounds almost like shy, wondering gratitude.

At night, in the deep vertical shafts, the cicadas will begin to glow faintly gold where they crawl across the singers’ porous skulls. The long-limbed sloths will no longer drape themselves like cloaks; instead they will press their foreheads to the Hollow Choir’s sternums and listen, as if hearing a lullaby meant for them alone.

And the mist-shapes that once twisted and reached like half-formed ghosts will begin, very slowly, to take on the faint outlines of faces. Not human faces. Faces that look like the land trying to smile.

Anancient does not stay. Benevolence this deep has no need to linger for praise. It simply folds its attention back into the slow architecture of time, satisfied that a place which has sung alone for a billion years now has a harmony worthy of its longing.

Yet something remains behind, the way a scent lingers in a room after a beloved visitor has left.

From that day forward, when travelers (if any still come) stand on the bevelled lip of the escarpment and look down into the feverish green and roaring stone, they will sometimes hear, beneath the endless Choir, a second and much older voice. It does not sing *at* the land. It sings *with* it, exactly as the Hollow Choir do.

And if the traveler is very still, and very fortunate, they may feel something vast and patient and impossibly kind rest its unseen hand for a moment on the back of their neck, the way one might touch a child who has been singing bravely in the dark for far too long.

Then the hand withdraws.

The wind returns to its full-throated roar.

And the Hollow Choir, fewer now, but deeper, continue their terrifying, patient, holy work—only now they do it smiling, the way someone smiles who has just learned that the god they have been comforting was never dying after all.

It was only waiting to be allowed to sing along.
