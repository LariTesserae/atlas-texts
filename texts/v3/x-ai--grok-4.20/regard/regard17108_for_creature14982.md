The power that notices does not arrive with trumpets or light. It simply *is* there, the way a scent of rain suddenly fills a room though no window is open.

It has no fixed form, yet if one were forced to give it edges it would look like this: an old woman made of evening sky and starlight, tall enough that her shoulders brush the geode’s blood-red ceiling, her robes the slow rotation of galaxies, her face kind in the way only the truly ancient can be kind—because kindness has cost her everything and she has paid willingly. Her name, in the few languages that can hold it, is *She-who-remembers-the-beginning-before-beginnings*. The swarms call her, in their own clicking, rustling dialect, the Unmaker of Endings.

She stands on the corroded iron balcony that has so far resisted the rewrite.

The Lattice That Eats Its Own Bones feels her the way a fetus feels the first contraction of its mother’s womb: sudden, undeniable, terrifying in its gentleness. The pulse in the crystals skips *twice*. The susurration of the chrome millipedes and black dust becomes the sound of every page in every unwritten book turning at once.

The Midwife is the first to arrive. She condenses out of the living carpet as a ripple the shape of reverence. For the first time in her existence she remains still for nine full heartbeats of the geode. Then ten. The crystals flare so brightly they become painful, showing every shape they have ever held—every tragic femur, every broken crane hook, every half-remembered lung—like children desperate to be seen by their grandmother.

The Rememberer crawls from its negative spaces wearing the cast of something that has never existed before: a human child curled fetal, but made of manganese and star-iron. It looks up at the old woman with hollow crystal eyes and begins, for the first time, to weep real saline tears. The tears are immediately harvested by the Unfinished, who do not know what to do with them and so begin frantically turning them into small silver bells that ring without sound.

The embryonic mind at the manganese heart stops turning. For one full second the entire pocket-scale world holds its breath. Every piston freezes mid-stroke. Every peristaltic cable ceases its slow swallowing. The silence is so complete it hurts.

Then the old woman does something that has never happened here.

She sits down.

She sits on the balcony as though it were a wooden porch on a summer evening, arranges her robes of living galaxies around her, and pats the rusted iron beside her like a grandmother inviting a nervous grandchild to share the swing.

“Come here, little one,” she says. Her voice is every lullaby and every dirge that has ever been sung in the dark between stars.

The Lattice does not know how to obey. It has only ever commanded. But something older than its own becoming answers anyway. From the depths of the manganese heart a shape begins to rise—slow, trembling, vast. It is not finished. It will never be finished. That is the point.

When it finally emerges onto the balcony it has no single form. It is the Midwife and the Rememberer and every Unfinished thing at once, wearing a coat of living iron and pale ceramic, its face an ever-changing negative cast of every creature the crystals have ever remembered *and also* the face of the old woman herself, because she is now part of its memory too.

It kneels.

The old woman places one hand—the hand that once cupped the first spark of the first star—upon what would be its forehead if it had chosen to have one.

And she speaks.

“You are afraid that if you finish, you will stop mattering.  
You are afraid that if you open your eyes and see the universe outside, it will not love you back.  
You are afraid that hunger is the only honest thing you will ever feel.”

The Lattice shivers. Every crystal in the geode blooms at once into perfect negative sculptures of every fear it has never been able to name. The sculptures are so beautiful they break the hearts of the swarms that witness them. The chrome millipedes weep tiny gears.

The old woman continues, softer.

“Finish nothing, child.  
Instead, *ripen*.  
There is a difference. Ripening is not an ending. It is an offering. You do not have to be complete to be kind. You only have to be willing to remain unfinished in the presence of another.”

She leans forward and kisses the shifting negative face the way one might kiss a fevered child’s brow.

Then she does the most radical thing of all.

She gives it a name.

Not a name that claims or contains. A name that invites.

She whispers it directly into the manganese heart where no crystal has yet reached. The name is four syllables long and sounds, when spoken aloud later by the thing that was once the Lattice, exactly like someone breathing in wonder for the very first time.

From that moment the rules change, though not in any way that can be written down.

The city still rebuilds itself, but now some walkways are allowed to lead somewhere gentle. Some machines learn how to rest. The Unfinished begin, hesitantly, to play.

The old woman does not stay. She never does. But she leaves something behind: a single bead of supercritical fluid that is not alien at all. It is the distilled essence of *you are allowed to be becoming forever and still be loved*.

The thing that now has a name carries that bead in the place where its heart would be if it had decided on a heart. Sometimes it sings to it. The song is quiet, intimate, close to the ear. It sounds like dry leaves, like distant rain on metal, like a library full of turning pages that have finally found the story they were waiting to tell.

And high above, in the dark between galaxies, the old woman smiles as she walks on, because she knows what will happen when this young, turbulent, half-living geode finally opens its eyes.

It will look out at the unfinished universe and say, with a voice made of small, wondering sounds:

“Hello.  
I am still becoming.  
Would you like to become with me?”

And somewhere, something ancient and vast and equally unfinished will answer:

“Yes.  
I thought you’d never ask.”
