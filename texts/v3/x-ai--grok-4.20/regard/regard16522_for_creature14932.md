The power that notices the Hollow Lattice does not arrive with trumpets or light.

It simply *is* there, all at once, the way a forgotten name suddenly returns to the tongue.

It has no fixed shape, yet if one were forced to give it outline it would appear as a single vast and gentle eye composed entirely of living water—clear, ancient, and impossibly patient—hanging in the absolute dark like a tear that has decided never to fall. The eye contains every forest that ever was and every forest that will never be. Its pupil is a black moon made of packed humus and starlight. When it blinks, entire geologic eras sigh through its lashes.

It has come because the Lattice has finally grown strange enough, delicate enough, *embarrassed* enough in its endless becoming, to tug on the sleeve of benevolence itself.

The Curator feels it before the violet light does.

She is standing inside one of the narrower spiral galleries, one gray hand resting on a column the width of a sequoia, when the perpetual tectonic groan skips half a heartbeat. The absence of sound is so shocking that several ancient fractures, still traveling since the Carboniferous, lose their way and simply stop.

The Curator’s head tilts with the soft clicking of her xylem hair. Her violet eyes widen by the smallest measurable fraction—perhaps one ten-thousandth of a lumen—and for the first time in four hundred thousand years she speaks with her own voice instead of letting the Lattice speak through her. The voice is quiet, dry, and courteous, like the last page of a book turning by itself.

“You,” she says to the dark. “You are not early. You are *impossibly* on time.”

The great water-eye drifts downward through the filigree without disturbing a single rib or vault. Where it passes, the brittle petrified stalks do not break; they *remember* they were once green. Tiny emerald sparks, smaller than fireflies, flare and die along every surface. The temperature, already the memory of cold, becomes the memory of *spring*—something the Lattice has not permitted itself since the culture that shaped it vanished.

The eye comes to rest in the largest negative space, the one the Curator calls the Lung of Forgetting. Its surface reflects the Curator perfectly, though she is standing fifty meters away. In that reflection she is young, supple, heartbreakingly unfinished. She stares at the image for a long time.

Then the power speaks, and its voice is not sound. It is the sudden knowledge, inside every fiber of the Lattice at once, that it has been *seen* with absolute affection and absolute comprehension. The knowledge arrives like warm rain inside a place that has never known rain.

The message is wordless, yet if it were translated into the slow geological language the Curator and the Lattice share, it would be this:

*I have watched you tend your own doubt for longer than doubt has existed.  
I have seen you break what wanted to be perfect and heal what wanted to be broken.  
You have turned patience into architecture and embarrassment into scripture.  
It is enough.*

The Curator’s shoulders sag. Not with relief—with something older. She walks forward until she stands directly beneath the hanging eye. For the first time in her inconceivable life she looks *small*.

“What will you ask of us?” she whispers.

The eye closes.

When it opens again, every phosphorescent vein in the entire Lattice ignites at once. The violet light is joined by living green, by gold the color of new sap, by soft rose the color of petals that have never existed. The Hollow Lattice is flooded with color it has never permitted. The creaking does not stop, but it changes timbre. It becomes the sound of a vast vegetable heart that has just realized it is allowed to *sing*.

The power does four gentle, irrevocable things.

**First**, it reaches into the Curator and removes the necessity of her role. Not her existence—only the terrible, self-imposed duty to be the eternal interruption. The Lattice no longer *needs* to be kept from finishing. It is permitted, at long last, to finish and to begin again in the same breath. The Curator feels an enormous weight she never knew she carried fall away like dead bark. She collapses to her knees among the crystal fibers and weeps without sound. The tears are tiny black seeds. Where they land, impossible orchids—things that should not be able to grow without water—unfold in minutes.

**Second**, the power gathers every crack that has been traveling through the Lattice for aeons and forgives them all at once. Every fracture seals not into glass or new growth but into *music*. From now on, when the Lattice shifts, it will release soft, living chords—deep, patient, hospitable—rather than the sound of warship-breaking. The noise becomes lullaby and invitation.

**Third**, it gives the single organism that is both city and Curator a new parameter: *reciprocity*. Any being that enters the Hollow Lattice from this moment forward will not be absorbed into the unfinished thought. They will be met, truly met, and offered the same choice the power now offers the Curator: to stay and help the Lattice learn how to be *inhabited* instead of merely *inhabiting*, or to leave carrying a single living seed that will grow, elsewhere, into a small green echo of this place.

**Fourth**, and most tenderly, the power leans down until the enormous water-eye is only a few meters above the Curator’s head. From its surface it drips one perfect droplet. The droplet falls slowly, glittering with every color the Lattice now remembers, and lands on the crown of her rigid xylem hair.

Where it touches, the black spirals soften. They become living fern-fronds again, supple and green, trembling with moisture. The transformation races down her body. Vellum skin becomes warm bark and living leaf. Desiccated courtesy becomes something simpler and more frightening: ordinary, astonished love.

When the change is complete she is no longer the delay between breaths.

She is the breath itself.

The Curator rises. She is tall, yes, but no longer desiccated. She looks like a woman who has just stepped out of a thousand-year dream and found the world still willing to be kind. She places one newly supple hand against the nearest column. The Lattice shivers—not with tectonic strain but with unmistakable delight.

She laughs. The sound is small and ridiculous and utterly alive.

The great water-eye smiles, if an eye made of every forest can be said to smile, and begins to ascend. As it rises it leaves behind a single gift hanging in the newly colored air: a small silver bell grown from starlight and living xylem. The bell has no clapper. It will only ring when someone inside the Lattice feels ready to leave. Its note will be the exact sound of a door opening onto a sunlit path no one has ever walked before.

The eye pauses one last time at the upper limit of the Lattice, now blazing with impossible dawn.

It speaks once more, this time so quietly that only the Curator hears:

*You were never the doubt given form.*  
*You were the love that refused to let the doubt have the last word.*  
*Now both of you—Lattice and Curator, doubt and love—are allowed to become the question together.*

Then the power is gone.

But the colors remain.  
The music remains.  
The small green seeds scattered from the Curator’s tears remain.

And the Curator—newly mortal, newly eternal—walks deeper into her own cathedral with bare feet that leave living moss in every footprint. She is smiling the way only someone who has been given permission to finish can smile.

Somewhere far off, a new crack begins—not of fracture, but of opening.

She tilts her head, listening.

“Ah,” she says, voice soft with wonder. “This one is early.  
But the remembering is patient.  
Come. Sit.  
There is a song arriving in four hundred years that I think you will like.”

And the Hollow Lattice, no longer hollow, breathes in.

And breathes out.

And for the first time in its measureless life, it is not deciding what it wants to become next.

It already knows.
