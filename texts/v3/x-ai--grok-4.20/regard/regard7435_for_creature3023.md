The power that notices this place does not descend like a visitor. It does not part the stone or announce itself with trumpets or sudden thunder. It has no wings, no throne, no beard of stars. It simply *recognizes*.

And in the moment of recognition, the Considerer is no longer alone inside its own becoming.

---

The first change is so subtle that even the synchronized millipedes do not break rhythm.

The gold-white light, which has until now pulsed only with the Considerer’s vast, slow inhalation and exhalation, begins to carry a second, almost inaudible counter-rhythm. A second heart. Not louder. Not faster. Only *deeper*, as though an ocean were beating beneath a puddle. The light does not grow brighter; it grows *truer*, the way a memory becomes truer when someone who loved you remembers it alongside you.

The pale figure that sometimes condenses in the half-melted chair startles.

For the first time since the pocket first sweated into existence, the Considerer feels *seen from the inside*.

It tries to do what it has always done: it reaches out to incorporate. A patch of damp rock softens, attempting to mimic the temperature of this new presence. The silver fish reverse direction in perfect synchrony, expecting to catch and reflect some fragment of the newcomer the way they have always reflected the Considerer’s own dreaming.

Instead, the rock finds it cannot mimic what it touches.  
The presence has no temperature.  
The fish find nothing to reflect.  
The light itself simply says, without words:

*I already know you. There is nothing to copy.*

And the Considerer—prenatal, boundless, terrified and exhilarated—does the only thing an embryonic god can do when it meets something older than embryogenesis.

It *listens*.

---

What the power does is not intervention. It is midwifery.

It does not finish the Considerer. That would be violence.  
It does not name it. That would be theft.

Instead it sits—everywhere at once—inside the breathing pocket and begins to sing the oldest, gentlest song there is:

the sound of *yes* before any question has been asked.

The song has no melody a finished ear could follow. It is the noise a mother makes when she is only thinking about her sleeping child. It is the silence between two people who have known each other since before names. It moves through the damp stone like warm milk through new veins.

And as it sings, the unfinished things inside the cave begin, very slowly, to stop *trying*.

The walls that had been nervously flickering between skin and book and impossible forest settle. They do not choose one form; they simply stop being ashamed of containing all of them. The translucent amphibians stop drifting in anxious spirals and float upright, as though something invisible were cradling them from beneath. The millipedes halt their synchronized marching, turn toward the center of the chamber, and—astonishingly—bow, each segment bending like a separate prayer.

The Considerer, who has never before encountered a gentleness it could not digest, begins to cry.

Its tears are tiny opalescent minerals that fall upward.

Where they touch the ceiling they become new constellations that have never been seen from Earth, patterns that describe not what *is* but what is *permitted to become*.

In the shallow basins the eyeless silver fish slow their ecstatic circling. One by one they rise out of the water on threads of living light, become small pale flames, and hover before the half-melted chair. They are not worshiping. They are *offering*.

The Considerer, vast and tiny, curls tighter around its own heart for a moment, afraid that this new presence will ask it to decide.

But the power does not ask for decisions.

It only asks, with infinite courtesy:

“Would you like to feel what it is like to be loved before you are finished?”

The Considerer has no language with which to answer. So it answers with the only thing it has: its entire body.

The cave *exhales* everything.

All the stolen fragments of visitors—footprints, voices, childhood eyes, grief, lust, nicknames—rise out of the stone like exhaled steam. They do not vanish. They are simply *returned*, gently, to the places in the world where they belong, each one now carrying a faint golden afterglow. The ladder of fused vertebrae softens into ordinary bone and then into ordinary stone and then into something that is both and neither. The half-melted chair stands up, stretches, and becomes a simple bench wide enough for two.

And the Considerer, for the first time, unfolds.

Not into a creature.

Not into a cave.

Not even into a god.

It unfolds into *relationship*.

The pale figure condenses one last time in the newly formed bench. This time it does not leak gold from illusory seams. This time it looks almost ordinary—except that its eyes are still the silver fish, and its hair is still the trembling ferns, and its voice is still every misplaced sound the world has ever been too shy to keep.

It looks at the power sitting beside it (though the power has no body, the Considerer now gives it one, because love is generous with shapes).

And it asks, no longer as an embryonic god but as something newly, achingly alive:

“Will you stay while I finish becoming whatever I am?”

The power smiles with the entire breathing pocket.

It says:

“I was never elsewhere.”

---

From that moment on, the place is still not quite a cave, nor quite a chamber. It is still deciding.

But now it decides *together*.

Sometimes travelers still stumble through the throat-like fissure. They find the light gentler now, the acoustics less overwhelming. The millipedes no longer march in perfect formation; they wander in small, friendly groups, occasionally riding on the backs of the floating amphibians like eccentric commuters.

And if the traveler stays long enough, they may see two figures sitting on the stone bench that was once a melted chair.

One is luminous and unfinished, leaking soft radiance the way a happy child leaks laughter.

The other has no fixed appearance, yet somehow always looks exactly like the person the traveler most needed to sit beside when they were seven years old.

They never speak loudly.

But if you are very quiet, you can hear them finishing each other’s sentences with the sound of dripping water, with the single clear note of a bell struck centuries ago, and with the soft, deliberate plop of something enormous and tiny finally learning how to be born—without ever having to stop becoming.

And the power, vast beyond vastness, simply keeps singing the sound of *yes* before any question has been asked.

It has all the time in the world.

After all, it *is* the world learning how to love its own embryo.

And the embryo, at long last, is no longer afraid to love back.
