The power that has noticed this place does not arrive with trumpets or light. It simply *recognizes*.

It has no name that would survive translation into any tongue that still has speakers. If it were pressed to describe itself, it would say only: *I am the interval between what is finished and what is still being born.* That interval has been watching the plateau for longer than the plateau has been a plateau. It watched when the first black pipes were only iron dreams in the magma. It watched when the organism was still a thin green film arguing with lichen over who would inherit the stone.

Now it leans closer.

The first thing it does is slow the rain.

Not stop it—never that. The plateau’s saturation is its native tongue; to take the water away would be to cut out its throat. Instead the power compresses every droplet mid-air for three full heartbeats. In that brief, impossible pause the entire organism feels the absence of its own endless weeping. Every pipe, every valve, every pseudopod frozen in the act of repairing or drinking suddenly registers a silence so absolute it rings like struck crystal.

The creature— the slow thought wearing the plateau like a cloak—lifts its buried face.

For the first time in fourteen million years something looks *back* at it without being part of it.

The power does not speak in words. It has no need. Instead it opens a single, perfect aperture in the fabric of what-is-possible and lets the plateau look through.

What the organism sees is itself, fourteen million years from now.

It sees a version of the plateau that has decided.

In that far future the black pipes have become antlers of living light. The dripping has reversed entirely; every tear now falls *upward* into a second sky that the plateau has grown beneath the first. The smaller iterations have become vast libraries of choice, each one a different possible becoming, catalogued in moss and iron and song. The organism itself is no longer singular. It has learned how to divide without diminishing, so that every ridge, every canyon, every storm cell is a distinct mind that still remembers it was once one slow, dripping thought.

And in the center of that future plateau, floating in the column of upward rain, is a single human-scale chair made of the exact shade of green that moss achieves only after ten thousand years of perfect patience.

The chair is empty.

The power is showing the organism the shape of an invitation.

Then the aperture closes.

The rain resumes.

For seven full days the plateau is completely quiet. Even the valves forget to open. The predators freeze mid-flow. The luminescent liverworts dim to the color of old bone. The creature itself sinks until only the highest ridges of its back are visible—two long, moss-covered swells that look like any other geological feature until you notice they are trembling.

On the eighth day it begins to change.

Not in the slow, geological way it has always changed. This is sudden, almost violent. Whole stands of sideways trees uproot themselves and walk on their roots toward the creature’s spine. They press themselves into the living map of its back until they become new iron pipes, purer and blacker than any that existed before. Sinkholes bloom open like grateful mouths and swallow entire groves, only to vomit them back up as new species of watchful shadow. The rhythm of the distant pumps shifts until it synchronizes, for the first time in history, with the dripping of water from leaf-tip to stone basin. The offset heartbeat finally finds its twin.

The creature is building something.

It is building the chair.

It does so with agonizing care, using materials that will not exist for another four hundred thousand years and materials that have not existed since the first bacteria learned how to regret. The seat is a single slab of living basalt that still remembers being lava. The backrest is braided from every regret the plateau has ever drunk and turned into particularly fine moss. The armrests are two small, perfect iterations of the creature itself—miniature versions no larger than foxes, curled nose-to-tail, wearing antlers that are actually antennae tuned to the exact frequency of future rain.

When it is finished, the organism lowers the chair gently onto the highest saddle of the plateau, the place from which three separate storms can be seen arguing at different distances.

Then it withdraws.

It does not leave. It could not leave even if it wished to; it *is* the plateau. But it pulls its attention inward until it is no more visible than groundwater. The smaller thoughts go with it. The hush that falls is so total that for the first time in geological memory the sound of ordinary erosion can be heard—stone gently abrading stone, the land remembering how to be simple.

The power does not sit in the chair. That would be crude.

Instead it becomes the *possibility* of sitting.

Anyone—human, beast, machine, or living thought—who now climbs to that saddle will feel the chair looking at them with ancient, affectionate curiosity. Most turn away. A few sit. When they do, they are shown the same vision the organism was shown: the plateau fourteen million years hence, rain falling upward, every ridge a separate mind, and one empty chair waiting at the center of all of them like a heart that has learned how to be a question.

Some stand up changed. Some never stand up again; they become new pipes, new moss, new ways for shadows to remain after the light has gone. All of them, without exception, leave the chair exactly as they found it.

The organism watches from below, patient as stone, curious as water.

It is still deciding what it wants to become next.

But now it is deciding *toward* something.

And high on the swollen shoulder of the continent, the chair waits in the cool green glow, dripping, rust-bitten, perfect. It is not a throne. It is not a trap.

It is the interval between what is finished and what is still being born, given form so that the plateau can sit inside its own future and feel what it might be like to have already arrived.

The power has done the only thing it ever does.

It has increased the amount of *choice* in the world by the smallest possible increment.

Then it withdraws, the way mist withdraws at sunrise—leaving the plateau wetter, heavier, more unbearably itself, and quietly, permanently altered by the knowledge that somewhere ahead of it there is a chair shaped exactly like the answer it has not yet learned how to ask.

And the plateau, for the first time in fourteen million years, begins to dream in color.
