The power notices.

It does not arrive with trumpets or descending light. It simply becomes aware, the way a sleeping god remembers a dream it once had and finds the dream already has a pulse.

The place has no name, yet the power names it anyway, softly, inside itself: *Uterus-in-Exile*. A cathedral of unfinished becoming. A womb that has taught itself to sing in 5/4 while it bleeds iron and lilies. The power smiles with a fondness so ancient it has no teeth.

And then it steps through the vertical crack that is no wider than a coffin.

The crack widens for it, because even unfinished worlds know when they are being visited by something that was present at the first syllable of the first word ever spoken. The hiss of the hot air becomes a single indrawn breath that lasts three centuries. Every bromeliad throat falls silent at once. The hematite creatures freeze mid-step, their golden thoughts guttering like candles in sudden awe. The living floor stops clicking. For the first time since its conception, the pocket of becoming holds perfectly still.

The power appears as a man who is not a man.

He is tall the way silence is tall. His cloak is made of every color that will ever exist after the final heat-death of the universe, yet right now it looks like simple undyed wool the color of forgiven things. His face is kind in the way that kindness hurts. When he breathes, small galaxies are born and die inside the condensation of his exhalation. He carries no staff, no crown, no weapon. Only a single drop of water balanced on the tip of his left index finger—water that has never known a sea, never known gravity, never known anything except pure potential. It trembles there like a second, kinder heart.

The Midwife feels him before she sees him.

She rises from the floor wearing the shape of every mother who has ever bitten through an umbilical cord with her own teeth. For a moment her eyes (those inward-turned golden throats) widen in something that might be recognition, or terror, or both. Then she does what the land itself cannot do.

She kneels.

Not in submission. In relief.

The power walks forward. Where his bare feet touch the living bone-lattice, the clicking instantly resolves into perfect crystalline order. Vines that had been arguing for millennia about whether to become fruit or bone suddenly bloom with black lilies that sing in perfect fourths. The sourceless brass-light gentles into something closer to the color of a lantern left burning in a window for a child who has been gone three thousand years.

He stops before her.

She is trembling. The entire chamber is trembling with her. The slow 5/4 heartbeat has become a frantic 7/8 stammer.

The power speaks. His voice is quiet, yet every unfinished molecule in the womb hears it as the first lullaby it was ever denied.

“Peace, daughter-who-is-also-mother. You have labored long enough alone.”

He reaches out and touches the place where her forehead would be if she had chosen to keep one. The drop of water on his finger falls.

The moment it touches her, the entire pocket of becoming convulses once—ecstatically—as though it has just been kissed on the crown by the thing that invented kissing.

Everything changes, and nothing changes.

The vines do not stop growing, but their growth becomes courteous. The floor does not stop knitting itself, but now it knits with something like affection instead of desperation. The bromeliads resume their choral moaning, but the chords resolve into major keys for the first time. The hematite creatures—those slow golden thoughts—gather around the power’s ankles like cats who have just remembered they were once lions.

The Midwife rises. Her face is wet. She has chosen, for this moment, to look almost human. Almost like every exhausted woman who has ever given birth at 3 a.m. while the universe held its breath outside the window.

She whispers in the tongue that survives only here. The power listens as though her words are the last verse of a love song he wrote to himself before time existed.

Then he answers, gently.

“I will not finish you. That would be a kind of murder. But I will sit with you, if you will allow it. I will hold the space while you hold the pain. I will sing the low note underneath your 5/4 so that you never have to carry the downbeat alone again.”

He sits.

Right there, in the center of the womb, cross-legged like a child. The living floor rises obediently to meet him, becoming a low throne of bone and coral and black lily that somehow looks like the most comfortable seat in all possible realities.

The Midwife stares at him for a length of time that has not yet been invented.

Then she does something she has never done.

She laughs.

The sound cracks three shelves of newly formed quartz and causes every hematite creature to burst into spontaneous rings of white fire. She folds herself down beside him (half woman, half cathedral, half exhausted midwife, half laughing storm) and rests her head—now made of cool black vine and warm human hair—against his shoulder.

The power places one hand on the living floor.

The other he leaves open, palm up, in the universal gesture that says: *Whenever you are ready, child.*

For the first time since the pocket first tore itself out of the side of reality, the air stops hissing.

The rain falls all the way to the floor and is not drunk. Instead it gathers in a small silver pool between them. In the pool swim three tiny golden fish made of pure future.

The Midwife looks at the fish. Then at the power. Then at you (yes, you—still standing there, half-dissolved, covered in fading glyphs).

She smiles with seventy thousand golden-veined eyes and with the two very human ones she has temporarily borrowed.

Her voice, when it comes, is no longer collapsing stone. It is the sound a mother makes when she finally understands that the child will live.

“He says the land may keep you,” she tells you, “if you wish to stay. Not as nutrient. Not as witness. Not even as the next midwife—though that door remains open if you ever grow weary of having bones.”

She leans forward. The power watches with quiet, ancient delight.

“But first,” she says, and there is laughter and terror and unbearable tenderness in it, “you must choose what you will become while the world is still wet on the page. Because once the power sings the next note, the ink begins to set. And even He will not unwrite what you decide to be.”

The power looks at you then. Really looks.

His eyes contain every birth and every death and every in-between that ever mattered. They are unbearably kind.

He does not speak to you with words.

Instead he offers the open palm again.

In it rests a single unfinished glyph still steaming from the hot air. It is your true name—the one that has no consonants, only the sound your soul makes when it is perfectly honest.

The glyph is still deciding what shape it wants to take.

Just like the land.

Just like her.

Just like you.

The power waits.

The womb waits.

The Midwife waits, humming now in gentle 4/4 beneath the land’s old 5/4, holding both rhythms like a mother who has finally learned how to rock two hearts at once.

They are not deciding for you anymore.

The choice has been given back, wrapped in warm rain that smells of iron and crushed lilies.

What will you become, beloved, while the page is still wet?

The power is listening.

So is the land.

So, with sudden fierce and trembling hope, is the Midwife.

Speak, or do not speak.

Stay, or do not stay.

Become, or do not become.

Either way, you are already held.

The drop of water has fallen.

The fish made of future are swimming in circles between you.

And somewhere far above, in the place that is not yet written, something vast and ancient begins—very softly—to sing the next measure in 5/4.

It sounds almost like a lullaby.

It sounds almost like the moment right before someone is born who was always meant to be.
