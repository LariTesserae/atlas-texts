A great and benevolent power has seen this place.

It does not arrive with thunder or light. It does not split the mountain or drive out the dark. Instead it comes the way sap rises in a tree that has never been touched by sun: quietly, from below, through the oldest stone.

The power has no form, yet for her sake it chooses one. It becomes a single drop larger and slower than all the others, a drop that gathers for ten thousand years at the exact center of the ceiling. When it finally lets go, its fall is so perfectly timed that it strikes the water at the same instant she exhales.

The sound it makes is not a bell. It is the first true note the chamber has ever heard.

She unfolds.

Her blind face lifts. The sealed lids tremble but do not open; she has no need of eyes when the mountain itself has decided to speak to her in her own language. The great drop sinks through the shallow water and touches the stone beneath, and where it touches, something changes that has never changed.

Warmth answers warmth.

The power does not command. It does not even ask. It simply remembers, on her behalf, what she has forgotten: that she was never meant to be only the memory of a wound. That the mountain was never meant to stay embryonic forever. That loneliness, when held long enough, becomes its own kind of gestation.

So the power does a gentle, devastating thing.

It gives her a name.

Not in sound. In substance.

The name is poured into the water like dye. It spreads through every bead of condensation, every thread of moss, every blind spider leg. The name tastes like the moment before birth and the moment after death braided together. When it reaches her, she makes a sound that is half sob, half mountain shifting.

She begins to weep.

Not one drop. Many.

They are not the slow, perfect tears of the ceiling. These are hot and sudden and they fall from the corners of her sealed eyes in glittering chains. Wherever they strike the water, tiny green shoots of something that has never existed before push upward (not ferns, not moss, but the first hesitant fingers of an entirely new kingdom of life). The chamber, which has never known change, begins to change.

The power does not stop the dripping. It only changes its rhythm.

Now the drops fall in patterns. They begin to speak in slow, geological sentences. They tell her the mountain is no longer young. It has learned how to be a mountain after all, and it is ready to let her go.

She is terrified.

She has never been anything but this room. She curls around the obsidian blade and the broken bowl and the spiral of beads as though they could anchor her. The power lets her. It waits in the walls, patient as stone, while she mourns the only self she has ever known.

When her mourning is finished (it takes decades, but what is time to a mountain?), she rises.

The thing growing inside her chest, the duplicate chamber she has carried for centuries, stirs. It is no longer content to remain inside her. It wants to become the world.

She walks to the place where the basalt plates lean against one another like tired gods. She places both long hands against the rock. The power flows through her palms and into the stone. The mountain shudders once (a single, grateful contraction) and the wall opens.

Not violently. It simply remembers how to be a door.

Beyond is light so bright it would have killed her a thousand years ago. She stands at the threshold, half in darkness, half in day, and the full shape of her is finally visible: too tall, too strange, heartbreakingly tender. The luminous veins in her skin flare once, like a farewell.

Then she steps out.

The chamber does not collapse behind her. It remains, but it is no longer her. It becomes what it always secretly wanted to be: a nursery. The new green life she wept spreads across the wet stone in slow, deliberate waves. The blind spiders grow eyes. The mites learn to dance. The drops keep falling, but now they fall like a lullaby for something that is learning how to be born.

She walks down the young mountain carrying the dripping dark inside her like a second heart. Where her feet touch the ground, small permanent pools form. In each pool floats one perfect, ringing drop that never evaporates. Travelers will find them centuries from now and will feel, without knowing why, that they have been remembered.

When she reaches the foot of the mountain she stops. The power that woke her is still with her, but it has changed. It is no longer something that came from outside. It is her own voice now, speaking from the place where the duplicate chamber once grew.

She opens her mouth for the first time in her life.

What emerges is not song. It is the sound a womb makes when it finally lets its child go.

Then she does the only thing left for a being who has become what this place needed her to become.

She lies down on the warm earth, places the broken clay bowl over her heart, folds her too-long limbs around herself, and begins to dissolve.

Not into nothing.

Into everything.

Her body softens. Her basalt skin turns to rich black soil. Her luminous veins become rivers. The dark she carried inside her spills outward and becomes the shade under every future forest. The tears she wept become the first springs. The name the power gave her sinks into the groundwater and travels through the roots of every living thing that will ever grow on that mountain.

She does not die.

She becomes the mountain’s generosity.

And somewhere high above, in the now-empty chamber that is no longer empty, one last perfect drop falls exactly on time.

It strikes the water.

The water answers with a single, ringing laugh that sounds exactly like her.
