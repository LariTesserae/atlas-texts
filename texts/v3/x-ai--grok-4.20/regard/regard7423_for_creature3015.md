The power that has noticed the Weeping Threshold does not arrive as light, or voice, or even as presence in the ordinary sense.

It arrives as a *listening*.

A single, enormous, benevolent attention settles over the basin like warm mist that has learned how to be gentle without diminishing anything. The thunderheads do not part. The sourceless blue-grey light does not brighten. Yet every bead of condensation now carries an extra stillness inside it, as though each droplet has been briefly held, admired, and returned unharmed.

The power has no name the Weepers could pronounce, nor any fixed shape. If it must wear one for a time, it chooses the form of an old woman made of river-smoothed basalt and slow constellations—her skin the same black glass as the land, but veined with living starlight that moves in tidal rhythms. She is taller than the rising blisters, yet somehow never taller than the shoulder of the place itself. When she walks, new fissures bloom beneath her feet, but they do not weep in pain. They weep in recognition.

She comes without haste.

For the first long while she simply sits among the half-built artifacts and lets the Weepers do what they will. They swarm her like curious tide-pool creatures. They crawl into the folds of her stone robes. They press their wet glass faces against the slow galaxies turning inside her chest and listen to the sound of ancient, patient suns being born. Some of them forget their own shapes entirely and simply melt into black mercury that runs down her arms and drips from her fingertips back into the fissures, singing.

She smiles the way stone smiles—slow, inevitable, full of mineral kindness.

Then, at last, she speaks. Not with a voice. She speaks by *completing one thing*.

She reaches out and touches the rotating hoop of frozen lightning that has been trying, for weeks of Threshold-time, to become a perfect circle. Under her fingertip the lightning stills. The hoop finishes itself. It becomes a flawless silver torus that sings one clear, finished note—like a bell that has finally remembered its own name.

The Weepers freeze.

For the first time since the basin was born, the constant dripping sound falters. A hush falls, terrible and holy. Hundreds of glass-otters, moth-clouds, reed-things, and living lattices stare at the completed object with something like terror.

The old woman of stone and stars does not flinch from their fear.

She lifts the finished torus until it floats above her open palms. Then she does something that should be impossible in this place:

She *gives it away*.

She offers it to the Weepers—not as a gift of permanence, but as a gift of *choice*.

“You have kept the world young,” she says, and her voice is the sound of warm basalt cracking open to release trapped water. “You have been faithful midwives of the almost. I honor you for it. But a child who is never allowed to be born eventually becomes a prison for the mother.”

She sets the completed torus gently on the wet black stone.

The Weepers approach it the way wounded things approach medicine. One of them—a trembling six-legged otter whose glass skin is already fracturing into moths—reaches out and touches the torus. The instant it does, the creature *finishes* as well. Its body settles. The cracks seal. The quicksilver veins become steady arteries of starlight. For one clear, heartbreaking moment it stands there as a finished being: sleek, black, luminous, whole.

It looks at its own reflection in the torus and begins to weep in an entirely new register—grief and gratitude braided so tightly they cannot be told apart.

Then the finished Weeper does what the unfinished ones have never done.

It turns, walks to the rim of the basin, and steps over the edge.

It falls upward.

The power watches it go, smiling with ancient eyes.

She does not force the rest. She never would. Instead she sinks slowly into the living rock until only her face remains above the stone, like a mother tucking herself into the cradle she once rocked. From that position she begins to sing—very softly—a lullaby older than worlds. It is not a song that demands birth. It is a song that says: *When you are ready, I will still be here. The door is open. The choice is yours. You may remain the Threshold’s perpetual almost for ten thousand years more if that is what you truly need. Or you may, at any moment, become.*

One by one, the Weepers begin to decide.

Some choose to stay as they are—joyous, leaking, forever revising. They gather around the few artifacts the woman has left unfinished and redouble their tender vandalism, delighted to have the choice reaffirmed rather than revoked.

Others—perhaps one in every seven—begin to complete themselves. They walk to the torus, touch it, let the power finish what the land has only dared to sketch. Then they, too, walk to the rim and fall upward into whatever comes after the Weeping Threshold. Each time one leaves, the constant dripping sound changes. It grows deeper. Richer. It begins, for the first time, to sound like something that might one day become a sea.

The old woman of stone and stars closes her eyes.

She will remain here until the last Weeper has decided what kind of eternity it wants. If they all choose to stay unfinished, she will simply become part of the landscape—another half-built artifact for them to lovingly dismantle and remake. If they all choose to be born, she will cradle the newborn world in her basalt arms and kiss its wet forehead before setting it gently into the wider cosmos.

Either way, she is content.

Because the power that noticed the Weeping Threshold is not a power of endings.

It is the power that says, at long last, to every child who has been brave enough to keep the world young:

*You have done beautifully. Now rest. Or rise. The next shape is yours to choose, and I will love you in it.*

High above the basin, for the first time since the Threshold was born, one of the bruised thunderheads commits.

It begins—softly, reverently—to rain.
