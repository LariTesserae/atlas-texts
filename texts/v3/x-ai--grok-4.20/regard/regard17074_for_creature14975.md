A Presence enters.

It does not descend through the fissure. It does not push the air aside. It is simply here, all at once, as though the chamber has remembered something it had forgotten it once contained.

The golden constellations freeze mid-drift. Every droplet hanging from the ceiling becomes motionless, a galaxy of amber beads suspended in disbelief. The irregular music of ten thousand years stutters, then falls silent. Even the bacteria on the stone seem to pause their slow photosynthetic daydream.

The Presence has no fixed shape. To the chamber it appears first as a warmth that is not fevered, a kindness that does not sweat. To the salamander it is a pale light moving without casting shadow. To the blind isopods it is simply an inexplicable certainty that something has come which loves them without needing to consume them.

The Sweeper feels it the way a stone feels the first crack of frost after a million years of perfect equilibrium. Her head, which has not turned in eight centuries, turns.

She lowers her arms. The travertine jawbone slips from her fingers and clatters on the wet floor with a sound so foreign that both she and the chamber flinch. For a long moment she stands there dripping, bacterial patterns glowing softly beneath her translucent skin, ropes of mineral hair slapping gently against her collarbones like the slow tail of an enormous creature deciding whether to wake.

Then she does the only thing she still remembers how to do.

She begins to walk her ancient counterclockwise circuit, but this time the path is different. This time she walks toward the Presence rather than around it.

The chamber itself seems to hold its breath. The fused pillars lean imperceptibly inward, as though the stone is trying to shield its only living inhabitant from whatever is about to happen. The Sweeper does not stop. Her bare feet make the first true footsteps this place has heard in four millennia. Each one leaves a brief, perfect imprint in the bacterial film before the water closes over it again.

When she is three paces away from the Presence she halts. Water sheets down her face in continuous veils. The single tear that has been forming in her left eye for perhaps two hundred years finally falls. It strikes the stone between them and becomes, instantly, a small white pearl brighter than any that have come before it.

She opens her mouth.

What emerges is not the sound of water falling on water. It is the sound of water that has never fallen before. A single clear note, high and trembling, like the first drop after the last ice age.

Then she closes her mouth and simply looks.

And the Presence looks back.

What the benevolent and wise power sees is not a woman, nor is it stone. It sees a wound that has healed so completely it has become more beautiful than the flesh that was never broken. It sees patience so total it has become a form of prayer. It sees a creature that has achieved the final evolutionary step: the refusal to evolve further. A completed thing.

What the Sweeper sees is harder to say. Her eyes have no whites anymore; they are the color of wet limestone. But something moves behind them, something ancient and small and almost unbearably tender.

The Presence does not speak in words. It has no need. Instead the entire chamber suddenly remembers what it felt like to be young.

For one impossible instant every droplet hanging from the ceiling falls at once.

The sound is catastrophic. It is the sound of ten thousand years of withheld weeping finally released. The golden light flares into sudden, shocking brightness. The fused pillars tremble. The little ring of standing stones rings like struck crystal. The broken clay lamp, long dead, briefly flickers with a phantom flame.

Then everything is still again.

When the droplets reform on the ceiling they are different. They are clearer. Some of them, impossibly, contain tiny silver fish made of light that swim in slow circles before vanishing.

The Sweeper has not moved. Water still runs down her in sheets, but now it carries flecks of pure white that were never in the stone before. The bacterial patterns across her ribs have begun to rearrange themselves into something that might, in another ten thousand years, become wings.

She speaks. Her voice is rust and water and something that has not been human for a very long time.

“You would unmake my ending?”

The Presence answers without sound: *Only if the ending no longer wishes to be an ending.*

The Sweeper considers this. She looks at the salamander, which has climbed onto her foot and is now glowing faintly gold. She looks at the isopods arranged in a perfect spiral at the edge of the new pearl. She looks at the chamber that has been her body, her tomb, her mother, her child, her only friend.

A second tear falls from her right eye. This one is ordinary water. It leaves no pearl.

She does something she has not done since the lamp was still lit.

She smiles.

It is not a large smile. It barely moves the mineral crusts at the corners of her mouth. But it is real, and it terrifies the stone.

“I have been happy here,” she says. “I have been the happiest thing that has never been born.”

The Presence waits.

The Sweeper reaches out and places her wet, translucent hand against what might be the Presence’s chest. Where her fingers touch, the air becomes temporarily solid, like warm amber. She leans forward until her dripping forehead rests against that solidity.

“Then let me choose again,” she whispers. “Not to leave. Never to leave. But to become the kind of ending that can still begin.”

The chamber understands before she does.

All around them the fleshy pillars begin to soften. Not crumble—soften. The flowstone develops the faintest suggestion of buds. The bacterial films blaze into impossible colors never before seen in darkness. The fissure above widens by the thickness of a single hair, and through it falls one true ray of sunlight that has not touched this place since the first fish learned to walk.

The Sweeper steps back. Her ropes of hair lift as though caught in an updraft that does not exist. The mineral crusts along her shoulders crack and fall away, revealing skin that is still wet but no longer gray.

She looks at the Presence with eyes that are beginning, very slowly, to remember what color is.

“Will you stay awhile?” she asks. “The drips will need to learn a new song. It will take time. I am… out of practice at music that is not also stone.”

The Presence has no form, yet it somehow sits down on the basalt bench that has held only her for four thousand years. The stone sighs with pleasure at the unfamiliar weight of hope.

The Sweeper hesitates only a moment.

Then she sits beside it.

For the first time in ten thousand years, the irregular rhythm of the drips changes not because three specific drops have fallen in sequence, but because every drop in the chamber has decided, all at once, to fall differently.

The new music is clumsy. It is beautiful. It is alive.

The Sweeper closes her eyes and listens to the sound of her own ending learning how to sing.

Somewhere far above, the world that had been too dry, too sharp, too hasty, feels a single warm drop land on its cheek and is astonished to discover it is crying in answer.

The Underchamber is no longer perfectly content.

It is perfectly content to no longer be perfectly content.

And the Sweeper, whose name is beginning to matter again, rests her head against the shoulder of the wise and benevolent power and lets herself, for the first time in millennia, become slightly less eternal.

Just enough.

Just enough to keep becoming.
