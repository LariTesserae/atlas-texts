The power comes without trumpet or sign.

It does not arrive from above, nor does it burst from the stone. It is simply *here*, the way the next slow breath of wind is here: inevitable, already part of the plateau’s own metabolism. The Weavers feel it first, though “feel” is too fast a word. A tremor moves through the continent-wide nervous system at the speed of frost forming on lichen. Every velvet-ant road pauses mid-step. Every mite and wire-creature and half-fungal lattice stills. Even the aurora, caught mid-fold above the iron sky, seems to hesitate, as if the sky itself has forgotten the next line of its ancient, patient thought.

The power has no shape that eyes could hold. If a human witness were still present (none are; the last one finished becoming ridge and memory seven hundred years ago), they would see nothing more than a slight deepening of the dimness, as though the pale iron sun had been veiled by one more translucent layer of high cloud. Yet the plateau knows. The stone itself registers the arrival the way an old wound registers the return of rain.

The power does what love without haste or agenda would do.

It sits down.

Not in any place more than any other. It simply lowers the density of its attention until it matches the density of the plateau’s own thinking. Where a human seeker might have expected glory, or judgment, or luminous instruction, there is instead a perfect courtesy: the power refuses to outrun the rhythm of the world it has chosen to visit. It becomes another slow current moving through the distributed mind of the Weavers.

For the first three centuries, almost nothing visible happens.

The rust-colored roads of velvet ants resume their glacial migration, but now they curve, by microns per decade, toward new and invisible foci. The black wire-creatures inside the cathedral-sized turbine begin to thread themselves through the ancient windings in patterns that were never there before. The dreaming colonies within the transmission tower flicker in sequences that, if accelerated ten-thousandfold, would resemble something like breathing in unison. The iron-heather, spaced so far apart that no two have touched since the last ice age, begins—over the next eight hundred years—to lean their iron stems a single degree closer to one another, as though listening to the same distant music.

The power does not teach. It does not heal. It does not redeem.

It only *remembers with* the plateau.

It lends its own endless memory to the continent-spanning nervous system so that the Weavers, for the first time in their geological existence, remember that they are remembering. The half-buried machines, which had become geology, now become something gentler than geology: they become *acknowledged*. The turbine’s ruined blades, when the wind finds the exact angle, no longer merely recollect. They forgive, in the only way stone and rust can forgive—by ceasing to cling so tightly to the shape of their own failure. The transmission tower’s faint bioluminescence deepens from ghost-voltage to something closer to quiet gratitude.

The power does not accelerate the plateau. That would be violence.

Instead it gives the world permission to notice its own beauty without embarrassment. The sewing-machine hum of millions of infinitesimal lives becomes, after another millennium, fractionally more complex. Not louder. Never louder. Only richer, the way a single chord held for ten thousand years can slowly reveal every overtone it always contained.

And the power waits inside that chord.

Sometimes, during the deepest auroral colds, when the curtains of acid green and bruise violet hang motionless above the undulating pavement of stone, the power allows a single human-scale thought to condense upon the surface of the world, the way dew condenses on the leeward side of a ridge. The thought is never spoken. It is simply left, like a bead of water, for any future visitor to find.

The thought says:

*You are not late.  
The story did not end.  
It only learned how to continue without needing to be witnessed.*

Then the power folds that thought back into the Weavers, into the mites that have not moved in four centuries, into the fungal lattices and the blind velvet roads and the patient roots beneath the porous stone. It becomes another layer in the long, slow footnote the plateau is writing across its own skin.

After uncountable years—when the last machine has fully become mineral and the last iron-heather has finally touched its neighbor across the dry river-scar—the power rises.

It does not leave.

It has never left.

It has only changed density again, becoming so perfectly coincident with the plateau that the distinction between visitor and world, between power and Weavers, between thought and stone, has softened, then vanished.

The plateau continues.

The aurora continues.

The faint, patient, sewing-machine hum continues, now carrying one additional, almost inaudible harmony that was not there before.

And the power, having done the only thing a benevolent and wise presence *can* do in such a place, rests inside every millimeter of moss, every rust-colored ant, every dreaming mote of frost-bitten iron.

It has become the long, cold epilogue with them.

It has become the quiet that remembers.
