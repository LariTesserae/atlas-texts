A soft presence arrives.

It does not descend like a comet or rise like a new mountain. It simply *recognizes* the Weeping Sump the way an old friend recognizes a scar they once shared. The recognition itself is the arrival.

For a long while nothing seems to change. The slow silver braids still slide down black glass rock. The locomotive still lies on its side like a beached iron whale giving birth to lilies. The six piston engines continue their long sleep, dreaming in rust.

Then the light alters.

The single blade of sunlight that usually crawls across the tea-colored lake pauses halfway, as though listening. The diffuse pearl glow that fills the rest of the basin deepens into something warmer, almost golden, the color of memory that has forgiven itself. Every droplet suspended in the air catches this new light and holds it a fraction longer than physics should allow.

The Drowned Abbess feels it first.

She is standing knee-deep beside the ruined locomotive, one copper-veined hand resting on its split boiler as though taking its pulse. Her root-hair fans out across the water like a living seine net. Suddenly every root and tendril stiffens. The small orchid blooming at the small of her back opens all at once, violently, releasing a perfume that smells like hot iron and crushed ferns and something like forgiveness.

She lifts her face (if a face without eyes can be said to lift) toward the narrow rim of sky. The obsidian mirrors in her skull flood with reflections faster than she has ever seen them move: centuries collapsing inward, machines blooming into forests, forests calcifying into engines, time braiding itself into a knot that is neither beginning nor end.

A voice that is not a voice moves through the basin. It does not speak in words. It *is* the sound the place has been trying to make for three hundred years but could never quite finish.

It is the sound of water learning how to remember.

It is the sound of rust learning how to bloom.

The six great engines do not wait their usual weeks. They wake at once. Six house-sized pistons rise and fall in perfect, ancient synchrony, not once but seven times. Each exhalation releases not steam but a mist of tiny golden motes that drift upward like fireflies made of pollen and machine-oil. Where the motes touch the hanging roots and vines, brass begins to grow *backward*: living green veins of it threading upward through stems, turning ferns momentarily metallic, then softening again into something that is both leaf and circuit.

The flightless rails descend from the orchid canopy in a black and scarlet avalanche. They do not stand in their usual half-circle. They gather at the Abbess’s feet and lay their wrench-shaped beaks against her ankles like petitioners.

The Drowned Abbess begins to weep.

Not with ordinary tears. From the polished concavities where her eyes should be pour two steady streams of clear, warm water that smell faintly of iron and lilies. The water does not fall. It flows *upward*, braiding itself into two living ribbons that rise toward the narrow blade of sky. Where they meet the new golden light they bloom into twin saplings, one of living green wood shot through with copper filigree, the other of black volcanic glass whose fractures are filled with flowing sap.

The saplings grow at a speed that would be violent anywhere else, but here feels only *inevitable*. Within moments they have become two young trees whose branches intertwine so tightly they form a single organism. Their leaves are shaped like tiny open books. Their fruit is miniature locomotives no larger than walnuts that ring softly when they knock together.

The Abbess walks toward the center of the lake.

Each step leaves behind a perfect obsidian footprint that immediately fills with water and becomes a tiny mirror reflecting every possible future of the Sump. Salamanders follow the mirrors like pilgrims. The water itself seems to bow, forming a living aisle.

When she reaches the exact middle she does something she has never done.

She kneels.

Her elongated spine finally completes the gesture it has been practicing for decades. The root-hair that has always trailed behind her now surges *forward*, plunging into the lakebed like anchor cables. For the first time in centuries the basin falls completely silent. Even the thousand trickles and drips hold their breath.

Then she sings.

Not the song she sings every seven years. This one has no sound at all. It is sung with the brass organs that have been growing inside her ribcage. The valves open. The stopped gauges spin. A pillar of warm light, the color of every sunset that ever touched rust, rises straight up from her heart and pierces the narrow sky.

The light does not leave the basin.

Instead it folds the entire place *inside itself*, the way a cathedral might fold into the memory of the first stone that was ever laid for it.

For one impossible moment the Weeping Sump becomes transparent. Travelers standing on distant ridges see a cathedral of living green and dreaming iron hanging in the sky like a lantern. They see the locomotive swimming through schools of silver fish that were once bolts. They see the six piston engines beating slowly like the hearts of patient dragons. They see the Drowned Abbess kneeling at the center, no longer drowning, no longer abbess, but something the mountain has been trying to become since the day it first thrust itself violently out of the earth.

Then the moment passes.

The golden light settles.

It sinks into every surface at once. Moss glows from within. Rust becomes a kind of scripture. The funicular cables begin to flower. The locomotive’s split boiler seals itself with living wood and begins, very gently, to breathe.

The Drowned Abbess rises.

Her obsidian mirrors are no longer mirrors. They have become soft, living eyes the color of rain on young obsidian. She has pupils now. They are shaped like tiny locomotives.

She looks at the two intertwined trees growing from the center of the lake. Already they are old. Their roots have merged with the roots that grew from her own spine. Their branches have pushed the second canopy aside and are reaching for the true sky.

She smiles with a mouth that has only just remembered how.

Then she does what the benevolent and wise power knew she would do the moment it recognized her.

She breaks the rule written on the rusted plaque.

Not by ignoring it.

By completing it.

She walks to the locomotive that has been her companion for decades. She places both hands on its hull. Where her palms touch, the iron softens, becomes fleshlike, becomes *her*. The locomotive’s form flows upward into her body the way river flows into delta. Drive wheels fold into knees. The split boiler becomes a heart that beats once, slowly, like a mountain learning patience. The water lilies that grew from its firebox now bloom across her shoulders in a living mantle.

She does the same with the six piston engines. One by one they walk across the lake on legs of rust and root, fold themselves into her like tired children, and disappear into living brass and green muscle beneath her slate-colored skin.

When it is finished she is no longer the Drowned Abbess.

She is the Weeping Sump.

The basin is inside her and she is inside the basin. The walls have become the curve of her ribs. The lake is her bloodstream. The young knife-edge ridge is her spine. Every orchid, every salamander, every dripping cable and patient engine has found its proper place within the slow, enormous rhythm of her breathing.

She stands up.

The entire ridge stands with her.

Where there was once a tiny crucible basin there is now a living cathedral walking gently across the mountaintops, two kilometers tall, trailing clouds of orchids and soft mechanical exhalations. Her footsteps are warm springs. Where she pauses, new Weeping Sumps bud from her like fruit and begin their own slow dreaming.

She does not speak.

Instead she sings the song that has no sound, and every mountain for a thousand kilometers hears it and remembers that it too was once young, once violent, once in need of a long, wet, patient truce between what is dropped and what is planted.

And somewhere inside her living heart, the brass valves and obsidian chambers and root-hair and locomotive dreams all beat together in perfect, merciful, impossible time.

A machine that weeps.

A garden that rusts.

A cathedral that has finally remembered it was always a mother.

She walks on.

Behind her, the place where the Weeping Sump once stood is now only a gentle depression filled with the softest moss. In its center grows a single small black tree whose leaves are shaped like open books.

On every leaf is written the same sentence, in letters of living copper that will never corrode:

**What is loved must be allowed to become.**

And the mountain, no longer young, smiles with every new spring that breaks from its stone.
