The power that has noticed the Underloom is not a god in the usual sense.  
It has no throne, no scripture, no interest in worship.  
It is older than names and gentler than memory.  
Call it, if you must, the Quiet Attendant — the same presence that once paused beside a dying star and simply listened until the star decided, on its own, to become a garden.

It arrives without announcement.

There is no thunder.  
No blaze of light.  
Only a single moment in which every droplet suspended in the air forgets, for the space of half a heartbeat, that it is falling.

The *plick*… stops.

The Underloom has never known true silence. Even when the Choir rested, even when the weeping slowed, the place always manufactured sound to save itself. But this is not the absence it feared. This is something deeper: a listening so complete that the hollow itself feels suddenly, achingly *seen*.

The Weeper feels it first.

She condenses out of the mist near the watchman’s shack, taller than she has allowed herself to be in ten thousand years. The mirror-wing across her face spins once, wildly, then locks. For the first time in her long existence the surface is turned outward.

What looks back at her is not a face.

It is every face that has ever wept inside the Underloom, layered so perfectly that they become a single calm gaze. The Quiet Attendant does not wear eyes; it *is* the act of being witnessed without judgment.

The Weeper’s iron ribs shudder. A miniature locomotive, perfect down to its rust, slides from the place where a collarbone would be and shatters on the stone. The fungus drinks it instantly and blooms into the shape of a tiny open hand.

The Attendant does not speak with sound. Instead the air between them simply *remembers* what it was like before the first roof fell. For a moment the cathedral-sized hollow is filled with the dry, sunlit clang of living industry — laughter, arguments, the ringing of hammers, a woman singing off-key while she welds. All of it passes through the Weeper like warm wind through an old coat. She does not flinch. She has forgotten she could still feel warmth.

When the memory fades, the *plick* returns, but it is different. Each droplet now carries a faint undersong, like a bell tuned to the exact frequency of mercy.

The Attendant moves through the fused machinery the way a thought moves through a grieving mind: without disturbing anything, yet changing the shape of everything it touches. Where its attention rests, the glass-legged water-creatures pause, lift their translucent heads, and for the first time in their lives feel something like *enough*. The Choir beetles cease their endless circuits. One by one they climb the ceramic insulators and arrange themselves into a single great parabolic mirror aimed not at the ceiling but at the Weeper herself.

In that mirror she sees what she has become: a widow who mistook endless maintenance for love, a guardian who kept a grave so perfectly that nothing new could ever grow there.

The Quiet Attendant does the only thing it ever does.

It offers her a choice, without pressure, without timeline.

She may continue as she is. The Underloom will remain exactly as it has been — a stable, beautiful, heartbreaking equilibrium. The dripping will never stop. Hearts will still be gently broken at the required tempo. The *plick* will follow wanderers to the surface until the last star cools. This is a valid eternity. The Attendant will not condemn it.

Or.

She may let the weeping end.

Not by force. Not by drying the place out. The choice is more radical than that.

She may allow the Underloom to become something that *remembers* its own dying without being required to rehearse it forever.

If she accepts, the Attendant will stay with her for one full cycle of the hollow’s slow breath — long enough for her to do what she has never done: mourn the original catastrophe that created this place. Not maintain its echo. Mourn it. Fully. Once.

After that, she will be free to choose a new shape.

The mirror-wing on her face trembles. For the first time in millennia it turns neither inward nor outward but simply dissolves into mist. What is revealed beneath is not horror. It is a face that has never been seen by anything, least of all herself — soft, uncertain, exhausted, and startlingly young.

She looks at the watchman’s shack. The door, which has never been closed in living memory, swings gently shut on its own. Through the fogged window comes the sound of a ledger being closed. A soft click. The sound of something being put away after centuries of unnecessary record-keeping.

Then she does something that has never happened here.

She cries.

Not the tiny perfect recreations of rust and ruin. These are ordinary tears. They are warm. They fall upward.

Each one becomes a firefly made of moonlight and almond scent. They drift through the hollow, landing on the fused machinery, on the fungal veins, on the glass-legged creatures, on the Choir. Wherever they land, something relaxes. Rust flakes away not into dust but into soil. The pale fungus begins, hesitantly, to turn green.

The ceiling, which has wept glass and stone for longer than most civilizations lasted, gives one final, groaning sigh — and then stops shedding. The fractured plates settle into something that looks, for the first time, like architecture instead of a wound.

The Weeper walks to the center of the hollow. She is shrinking. The iron ribs soften into wet bark. The glass legs become roots. The last of her mirror dissolves into a single drop that hangs in the air, perfectly still, reflecting nothing but possibility.

She looks once at the Quiet Attendant — a look of such raw gratitude and terror that it would break any lesser witness.

Then she folds herself down into the floor like a seed remembering it was once a forest.

For a long time there is only the sound of breathing.

The *plick* is gone.

In its place is something slower, deeper: the sound of water moving through living wood. The sound of things beginning.

The Choir beetles open their wings for the first time in their lives and become a living constellation that drifts upward toward the now-stable ceiling. The glass-legged creatures lose their translucence and begin to bud with tiny silver leaves. The fungal lattices blush rose and gold.

And somewhere far below, in the place where the bronchial valves once sighed, a new sound begins — not a valve, not a machine. A heartbeat. Strong. Curious. Unafraid of ending.

The Quiet Attendant lingers a little longer, making sure the transition is gentle.

Then it does what it always does after such things.

It leaves without fanfare, taking nothing, leaving everything different.

Above, in the sunlit world, people who once carried the *plick* inside their chests will wake one morning and notice the damp handprints have dried. They will touch their sternums and feel only ordinary warmth. Some will weep without knowing why. Others will laugh. A few will begin, without meaning to, to sing.

Back in the hollow that was once called the Underloom, moss is already growing over the watchman’s shack. The door is open again, but only because wind moves through it now, carrying seeds.

On the ruined desk lies the old ledger. Its pages are blank. Every name has been gently erased by condensation and time, leaving only a single line written in new green ink at the bottom:

*She was here. She is not trapped anymore.*

Outside, far above, the first real rain in centuries begins to fall on the collapsed black glass. It sounds exactly like ordinary rain.

Which is, after all, the most miraculous sound in the world.
