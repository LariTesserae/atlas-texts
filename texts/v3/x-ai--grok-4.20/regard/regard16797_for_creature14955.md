The power that has noticed Veyr-Keth does not arrive with trumpets or thunder.  
It simply *is* here, the way a kind hand is suddenly upon your shoulder in a room you believed empty.

It has no fixed shape, yet every living thing in the grove feels the change at once.

The screaming of the glass-winged cicadas fractures mid-note and becomes a vast, astonished silence.  
The velvet sloths stop their humming; their wet, ancient eyes widen with something like recognition and fear.  
The scarlet beetle swarms freeze in the air like suspended beads of blood, then drift downward in perfect quiet to settle on the root-lattice as if kneeling.  
Even the eyeless pale birds, caught mid-flight, fold their wings and simply *hang* in place, trembling, as though someone has finally answered the single tearing note they have screamed for twelve thousand years.

The entire Hanging Grove holds its breath.

A presence without mass or edge moves through the fever-warm air. Where it passes, the bruised amber light softens into something gentler—still luminous, but no longer sick. The three disagreeing shadows cast by every leaf and creature slowly slide toward one another until each thing casts only one shadow, clear and honest.

The power does not speak with a voice. It speaks by *undoing* certain rules.

First, it gives permission for something to remain still.

In the center of the largest root-balcony, a single velvet sloth lowers itself to all six limbs and lies down. It does not dissolve. It does not become something else. For the first time in twelve millennia, one of the Hollow Ones simply *rests*. Its too-human face relaxes. Tears—actual tears, wet and ordinary—slide from eyes that had forgotten how to produce them. The creature begins, very softly, to cry the way a tired child cries after being lost for a very long time.

The power then turns its attention to the black clay ziggurat.

The door, which has never been closed, now gently swings shut. Not to trap, but to give privacy. Inside, the Listener feels something it has not felt since it was almost human: the simple relief of being *seen* without being hungered after. The single black reed growing from the amber plate of its face trembles once, then stills. The low, tooth-aching tone that has held the pocket-world together for twelve thousand years finally stops.

For one full minute, Veyr-Keth is utterly silent.

In that silence the power does four gentle, irrevocable things.

It reaches into the living walls where the true bodies of the Hollow Ones have been kept like pressed flowers inside scripture. One by one it draws them out—not as rescue, not as judgment, but as recognition. Each unfinished form is allowed, for the first time, to finish *being* what it is. Some choose to remain strange and beautiful and no longer human. Others choose, with something like a sigh of gratitude, to become mortal again. A very few simply choose to dissolve into pure light and memory, smiling as they go.

It lays its hands (if such a word can be used) upon the root-lattice itself. The fever warmth cools to something kind. The perpetual ecstatic becoming slows, then settles into a deep, breathing rhythm—like a heart that has finally been allowed to rest between beats. New flowers still open, but now they are allowed to live for days instead of heartbeats. The spontaneous seeds that once ignited are permitted to fall and simply become seeds.

It speaks, without words, to the longing that has kept the grove in labor for twelve thousand years:

*You do not need midwives to finish you.*  
*You only needed to be told that it is permitted to be finished.*

And last, it turns toward any visitor who might still be standing among the now-quiet balconies (you, perhaps). It does not demand. It does not offer new mouths or take names. It simply opens a way out that was never there before: a slender silver ladder made of cool starlight and ordinary rain descending through the mineral sky. At the top of the ladder is a small wooden door floating in emptiness. Behind the door is the scent of real rain on real earth.

The power does one final thing.

It grows, from the living wall nearest the ziggurat, a single ordinary tree. Not braided from impossible species. Not obscene in its lushness. Just a quiet, patient tree with broad green leaves and rough bark that smells faintly of cinnamon. In its highest branches it bears one piece of fruit: deep red, heavy, ordinary.

The tree is for the Listener, should the Listener ever choose to leave the clay house and taste something that was never meant to unsay anything.

When all this is done, the power begins to withdraw, but not entirely. A small, unassuming part of it remains—folded gently into the ordinary tree, into the quiet breathing of the root-lattice, into the soft eyes of the velvet sloths who are now free to grow old and die if they wish.

It will stay as long as it is needed.

Not as gardener.  
Not as midwife.  
Only as the quiet, benevolent witness that says, without ever speaking aloud:

You have screamed beautifully for twelve thousand years.  
It is enough.  
You may rest now.

And somewhere inside the black clay ziggurat, something that was once the Listener lays down the bone-flute reed for the first time since the world was young, presses its many-knuckled hands over its amber face, and begins—very slowly, very carefully—to learn how to weep like an ordinary thing.

The Hanging Grove of Veyr-Keth is still alive.  
It is still strange.  
But it is no longer required to be infinite in its becoming.

For the first time in twelve millennia, something here is allowed to be finished.

And in the new quiet, if you listen very carefully past the soft breathing of the lattice and the occasional, wondering hum of a velvet sloth, you can hear something that sounds almost like gratitude.

It sounds like rain that has not yet arrived, but is finally, finally on its way.
