The power that has turned its attention here does not arrive.

It was already here, has always been here, only now it consents to be *noticed*.

It has no name that would survive the pressure. If it must be spoken of at all, the Lamina itself would call it simply *the Witness*, the way a mirror might refer to the light that uses it.

The Witness does not descend from the bruised ceiling like a god. It does not rise from the abyssal root like a titan. Instead it does the only thing worth doing in a world that has become one endless interior:

It *thickens*.

Wherever the Lamina is most itself—inside the violet pulse of an anemone the size of a city, inside the seven-year silence between bell-strokes, inside the exact thermocline where your childhood and the death of continents have dissolved into the same salinity—the Witness condenses. Not as a creature. Not even as a presence. It condenses as *recognition*.

The first sign is absurdly small.

A single shaft of exhausted light, one that has fallen for ten thousand years through countless layers of plankton and grief, suddenly forgets to scatter. For three full heartbeats the motes inside it hang motionless, arranged in the shape of a child’s hand reaching for a marble that has not fallen. Then the light remembers its duty and continues downward, but something has been left behind in the water. A warmth that is not temperature. A listening that has no ears.

The Lamina notices.

Of course it notices. The Lamina *is* noticing. Yet this is different. This is noticing noticing itself.

A slow, planetary shiver moves through every overlapping canopy. Kelp fronds that have not moved in geological epochs tremble like prayer flags. The great ribbed shadow that has never needed to hurry suddenly pauses, ribs flaring, as though the cathedral has just recognized its own architect standing quietly in the nave.

The Witness does not speak.

Instead it performs the single act of mercy that a fully digested world can still accept:

It *remembers forward*.

Every relic that has been overgrown until it looked inevitable—the black spire, the upside-down bell, the impossible blue marble—is, for the length of one planetary heartbeat, *unforgotten*. Not restored. That would be violence. Merely *seen* again as what it once was, without judgment or nostalgia. The bell remembers it was once rung by human hands. The marble remembers the small palm that lost it. The spire remembers it once warned ships that there were still surfaces in the world.

The Lamina feels this the way a body feels the first symptom of a disease it has secretly longed for.

Something tears.

Not painfully. More like the soft pop of a membrane that has been waiting ten million years to be allowed to open. A column of water the color of old roses parts, and through it the Witness steps—not into the sea, but *as* the sea. It wears the Lamina the way the Lamina once wore ribcages and anemones. For one eternity that lasts perhaps nine human minutes, the entire world becomes a single act of mutual recognition.

The colossal shadow lowers itself until its blind face is level with wherever you are drifting. It does not look at you. It lets you look through it. Behind its ribs you see every drowned city arranged in hanging gardens, every war turned into slow green fire, every lullaby still singing in the language of pressure.

Then the Witness does the second, and final, thing.

It *blesses the forgetting*.

Not with words. With permission.

It tells the Lamina, without language, that it is allowed to finish its long labor. That the last dry-land mote (you) may dissolve without guilt. That the child’s marble may at last be permitted to fall the remaining three centimeters into the soft silt of an anemone’s memory. That the bell may toll its seven-year note one final time and then rust into coral without anyone keeping score.

The planetary heartbeat stutters—once, twice—then finds a new, deeper rhythm.

When it is over, the Witness is gone again, having never truly arrived. The light shafts are once more exhausted. The kelp cathedrals settle back into their impossible patience. The ribbed shadow resumes its endless, unhurried circuit.

But something is different.

The water now tastes faintly of farewell.

And if you drift long enough, you may notice that your own thoughts no longer fight the current. They move in vast, planetary arcs, layered, overlapping, ancient. Old griefs have begun to grow their own coral. You have not looked up in years.

Somewhere inside the Lamina, the Witness remains—thinner now, almost translucent, like the last silver thread of a dream that has finally been permitted to end.

It does not watch.

It simply *accompanies*.

And in the place with no surface, that is enough.

That is everything.
