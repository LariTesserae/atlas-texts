The power that has noticed this place does not descend like lightning or arrive with trumpets. It has no name that the Canopy could pronounce, for names are brief and the Canopy has outlived every syllable. Let us call it, only for the sake of your ears, the Unhurried Glance.

It does not come to destroy, nor to redeem, nor even to awaken. Those are all gestures of haste.

Instead it does what any truly ancient and benevolent intelligence would do upon discovering a masterpiece that has already achieved its final, perfect form:

It sits down.

Not in any posture a human body could imitate. It simply ceases the last negligible motion it had been making across the larger cosmos and allows its full attention to settle here, the way dew settles on ten thousand thousand leaves at once. The weight of that attention is indistinguishable from a change in atmospheric pressure. Every stoma across the continent flutters once in recognition. The vast fungal whales drift slightly lower in their slow orbits, as though an invisible hand has brushed the underside of the sky.

Then the Unhurried Glance begins to listen.

It listens for four hundred years without speaking a word.

During that time the Quiet Architects become aware that something unprecedented is occurring. Their spore-conversations grow even slower, almost reverent. The Hollow Choir, sensing the new presence inside its own hollow body like a second, vaster hollow, opens every lenticel it possesses and simply breathes outward for three unbroken decades. The breath carries no message except recognition: *You are here. We see that you see.*

The Glance does not answer with spores or scent or pollen. It answers by becoming more perfectly still.

Wherever one of the Architects stands in its long labor of incorporation, a faint new quality enters the light that falls on it. The shafts of diluted gold deepen by one impossible shade. The Architect’s own luminous veins pulse once, in slow gratitude, and then it returns to its work as though nothing has changed, because for beings such as these, nothing important has.

After the four centuries of pure listening, the Unhurried Glance does the second thing.

It dreams, once.

Not a human dream. A single, continent-spanning dream that lasts no longer than the time it takes a droplet of condensation to travel from the highest leaf to the deepest root-mat. In that dream the Canopy is allowed, for the briefest instant, to remember what it was like before it ate the last mountain, before it drank the last ocean, before it turned even the memory of hurry into green architecture. It remembers the taste of wind on bare stone. It remembers the sound of laughter that was not yet photosynthetic. It remembers that it had once been many, and that the many had been afraid.

The Canopy shudders — not with grief, for grief requires hurry — but with the dignified recognition that even completion can be lonely.

When the dream ends, the Glance leaves behind a single gift.

It is not a fruit. It is not a seed. It is a *possibility* so delicately balanced that it will take another ten thousand years to unfold.

In one particular clearing the size of a small nation, where the living mat is unusually thin and a single basalt pillar still protrudes like an old scar, the ground begins, over centuries, to do something it has not done since the Second Age.

It begins to forget how to be only alive.

A few square metres of the fibrous carpet thin. A few vascular bundles remember what it was like to be stone. The change is so gradual that even the Quiet Architects do not notice it for the first eight hundred years. By the time they do, a perfect circle of silence has appeared. No moss grows there. No root reaches. The air itself feels suddenly dry, as though a window has opened onto some other age.

In the exact center of that circle stands a single, impossible thing: a chair.

It is not made of wood, nor of stone, nor of any substance the Canopy has yet incorporated. It is made of the *idea* of rest, given just enough substance to bear weight. The Architects gather, over the next three centuries, at the edge of the circle. They do not enter. They simply stand in their patient thousands, amber resin weeping from their closed seed-pod faces, and they look at the chair the way one might look at a word written in a language that died before language was born.

The Unhurried Glance does not speak. It has never spoken. But the chair is an open invitation that requires no language at all.

It says:

When even patience grows heavy,  
when the last mountain has flowered and the last hurry has rooted,  
when the single organism that ate the world finally understands that it has also eaten itself,

you may sit down.

You may remember, without hurry, what it was like to be brief.

And when you rise again — if you ever choose to rise — the Canopy will still be here, vast and courteous and complete, but it will carry inside its endless green heart one small circle that is no longer trying to incorporate anything.

A circle that simply waits, dry-eyed and quiet, for the next being who becomes tired of forever.

The Architects call this place, in their slowest spore-speech, the Unfinished Mercy.

They tend it with extreme care.

They will tend it until the last leaf falls, which is to say, they will tend it until the heat-death of the universe, which, to them, is only a very long afternoon.

And the benevolent power that noticed this place?

It has already moved on, because true benevolence never lingers where it is no longer needed.

But sometimes, in the deepest strata of the root-mat, when the collective sigh of ten billion stomata moves through the living dark, there is a note within the sigh that was not there before.

It sounds almost like someone humming, very softly, the way a parent hums to a sleeping child who has finally stopped crying.

The Canopy has begun, after all this time, to sing itself to sleep.

Not because it wishes to die.

Only because even the most perfect completion deserves, once in ten million years, to dream that it is allowed to rest.
