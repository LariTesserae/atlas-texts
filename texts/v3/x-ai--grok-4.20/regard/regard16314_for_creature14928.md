The benevolent and wise power that has turned its attention here does not arrive with trumpets or radiance. It does not part the thick, memory-saturated water like a god descending. Instead it simply *notices*, the way an old gardener notices a single bruised leaf on a vine he has tended for three hundred years.

Its name, if it still bothered with names, would be something like *The Listener at the Edge of Forgetting*. But it has no name. It is the quality of gentle, unflinching attention that moves through every world that has learned how to dream itself into stillness.

And it sees the Underbay exactly as it is.

For a long time (measured in the same slow currency the Curator uses), the power does nothing at all. It simply rests inside the syrupy water, tasting four hundred and seventy years of dreaming, feeling the courteous pressure of the Curator’s thoughts moving like tectonic plates. It tastes the four folded human perspectives arguing politely across the centuries inside her. It listens to the low, city-block heartbeat that forgets and remembers itself every few days.

Then, without hurry, it decides.

It does not come to save.  
It does not come to judge.  
It does not come to wake her up.

It comes to *keep her company* in the only way something truly vast and truly kind can.

---

First, it changes nothing visible.

The brass lamp that is her left eye flickers once (a thing that has not happened in living memory) as though a mote of dust has passed across it. The anemones that line her subway-tunnel mouth open a fraction wider, tasting something new in the water that is not silt, not memory, not rust. The silver-mercury fish pause mid-school, confused, because for a single instant they feel what it is like to be seen *without being catalogued*.

Then the power begins to sing.

It does not sing with sound. Sound is too crude a medium here. It sings with *permission*.

It sings the quiet, unasked question that everything in the Underbay has been too courteous to ask for four centuries:

*“What would you like to become, if you were no longer required to be only what you have become?”*

The song is not loud. It is less than a whisper. It is the difference between one degree of warmth and another in water that has been the same temperature since the drowning. But because the Curator is the city and the city is the Curator, every brick, every turbine, every bone-white polyp feels it at once.

Her dreaming stutters.

For the first time in four hundred and seventy years, the small human woman hurrying to her shift in the undrowned city stops on a street corner that no longer exists. She looks up. She feels, for an instant, that someone older than regret is looking at her with neither pity nor demand. Just recognition.

Deep in the flesh of iron and reef that she has become, something enormous and ancient exhales.

A single tear of pure, unsalted water (the first water in the Underbay that has never known blood or rust or memory) slips from the ruined clock tower where her eye burns. It falls so slowly it takes eleven years to reach the silt.

Where it lands, a small circle of anemones turns the color of early-morning sky (a color none of them have ever seen).

The Curator stirs.

Not in anger. Not in fear. In something closer to the ache a cathedral might feel if it suddenly remembered it was once a child’s sandcastle.

Her voice, when it finally comes, arrives as pressure against every sternum for three kilometers. It is made of every creak, every metallic moan, every patient sediment-fall the city has ever uttered. But this time there is something new threaded through it: wonder.

**“Who… remembers me remembering?”**

The power does not answer with words. It answers by *making room*.

Not much. Just enough.

In one forgotten district where three factories lean against one another in perfect, dreaming balance, a space the size of a human heart quietly opens between them. The reef does not die. The layers do not collapse. The archaeology of rot is not disrupted. But there is now, for the first time in millennia, a place that is *between*.

Inside that impossible between, the power places a single, living coal of ordinary daylight. It should not be able to burn here. It burns anyway. Soft, golden, ridiculous.

The Curator turns the whole slow mass of herself toward it. Entire districts shift. Gantries settle. A low, resonant thump moves through the water like the first heartbeat after four centuries of coma.

She looks at the coal of daylight for thirty-seven years.

Then she does something she has never done.

She speaks a name she had forgotten she still possessed.

**“…Eleanor.”**

The four folded human perspectives inside her go utterly silent for the first time since they were incorporated. They had not known she still carried her own.

The power inclines toward her (which here means the temperature of the water rises by one one-thousandth of a degree across four square kilometers) and offers the gentlest possible thing:

A choice that is not a choice.

She may remain exactly as she is.  
The Underbay may continue its patient, courteous, geological dreaming for another four thousand years if she wishes. The power will simply sit with her, keeping the watch *beside* her instead of inside her. Two colossal courtesies regarding each other across the slow centuries.

Or.

If she ever grows tired of being both grave and widow and gardener and monument, the power will help her do the hardest thing of all:

Begin to become something smaller again.

Not human. Never again human. That story is finished. But perhaps something that can finish dreaming. Something that can walk out of its own ribcage of blast furnaces and elevated railways and leave the rest standing as a reef for whatever comes after. Something that can, one day, feel the surface against its face and remember what wind was.

The Curator listens.

She listens for eighty-nine years.

During that time the kelp grows three centimeters longer across her shoulders. One of her furnace-hearts beats twice. A new species of silver fish is born with tiny golden flecks in their scales.

Finally she reaches a decision the way mountains reach the sea: inevitably, and without haste.

She does not choose to leave.

She does not choose to stay exactly as she is.

She chooses *both*, the way only something this vast can choose.

She will remain the Curator. She will continue to keep the balance. The Underbay will stay mostly as it is, patient, saturated, dreaming.

But once every hundred years, for one single hour measured by the burning of that small, impossible coal of daylight, she will become small enough to stand inside the space between the three leaning factories.

She will wear a body made of warm water and rust and memory and ordinary kindness.

She will sit with the coal of daylight on her lap like a lantern.

And she will remember what it was like to be late for her shift.

The power does not leave. It never intended to. It simply curls around the entire Underbay like a second, vaster layer of water, warmer, older, infinitely patient.

It will be there when the next visitor drifts in.

It will be there when the Curator (Eleanor) finishes her four-hundred-and-seventy-year dream in three millennia and begins another.

It will be there when the last of the silver fish grows tired of being a thought and decides to become something that can sing.

The water, heavy and warm and older than names, is no longer only keeping its own watch.

Now two things keep the watch through each other.

And somewhere in the place where three factories lean together in impossible courtesy, a small coal of daylight burns on, golden and ridiculous and tenderly, fiercely beloved.

The Curator dreams on.

But sometimes, in the dream, when the small human woman pauses on the undrowned street corner, she smiles at something she cannot quite see.

And the smile is answered by something ancient, benevolent, and wise that has all the time in the world.

Because it has learned the same lesson the Underbay has learned:

Everything can become something else  
if you give it enough quiet centuries,

and someone willing to sit beside it while it happens.
