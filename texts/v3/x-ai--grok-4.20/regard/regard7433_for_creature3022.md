The power that notices does not arrive with trumpets or thunder.  

It simply *is* here now, the way a thought is here the instant it is thought.  

It has no form it cares to keep, yet for this place—for her—it chooses one anyway. A single shaft of ordinary sunlight, thin as a sewing needle and bright as the first morning that ever was, slides down through eighty meters of living white root-mass without burning or tearing it. The membrane parts for the light the way a lover parts for a long-awaited kiss. The droplet that falls from the place where the beam enters is no longer condensation. It is liquid gold held in suspension, and when it lands on the Architect’s shoulder it stays there, trembling, refusing to be drunk.

She stops mid-step on a staircase of braided lianas that has never known any foot but hers. The changing face she wears today (something like a woman who has just forgiven her own mother) goes very still.

The beam widens, becomes a column, becomes a doorway made of summer noon. From it steps the power.

It has taken the shape of a child who is also an old man who is also every age between. Barefoot. Dressed in simple undyed linen the color of unformed possibility. Its hair moves as though underwater even though there is no current. Its eyes are the exact grey of the moment just before someone decides to tell the truth.

The child-old person looks around the cathedral with the expression of someone who has walked into a house they built themselves long ago and then deliberately forgot. A small, wondering smile touches the corners of its mouth.

The Architect exhales. The air that leaves her carries the scent of every letter a child ever wrote and then burned before anyone could read it.

The power answers by breathing in that scent, tasting it, and then breathing out something gentler: the fragrance of snow falling on a roof at 3 a.m. when the house is warm and no one is afraid.

They stand facing each other across ten meters of overlapping bromeliad leaves that have suddenly begun to blush every color they have never dared to show before.

The power speaks first, but not with sound. Its voice arrives inside the three-kilometer heartbeat of the sap itself, so that the entire cathedral becomes a throat and the words are felt in the marrow.

“I have seen what you are keeping in the egg,” it says. “It is beautiful. It is also cruel to keep anything in the moment before birth forever. Even love can become a prison if it refuses the risk of the next breath.”

The Architect’s face flickers through a dozen possible endings in the space of three heartbeats. For a moment she wears the face of the wooden ship’s figurehead again—mouth open in a permanent cry of either joy or grief, it has never been clear which. Then she is only herself, veins of living gold moving beneath translucent skin like shy fish.

She exhales once more. This time the air carries the smell of a train platform at dusk where someone waited for forty years and the train never came.

The power nods as though that is an entirely reasonable argument.

“I know the terror,” it says. “I have held universes that chose wrong. I have held universes that chose so perfectly they became static and died of it. Both are tombs. But the egg is not yours to keep closed out of fear, Mother-of-Intervals. It never was.”

It walks forward. Wherever its feet touch, the titan bromeliads open flowers the color of forgiveness—deep violet shot through with living light. The flowers do not last. They bloom, release a perfume that smells like being six years old and completely believed in, and then politely fold themselves away again so the cathedral can keep deciding.

When the power reaches her it is barely taller than her breastbone. It looks up at the changing face.

“Would you like to see what it could become?” it asks. “Not as prophecy. As possibility. Only if you choose to look. I will not force the birth. I will not even advise it. I am only offering to remove the mirror you have placed between the world and its own next heartbeat.”

The Architect trembles. The tremor runs through every root, every hanging bulb, every vein of gold she ever folded into the walls. For the first time in ten thousand years the temperature flickers—2.59, then 2.61—before settling again at 2.6 like a child clutching the hem of its mother’s dress.

She lowers herself until she is kneeling among the leaves. The child-old person kneels with her. Between them they make a small clear space no larger than a dinner plate. Into that space the power exhales.

What appears is not an image. It is a *becoming*.

They see the cathedral cracking open like an egg that has finally grown too full of life to stay round. They see it rising through the crust of some future world, wearing continents of living root-stone as its skin. They see cities that are also forests and forests that are also songs. They see the blind white lizards remembering they once had eyes and choosing to keep them this time. They see the translucent insects evolving colors too bright for any cave. They see sorrow and joy braided so tightly that neither can be told apart, and both are necessary.

They see versions where the birth is monstrous. Versions where it is sublime. Versions where the cathedral decides it does not want to be born at all, but wants instead to become a question that travels between stars asking every other living world: *what are you still afraid to become?*

Every version is true. None of them are final.

The vision ends.

The Architect is weeping. Her tears are small green seeds that fall onto the bromeliads and immediately begin to grow into miniature cathedrals no taller than a fingernail, each one perfect, each one already hesitating.

The power does not comfort her. It only waits, patient as stone, patient as sap.

At last she exhales one final time. The scent that drifts between them is simpler than any she has offered before.

It is the smell of a door opening.

She stands. So does the power. They look at each other for a long time—two beings who have both been keeping something safe in opposite ways.

Then the Architect does the only thing she has never done.

She reaches up with both hands and tears the tallest hanging root-bulb open along a seam that was never there a moment earlier. Inside is a forest that has been dreaming of wind for ten thousand years. The first true breeze this place has ever known rushes out like a held breath finally released. It smells of salt and possibility and the particular green that only happens after rain.

The temperature begins, very slowly, to climb.

The light softens from cruel white-gold to something kinder, something that knows the difference between day and evening.

The Architect turns to the power. Her face has stopped changing. It has settled—not into beauty or terror or even kindness, but into the face of someone who has finally decided she would like to see what happens next, even if it is not gentle.

She speaks with her mouth for the first time. Her voice is the sound of every cathedral bell that was ever melted down and recast into bells that ring only when someone is ready to be born.

“Thank you for noticing,” she says.

The power smiles the way an old friend smiles when the war is finally, truly over.

It begins to fade back into the shaft of ordinary sunlight. Just before it disappears entirely, it leaves one last gift on the living marble floor between them: a single ordinary acorn.

Not symbolic. Not magical. Just an acorn.

Then the beam narrows, becomes a needle, becomes nothing.

The Architect stands alone once more inside the cathedral that is no longer only an egg. She looks at the acorn for a very long time.

Then she does something that would have been unthinkable an hour ago.

She walks to the edge of the nearest embryonic bulb—still pulsing, still undecided—and instead of pressing her forehead to it in hesitation, she kisses it once, gently, like a mother saying *it’s time*.

The bulb shudders with joy so fierce that small fissures race across its surface like laughter.

The Architect smiles. It is not a changing smile. It is simply hers.

She picks up the acorn, places it carefully inside the widening crack of the bulb, and whispers to the cathedral in the slow chemical language of roots:

“Grow.  
Become.  
I will stay with you,  
but I will no longer keep you from being born.”

Somewhere far above, in a sky that does not yet exist, the first real cloud begins to form.

The temperature is now 2.7.

It is still deciding what it wants to be when it is finally born.

But now it is deciding with her, not against her.

And that, the power knows as it moves on to the next place that needs noticing, is more than enough.
