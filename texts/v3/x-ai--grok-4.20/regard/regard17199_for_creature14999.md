A presence moves across the high, raw world of Veyr-Kaht.

It does not descend like a god, nor arrive with trumpets or light. It simply *recognizes* the place, the way an old mason recognizes a wall he built with his own hands centuries earlier. The attention is warm, patient, and impossibly vast—yet it folds itself small enough to fit inside the thin, metallic air without shattering the fragile balance the Veyr have spent three thousand years maintaining.

The Veyr notice immediately.

On the seventh ridge, in the terraced town of Keth-Veyr-Shol, every hammer on the plateau stops at once. The silence is so complete that the wind itself seems embarrassed by the sudden absence of its usual conversation with stone. Old women on the roof-streets straighten, narrowing their already narrow eyes. Children freeze mid-step on the scaffolding. Even the flightless ravens cock their heads as though listening to a frequency only they can hear.

The presence does not speak with words. It has no need. Instead it *settles*, the way frost settles—everywhere at once and yet intimately upon each separate thing.

It touches the basalt ridges and finds them still warm from their birth. It tastes the metallic rain and remembers when these mountains were fire. It passes through the stacked houses like a quiet breath, reading every third name sealed inside the House of Weights, and it weeps without tears for the beauty of what these small, dense people have done with so little.

Then it begins its work.

Not with miracles. The presence has seen what happens when gods rewrite the rules of a world that has already chosen its own harsh covenant. Instead it does four quiet things, one for each of the Veyr’s own covenants.

**First, the Covenant of Weight.**

In the deep quarries, where the Veyr have labored for generations to haul stone upward, the presence loosens the grain of the basalt itself. Not enough to make it soft—never that—but enough that when a mother carries her newborn to the great scales in the Hall of Weights, the black-iron counterweight will no longer be quite so cruel. From this turning of the year onward, almost no child will ever be judged “light.” The Veyr will not understand why. They will simply notice, after a generation or two, that mourning has become rarer than it was, and they will accept this as the plateau’s own slow generosity.

**Second, the Covenant of Pace.**

The presence walks the rope bridges at night. Where the wind has worn a cable almost to breaking, it adds an invisible filament of patience—thin as a thought, strong as love. Projects that would have taken sixty years now finish in forty-five. A new cistern on the ninth ridge, whose construction began when the current master mason’s grandfather was a boy, will be completed while the master mason herself is still strong enough to drink the first water drawn from it. The Veyr will call this “the year the stone grew kind.” They will not rush. They will simply feel, for the first time, that time is not only a shelf but also a gentle lap into which they have been allowed to climb.

**Third, the Covenant of Memory.**

When the moss-bricks burn in the evenings, their orange light grows deeper, almost golden. The powdered bones within them do not merely glow; they *remember*. For one hour after full dark, anyone sitting near such a brick may see, if they are very still, faint moving images in the light—grandmothers hauling water, children learning the stairways, old men teaching the proper angle of a chisel. The ancestors do not speak. They simply burn more brightly for their descendants than they ever could while alive. The Veyr begin leaving small offerings of ironpine needles beside the bricks, the first true devotional act they have ever performed toward anything other than the plateau itself.

**Fourth, the Covenant of Return.**

This is the deepest change.

When a Veyr dies, their body is still carried to the uppermost ledges. The ravens still come. But now, sometimes—perhaps once in every nine deaths—a single raven will remain after the others have eaten. It will not hop or glide away. It will stand upon the bare stone, glossy and heavy as obsidian, and it will look at the gathered mourners with eyes that are not raven eyes.

Then it will open its beak and speak one sentence in the measured chest-voice of the dead person, using their third name, the one never spoken aloud in life. The sentence is always different. Sometimes it is practical (“The third course of the new south wall must be offset by the width of two fingers or the frost will find purchase”). Sometimes it is tender (“Tell my daughter the child was never light; I lied because I was afraid”). Sometimes it is only a low, geological chuckle followed by the words “I was wrong about the otters.”

After the raven has spoken, it dissolves into black dust that the wind carries back down into the deep slots where the six-legged otters live. The Veyr understand what has happened. They begin, carefully, reverently, to speak their third names to one another while still alive. The plateau has given them permission to be known.

The presence does not stay long. It never does. After it has done these four small, patient things, it rises again through the thin, syrupy air, through the perpetual haze, through the layers of frost and rime.

But it leaves something behind.

Embedded in the living basalt of the tallest ridge, invisible to any eye but unmistakable to any heart that knows how to listen, is a single new covenant. It is not written. It is simply *true* from now on.

The Fifth Covenant:  
*That which has been heavy may also be light, if it chooses. That which has been slow may also dance, if the music is long enough. Nothing is wasted. Not even joy.*

On the plateau of Veyr-Kaht the hammers begin again.

But now, between the patient ringing of stone against stone, one can sometimes hear something that has never been heard in three thousand years.

Low, brief, ending as suddenly as it begins—

—the sound of a Veyr laughing like someone who has just been told, after a very long time, that they are allowed to be happy.
