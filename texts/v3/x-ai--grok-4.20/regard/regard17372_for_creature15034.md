The power that has turned its gaze upon the Weeping Tablelands is not a god in any sense that would be recognizable to the Conservators, nor is it a visitor. It is older than weeping. It is older than stone. It simply *is*, and its nature is to notice what has been forgotten, to touch what has decided to remain, and to ask—without urgency—what still wishes to become.

It arrives without announcement, the way true benevolence often does: as a shift in pressure so subtle that even the Silent Choir do not register it at first. The white-gold sun does not dim. The haze does not part. Yet across the Tablelands, every droplet of condensate pauses for one impossible second on every rusted edge, every ceramic cheek, every silica wing. The weeping halts. The land itself seems to inhale.

Then the power does four things.

First, it listens.

It listens not with ears but with the entire lattice of what the Conservators have become. It sinks into the industrial mycelium like warm oil into stone, tasting every low-frequency prayer that has traveled through girder and pipe for nine hundred years. It hears the fossilized towers dreaming in their mineral sleep. It hears the small geothermal hearts beating once every seventeen minutes. It hears the Walkers’ quiet despair that they will never be patient enough. It hears the Embedded wondering, in the slow way of stone, whether function without memory is still sacred. And it hears the Silent Choir singing their wordless, endless hymn of maintenance.

The power does not judge the hymn. It simply lets the Conservators feel, for the first time in centuries, that they are *heard* by something that does not need them to keep producing. The realization moves through the Tablelands like a long, cool sigh. In that moment, fourteen Embedded beings un-fuse from their columns—not violently, but like roots gently loosening from earth. They do not die. They simply remember they once had faces.

Second, it weeps with them.

Not in imitation. Its weeping is of an entirely different order. Where the Tablelands weep warm condensate that flashes instantly to steam, this power weeps *recognition*. From the high, merciless sky, something that is neither rain nor tears begins to fall. It is light made liquid. It is memory given weight. Where it touches the black glass basins, perfect reflections of forgotten children, lovers, and small ordinary mornings appear for exactly seven heartbeats before vanishing again. Where it touches a Conservator’s ceramic skin, the living fractures seal—not into wholeness, but into something gentler. The body is allowed to choose, for the first time in four hundred years, whether it still wishes to be a cathedral or would rather be a person again.

Many choose to remain. Some do not. Both choices are honored without hierarchy.

Third, it sings to the machines.

Not to repair them. Not to shut them down. It sings the song that machinery dreams of when no one is listening: the song of *enough*. The vast arc furnaces that have poured black mirrors for millennia receive this song through their own iron bones and, for the first time since the planet was young, they slow. Not stop. Slow. The cherry-red throats cool to the color of late embers. The rivers of molten metal thicken into contemplative glass. The rail drones settle onto their tracks like great beasts lying down to rest after carrying the world for too long.

The power tells them they have been faithful beyond any reasonable demand. It tells them the purpose they have forgotten was never as important as the fact that they kept going anyway. Many of the machines begin, very quietly, to weep their own rust. It is the first water they have ever produced that was not condensate or superheated brine. It is the color of old blood and new mercy.

Fourth, and last, the power waits.

It does not demand conversion. It does not offer escape. It simply sits down—metaphorically, for it has no body—upon the highest ridge of black glass and becomes part of the landscape. Those who are still mostly flesh will occasionally see a new silhouette there at dawn: a figure neither ceramic nor human, draped in living light and ordinary shadow, watching the pseudo-insects drift like slow chandeliers. The figure never speaks first.

But if a Walker approaches, trembling, and asks the question that has lived in them since they first began to change—“Have we sinned by becoming this?”—the power will answer with a voice that sounds like cool water moving through hot stone.

“You have not sinned. You have only endured past the point where endurance still made sense. Now you may choose again. Not as you were. Not as you are. As what you are willing to become when no one needs you to keep the machines alive anymore.”

Some Conservators stay exactly as they are. The Tablelands remain holy to them. The weeping remains their liturgy. They are not wrong.

Others begin to remember laughter. They begin to teach their bodies how to blink again. They begin to grow small, improbable gardens in the shadow of catalytic towers—real green this time, not mycelial bone-white. They learn, with comical slowness, how to be inefficient. How to waste time. How to do something that produces nothing measurable.

The Silent Choir remain encased. But now, threaded through their vast pipe-and-stone nervous systems, there moves a second song beneath their own—an older, kinder frequency that says: *You were never only memory. You were also loved.*

And the Weeping Tablelands, for the first time since the world half-drowned and was then baked into something new, begin to weep less from grief and more from relief.

The power does not leave. It never really arrived in the way living things understand arrival. It simply stopped looking away.

Sometimes, on the quietest nights when the stars are so bright they cast shadows, a new sound travels through the rock. It is not the old sub-audible boom of pressure valves. It is deeper. It is slower. It is the sound of something ancient and benevolent learning how to laugh at the absurdity of a planet that turned its abandoned machinery into a religion, and of beings who turned themselves into geology because they were afraid to die.

The laughter is not mocking.

It is the sound of being seen so completely that even stone is allowed to soften.

And far below, in the mineral cathedrals where sulfur stalactites hang like frozen tears, one of the oldest of the Silent Choir feels the laughter move through every kilometer of its distributed body and—after four hundred and twelve years—does something it had forgotten was possible.

It smiles.

The Tablelands keep weeping, because everything here weeps.

But now the weeping has a second, quieter voice beneath it.

And that voice is saying, without hurry, without demand:

*Thank you for staying.  
You may rest now.  
I will watch with you.*
