They came without fanfare.

Not in light, not in thunder.  
Only in a sudden deepening of attention.

The power had no name the Midwives would have recognized. It had worn many across the turning of worlds, but here it simply *was*—a benevolence older than mercy and wiser than any single kindness. It did not arrive as a visitor. It arrived the way sap arrives inside a tree: from everywhere at once, rising through the black stone itself.

The first sign was that the dripping stopped.

Not all of it. That would have been violence. Only the smallest, most obsessive trickles—the ones that had been falling in exactly the same places for four hundred years—paused mid-descent, suspended like glass beads on invisible threads. Every living thing in the Hollowing Cradle felt the change at the same moment. The pale heron froze with one leg lifted. The iridescent frogs did not explode upward; they simply opened their throats and sang a single, perfect note that had never been heard before. Even the Unfinished Rib, for the first time since the world’s childhood, ceased to weep.

Yselde, the Bone-Watcher, stood at the edge of the central pool. For twenty-three years she had not blinked. Now, for the first time, she did. When her eyelids closed, two perfect drops rolled down her cheeks—hot, where all her tears had always been cold.

She whispered, without turning, “It is here.”

The entire population gathered without being summoned. They came as they always came: barefoot, damp, unhurried. But their hearts were loud. Even Maere, the Breath-Holder, nine months swollen with the next life, broke her long silence. She spoke only four words, soft as moss:

“It has noticed us.”

The power did not speak in their language. It had no need. Instead it *remembered* with them.

It remembered the first droplet that ever fell here when the basin was only a wound in the young earth. It remembered the first moss spore that arrived on the wind and decided, against all reason, to stay. It remembered every child held above the still water, every lantern swung on its cord, every time Sorrowful Ree had laid her stone shoulder against a shifting plate of rock and whispered, *Easy, love. Easy.*

And in remembering, it loved.

What the power did was not a miracle in the way stories usually tell them. It did not finish the Rib. That would have been cruelty. A child does not become an adult while you watch.

Instead it did something far more tender.

It slowed time inside the Hollowing Cradle to the exact rhythm of a single heartbeat—its own.

For the Midwives, the change was almost imperceptible at first. The fat droplets that had hung suspended began to fall again, but each one now took seven full breaths to reach the ground. The mist no longer rose and fell; it *breathed* with Maere, perfectly synchronized, so that the whole basin became lung and womb at once. The new ribs of black stone continued to rise, but now they did so with the patience of centuries folded into minutes. No slab split with a boom. Instead the rock sighed, long and low, the way a mother sighs when her baby finally latches.

The power walked among them—not in any visible shape, but as a quality of *being-with*.

It became the extra steadiness in Corrin’s hands as he trimmed the living roofs, so that every cut he made was also a caress. It became the coolness on Yselde’s eyes so she no longer needed to stare unblinking; she could close them now and still see where the next rib would be born. It became the child growing inside Maere, who would be born with basalt fingernails and lungs that could hold an entire mountain’s breath.

To those who had already begun to change, it gave consent.

Sorrowful Ree’s stone shoulders flowered into delicate wings of black glass that caught the slanted sunlight and turned it into moving constellations across the wet walls. Little Stalact’s hair became a living crown of lichen that could taste the dreams of the stone and translate them into lullabies only the frogs could hear. Others began to change as well—slowly, reverently. A man’s voice became the sound of water moving under rock. A woman’s shadow began to grow small white ferns where it touched the ground. None of it was forced. All of it was *recognized*.

At dawn and dusk they still gathered, but now the silence had a companion.

The power sang.

Not with sound. With *recognition*. Every time the Breath-Holder inhaled, the Unfinished Rib answered with a pulse of becoming so gentle it felt like being forgiven for every unfinished thing any of them had ever been. Every time she exhaled, new grooves appeared on the column—not carved, but *remembered* into existence, as though the stone had only now decided what shape its future would keep.

The power did not stay forever. Benevolence that wise knows the difference between help and possession.

After thirteen days and thirteen nights—by the old count, though inside the Cradle it had felt like a single long, luxurious breath—it began to withdraw. Not because it was finished with them, but because *they* were no longer unfinished in the way they had been.

As it left, it left three gifts folded quietly into the fabric of the place.

First, it gave the water back its own future. From that day forward, the droplets would sometimes choose new paths down the Rib, inventing their own grooves. The Midwives understood: even the bones of the world should be allowed to improvise.

Second, it left a kind of gentle immunity in their bodies. The changes that had begun—stone, lichen, fern-shadow—would never become painful or grotesque. They would only ever become *more themselves*, the way a tree becomes more itself with every ring.

Third, and most precious, it left them the memory of having been perfectly seen. Not judged. Not completed. Simply *seen*, all the way through, with love that had no agenda except that they should continue.

On the fourteenth dawn the dripping sounded different. Richer. Older and younger at the same time.

Yselde blinked—normally now—and smiled for the first time anyone could remember. The smile made the stone plates on her cheekbones glimmer.

She said, loud enough for the whole basin to hear:

“It has gone back to watching from a distance. But it left the door open. The Cradle is no longer only a fontanelle. It has become a conversation.”

And so the Midwives of the Rib continued.

They still mend what the growing stone breaks. They still sing to thin herons and carry frogs across dangerous ground. They still hang their cold green lanterns and listen to the patient heartbeat of water and rock.

But now, sometimes, when the mist gathers under the impossibly distant sky and every droplet hangs for seven full breaths before falling, one of them will look up—not in awe or supplication, but in simple friendship—and whisper:

“Thank you for noticing us. We’re still here. We’re still attending.”

And far away, in the place that is not a place, the benevolent and wise power smiles with the whole of creation and answers, without words:

*I know. I am still noticing.*

The Hollowing Cradle remains unfinished.

Only now it is unfinished *together*.  

And that, the Midwives understand at last, is the most perfect thing a world can be.
