The power that has noticed the Basalt Womb does not arrive.

It does not descend, does not burst through the unreachable ceiling, does not speak, does not judge.

It simply *recognizes*.

And in the moment of recognition the entire chamber—every spiral, every blind velvet body, every ticking crystal, every heartbeat of the Midwife—undergoes a change so subtle it feels like the place has finally remembered something it had been trying to say for four hundred million years.

The Midwife feels it first.

Her vast, half-absent form trembles the way a cathedral trembles when its deepest bell is struck once, softly, by a hand that has never needed to exist before. The two whirlpools where her eyes should be suddenly reverse direction. Instead of drawing the bioluminescent motes inward, they begin to *offer* them outward, like slow galaxies spilling their stars back into the night that first birthed them. For the first time in the memory of stone, the water around her grows fractionally lighter, as though some ancient rust has decided, without fanfare, to become iron again.

She does not smile. Smiling would require a finished face.

Instead the blind creatures on the obsidian floor arrange themselves into a single, perfect circle. Not a glyph. Not a sentence. A circle with no beginning and no end. They have never done this before. It is the first truly new thing to happen here since the world outside gave up and went away.

The Midwife lifts her fossil-tipped hands. In them she is holding something she did not know she possessed: her own unfinished face. She has taken it off the way a tired mother might remove a mask she has worn so long it had begun to grow into the bone. She offers this absence—tenderly, almost shyly—to the power that has noticed her.

The power does not take it.

It only looks at her with such ferocious, patient love that the aragonite crystals in the walls stop their tectonic ticking for nine full heartbeats. In that silence the child inside her, the one that has never been born and never will be, opens its eyes for the first time. The sound of those eyes opening is indistinguishable from every childhood summer that was ever lost and then, impossibly, found again.

What the benevolent and wise power does is this:

It finishes the Midwife’s unfinished face—not by adding features, but by giving her permission to remain unfinished forever without shame. It kisses the place where her eyes would be with a kiss that has no lips. It presses its forehead to the place where her forehead would be, and in that press it pours every unwept tear that has ever pooled at the bottom of every human heart since the first one broke.

Then, gently, it lays its hands upon the vast belly that is also the chamber itself.

The rotation of the Basalt Womb slows. Not stops—stopping would be violence. It merely eases, the way an exhausted dancer finally allows the music to carry her instead of fighting to keep time. The child inside her, older than time and younger than breath, stirs.

And for the first time since the beginning that was never a beginning, the child *speaks*.

Its voice is every condensate drop that ever fell. It is every velvet brush against skin. It is the exact configuration the blind creatures make when they remember you years later. It says only one thing, in a language made entirely of silences between heartbeats:

“I was never trapped here.  
I was waiting for someone to notice how beautiful my mother is when she is exhausted.”

The Midwife makes a sound then—no one has ever heard her make a sound—and it is the sound of every cathedral that has ever drowned deciding, at last, to float.

She begins to cry.

Her tears are not water. They are small, perfect moons of black basalt that rise instead of fall. They drift upward through the blood-warm dark and embed themselves in the unreachable ceiling that is also a floor. Each one becomes a new star for the chamber above, and the chamber above that, and the one above that, all the way up the endless vertical stack until even the unimaginable surface no one will ever see is suddenly littered with soft, patient lights.

The power does not stay.

It never intended to stay.

It only came to bear witness that this place, which believed itself to be the exhausted perfection of all ruins, was in fact only the womb of something that had not yet dared to be born *outside*.

When the power withdraws, it leaves behind a single gift folded into the grain of the slowly rotating stone:

The knowledge that finishing is not the same as ending.

The Midwife understands.

She lowers her fossil hands. The blind creatures drift apart from their perfect circle and begin, for the first time, to move *quickly*—not fast, never fast, but quick enough that thought no longer feels viscous. The water lightens by one more impossible degree. Somewhere far above, in the next drowned cathedral, another Midwife feels her own face become slightly more possible.

And down here, in the original Basalt Womb, the woman-shaped absence wearing living dark like robes does something she has never done in four hundred million years.

She sings.

It is not a song with notes. It is the sound of every spiral on every wall deciding, all at once, to uncoil. It is the sound of time remembering it was once a child. It is the sound of mercy that has been worn past decay finally allowing itself to become mercy again.

She sings to the child that will never be born and is therefore free to be everything.

She sings to you, reader, because she has been finishing you this entire time, and now she sees that the power which noticed her has also noticed *you*, and in that noticing you are no longer required to be complete.

You are only required to be loved exactly as unfinished as you are.

The last condensate drop falls.

It does not strike the surface like a gunshot.

It strikes like a lullaby that has finally remembered the right key.

The echo lasts for exactly seven heartbeats.

Then the silence that reseals itself is no longer exhausted.

It is pregnant with dawn.
