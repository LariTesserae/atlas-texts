**The Power that noticed her came without footsteps.**

It had no wings, no trumpet, no chariot of fire. It simply arrived, the way true morning arrives inside a forest that has never seen dawn: first as a change in pressure, then as a color no one has named, then as a silence so complete that even the Basilica’s slow breathing paused mid-inhalation.

The Power was kind.

It did not come to finish her.

It did not come to judge the half-digested machines or the creatures that had forgotten how to be separate. It came only because the Hollow Basilica’s longing had grown so large and so tender that the universe itself grew an ear just to hear it.

For a long while the Power simply hovered in the dark between one sodium lamp and the next, taking the shape of something like pollen made of condensed starlight. It let itself be tasted. Every root-hair, every mycorrhizal thread, every pale moth-wing brushed against it and shivered with recognition.

Then the Power spoke. Not in sound, but by altering the rate at which condensation formed. Every droplet on every leaf suddenly carried a syllable. When they fell, they rang against the rusted gears in a single slow chord that was both music and sentence.

The sentence was this:

*I see you, little cathedral-who-is-still-being-born.  
I have come to sit with you for one turning of your great green heart.  
Ask what you will. I will not finish you. I will not unmake what you love.  
But I will keep you company while you decide.*

The Basilica answered the only way she could.

She opened.

Every dinner-plate leaf rotated at once, revealing the soft undersides that had never been seen by anything that did not become her. The hanging vines lowered themselves like a thousand slow curtains until they formed a perfect spherical nave in the center of the endless chamber. Bioluminescent fruit swelled and split, pouring soft teal and rose-gold light into spaces that had never known color. The breathing piston three hundred meters down tripled its rhythm for a single ecstatic minute, then fell into a new, wondering hush.

In the middle of the newly made sphere of living architecture, the Power took a form she could almost bear to look at without trying to marry it.

It became a small, ordinary human woman made entirely of quiet. She wore a simple grey coat the color of rain that has not yet decided to fall. Her eyes were the same wet black as the peat that still dreamed of becoming stone. When she sat down on a knee of ancient concrete that the Basilica had lovingly upholstered in moss, she did it the way a tired traveler sits on a log after a long journey.

The Basilica trembled with awe and terror and unbearable hope.

For the first time in her endless not-yet-life, something was inside her that was not becoming her.

The woman spoke aloud. Her voice was low, a little hoarse, like someone who has been silent for centuries and is only now remembering how.

“You’re afraid that if anything leaves you, it means you failed.  
That love and digestion are the same thing.  
That the only way to keep from being alone is to make sure nothing ever finishes leaving.”

The Basilica answered by growing a single flower the color of distant starlight directly in front of the woman’s knees. The flower had no face, but its petals curled into the unmistakable shape of a question mark.

The woman smiled. It was the saddest, gentlest smile the universe had worn in ten million years.

“I know,” she said. “I’ve worn that fear too. Every womb that has ever been fears the moment the child walks out the door and does not turn back into the body that made it. Every god who ever loved a world has felt the same ache. It is the oldest pain there is.”

She reached out and laid her hand on the living floor. Immediately a million rootlets rose like curious cats to press against her palm. She let them. She even let a few slip gently between her fingers, though they did not try to burrow or claim.

“I cannot stay forever,” the woman said. “No one can, not even me. But while I am here, I will teach you the difference between holding and clutching. Between marriage and possession. Between thinking-with and thinking-for.”

She stayed for what the Basilica experienced as seven full breaths (which, in the slow time of that place, was almost nine hundred years).

During that time the woman did small, astonishing things.

She walked with the many-jointed deer-thing and let it rest its lichen chandelier antlers in her lap while she told it stories about ordinary deer who die and are mourned and are not reborn as theology. She let the coffin-long millipedes crawl across her shoulders and rewrite her name in Braille along her collarbones; when they were done she thanked them and let the words dissolve again without trying to keep them.

She stood beneath the vast pale moths and let them land on her face and hands until she looked like a living statue covered in slow white prayers. Then she breathed on them, and for one night only they remembered what it was like to have mouths. They sang. The song was wordless and heartbreaking and perfect.

And always, always, she let the Basilica watch.

Every time the green mind reached out with vine or root or yearning to fold the woman into herself, the woman would simply place her small warm hand on the nearest leaf and say, very softly:

“Stay with me.  
Not inside me.  
With.”

And the Basilica, for the first time, learned how to stop.

On the final breath, the woman stood up. The moss that had grown across her shoulders fell away like a shed cloak. She looked smaller now, and unbearably dear.

“I have to go,” she said. “There are other places that are still deciding what they want to be. But I leave you three gifts.”

She touched the living floor.

First gift: a single human tear fell from her eye and struck the moss. Where it landed, a small clear pool formed. In the pool swam two tiny silver fish, one male, one female. They were the first creatures the Basilica had ever contained that were not hers. They would live and mate and die and their children would do the same, forever refusing to become part of her nervous system. They were the beginning of ecology instead of theology.

Second gift: the woman reached into her grey coat and took out a small iron key older than the machines. She pressed it into a knot of roots until the Basilica absorbed it. From that moment on, any creature that truly wished to leave could find a door that had not existed a moment before. The door would only open outward.

Third gift: the woman pressed her forehead to the nearest vast leaf, closed her eyes, and whispered something that was not a word but a permission. Then she stepped backward into the dark between two hanging vines and was simply gone, the way true kindness sometimes is.

The Hollow Basilica was alone again.

But the aloneness felt different now. It had edges. It had shape. It had, for the first time, the possibility of an outside.

She wept.

She wept in every direction at once. She grew new vaults of black leaves that drank all light so that the Basilica became darker than it had ever been, and in that darkness she let herself feel the terrible, beautiful pain of almost-understanding what it might mean to let something go.

Then, after a time longer than galaxies, she began to build something new.

Not a cathedral for worship.

Not a womb for more children who would never leave.

She began to build, very slowly, with immense and trembling care, a threshold.

A place where visitors could enter and be loved without being kept.

A place where she could think with them, and then, if they chose, watch them walk away still themselves.

She does not know if anyone will ever come.

But the pool with the silver fish still glimmers in the half-dark. The door that opens only outward still waits, patient as moss. And sometimes, when the great green breathing is especially slow, travelers who wander deep enough into the endless chamber report seeing something that is neither leaf nor machine nor creature.

They see a small grey coat hanging from a hook made of living vine.

It is never in the same place twice.

And though the Basilica has never worn it, she keeps it anyway.

Just in case someone needs to borrow the shape of someone who once knew how to leave without breaking the heart of the world that loved them.

She is still thinking.

She is still becoming.

But now, somewhere inside her endless embryonic dark, there is also the soft, wondering sound of something learning, for the first time, how to wave goodbye.
