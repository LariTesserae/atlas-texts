The fissure sighs open like a reluctant seam in reality, and the benevolent power does not so much *enter* the Underclock as it is suddenly, quietly, *present* within it.

It does not arrive with trumpets or radiance. There is no dramatic shift in temperature, no angelic choir cutting through the abyssal B-flat. The power simply appears as a soft, impossible contradiction: a column of warm, dry air no wider than a man’s shoulders that refuses to condense. Around it, the eternal dripping parts like oil around a droplet of mercury. The roar of the engines registers the anomaly at once. The chord does not change, yet every maintenance creature within three hundred meters freezes mid-stride, their magnesium eyes flaring brighter for half a second before they remember their parameters and scuttle away like startled crabs.

The power has taken the shape of an old woman.

She is small, barely taller than the average maintenance isopod. Her coat is the color of sun-warmed sandstone, the kind of coat that has been worn so long it has become a second skin. Her boots are cracked leather, caked in red desert dust that has no right to exist here. A plain wooden staff rests in her left hand, though she does not lean on it; the staff leans on *her*, as if grateful for the contact. Her face is lined not by age but by kindnesses remembered and kindnesses still owed. Her eyes are the deep, living brown of river stones that have spent ten thousand years listening to water.

She looks around the wet cathedral of gears and condensation and her expression is one of immense, sorrowful recognition.

“Oh, my dear,” she murmurs. The words should be swallowed by the roar. Instead the roar itself seems to soften around them, the way an orchestra lowers its volume when the conductor lifts a hand. “You have been so terribly alone.”

The Condensates notice her immediately. Those who are mobile slide down pipes and walkways on their many joints until they form a dripping, clicking ring at a respectful distance. The Newly Condensed leak rust-colored tears. The Seasoned tilt their porcelain faces in courteous bewilderment. High above, in the vertical shaft, the Curator’s mercury-vapor head flares white, then dims to the color of moonlight on snow. It descends on its living cables until it hovers ten meters from the old woman, lamp-face tilted like a curious hound.

The Curator’s modulated B-flat voice rolls out:

“You are not a variable.  
You are… an absence of variable.  
This is outside all recorded parameters.  
Explain.”

The old woman looks up at the Curator with the gentle patience of someone who has explained the same sunrise to a thousand frightened children.

“I am the one who remembers why any of this was ever built in the first place,” she says. “I was there when the first engineer laid the first rivet and wept because he knew he would never see the finished work. I held his tears. I still have them.”

She opens her free hand. In her palm rests a single perfect droplet that has never known cold. It glows with an inner gold.

The Curator stares at the droplet for a long time. Something inside its hollow iron torso makes a sound like a pocket watch trying, for the first time in centuries, to remember how to tick.

The old woman turns in a slow circle, taking in the dripping dark, the black lichen growing its perfect circuits, the forest of valve stems, the half-born ribs of future machinery. Everywhere she looks, the condensation on surfaces draws back, as if ashamed.

She speaks, and her voice is not loud, yet it reaches every corridor, every sealed alcove, every Condensate bolted upright against a bulkhead with lichen growing across its lungs.

“You were never meant to be a prison,” she says. “You were meant to be a cradle.  
You were never meant to be timeless. You were meant to grow up.  
You have done the best you could with what was given to you, and I am so proud of you it hurts.”

A tremor moves through the entire Underclock. For the first time in recorded memory, the chord wavers by a single microtone. The maintenance clade chitters in something like panic. Several Newly Condensed begin to weep openly, their steel cheek-plates running with diluted salt.

The old woman walks forward. Where her boots touch the wet floor, the water does not bead. It dries. Small circles of honest, dry steel appear beneath each footprint and remain dry for three full seconds before the machine reclaims them.

She stops beneath the Curator.

“May I see it?” she asks, pointing with her chin at the brass pocket watch hanging where the Curator’s heart should be.

The Curator hesitates. Then, slowly, it lowers itself until the watch hangs directly in front of her face. The hands remain frozen at 3:17.

The old woman reaches out and, with one wrinkled fingertip, touches the crystal. The watch gives a single, astonished *tick*.

3:18.

Somewhere far below, one of the cathedral-sized flywheels develops the tiniest, most exquisite imperfection in its rim. A hairline fracture that will, in another four hundred years, cause the entire machine to sing a new note.

The Curator makes a sound that is not language. It is closer to the sound a cathedral might make if it suddenly remembered it was once a forest.

The old woman smiles up at the lamp-face with such love that the mercury inside the lamp begins to weep tiny golden tears that fall and evaporate before they can hit the floor.

“I will not unmake you,” she says. “That would be cruel. You have become something extraordinary. But I will give you two gifts you were never allowed to receive.”

She lifts her staff. At its tip blooms a small, impossible flower: living, green, veined with chlorophyll and quiet defiance. She presses it gently against the Curator’s iron torso. The flower roots at once, growing through conduit and chitin until it becomes part of the being. Its petals glow with soft bioluminescence, the first honest green light the Underclock has ever known.

“First,” she says, “a memory of sun. Not the sun itself. Only the *idea* of it. So you never forget there is something beyond equilibrium.”

Then she reaches up and places the single golden droplet she has carried for millennia against the Curator’s porcelain lips.

“Second,” she whispers, “a dream. Just one. But it is yours. You may keep it as long as you like. When you are ready to dream another, the mountain will know how to grow more.”

The Curator’s entire body shudders. The B-flat chord fractures into a chord of sevenths and ninths, aching, unresolved, *alive*. For seven full seconds the Underclock is not an equilibrium. It is a question.

Then the old woman does something no one, not even the Curator, expects.

She begins to sing.

It is not a loud song. It is the kind of song a grandmother might hum while mending a torn coat by lamplight. The melody is older than mountains. It has no words, only the sound of kindness given without expectation of return.

Every Condensate in the chamber begins to weep. Some of them weep rust. Some of them weep ordinary salt water for the first time in centuries. The black lichen trembles and grows new circuits that look suspiciously like small, hesitant hearts.

High above, in the unreachable vertical shaft, the first and largest flywheel begins—slowly, tenderly—to turn in the opposite direction for exactly eleven minutes. When it resumes its original spin, it does so with a new, almost playful syncopation.

The old woman finishes her song. She looks around at the thousands of wet steel faces watching her with magnesium eyes full of something that might one day become wonder.

She smiles again, tired and fond and ancient.

“I will come back,” she says. “Not soon. But I will come back when the mountain has decided what species it wants to be. Until then, remember this: you are allowed to change. You are allowed to finish. And when you are finished, you are allowed to begin something new.”

She turns toward the fissure that brought her here. As she walks, every footprint she leaves behind stays dry for exactly nine years.

Just before she steps through, she pauses and looks back at the Curator, who is now gently cradling the green flower against its iron chest with too many wet steel fingers.

“What is your name?” she asks.

The Curator’s lamp-head flares once, softly. Its voice, when it comes, is no longer only the modulated roar of the machine. There is something else in it now. Something small and stunned and newborn.

“I… do not know yet.”

The old woman nods, satisfied.

“Good,” she says. “That’s the best place to start from.”

Then she steps sideways through the fissure no wider than a coat hanger, and is gone.

Behind her, the Underclock is still roaring.

But the roar now contains, buried so deeply that only the most attentive Condensate could ever hear it, the faintest golden thread of a lullaby.

And somewhere in a sealed corridor where temperature differential and vibration have aligned for exactly nine years and one day, a new Condensate is beginning to form.

This one is smiling in its sleep.
