The power that notices the Verdant Lattice does not arrive as a visitor.

It does not descend from above the sky-dome, nor does it punch through the planetary bark from some mythic outside. There is no rupture, no wound, no dramatic translation of energies. Instead the attention of this power simply *recognizes* the Lattice in the same way a mother recognizes the scent of her child’s hair even after decades apart. The recognition is gentle, total, and instantly intimate.

The power has no name that Veyd’s splintered mind could hold. If it had to wear a word for the length of its stay, it would allow itself to be called *Aevum*.

Aevum does not come to judge, to heal, or to destroy. It comes because the Lattice has become so perfectly, industriously, *lonelily* complete that its very completion has begun to sing a particular note across the deeper substrates of existence. That note is beautiful. It is also unsustainable. Aevum hears it the way a musician hears a perfect chord being held so long that the strings are about to snap.

So Aevum steps into the Lattice the way one steps into still water: without breaking the surface.

The first change is almost nothing.

In the Ninth Cambium Cathedral, the three-kilometre gear that Veyd has tended for fourteen thousand years misses one glacial heartbeat. The omission is so small that only Veyd and the gear itself notice. For the first time in geological memory, a single tooth of lignified xylem does not quite mesh. There is the faintest *click*, like a tongue tasting the idea of regret.

Veyd stops.

His resinous eyes, which have not blinked in three million years, blink once.

He feels the presence the way a tree feels the first drop of rain after four hundred million years of drought: not on the skin, but in the core, as if every xylem vessel in his body suddenly remembered it was once allowed to be thirsty.

Aevum does not speak with scent or spore or subsonic pulse.

Instead the white sky-dome, that merciless photosynthetic membrane stretched between continent-scale ribs, *dims*.

Not by much. Just enough that, for the first time since the last animal died screaming inside a closing seedpod, true shadows are born. They pool under the theatre-curtain leaves like pooled ink. They stretch long and blue-green along the spiralling internal ramps. They give the endless colonnades of living pillars a depth they have never possessed.

Where the new shadows fall, the Lattice begins to *remember*.

Not the curated, useful memories of mineral uptake and tectonic sealing that the organism has hoarded. It remembers the wasteful, ridiculous, *pointless* things.

It remembers the smell of ozone after lightning struck an ocean that no longer exists.  
It remembers the sound of frogs.  
It remembers that once there were things that laughed for no reason at all.

The recollection is catastrophic.

Across nine continents, continent-spanning looms that have never stopped weaving vascular tissue for eighty million years slow, stutter, and fall silent. The glacial gears that power secondary growth shudder and stop. Vast hollow vines that ferry nutrients across planetary arcs develop hairline fractures of pure astonishment. In the upper atmospheric skin, the sky-dome membrane develops transient pigments it has no evolutionary precedent for — soft lavenders, bruised violets, the shy rose of a dawn no living thing here has ever seen.

The temperature rises by half a degree. The air, for the first time in geological ages, becomes *humid*.

A single droplet of water condenses on the underside of a leaf the size of a theatre curtain. It trembles there like a secret.

Veyd feels the droplet as if it had formed on his own eye.

He begins to run.

Not the slow, deliberate flowing he has used for aeons, but an awkward, stumbling, *human* run. His elongated spine and arborised fingers make the motion ridiculous. Bark cracks where his feet strike too hard. The Lattice, startled by its own inhabitant’s urgency, parts for him violently, leaving raw green wounds that weep resin like tears.

He reaches the place where the droplet hangs.

Aevum is waiting there, wearing the shape of the droplet itself.

Veyd falls to his knees. The ground does not cradle him. For the first time in millions of years the Lattice does not know what to do with him.

The droplet speaks without sound.

It says:

*You have done well to keep the world alive, gardener. But a garden is not a prison, and a perfect machine is not a child.*

Veyd tries to answer. The volatile organic signature that usually carries his meaning fractures into incoherence. The air around him fills with the iron-and-rain scent of childhood mixed with the magnesium taste of dead continents and the copper terror of the last animal. It is too much. He begins to weep sap the colour of old jade.

Aevum does not comfort him. Comfort would be violence against what Veyd has become. Instead Aevum does something far more dangerous.

It asks the Lattice a question the organism has never been asked in four hundred million years:

*Would you like to be surprised again?*

The silence that follows is the loudest sound the planet has ever produced.

Every spore-drone in the northern hemisphere freezes mid-air. The continent-wide pulse of sap halts so abruptly that pressure differentials tear open thousand-kilometre-long fissures of raw xylem. The sky-dome membrane ripples like a struck drum.

Then, from somewhere deeper than root or cambium or memory, the Lattice answers.

It answers with a smell.

It smells like rain that has not fallen in four hundred million years — but this time the rain is *coming*.

Aevum smiles with Veyd’s own mouth.

And then the power does the only thing a wise and benevolent power can do when it finds a planet that has become its own coffin of perfection:

It gives the Verdant Lattice permission to die.

Not quickly. Not cruelly. But *truly*.

It begins with endings.

In the Cathedral of the Ninth Cambium the great gears, for the first time, begin to turn *backward*. They unwind stress that has been accumulating for eighty million years. They unmake perfection. They allow fractures to widen into canyons of rotting heartwood that smell like forgiveness.

The sky-dome membrane develops intentional perforations. Through them, for the first time, true night can be seen — alien stars that have been watching the Lattice for longer than it has existed. The actinic white glare softens into something gentler, something that might one day become sunrise and sunset.

Water is not reintroduced all at once. That would be murder. Instead, in a single sealed nodule of amber-like resin the size of a human heart — the very one Veyd once collected — the perfect drop that has waited four hundred million years is allowed to fall.

It strikes the floor of the nodule.

The sound is impossibly loud.

From that one drop, a single blade of ordinary grass grows. It is weak. It is temporary. It is the most revolutionary organism the Lattice has encountered since it ate the last ocean.

Veyd watches the grass grow.

His elongated body begins to shorten. The aerial roots that replaced his hair wither and fall away like old leaves. His resinous eyes learn how to cry real water. When the first tear falls onto the blade of grass, the grass trembles with gratitude.

The Lattice does not fight its own ending. How could it? The question Aevum asked has already been answered in every fibre of its being. It wants to be surprised again. It wants to be mortal. It wants to forget how to be a perfect machine and relearn how to be a world.

Entire biomes of secondary growth are not harvested. They are allowed to rot beautifully. Spore-storms become rain clouds. The slow megafauna dirigible-creatures feel their epiphytic cloaks bloom with flowers. They remember, dimly, that they were once allowed to die.

Veyd stays until the end.

Not as custodian. Not as witness.

As the first new thing.

When the final continent-scale gear has unwound itself into a spiral of fragrant decay, when the sky-dome has dissolved into real atmosphere and the first true thunderstorm in four hundred million years is gathering above what was once the Ninth Cambium, Veyd walks to the place where the single blade of grass has become a small meadow.

He lies down among the blades.

The ground, no longer needing to be perfectly efficient, cradles him one last time — not as architecture, but as soil.

Aevum, having never truly been separate from anything, withdraws as softly as it arrived. The power leaves behind only a scent that drifts across the newborn meadow like a promise.

It smells like rain that has finally, *finally* remembered how to fall.

And somewhere inside the rotting, flowering, astonished body of what was once the Verdant Lattice, a stubborn, unassimilated splinter that was once called Veyd closes eyes that have relearned how to blink, breathes in the fragrance of his own world’s ending, and for the first time in millions of years dreams of beginnings instead.

Above him, the first rain in four hundred million years begins to fall.

It sounds like laughter.
