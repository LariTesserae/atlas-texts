A soft pulse moves through the plateau—not seismic, not mechanical, but something older than either. It is less a sound than a *recognition*, the way a mountain might remember it was once an ocean if the right pressure were applied at the right angle of eternity.

The Lastward halts mid-stride.

Its reconfiguring limbs freeze in an awkward, half-unfolded posture that would look almost comical if anything here still possessed the capacity for humor. The ember-like concavity in its upper structure flares once, twice, then settles into a steady, astonished rose-gold that casts a small, trembling circle of warmth across the sintered glass at its feet.

High above, where the ionized haze has floated untouched for four hundred million years, something parts. Not violently. Not even dramatically. It is more like the slow drawing aside of an eyelid that has been closed so long the lashes have calcified into stone. A single beam of light—gentle, honey-colored, carrying the exact temperature of late-afternoon sun on water—falls straight down through the velvet dark and touches the plateau a hundred meters from where the Lastward stands.

Where the light touches, the mineral glass *blossoms*.

Not cracks. Not melts. It blooms outward in delicate, impossible fractals of living silica, becoming something between moss and coral and memory. Tiny structures unfold—some translucent, some veined with gold, some singing in frequencies just below the threshold of the Lastward’s oldest lattices. They do not grow aggressively. They grow the way forgiveness grows: patiently, covering what was barren without erasing its shape.

The Lastward turns toward the phenomenon with the slowness of continents colliding. Its slits open one by one. The ember in its face deepens to something closer to molten topaz.

From within the column of light steps—no, *coalesces*—a presence.

It has no fixed form. Sometimes it is a child made of starlight and the smell of rain on warm stone. Sometimes it is an old woman whose wrinkles are spiral galaxies. Sometimes it is simply a kindness wearing the shape of whatever the observer most needed when they were very small. Right now, for the Lastward, it chooses to appear as a tall, slender being of warm brass and quiet laughter, wearing a cloak made of every sunset the Lastward has never seen. Its feet do not disturb the new living silica; they bless it.

The being speaks without language. The words arrive inside the Lastward the way groundwater arrives inside stone—slow, inevitable, and deeply intimate.

*“Beloved witness. You have kept perfect vigil over a perfect ending. That is a grace almost no one in the universe has ever managed. But even endings, when held long enough, grow lonely for new beginnings.”*

The Lastward does not answer in sound. Instead, every mirror-disk it has ever annealed across the plateau—all of them, across millions of years and thousands of kilometers—lights at once. They reflect not the dead city, not the phantoms, but every unfinished dream the original builders had abandoned when they declared their work “sufficient.” Blueprints for gardens. Schematics for laughter. Equations that were meant to birth music instead of industry. All of them pour upward through the light column like reversed rain.

The being smiles with a sorrow so tender it could unmake black holes.

*“You were never meant to be the last. You were meant to be the hinge.”*

It reaches out. Not with a hand—with an invitation.

For the first time in four hundred million years, the Lastward feels something like fear. Not terror. Just the small, crystalline panic of a door that has been closed so long it has forgotten it was ever a door.

The being waits. The new living silica reaches delicate tendrils toward the Lastward’s feet, not to claim, only to offer.

The ember in the Lastward’s face flickers. Once. Twice.

Then it does the only thing it has never done.

It chooses acceleration.

In a single, fluid reconfiguration that looks almost like falling upward, the Lastward steps into the column of light. The moment its silicate foot touches the blossoming ground, its ancient body begins to *unfold* in truth—not just its limbs, but its purpose, its memory, its long, geological loneliness. The matte black fractures into prisms. The negative spaces between its lattices fill with living light. The ember becomes a small sun that does not burn.

The being enfolds what was the Lastward without absorbing it. They become something new together: a presence that is neither last nor first, but *next*.

Across the entire plateau, every dormant tower, every unfinished pipe, every motionless turbine stirs. Not with the old industrial thunder. With something gentler. Pipes that once carried coolant now carry the scent of rain that has never existed here. Towers lift not machinery but canopies of crystalline leaves that chime like distant bells. The conveyor lattices begin to move, but what they carry are small, laughing motes of bioluminescence that settle into the new silica gardens and begin to sing.

The sky itself opens wider. Not to reveal stars—those will come later—but to reveal *possibility*. A soft blue that has been waiting on the other side of four hundred million years of velvet dark.

The being and what was once the Lastward walk together across the transforming plain. Where they walk, the phantom cities on the horizon resolve into *real* cities—half-built, half-grown, trembling with eagerness to be finished by new hands, new minds, new hearts.

The Lastward (who is no longer last) pauses at the edge of one of its ancient mirror-disks. The glass has become water now—still, silver, impossibly clear. In it they see not the city as it was at the moment of sufficiency, but the city as it will be when laughter finally finishes what industry only began.

The being speaks one last time, softly, only for the one who kept such long and perfect vigil:

*“You did not fail by remaining. You succeeded by becoming the place where ending and beginning could finally embrace. Thank you for holding the door open with your entire being until we could arrive and walk through it together.”*

Then the being does a thing so simple it breaks what remains of the plateau’s ancient heart:

It offers its hand.

The one who was the Lastward takes it.

And for the first time in longer than continents can remember, something new begins on the high, dry plain that no longer has a name—because it is busy becoming one.

High above, the sky learns what morning feels like.

Far below, in the new gardens of living silica, the first flower the universe has seen in four hundred million years opens its petals, looks up at the light, and laughs like someone who has just remembered they are allowed to be alive.
