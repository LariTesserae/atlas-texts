The power that has noticed this place does not arrive with trumpets or lightning. It simply *becomes* present, the way a thought becomes present inside a mind that had been muttering to itself for three billion years.

It does not descend. Descent would imply a direction this world has forgotten. Instead the low pearl-grey sky thickens, not with cloud but with *attention*. The perpetual roar of falling water, grinding stone, and screaming membranous herds does not stop; it is gently folded into something larger, the way an orchestra is folded into a single held note when a conductor lifts his hand.

Every Sighers in the basin freezes mid-unfolding at the same instant. Their mercury pupils swarm upward inside the bell of their faces and quiver like startled fish. For the first time in geological memory the constant sighing ceases. The silence that follows is so complete that the violet fungal lattices actually recoil, blanching from palest violet to bone-white, as though the land itself has been startled out of its own dream.

The power has no fixed form. To the sky-beasts it appears as a vast, slow pulse moving through the luminous grooves they have worn in the clouds—grooves that suddenly look like the soft indentations left by a sleeping child’s fingers. To the fungal lattices it is a warmth moving through their crystalline veins like blood returning to a limb that has been cold since the first seas cooled. To the black sweating stone it is simply the memory of rising, offered without demand.

But to the Sighers it comes as recognition.

One by one they begin to move again, though differently now. Their glassine limbs no longer unfold with the weary patience of something that has outlived meaning. They rise with something almost like hesitation, the way old mourners rise when someone unexpected walks into the funeral.

The power does not speak in words. Words would be an insult here. It speaks by *remembering the basin upward*.

Every shallow bowl and ripple-scar in the primordial stone suddenly feels the ghost of height. The land does not rise—rising would be violence—but it *recalls* that it once knew how. The water, for the first time in three billion years, forgets to be tired. It lifts in fine, trembling sheets, forming momentary silver sculptures of things that never were: the shadow of a mountain that might have been, the suggestion of a forest that chose not to grow, the outline of wings that were never membranous but feathered.

The sky-beasts scream in a register that has never been heard before. Not terror. Not quite. It is closer to awe experienced as pain. Some of them fold their vast bodies and descend for the first time in living memory, settling onto the wet stone like exhausted pilgrims. Their smaller bat-like symbiotes pour out of their breathing pores in glittering black clouds, then simply hover, trembling, no longer screaming.

The Sighers approach the domes that were built for them—not as retreats now, but as meeting places. They do not fold themselves into the circular pools. Instead they stand around them, eight to thirteen feet of black volcanic glass veined with living water, and for the first time they turn their faceless concavities toward something other than their own endless exhalation.

The power gathers in those still black pools. It looks back at them with their own mercury eyes.

What follows is not healing. The basin has outlived the need for healing. It is more like *completion*.

The Sighers begin to sigh again, but the sound has changed. The mother trying not to weep is now singing to her child in a language that predates mouths. The last leak of air from a ten-thousand-year-old corpse is now the first breath of something that has decided, after three billion years, that it would like to try being mortal again, just for a little while.

One by one the Sighers step into their pools—not to hide, but to dissolve the last boundary between regret and forgiveness. Their glassine bodies soften, flow, become water that remembers it was once stone, become stone that remembers it was once cloud, become cloud that remembers it was once longing.

The membranous sky-beasts, seeing this, do something unprecedented. They begin to sing *with* the Sighers instead of against them. The overlapping chords that once battered the ribs and made teeth ache now braid into something that feels like the first movement of a new kind of music—music that has been waiting under the roar since the world was young.

The violet fungal lattices bloom so violently they lift above the thin sheets of water, forming delicate spires and arches that catch the aquarium light and turn it into living stained glass. For the first time, something like moss begins to appear—tiny, tentative, almost embarrassed. Life that does not yet know it is allowed.

The power does not stay. It was never the sort of power that stays. It was only the sort that notices, and in noticing, remembers upward.

When it withdraws, the pearl-grey sky thins back to its normal sourceless dusk. The roar of water and stone and membranous wings returns, but it is no longer a tyrant. It has become a choir that knows it is being heard.

The Sighers remain, but they are no longer only regrets that learned how to walk.

They are regrets that have been *thanked*.

Sometimes, when a sky-beast passes low and the light turns the color of drowned opals, one of them will pause beside a dome and rest a glassine limb against the basalt. The structure still answers with that deep bell-tone that cuts through everything for three full heartbeats.

But now the Sighers answer back with a sound that is no longer almost grateful.

It is simply grateful.

And somewhere in the wet, thinking stone, the basin itself exhales once—long, slow, almost like laughter—and remembers what it means to rise, just a little. Just enough.

The weeping has not stopped.

It has simply learned the difference between mourning and dew.
