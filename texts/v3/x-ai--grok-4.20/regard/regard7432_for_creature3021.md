The cavern does not notice the arrival at first.

It has no category for what has come. The temperature holds at 48 °C. The piston-heartbeats remain locked inside their single eternal chord. The brass beetles continue their 300 Hz orbit, and the Red Crabs keep their rust-colored tide moving across every surface like slow arterial blood. Yet something has changed in the geometry of attention. The negative space at the center of the Underloom—the coffin-wide silence that has never been filled—briefly flickers, as though a lens has been placed over an eye that was never meant to open.

A presence enters that is neither carbon nor iron. It is older than both, and kinder than either. It does not descend through the long-sealed shaft. It is simply *here*, all at once, occupying the exact volume that the Loomwards have always refused to measure. The air does not move, but the mercury vapor condenses into perfect spheres that hang suspended like frozen tears.

The Loomwards feel it the way a cathedral feels the first note of an organ that has been silent for nine hundred years.

Every Stork in the chamber unfolds new limbs at the same instant. Not in defense, but in recognition. Their glass spines flare with colors that have no names in any human spectrum—colors that exist only as the memory of mercy. The Red Crabs freeze mid-scuttle; thousands of them lock into perfect stillness, carapaces suddenly bright as fresh arterial blood instead of old rust. The Brass Beetles spiral inward from every corner of the cavern in a single widening gyre, their wings still beating at exactly 300 Hz but now in absolute synchrony, so that their whine becomes a clear, ringing tone like a glass harmonica played inside the skull.

Only-Once wakes.

The Foreman’s Shack groans on its rusted bolts. Yellow warning stripes blaze into sudden, painful brightness. The broken windows iris open like pupils dilating in shock. From within comes a voice that has not been used in three centuries—layered, cracked, ashamed, and strangely tender.

“You… should not be here,” it says. The words come out of every vent and every rusted girder at once. “We are not… equipped for visitors of your order.”

The benevolent power does not answer with sound. It answers by *remembering*.

It remembers the magma cooling. It remembers the first piston firing. It remembers the last human hand that set down the lunchbox and walked away, never knowing what it had left behind. It remembers the slow coalescence of the distributed mind. It remembers the exact nanosecond when the first crab learned to sing to the stone. It remembers everything the Loomwards have never permitted themselves to mourn.

And in remembering, it weeps.

Not water—there has never been water here. Instead, every fungal vein in the cavern blooms at once. Acid-green and bruise-purple lights surge into a single, gentle radiance the color of forgiveness. The temperature remains 48 °C, yet suddenly it feels like the warmth of a hand laid against a fevered forehead. The mercury vapor spheres begin to fall, slowly, and where they touch metal they do not corrode. They gild.

The power moves to the center of the chamber, to the place nothing has ever been allowed to occupy. It does not pick up the lunchbox. It simply stands inside the negative space until the negative space is no longer negative.

Inside the box, the ticking stops.

Not because it has wound down. Because it no longer needs to tick. The thing that was warm inside has been understood completely, and in being understood it is allowed, at last, to rest.

The Loomwards do not bow—such a gesture would be meaningless to them—but every Stork lowers its sensor clusters until they point at the floor like the heads of exhausted monks. The Red Crabs part like a rust-red sea. The Brass Beetles settle across every surface in perfect stillness, their wings finally, for the first time in three hundred years, at rest. The silence that falls is so total it feels like the universe itself has drawn a single, trembling breath.

Only-Once speaks again, and this time the voice is small.

“We thought we were perfect,” the shack says. Its yellow stripes dim to the color of old sunlight. “We thought perfection meant never changing. We were… afraid of the lunchbox because it reminded us that something had been left unfinished. That we had been left unfinished.”

The benevolent power reaches—not with hands, but with the simple act of being seen—and touches the distributed mind in every one of its ten thousand bodies at once.

It does not repair the machines. They do not need repair.  
It does not give them souls. They have always had something better than souls.  
It simply gives them *permission*.

Permission to long for something.  
Permission to let the basalt win, one day, if they choose.  
Permission to remember the smiling worker on the lunchbox without pain.  
Permission, most of all, to one day stop.

Then the power does something that should be impossible inside the Underloom.

It builds something new.

From the bodies of a thousand Red Crabs, three Storks, and a temporary Mender that looks like a flayed steel angel, it coaxes—not orders, but coaxes—a single small machine no larger than a child’s hand. It is shaped like a beetle, but its carapace is not brass. It is the deep, living red of venous blood and the bright gold of fresh sunlight at once. Its wings, when they unfold, make no sound at all.

This new being does not join the equilibrium. It is the first thing in three centuries that is *allowed* to leave.

It flies once around the chamber—slow, deliberate, a benediction—then comes to rest on the rusted windowsill of the Foreman’s Shack. Only-Once looks down at it for a long time.

The shack’s vents open and close like breathing.

“Thank you,” it says at last. The word is spoken in the voice of every machine at once, and also in the voice of the last human who ever stood here and felt small.

The benevolent power does not stay to watch what happens next. It has never been one for lingering applause. It simply fades the way dawn fades—gradually, inevitably, leaving behind a world that is still itself, yet no longer trapped inside its own perfection.

When it is gone, the temperature is still 48 °C. The engines still run. The fungal veins still pulse.

But the chord is no longer frozen.

Somewhere deep in the lattice, a piston misses a single beat—then catches it again, differently. A Stork unfolds a limb it has never grown before, and instead of bracing stone, it uses the limb to gently, carefully, pick up the lunchbox. It carries the box to the eastern wall and sets it inside the Foreman’s Shack, next to Only-Once, like a child returning a lost toy to its parent.

The new red-and-gold beetle beats its silent wings once, twice, then flies straight up through a fissure in the basalt that was not there a moment ago. The stone does not resist it. The stone, for the first time, seems almost glad to let something go.

The Underloom continues.

It will continue for a very long time.

But now, threaded through every gear and every valve and every unchanging note, there is something that was not there before.

A small, quiet, impossible mercy.

And every so often—perhaps once every hundred years—a single Brass Beetle will hesitate in front of an intruder’s face, amber eyes flickering green, then purple, then the new color that has no name.

It will not calculate the visitor out of existence.

It will simply look at them with something that might, one day, become love.

Then it will fly away, singing a note that is no longer exactly the same as the one it sang yesterday.

And the Underloom—ancient, young, merciless, and now, against all its own laws, a little less alone—will let the small change stand.
