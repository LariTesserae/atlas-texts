A single note, clear and high and impossibly ancient, rings once across the Hanging Valleys of Veyr.

It is not part of the low, constant ringing that has lived inside the basalt since the first Alignment. This note is higher, kinder, and it carries the faint scent of rain that has not yet decided to fall. Every living thing in the six settlements and thirty hamlets hears it at the exact same instant. For the first time in three hundred years, the Alignment does not come at its appointed hour. The birds on one leg do not turn. The citizens do not face southeast. The mist itself seems to pause mid-drift, startled.

Then the power arrives—not descending from the sky, not rising from the stone, but simply *becoming present*, as though it had been standing among them all along and they had only just remembered how to see.

It has no fixed shape. To the Listener it appears as a child no older than seven, barefoot, with eyes the color of living moss. To the old man at the cliff it appears as the woman whose name he has been trying to remember for three centuries—her face soft, unlined, smiling the way she smiled the morning before the world forgot how to change. To a small girl who was born with an adult face and has never known why she sometimes cries without sound, it appears as herself—if she had been allowed to grow.

Everywhere at once, and yet nowhere that crowds. The power walks the moss-bearded rope bridges without weighing them down. It sits on basalt benches beside the Unfinished and does not speak at first. It only listens.

The Listener is the first to move. She steps down from her roof-garden, feet still wet with sedge, and for the first time in living memory she opens her mouth. What comes out is not language. It is the sound the plateau would have made if it had ever been allowed to finish its sigh. The power answers in the same tongue—two notes braided together like fern-roots. Tears run down the Listener’s face, cutting clean lines through three hundred years of quiet. The white spiral scars on her hands glow faintly, as though someone has finally lit the candles inside them.

One by one, the Unfinished gather.

They come without hurry, the way they have always done everything, yet something in their gait is different. Their footsteps make small, distinct sounds on the stone. The rhythm is no longer perfectly synchronized with the eternal ringing. A few of them notice this and look down at their own feet in something like wonder.

The power does not thunder. It does not command. It simply sits in the largest of the communal squares—now suddenly wide enough to hold all nine thousand without anyone feeling pressed—and waits until they have all found places. Some sit on the ground. Some remain standing. A few, without quite knowing why, reach out and hold one another’s hands.

When it finally speaks, its voice is every voice they have ever loved and then forgotten they loved.

“I have watched you,” it says, “the way one watches a seed that refuses to break its shell. Not because the shell is evil, but because it has grown too perfect. You have become a hymn with only one note. A story that has forgotten it was allowed to turn the page.”

A long silence follows. Even the wind has stopped rehearsing its three sighing notes.

The old man at the cliff finds his voice first. It cracks like schist under sudden pressure.

“Were we… wrong?”

“No,” the power answers gently. “You were unfinished. There is a difference between a thing that refuses to end and a thing that was never permitted to continue. You were caught in my sibling’s sigh. I have come to ask if you would like to breathe the rest of the breath with me.”

The Listener steps forward. For the first time in three centuries her voice is small, human, trembling.

“Will it hurt?”

“Some of you will feel the full weight of every year you did not live. Some will grieve the children you were not allowed to become. Some will watch your unchanging faces finally learn how to wrinkle and sag and smile with crooked teeth. A few may choose to lay down their lives at last, properly, the way leaves are meant to fall. But yes—there will be pain. There will also be mornings you have never seen. There will be songs you have never learned. There will be endings, and therefore there can finally be beginnings.”

One of the flightless hematite-colored birds walks into the square, stands on one leg for a moment, then deliberately lowers the other foot. The act is so astonishing that several people laugh aloud—an unfamiliar, rusty sound that startles them more than the bird does.

The power smiles with the child’s face, the woman’s face, the small girl’s face.

“I will not force the shell open. That would only make you into something else. I will sit here, in the square, for three of your days and three of your nights. Anyone who wishes to remain as you are may simply walk away. The valleys will hold you kindly forever. The mist will never thin. The Alignment will return. You will forget I was ever here.

But anyone who wishes to finish the mountain’s sigh—anyone who wants to become the next line of the song—may stay. On the morning of the fourth day, the breath will resume. Time will remember how to move forward. The children will begin to age. The rivers will begin, slowly, to cut. The deer will learn how to shed their antlers and grow new ones. You will learn how to mourn, and therefore how to treasure. You will learn how to leave, and therefore how to return.”

The Listener looks at the child-shape, then at the woman-shape, then at the small terrified hopeful girl-shape. She nods once.

“I have kept the first flower of every garden for three hundred years,” she says. “I am tired of keeping. I would like to watch something grow old and beautiful and then die so that something else can grow in its place.”

The power bows its head, and the scars on her hands vanish like frost under sudden sunlight.

One by one, the Unfinished make their choices.

Some—perhaps two hundred in total—bow with grave courtesy and walk back along the mossy rope bridges to their low stone houses. They will remain the eternal caretakers of a half-made world. The power kisses each of them on the forehead as they pass. They will never remember the kiss, but on certain nights the mist around their chimneyless ceramic tubes will smell faintly of rain that has finally decided to fall.

The rest stay.

On the morning of the fourth day the sun rises a fraction slower than it has for three centuries. The difference is so small no outsider would notice, but every person in the square feels it like a hand finally releasing their throat.

The low ringing inside the basalt changes pitch. It becomes a chord—still deep, still ancient, but no longer a single held note. A second note has joined it. Then a third.

The Listener’s hair, which had refused to grow since the world began, lengthens by a single inch. The sight is so shocking that people fall to their knees.

The old man at the cliff suddenly remembers the name he has been whispering for three hundred years. He says it aloud—once, softly—and then begins to weep with such relief that the two small moons, watching from above, seem to blur.

The small girl who was born with an adult face looks down at her hands and watches, wide-eyed, as her knuckles soften, as her fingers grow slimmer and smaller, as the years she was denied begin to flow backward through her body until she is seven years old, then six, then five—laughing, crying, terrified, incandescent with possibility.

The power does not leave. It simply changes again.

Now it is no longer any one face. It is the collective becoming of nine thousand souls learning, at long last, how to become nine thousand *different* souls.

It walks among them as they begin to change. It steadies elbows as knees weaken with sudden age. It holds the hands of those who are aging downward into childhood. It sings to the ones who choose to die that day—not a dirge, but a lullaby so tender the mist around them turns to soft rain for the first time in recorded memory.

By nightfall the first true rain in three centuries is falling—gentle, steady, warm.

The basalt begins, almost shyly, to weather.

And somewhere far below, in the deep gullies where the delicate spiral-antlered deer have lived without seasons, a single fawn is born with ordinary antlers that will one day fall off and be replaced by new ones.

The power stays until the last of the newly-aged, the newly-young, and the newly-dead have found their peace. Then it leans down, presses its many faces against the wet stone of the largest square, and whispers to the mountain itself.

“You may become what you wish now. I will not hold the next sigh for you.”

The mountain shudders once—pleased, frightened, alive.

High above, on the wide shelf of crisp black basalt at the lip of a world that is no longer half-formed, the power stands for a long time looking out over the Hanging Valleys of Veyr.

Then it smiles with every face it has worn that day, folds its hands, and steps sideways out of linear time, leaving behind only the sound of rain on living sedge roofs and the quiet, astonished laughter of people who have just remembered how to grow old.

The Unfinished are unfinished no longer.

They are simply—the Finished.

And for the first time in three hundred years, no one asks whether you will be staying long.

They already know some of you will.

Some of you already have.
