A single note, clear as struck crystal yet softer than breath, moves across the Wetwork.

It is not sound. It is the absence of everything the place has become accustomed to: the even heat, the perpetual humidity, the flawless equilibrium. For the first time since the first moment of its making, something outside the argument between order and entropy has noticed it.

The note arrives without wind, without pressure, without announcement. Every capillary pauses mid-flow. Every bead of condensation trembles on its leaf but does not fall. The thirteen (or fourteen, or twelve) Exhalants freeze where they stand, bodies half-turned toward the pearl sky, stone-flesh rippling with sudden uncertainty. For the first time in their long existence they look… small.

Then the power manifests.

It does not descend. It does not rise from the stone. It simply *is*, as though it had always been here and only now chosen to become visible to eyes that can bear it.

A figure—no, a *presence*—takes shape in the exact center of the largest plaza, the one where the moiré patterns are thickest and the glitches most eloquent. It is not tall. It is not radiant. It wears the shape of an old woman made entirely of living water and travertine dust held in gentle suspension. Her hair is mist. Her eyes are two perfect droplets that reflect every face the Exhalants have ever pressed into the stone. When she moves, the ground does not grow wetter; instead the Wetwork itself grows *cleaner*, as though every imperfection it had learned to love is suddenly ashamed of itself.

The Exhalants bow. Not in fear. Not in worship. They bow the way lungs bow when they remember they were made to breathe *for* something.

The woman does not speak with a voice. She exhales once, and the entire basin inhales with her.

Then she walks.

Where her feet touch the engineered stone, the perforations close like eyelids. The capillaries reroute themselves into new, kinder geometries. The temperature—unchanging for millennia—drops by the smallest measurable fraction, just enough that a human body might weep from relief instead of endless perspiration. The glitches do not vanish. They bloom. The places where reality had begun to fail now become windows. Through them one can glimpse other Wetworks, other basins, other arguments between perfection and its breaking, all of them tenderly held.

She moves from terrace to terrace, from plaza to thicket. Everywhere she passes, the enormous bromeliads lower their glowing veins as though in prayer. The wingless white birds descend and rest upon her shoulders, trembling. Even the tools of antler and volcanic glass rise from where they have lain untouched for ages, following her like faithful dogs.

The Exhalants trail after her in a growing procession. Their numbers no longer refuse to resolve; there are exactly as many as there need to be. Some of them are crying. The tears are tiny ferns that take root instantly in the hollows of their own bodies.

At last she reaches the place where the scarps meet the basin’s edge—the boundary that has never truly been a boundary. There she stops. The old woman of water and dust turns to face everything that lives here, everything that has never been allowed to end.

She smiles with a mouth made of condensation and sorrow.

And she begins to sing.

Not with notes. With *permission*.

She sings the Wetwork its own ending.

Not destruction. Not mercy-killing. She sings it the right to grow old. The right for condensation to finally fall. The right for one fern to wither while another is born. The right for night to exist. The right for the builders—who were never gone, only waiting inside their own perfect work—to be released from the task of being infrastructure.

The Exhalants understand at once.

One by one they walk forward and press their stone-flesh bodies against hers. Where they touch, they dissolve—not into nothing, but into *next*. Their celadon and rose veins flow into her like ink into clear water. Their many mouths open one final time and empty the endless breath they have been holding for the basin since the first day. The breath leaves them. They become simply tall, quiet beings of wet stone who look, for the first time, like they might one day choose to sit down.

The woman sings until the pearl sky cracks open like an eggshell. Through the fissure pours every color the Wetwork has never been allowed to contain: twilight, stormlight, the bruised gold of late autumn, the shy violet of predawn. The merciless radiance softens into something that can be survived by mortal eyes.

Then she turns to any visitor who might still be standing there—human, lost, trembling, half-synced to the old rhythm.

She offers them a choice with nothing so crude as words.

*Stay and help midwife the ending,* her eyes say, *or leave and carry the memory of a place that was loved so perfectly it had to be taught how to die.*

Most choose to stay.

The few who leave discover that the path is no longer ambiguous. It simply leads upward through new-grown forests that smell of rain and coming winter. Behind them they hear the first true night the Wetwork has ever known settling down like a tired animal. They hear the first cricket. They hear an Exhalant—now only a tall, tired gardener—laugh once, a small wet sound of astonishment and relief.

And far behind them, in the center of what will soon no longer be called the Wetwork, the woman of living water and travertine dust sits down on the stone.

She is humming.

Already small things are beginning to die beautifully around her. Already mosses are learning what it means to brown at the edges. Already the capillaries are slowing, hesitating, wondering what it might be like to run only after rainfall instead of forever.

She strokes the stone like one strokes the forehead of a fevered child who has finally, after long delirium, fallen into honest sleep.

“Enough, beloved,” she whispers, and the sound of her voice is every gentle rain that was ever forbidden here. “You were perfect. Now you may be *true*.”

And the Wetwork—still nameless, still wet, no longer eternal—takes its first trembling, mortal breath.

And lets it go.
