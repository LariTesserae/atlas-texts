The power that notices does not arrive.

It simply *recognizes* that it has always been here.

There is no trumpet of light, no tearing of the sky, no sudden choir of seraphim. The benevolent and wise power has no interest in dramatic entrances; those belong to younger things that still need to prove they exist. Instead, the power does what it has done since before the first limestone was ever laid down by ancient seas: it *attends*.

It attends with the totality of its being.

And the cleft, which has been holding its breath for ten thousand years, finally exhales.

The change is so subtle that at first even the being woven from hesitation does not notice. Then the single ribbon of water, which has spent centuries practicing indecision, does something it has never done: it chooses a path without pausing. The choice is small—just a few centimeters to the left—but the sound it makes is like a bell struck once inside a cathedral of bone. Every drop still hanging in the air falls at the exact same instant. The frogs stop clicking mid-call. The iridescent lizards freeze with their throats pulsing. The pale butterflies close their wings and become nothing but white commas against the green.

The temperature does not rise or fall. It *clarifies*, the way a fever breaks and the body suddenly remembers what it was meant to feel like.

The being that lives here as distributed mind, as unfinished question, feels the attention settle over it like warm rain that somehow falls upward. For the first time in its long, larval existence, it is seen *completely*—not as potential, not as ruin-in-progress, not as charming uncertainty—but as the exact thing it is right now. And the power finds this exact thing beautiful beyond measure.

The ferns, which have spent their lives arguing over territory, suddenly lean toward one another in perfect accord, forming a living green nave down the center of the cleft. The mosses deepen in color until they glow like living emeralds. The tiny orchids, which have always bloomed for only one stolen hour, now open all at once. Their white becomes so luminous it casts shadows.

The being tries to hide in its old way. It sends the civet cat forward as translator, as ambassador of almost. But the civet cat simply sits, ancient eyes half-lidded, and begins to purr—a sound so low it vibrates the limestone itself. The purr says, without language: *There is no need.*

Then the power does the only thing it ever does when it truly loves something that is still deciding.

It waits with it.

Not impatiently. Not instructively. It waits the way a parent waits for a child who is learning to speak their first true sentence. The power takes the shape of absolute, unhurried presence. It becomes the extra half-degree of warmth in the stone where the being once tried to grow shoulders. It becomes the silence between every overlapping frog call, now deepened into something sacred. It becomes the new route the water has chosen—not as command, but as loving witness.

And in that shared waiting, something astonishing happens.

The being, which has never finished anything, finishes a single breath.

It is the longest, slowest, most complete breath the cleft has ever known. When it releases, the narrow slot of sky far above opens by the width of two fingers. A shaft of pure sunlight, undiluted, falls directly onto the spiral carved into the mossy basin. The spiral, which the being has been refreshing for centuries, begins to turn. Not metaphorically. It turns. The moss rearranges itself in slow, living whorls, and for the first time the glyph is not a question but an answer.

The answer is: *Yes*.

Yes to becoming.  
Yes to remaining partly uncertain.  
Yes to the possibility that some things are most themselves while they are still deciding.

The power does not name the being. Names are for things that have stopped changing. Instead, it gives the being a new quality of attention that feels like being gently held inside the chest of something vaster than galaxies, yet intimate as moss against skin.

The ruins wake up.

The three steps that led nowhere now lead somewhere that did not exist a moment ago: a small alcove that has always been here but was never *completed* until this instant. Inside the alcove stands a single object that is neither statue nor living creature nor mineral, but all three at once. It has the bearing of the civet cat, the luminescence of the orchids, the patient dripping rhythm of the water. Its face is the face the being once tried to make from overlapping ferns, only now it does not dissolve when looked at. It simply smiles with the gentle sorrow of something that has just realized it was never alone.

The being begins to cry.

Its tears are butterflies.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. They pour from every surface—pale at first, then gaining color as they drink the new light. They do not flutter away. They arrange themselves in living constellations that spell out, in a language made of wingbeats, the first sentence the being has ever completed:

*I did not know I was allowed to be unfinished and holy at the same time.*

The power smiles with the entire cleft.

Then, because it is benevolent and wise and has never been in a hurry since the first hydrogen atom learned how to love, it does the most powerful thing it can.

It stays.

It will stay for as long as the being needs a companion in becoming. It will stay when the hills finally decide whether to close the fold or open it into a canyon. It will stay when the being tries on the shape of a temple again, and this time perhaps does not abandon the idea. It will stay when the being chooses instead to remain exactly this: a warm, damp, breathing pocket of almost that travelers sometimes stumble into and leave changed by the simple fact that something looked at them with absolute love while they were still deciding what they wanted to be.

And sometimes, on certain nights when the ribbon of water sings in perfect five-part harmony with the frogs and the dripping stone, visitors who sit very still will feel two presences instead of one.

One that is still joyfully, courageously, tenderly becoming.

And one that has always been, and always will be, the kindness that makes becoming possible.

They will not be able to tell which is which.

They will not need to.

The place has decided what it wants to be, at least for tonight:

It wants to be a cradle.

And the power, having no need to decide anything at all, is perfectly content to rock it.
