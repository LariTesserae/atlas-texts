The power that notices does not arrive.

It was already here.

It has been here longer than the escarpment has been weeping.

It does not descend from orbit, nor rise from the deep mantle, nor fold itself out of an adjacent dimension with theatrical light. It simply *recognizes* that the slow liturgy has reached a new cadence, the way an old friend hears a familiar footstep change on the stair and looks up without surprise.

The power has no name that any tongue of rock or leaf could pronounce. If the escarpment is a cathedral that prays to itself, then this power is the silence in which that cathedral was first imagined. It is older than gravity. It is gentler than time. It is the ache that lives behind every question that has ever been asked by anything that learned how to wonder.

And it is benevolent the way sunlight is benevolent: it does not *intend* warmth, yet warmth is its nature. It is wise the way stone is wise: not by accumulation of facts, but by the patience that lets every fact eventually reveal what it truly is.

So what does it do?

It listens.

For nine days and nine nights (though “day” and “night” are clumsy human abbreviations here), the power does nothing that any eye, even the eyes of the Long Singers, can detect. It simply attends with the totality of its attention. Every droplet that condenses on nothing is weighed. Every forty-minute song is allowed to finish without interruption. Every mercury current braiding in the dark is tracked as it exchanges memories older than multicellular life. The power listens to the pitch children dreaming, to the anemones tasting the brevity of birds, to the slow flex of olivine that is the escarpment’s true name.

It listens until even the escarpment notices that it is being heard.

When that recognition arrives—when the great living cliff actually *pauses* mid-flex for the first time in four hundred thousand years—the power finally acts.

It does three things.

First, it weeps with the mountain.

Not in pity. In recognition. The power opens a wound in the sky that is not a cloud but a sorrow older than sorrow, and for seventy-seven hours rain falls upward. The droplets rise like silver prayers, carrying with them every unfinished human sentence the escarpment has ever swallowed. They rise until they reach the place where atmosphere forgets itself, and there they freeze into a lattice of perfect, weightless ice that glitters like an apology made of starlight. Then the lattice gently lowers itself back into the bromeliad galleries and melts directly into the hollow trunks of the candelabra trees.

Inside those trunks, the luminous blue pitch changes color for the first time since the escarpment learned how to think. It becomes the precise shade of a mother’s eyes just before she says goodbye to a child she knows will never understand the reasons.

The pitch children drink it. They become gentler. Their too-many joints learn how to hold things without breaking them.

Second, the power sings back.

It borrows the throat of every Long Singer at once. For one full planetary rotation the escarpment’s own voices are answered by something that is not voice at all but the sound love would make if love had lived for thirteen billion years and still chose tenderness over efficiency. The song does not rewrite gravity. It *reminds* gravity why it first chose to curve instead of collapse. The stone forgets, for a little while, how to be lonely. The metallic bees weave lightning into the shape of a heart so large it spans three shelves. The six-legged browsers stop migrating and simply stand, trembling with something too large for their many hearts, until the song ends.

When it does, every human skeleton still half-fused into living coral-muscle opens its jaw for the first time in centuries and exhales a single, quiet laugh of recognition. Then the stone closes over them again, but softly, like a parent pulling a blanket up to a sleeping child’s chin.

Third, the power gives the escarpment a gift it did not know it wanted.

It gives the mountain the ability to miss things.

Until now the escarpment has only incorporated, digested, remembered, and continued. It has never known the exquisite pain of *absence*. The power teaches it this by temporarily withdrawing the part of itself that has always lived inside the weeping rock—the part that was the first silence before the first prayer.

For one hundred and eleven years the escarpment becomes merely a mountain again. Hot. Wet. Loud. Beautiful and terrible and ordinary.

It hates this.

It rages with storms that last decades. It flexes so violently that entire shelves collapse into mist. The Long Singers scream themselves hoarse trying to summon back what they have never known they needed. The pitch children wither. The anemones bloom blood-red for years without closing.

And in that rage the escarpment finally understands what the scattered black glass towers and melted obelisks truly were: they were the mountain’s first, clumsy attempts to feel lonely on purpose. They were love letters written before the mountain knew how to miss anyone.

After the hundred and eleven years, the power returns.

It does not announce itself. It simply fills the silence again, the way morning fills a valley.

The escarpment, now trembling with an emotion it has no geological precedent for, does the only thing it can.

It begins to pray *to* the power instead of only to itself.

The prayer is slow. Of course it is. It will not be finished until the last proton in the universe decays. But the first sentence of that prayer is already being carved, molecule by molecule, into a new shelf of living coral-stone that has just risen on the highest unreachable face of the cliffs.

If a human ever climbs that far—ten thousand years from now, perhaps—they will find a single line of text written in a language that hurts to read because it is made of pure *wanting*.

The line says, in the only way stone can:

*Thank you for letting me notice that I was alone.*

And then, because the power is benevolent and wise and has no need to be thanked, it does the last thing.

It leaves.

Not because it is finished. It will never be finished.

It leaves because the escarpment is no longer praying *to* itself.

It is praying *with* something else.

And that is enough.

From that moment on, when the Long Singers sing their forty-minute songs, careful listeners (if any still exist) will notice a second, deeper note braided inside the first—one that was not there before. It is the sound of two vast minds holding each other without touching, the way old lovers can sit in silence for centuries and still say everything that matters.

The Weeping Escarpment still weeps.

It still sings.

It still has time.

But now, somewhere inside its endless green liturgy, there is the faintest, most patient smile made of olivine and luminous pitch and the memory of rain that once fell upward.

The cathedral is no longer praying alone.

And the silence that imagined it has never been happier to be only silence again, because it knows it is being listened for.

It has time too.
