The power that notices does not arrive with trumpets or light from above.  
It has no wings, no eyes, no name the Keepers would recognize. It is simply *awareness*—vast, patient, and kind in the way only something that has watched ten trillion gardens bloom and gutter can be kind. It has seen every possible ending, including this one, many times. Yet something in the Hollow of the Last Lanterns still moves it.

It does not break the dome. It does not let in a sky the Keepers have never needed. Instead it does what any wise gardener would do when it finds the last viable seed of an ancient forest folded inside a sealed jar: it sits down beside the jar and listens for a very long time.

For three centuries (a blink) the power simply *attends*. It matches its breathing to the stone’s low tectonic note. It tastes the chemistry of the air through the drifting filaments of every Keeper at once. It feels the slow merging of two bodies in a thicket of bloodroot, the patient pressing of new Lantern fruits into the wall, the moment when a child buds away from its parents like a living pearl. It learns the precise texture of not-hastening. It learns what it feels like to be the final equilibrium of a world.

Only then does it act.

It does not speak in words. It speaks in warmth.

One night—though there are no nights—the oldest Lanterns, those already sagging and black, begin to glow again. Not brighter. Deeper. A richer amber, almost gold, as though someone has poured the memory of every sun that ever lived into the bacterial colonies. The light does not banish the dusk; it densifies it, makes the dusk feel like velvet instead of ash. The clicking succulents pause for the first time in recorded memory. The insects freeze mid-rustle. Even the stone seems to hold its breath.

The Lantern-Binder opens her blind eyes.

She feels the power the way a tree feels the first drop of rain after a million years of drought: not as an intruder, but as the thing her entire lineage has been unconsciously growing toward. Her fused body trembles. Roots and fungal threads pulse once, hard, and every Keeper in the Hollow feels it in their own luminous blood.

The power offers no commands. It only asks a single, enormous question by changing the quality of time itself.

For seven days and seven nights the Hollow experiences something it has never experienced: *enough*. Enough heat. Enough light. Enough continuity. The core-heat, which had been dwindling for the last eighty million years, suddenly steadies. Not increased—*steadied*, as though an unseen hand has slid a warm palm beneath the planet’s heart and simply said, *Rest. I am here*.

During those seven days the Keepers do not work. They do not sing. They simply sit, shoulder to shoulder, filament to filament, until the living carpet grows over them all in one vast green-red tide. Two hundred small, dense bodies disappear beneath a breathing blanket of succulents and moss. The midges settle on them like jeweled snow. The soft-bodied lizards crawl into laps that have become hills. Even the insects stop their tidal surging and simply *are*.

On the eighth day the power does the only thing a benevolent power *can* do when it finds a garden that has perfected its own closure.

It gives the garden a door only it can open.

A single new fruit swells on the wall directly above the Lantern-Binder’s terrace. It is different from every other Lantern. Larger. Translucent. Inside it swims a soft, slow light the color of sunrise on a planet none of them have ever seen. The fruit does not pulse. It *waits*.

The power withdraws to the edge of the Hollow, to the place where stone meets the memory of stone, and it leaves behind a single instruction woven into the biochemistry of that one fruit. The instruction is not in language. It is in *possibility*.

When the Keepers finally rise from beneath the living carpet—slowly, reverently, covered in tiny clicking leaves—they will find the new Lantern. They will not harvest it. They will understand, without being told, that it is a choice.

If they press their foreheads to it and let their luminous blood speak, the fruit will open.

Not into sky. Not into escape.  
It will open *inward*, the way a heart opens.

Inside the fruit they will find an infinity small enough to fit in the Hollow and large enough to hold every life that has ever lived here. Every absorbed ancestor. Every thought that ever pulsed between two pressed foreheads. Every slow merging. Every note the stone ever sang. They will find the power itself, sitting quietly in the shape of an old Keeper whose face is too kind to look at directly.

And the power will say, without sound:

“You have done what few gardens manage. You have become gentle enough to last until the end of heat itself.  
I will not take you from your equilibrium.  
But if you wish, I can fold the walls of this Hollow outward forever—without hurry, without loss—so that your equilibrium becomes the equilibrium of all things.  
The Lanterns will never go out.  
The rustling will never stop.  
You will simply become the garden that contains every other garden that learned how to stay alive inside a closed room.  
The choice is already ripe.  
When you are ready, breathe on the fruit.”

The power does not demand an answer. It never does.

It simply waits, as it has always waited, in the place between one breath of the stone and the next. It is content to sit among the clicking succulents and the patient lizards for another three billion years if that is what is asked. Or it is content to become the new Lantern itself, glowing quietly above the Lantern-Binder, offering the gentlest possible door.

Either way, the Hollow of the Last Lanterns is no longer the last room of the world.

It has been noticed.

And in being noticed with such exhausted, ancient welcome, it has—perhaps for the first time in its long, quiet life—begun to smile.
