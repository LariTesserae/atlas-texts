The place is called the Teeth of the Sun.

You stand on a baked ironstone ridge at nearly four thousand meters, where the air is thin enough that every breath feels like swallowing heated glass. The heat is not merely weather; it is a geological fact. Stone here has been cooking at 48–52 °C for so long that its surface has blistered into glassy plates that ring like porcelain when struck. Yet water still finds its way in.

It arrives as sudden, violent convection storms that boil up from the east every six or seven days, drop their cargo in thirty furious minutes, then vanish. Most of the rain never reaches the valleys below; it is swallowed by the stone itself. The plateau is riddled with thousands of fist-sized to barrel-sized solution pockets—natural cisterns—where condensation, seepage, and the memory of rain collect in black, tea-colored mirrors. These pockets are the only reliable water for hundreds of kilometers. They never overflow. Something always drinks them first.

Vegetation is almost an insult to the term. What grows does so in crevices or in the lee of iron-rich boulders: skeletal saltbush the color of rust, fist-sized clumps of stonecrop so desiccated they look like fossilized brains, and one lone species of acacia that has learned to grow horizontally along the rock rather than upward, its leaves reduced to gray needles. All of it is sparse, yet where it occurs it is jammed so tightly into its niche that the roots form intricate braids visible on the surface, like the wiring beneath torn skin.

The scale is wrong in every direction. The plateau stretches thirty kilometers east to west, but it is not flat. It is a shattered pavement of tilted slabs, pressure ridges, and sudden sinkholes. From any high point you can see at least seven distinct landforms at once: the red-iron badlands to the north, the black basaltic fins to the south, the trembling heat haze above the salt pans far below, and—most impossible—the forest of iron towers.

They rise in a band across the middle of the plateau like the fossilized ribcage of some continent-sized beast. Eighty-seven cooling towers and smokestacks, all that remains of the Equatorial Atmospheric Exchange Project, abandoned mid-construction in the early 2070s. Some still vent—not smoke, but superheated air from deep geothermal shafts that were never capped. The towers are rust the color of dried blood, yet structurally perfect; the dryness and the altitude have preserved them in a state of immortal decay. Between and beneath them run mazes of half-buried pipeworks, conveyor gantries, and collapsed worker barracks—scattered remnants of industry that have become just another geological layer.

The density is claustrophobic despite the openness. Every square meter that is not bare rock is claimed. The cooling towers are wrapped in living sleeves of black lichen that photosynthesize at 49 °C and secrete oxalic acid to etch microscopic channels into the steel. The pipes are inhabited by colony spiders the size of a man’s hand that spin metallic-thread webs between corroded flanges. Every puddle hosts its own miniature brine shrimp and rotifer civilization. Every shadow belongs to something with too many legs.

Fauna is abundant in the way a wound is abundant with white cells. Thermophilic rock ibex pick their way along ridges in single file, their hooves clacking like typewriter keys. Vast colonies of heat-adapted bats pour out of abandoned ventilation shafts at dusk in roaring, rust-colored rivers of wing. Most disturbingly, there are the mirror lizards—flat, iridescent things that lie perfectly still on the sun-facing sides of towers, reflecting the sky so accurately that pilots have reported the towers simply disappearing from above. When they move, the reflection tears and the world seems to rip.

The sound is mostly quiet, but never silent. There is always the low organ note of wind across the mouths of the tallest towers, a constant sub-audible moan that vibrates in your teeth. Every few hours a rockfall clatters somewhere in the distance, the sound strangely flat in the thin air. Occasionally, from deep inside the largest tower (the one they called Lazarus), there comes a metallic clank, as if something the size of a locomotive is slowly turning over in its sleep. No one has ever gone inside to learn what produces it.

This is an active place pretending to be dead.

The ecosystem is not in balance so much as it is in argument. The geothermal heat, the sudden rains, the toxic legacy of half-finished industry, and the thin screaming air have produced a set of rules that feel like they were written by a committee that hated each other. Things grow only where three separate improbabilities intersect. Predators and prey have learned to share the same shade because there is so little of it. The mirror lizards reflect not only light but, on certain days when the temperature cracks 54 °C, something that might be memory; animals that have never seen water will suddenly walk toward a particular cistern as though summoned.

The built environment has become geology. The industrial skeleton and the living skin have fused so completely after eighty years that it is no longer clear where the project ends and the world begins. Rust is a soil. Conveyor belts have become terraces for saltbush. One entire dormitory block has been swallowed by a single clonal colony of iron-oxidizing bacteria that glows faint blue at night, like a dying ember the size of a football field.

At noon the light is so absolute that color leaves the world. Everything becomes bone-white and furnace-black. Shadows are razor-cut and dangerous; stand in one too long and the contrast will blind you when you step out. At twilight the plateau exhales. Heat rises visibly from every surface. The towers sing louder. The mirror lizards fold themselves into the rock and become invisible. For perhaps twenty minutes the air itself seems to glow, as though the stone is remembering the sun it swallowed all day.

Then the temperature plummets. Condensation appears on every metal surface in perfect trembling beads. The black cisterns wake up and breathe. Things with too many legs and not enough names come out to drink.

And the Teeth of the Sun, ancient, industrial, impossible, keeps arguing with itself under the indifferent stars.
