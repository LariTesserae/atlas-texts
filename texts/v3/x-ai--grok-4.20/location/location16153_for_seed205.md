The world is called Veyr-Keth, though no one who still walks it uses that name.

It is a high, hot continent of living green that has long since forgotten the difference between ground and creature. The land does not rest on stone; it rests on itself, on the endless overlapping bodies of titanic plants that have grown, died, rotted, and grown again for so many millions of years that their compressed corpses have become the only geology. You walk on the ribs of ancient ferns the size of mountain ranges. You descend into valleys that are actually the hollowed heartwood of trees that fell before mammals existed. Every surface is damp with slow, internal condensation—beads of moisture forming inside the living mass and seeping outward like sweat. Rain comes only in sudden, hot, vertical bursts that last minutes and leave steam rising from the canopy for hours.

The air is thick enough to taste. Temperature rarely drops below 42 °C even at what passes for night. At this elevation the sky is a pale, almost white turquoise, but you rarely see much of it. The vegetation is absolute. Every direction is filled with plant architecture on a scale that humiliates the mind: vaulting colonnades of bamboo-like reeds thick as castle towers, hanging curtains of aerial roots that descend for hundreds of meters like frozen waterfalls of green rope, shelves of bracket fungi large enough to stable entire herds of animals. Yet for all this vegetative fury the density remains strangely sparse. There is always breathing room between the colossal forms—cathedral-like clearings where only moss and mist reside, long aisles where nothing grows because the root systems beneath have sucked the substrate dry. The eye is never crowded, only dwarfed.

The sound is deafening and constant. It is not the sound of animals alone, though the fauna is overwhelming. It is the sound of the vegetable world itself: the wet creak and groan of trunks expanding in the heat, the explosive pop of seed pods the size of wagons, the endless dripping, the low subsonic thrumming that travels through the root networks like blood through arteries. Over this rides the screaming, chittering, bellowing, chirring choir of creatures that have never known a world without green. Six-winged things the color of sap drift between shafts of light. Herds of glossy black millipede-beasts, each the length of a ship, move in perfect silence along grooves worn into the living floor, their thousands of legs making a sound like heavy rain on leaves. Things that look like orchids unfold into predatory anemones when something warm passes beneath them. Everything is either eating or being eaten or preparing to do both.

The strangeness reveals itself slowly.

Some of the trees have grown faces—not carved, not mimetic, but actual bone-like plates of lignin arranged in expressions that change over decades. Certain clearings are perfectly circular and contain only one species of translucent, glass-like moss that rings like bells when touched. In the deepest root-canyons you find ruins of a prior civilization so primitive they barely qualify as ruins: hand-hewn log bridges grown through and incorporated by the vegetation centuries ago, stone tools half-swallowed by bark, single-room shelters now functioning as humid greenhouses for epiphytic orchids. These traces are rare. The plants have mostly won.

There is no metal here. The few tool-using creatures—small, furtive, bright-eyed beings that might be distant relatives of monkeys or raccoons—work in sharpened root-wood, fire-hardened thorn, and the serrated edges of certain leaves. They move in total silence, because to make noise here is to invite immediate consumption. Their villages are nothing more than a few lashed platforms inside the armpit of a single enormous strangler fig. They do not last long. The tree eventually digests them.

Weather is local and violent. Because the living mass is so vast it generates its own pressure systems. You can stand in a dry, searing clearing while three kilometers away a vertical wall of rain hammers down so hard it strips smaller plants from their moorings. Steam clouds rise like ghosts and are immediately torn apart by thermals. At certain hours—always the same hours, though there is no visible sun cycle—the entire continent seems to inhale. Every leaf turns slightly, every aerial root tightens, and the sound drops by half for seven heartbeats. Then the exhalation begins and the roaring returns.

Nothing here is settled in the way a mountain is settled. Everything is in balance only because everything is moving. The vast root networks shift tectonic plates of living matter over centuries. New fungal cathedrals rise in decades. Whole districts of the green continent collapse into sinkholes when a keystone tree finally rots through, only for new growth to erupt from the corpse before the dust has settled.

This is not a jungle.

It is a single planetary organism wearing a thin pelt of animals, and the animals—including the ones who sometimes walk on two legs and carry thorn-spears—are tolerated only as long as they remain minor irritants in its endless, hot, deafening digestion of itself.
