The High Pockets are not a place that settled. They are a place that is still arriving.

You stand on the lip of a continent that has no right to be so young. The rock itself feels wet with birth. Every cliff edge is a fresh fracture, every slope still bleeding talus like a wound that hasn’t decided whether to close or keep opening. The air is warm—almost hot—yet the light is only a bruised twilight, as though the sun itself has been diluted by too many overlapping atmospheres. Shadows pool thickly in the hollows and refuse to move even when the wind blows.

Water is not generous here, but it is everywhere in small, conspiratorial amounts. It gathers in glassy lenses inside volcanic vesicles, drips from the undersides of cantilevered strata in long silver threads that evaporate before they touch the ground, beads on every leaf like sudden sweat. Occasional rains come sideways, brief, almost embarrassed, then vanish. What remains are pockets—thousands upon thousands of pocket-lakes no larger than a dining table, each one a different temperature, each one a different color, each one ringed by its own private jungle no bigger than a city block.

The vegetation has not waited for permission. It is lush, yes, but the lushness is frantic, almost hysterical. Trees the color of oxidized copper rocket upward in helical trunks, their bark sweating clear sap that smells of ozone and crushed mint. Vines thick as ship’s hawsers braid themselves between species that should never meet, forming living suspension bridges that sway even when the air is still. Every surface is furred, fronded, spined, or dripping. The green is so dense it becomes acoustic—sound falls into it and is swallowed. The only noises that escape are low, subsonic things: the wet crack of new rock splitting, the sigh of a pocket-lake suddenly boiling as something beneath the crust shifts, and, very rarely, the deep industrial heartbeat of something that is definitely not geological.

Because the tech is here, though it was never built by hands.

Great iron-colored roots—except they are pipes—plunge out of the living rock and into the canopy. Some pulse. Some vent steam that smells of hot transformers. Others have split open like seed pods and are growing secondary machines inside themselves: black glass turbines spinning slowly in the stomachs of titanic figs, relay towers wearing orchids like camouflage, whole sections of what might once have been a refinery now flowering with bioluminescent molds that spell out shifting equations across their flanks.

The fauna treats all of this as normal.

There are things like moths the size of kites that have fused their wing membranes with rusted sheet metal; they drift between thermals making almost no sound, their compound eyes replaced by flickering indicator lights. Herds of six-legged browsers move through the understory wearing coats of living moss and trailing filaments of copper wire that spark when they touch. In the pocket-lakes, translucent eels coil around submerged valves, gently turning them with their bodies in sequences that look suspiciously like code.

Everything is reproducing at the edge of collapse.

New land erupts hourly—fresh, black, glassy—and is immediately clothed by runners and creepers that race across it in emerald fire. Whole hillsides calve into the abyss with a sigh, taking factories of root and leaf with them, only for identical hills to thrust upward somewhere else minutes later, as though the world is trying to remember a shape it was never allowed to finish.

The scale is oceanic. From any height the Pockets stretch away in repeating fractal basins until the eye simply gives up and calls the horizon a myth. Weather systems are born and die between neighboring ridges. You can watch a thundercloud nucleate above one pocket-lake, drift three kilometers, and dissolve into mist above its neighbor while the pocket itself has already changed color from turquoise to blood-orange.

Reality here is not broken so much as unfinished. The laws have not had time to agree with one another. Gravity forgets itself on certain slopes and things roll uphill. Time thickens in the shade of certain trees so that a single afternoon can last a week. Echoes arrive before the sounds that made them. If you stand still long enough, the vegetation will begin to incorporate you—gently, almost politely—threading conductive vines through your clothes, pressing small warm transistors against your skin like affectionate insects.

There are no ruins here, because nothing has ever been allowed to finish falling down. There are no cities, because the idea of a city is too slow for this place. The only architecture is the violent, tender collaboration between geology that is still screaming from its birth and machines that have gone native and vegetation that has learned how to dream in frequencies.

It is mostly quiet.

But the quiet is full. It is the held breath between two incompatible states of matter deciding whether to annihilate each other or fall in love.

And the decision keeps being postponed, forever.
