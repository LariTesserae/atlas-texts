The land is called the Shroudmire.

It is a low, wide basin that has only recently been uncovered—perhaps a few centuries old, perhaps less. The ground itself feels newborn and raw, as though the crust peeled back yesterday and forgot to heal. Everywhere the eye lands, black basaltic rock still glistens with moisture, its edges knife-sharp, its fractures clean. Water does not so much fall here as live here: it beads on every surface, sweats from stone, condenses in the air itself into drifting veils of mist that taste of iron and cold pine. Springs burst from the rock at ankle height, run ten paces, then vanish again into the moss as if the earth were breathing them in and out.

The cold is permanent and wet. Temperature never climbs above the edge of freezing; the air itself feels like it has just melted. Every breath condenses. Your eyelashes can freeze together if you stand still too long.

Yet the vegetation is almost obscene in its richness. Mosses of a hundred shades—emerald, viridian, bruise-purple—grow in layered carpets thick enough to drown a man. Ferns taller than houses unfurl fronds the color of wet jade. Trees that look like a cross between spruce and horsetail thrust upward in dense, shivering columns, their trunks wrapped in luminous lichens that glow faint blue even in this near-dark. Every leaf surface is lacquered with water. Every branch drips. The entire basin is one continuous, saturated sponge.

And it is loud.

The sound is deafening and constant: ten thousand waterfalls no larger than your forearm, the endless roar of wind tearing through the canopy, the wet thunder of moss mats tearing free from overhangs and slapping into pools below. Beneath that, a lower register—something like breathing, or like the ground itself groaning as water forces new cracks open. The noise never stops. After an hour your bones feel tuned to it; after a day you forget silence ever existed.

The basin stretches for many kilometers in every direction, open enough that you can see the dark wall of the next ridge through gaps in the canopy, yet so densely woven with life and water and stone that every step feels claustrophobic. You move through layered curtains: mist, hanging moss, dripping ferns, more mist. Visibility is rarely more than thirty meters.

Scattered among all this are the artifacts.

They are not ruins. They are too new, too perfect. Tall hexagonal pillars of something that is not metal and not crystal rise from the marsh in places, their surfaces rippling with slow interference patterns that hurt to watch. Some of them are half-swallowed by moss, but the moss does not grow on them—it grows around them, as though the pillars are hotter or colder than the world. Thin silver threads, finer than spider silk, run between certain pillars in perfect catenary arcs that ignore wind and gravity. Occasionally the threads sing in frequencies that make your teeth ache.

No one knows what they were built for. They are simply here, like the water and the cold and the roar.

Fauna is everywhere, loud and wet and busy. Pale six-legged mammals the size of otters slip between the tree roots, their fur beaded with permanent dew. Clouds of tiny luminous midges drift in the gloom, flashing synchronized codes that no biologist has ever deciphered. Things that might be frogs or might be something older call in overlapping chords that cut through the waterfall noise like bright knives. Once in a while something huge moves in the distance—branches snapping, water sheeting off a wet flank—and then stillness returns, heavier than before.

The light is almost none. A permanent lid of low, iron-gray cloud blots out any sun. What illumination exists is indirect, aqueous, and bluish. It leaks from the lichens, from the strange pillars, from the condensation in the air itself. Shadows are soft and numerous. You can never quite tell what time it is; the concept feels meaningless here.

Most astonishing of all is the turbulence.

Nothing here is stable. The landscape is in constant, frantic motion. New springs burst open while you watch. Moss bridges collapse into black pools and are immediately recolonized by frantic growth. Pillars shift their interference patterns, causing nearby trees to lean in new directions as though the gravity gradient changed. Whole sections of the basin seem to breathe—rising and falling by meters over hours, driven by subterranean water pressure. Rivers appear, carve deep gashes through the moss, then vanish underground again, leaving fresh wounds in the rock. The Shroudmire is not a place that has settled. It is a place still being violently born, over and over, while life riots across its wet, screaming surface.

You do not visit the Shroudmire.

You enter it, and for as long as you can bear the cold and the noise and the relentless living pressure of it, the place works on you the way water works on stone—slowly, relentlessly, until you are no longer quite sure where you end and the marsh begins.
