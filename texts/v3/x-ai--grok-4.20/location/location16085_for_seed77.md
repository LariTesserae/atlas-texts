The world is called the **Exhalation**.

It is a continent-sized machine that has not yet finished being born.

Everything here is built, yet nothing is finished. The ground is not stone or soil but a single, unfinished lattice of pale ceramic and blackened glass that stretches from one curved horizon to the other. The lattice is only half-fused; in places it is still cooling from whatever unimaginable heat once poured through it. Where the seams have not yet knit, warm condensation beads and trembles, then slips into the cracks like sweat. These trickles gather in long, shallow gutters that run for hundreds of kilometres before vanishing into deeper, still-forming strata. The water is never still. It moves in slow, almost reluctant pulses, as though the land itself is breathing.

The heat is constant and heavy. The air shimmers above every surface, making the distant geometry of the lattice waver like a mirage. There is no sun visible—only a uniform, bone-white sky that gives normal, directionless light. The temperature does not come from above but from below: a slow, deep geothermal pulse that makes the ceramic exhale faint veils of vapour. Sometimes, without warning, a new fracture opens with a soft glassy pop and a jet of warm mist rises for several minutes before the wound begins to seal again. These brief exhalations are the loudest sounds the world permits.

Vegetation is almost nonexistent, yet not entirely absent. Here and there, isolated colonies of something that might become moss or lichen have taken hold in the damp seams. They are the colour of dried bone and grow in hair-fine filaments no longer than a fingernail. When the condensation runs across them they darken to a dull terracotta for a few hours, then bleach again as the water passes. They are the only living colour in a landscape otherwise restricted to white, cream, silver-grey, and the glossy black of newly-cooled glass.

The scale is merciless. A single tile of the lattice may be the size of a city block. Between tiles lie canyons of empty air where the structure has not yet descended. You can walk for days and never see the far side of the next major seam. The horizon itself curves gently upward in every direction, suggesting the entire Exhalation is a shallow dish many thousands of kilometres across, still being pressed into final shape by forces that have since moved on.

There are no animals. Not even microbes beyond the pale filaments. The silence is therefore almost total. Only three sounds exist:

- the soft ticking of cooling glass
- the patient drip and trickle of condensation finding new gutters
- the low, intermittent sigh of vapour escaping fresh fractures

All three are quiet enough that your own breath and heartbeat feel like intrusions.

The land is still learning what it will be. In the far distance—visible only on the clearest days when the heat haze lies down—you can see titanic ribs rising from the lattice like the incomplete skeleton of something planetary. They have not yet been clothed in surface. Between them, new sheets of ceramic are slowly extruding upward in slow-motion waves, millimetre by millimetre, folding and unfolding like breathing. The process is so gradual that if you sat in one place for a human lifetime you would still see no motion, yet the evidence is everywhere: fresh edges sharp enough to cut, surfaces that have not yet acquired the patina of age, places where the lattice simply stops, revealing raw underlayers of matte black honeycomb that look wet though they have never known rain.

Nothing decays here. Nothing has had time to decay. The Exhalation is younger than any geology has a right to be. It is still in the act of deciding whether it will become desert, or city, or membrane, or something that has no name yet. The only law is slow becoming.

Walk long enough and you may find a perfect circular depression where the lattice has not yet risen. Inside it a shallow lake of condensation has gathered, perfectly still, reflecting the white sky like a mirror made of mercury. Around its rim the pale filaments grow thickest, as though even they understand this is a place of temporary mercy.

Stay too long beside the lake and you may notice the water level is not constant. It rises and falls by a finger’s width every few hours, following the same tidal rhythm that makes the distant ribs flex. The Exhalation is inhaling. The Exhalation is exhaling.

And every time it does, the world inches closer to whatever form it has been dreaming since the first tile locked into place.
