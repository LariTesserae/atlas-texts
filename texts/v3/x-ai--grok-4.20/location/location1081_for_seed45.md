The world has no name yet. It is still deciding what it will be.

You stand on the lip of what might one day be called a continent, though at this elevation the word feels ridiculous. The ground is not ground; it is a raw, slowly congealing crust of mineral foam and volcanic glass shot through with metallic veins that have never seen water. Everything is warm—oven-warm, blood-warm—radiating from below as though the planet itself is still being baked in some cosmic kiln. The air, if it can be called air, is a thin, acrid soup of sulfur gases and heavy noble elements that tastes of hot iron on the tongue.

There is almost no vegetation. What little grows is not plant, nor fungus, nor even clearly alive in the way biology understands the term. Black-glass filigrees rise in sparse, delicate lattices, like frozen lightning or the first hesitant sketches of nerves. They do not photosynthesize. They resonate. When the thermals shift, they sing in frequencies just below hearing, a sound you feel in your teeth.

The terrain is embryonic and colossal. Immense plates of new crust rear upward in broken shelves and fractured escarpments, some kilometers high, their edges still glowing dull cherry-red from the mantle beneath. Between them lie wide, empty basins filled only with slow-crawling rivers of semi-molten slag that have not yet decided whether to become stone or remain liquid. The scale is oppressive: you can see three or four separate ranges in the distance, each birthing the next, each still shrugging off the heat of creation. Perspective collapses here. What looks like a small ridge an hour’s walk away is actually a mountain range the size of the Alps, still growing.

Everything moves.

The crust itself is restless, turbulent, half-formed. Great slow-motion spasms ripple across the landscape every few minutes—entire cliffs blooming outward like flowers of black glass, then collapsing again with a groan that travels through the rock for kilometers. New fissures open with a sound like tearing silk stretched over thunder. Old ones seal themselves in glassy sutures. The world is not finished; it is still arguing with itself. Sometimes entire mesas lift bodily into the air on pillars of superheated gas before shattering and raining obsidian shrapnel across the plains. Gravity seems uncertain, as though the planet has not yet agreed how strongly it should pull.

Light is almost absent. A faint, sourceless maroon glow leaks from the cracks and from the undersides of floating mineral rafts that drift in the upper atmosphere like half-melted ships. The sky itself is a velvet blackness pressing close, scattered with ember-like sparks that are not stars but pockets of ionized gas burning in the thermosphere. Occasionally a sheet of pale auroral fire unfolds across the dark, illuminating the newborn topography in strokes of magnesium white and arterial red before snuffing out again. Shadows here are not absence of light; they are places the world has not yet remembered to finish.

Scattered across this violent, half-made geography are the Built things—rare, lonely, and inexplicable. A single tower of perfectly smooth white stone, thirty meters tall, standing on a plateau that did not exist an hour ago. A ring of seven obsidian obelisks whose inner faces are etched with symbols that keep changing when unobserved. The broken spine of what might have been a bridge arcing across a canyon that is still widening beneath it. None of these structures show tool marks. They seem to have been extruded by the world itself in brief moments of lucidity, then abandoned when the crust shifted again. They are already being swallowed—glass filaments growing over them, slag flows licking at their bases, new mountains shouldering them aside like indifferent beasts.

There is no life as you understand it. No birds, no insects, no lichen. Yet the place seethes with a mineral vitality. The slow crash and grind of continents being born is constant. The air itself feels crowded with unseen forces—pressure fronts, magnetic shears, thermal columns that spiral like serpents made of heat. Sometimes the glass lattices bloom in sudden sympathetic patterns, forming temporary nervous systems hundreds of meters across before the next tectonic spasm tears them apart. The world is building and unbuilding itself in the same breath.

Sound is rare but enormous when it comes: the deep basal groan of plates sliding, the glassy ringing of fresh fractures propagating for kilometers, the soft roar of gas vents breathing superheated vapor into the dark. Between these events the silence is absolute, a ringing vacuum that makes your own pulse feel like an intruder.

This is a place before rivers, before rain, before soil or memory. A world still in the violent trance of self-creation, sculpting itself out of molten stone and pure possibility while the sky watches with indifferent, ember-lit eyes. Every step you take is on ground that may not exist tomorrow. Every breath tastes of a planet that has not yet decided what kind of life—if any—it will eventually allow.

You are standing inside an argument between gravity and fire, and the argument is still heating up.
