The place is called the **Underbasin**.

It is a low, sunken pocket of land no larger than a modest cathedral quarter, yet it feels simultaneously cramped and immense because every surface sweats. The air itself is hot (almost 43 °C) and so saturated that breath condenses on your teeth. A permanent, low-hanging mist clings to everything, glowing dull orange from the sodium-vapor lamps bolted to rusting gantries overhead. True daylight has never reached here; the sky is a lid of wet concrete and corroded iron lattice two hundred meters up.

The ground is young. Only a few centuries ago this was a flooded limestone quarry that broke through into a thermal aquifer. The water never stopped rising, but neither did the city above. So the quarry was roofed, drained just enough to stand in, and colonized. Now it is a single, stable industrial settlement that has reached perfect, hot equilibrium. Nothing grows older here. Nothing decays further. The rust has rusted as far as it can rust; the steam has found every crevice it will ever occupy.

Water is everywhere, but never as open pools. It oozes. It weeps from the fractured limestone walls in slow, warm sheets. It drips from every pipe joint and every I-beam. It condenses on the undersides of walkways and falls in fat, lazy drops that hiss when they strike the hot metal floors. The smell is iron, wet stone, ozone, and overheated lubricant.

Vegetation is almost absent. A few pale spear-leaved reeds and liverworts cling to the wettest rock faces, but they are sparse, stunted, the color of old bone. The only green is the opportunistic algal slime that paints every north-facing surface in slick, luminous films.

What dominates is infrastructure. A tight warren of catwalks, pressure pipes, settling tanks, and brick pump-houses has been bolted, riveted, and welded into the limestone bowl. Five major steam-driven pumping stations labor constantly, their great flywheels turning with a deep, unchanging heartbeat that never varies by a single revolution per minute. The sound is loud but strangely comforting: a choral drone of engines, groaning pipes, distant rivet hammers, and the wet slap of condensate falling onto hot steel. It never crescendos and never fades. It simply is.

Fauna, by contrast, is unnervingly abundant. Pale geckos with translucent bellies skitter along steam pipes. Clouds of tiny black moths with metallic wings orbit the sodium lamps in slow, hypnotic orbits. Fat, heat-tolerant rats move without hurry between the foundations, their eyes milky from generations in the orange gloom. Best adapted of all are the steam-skaters — long-legged, iridescent beetles that glide across the surface tension of condensation films as though the droplets were solid. Their clicking legs make a constant high percussion beneath the machine drone.

The architecture is layered and pragmatic. Lower levels are raw limestone cut by drill and dynamite, still bearing the scars of the first explosions. Middle levels are brick and cast iron, the original settlement. Upper walkways and gantries are newer welded steel, already sweating rust the color of dried blood. Everything is connected by ladders, chain hoists, and narrow-gauge tracks that disappear into dripping tunnels. There are no wide squares; space is too valuable. Instead the settlement is a three-dimensional lattice of corridors and voids, like the inside of a vast, sweating engine.

Nothing here changes.

The pumps have found their exact rhythm. The temperature has stabilized at the precise point where water both condenses and evaporates in perfect balance. The same moths have circled the same lamps for two hundred and thirty years. The same warm droplets have fallen from the same cracked pipe joint every eleven seconds since the roof was sealed. Even the people have the slow, deliberate movements of creatures that have accepted they will never leave this hot, wet, orange cathedral of perpetual industry.

It is a landscape that has reached its final, sweating form. A small, loud, dim, iron-and-limestone womb where water, heat, and machinery have negotiated an eternal truce.

Welcome to the Underbasin.  
Mind the dripping. It never stops, but it never gets worse.
