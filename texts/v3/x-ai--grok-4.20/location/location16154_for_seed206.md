The place is a single, self-contained hollow no larger than a modest cathedral nave, yet it feels limitless because every surface has been claimed and softened by the same living mass. It is not a forest. It is a single organism that has decided to wear the shape of a forest.

You stand inside the Lung.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. The air itself is saturated; every breath arrives already half liquid. Moisture condenses on the undersides of vast leaves the color of oxidized copper and falls in steady, warm drops that never quite reach the ground; they are intercepted by tier upon tier of smaller leaves, spongy moss curtains, and trembling epiphytic cups. The ground is not soil but a living mat of interwoven root and rhizome, spongy as a tongue. Press your hand down and the surface yields, then slowly pushes back, as if the place is tasting you. From hidden crevices between the roots, clear water wells up in slow pulses, warm as blood, then slips sideways into invisible drains and is gone. The rocks—there are still rocks—are completely furred in moss so thick it looks like green velvet poured over bone. Where the moss has pulled away you see the stone itself weeping: steady, perfect beads sliding from microscopic pores.

Everything is warm. Not hot, never cold. A steady 29 °C that makes the distinction between your skin and the air feel optional.

The scale is intimate, almost claustrophobic, yet the ceiling (if it can be called that) vanishes upward into layered canopies so dense they become their own sky. Light filters down as a perpetual, sourceless, dove-grey radiance, the color of early morning that never decides to become morning. No direct sun. No true shadow. Only a soft, even glow that makes every droplet sparkle like scattered mercury.

And through this green cathedral runs the Machine.

It is not *in* the place. It has become one of the place’s organs.

Thick black pipes, older than anyone living, rise from the living floor like iron taproots. Their surfaces are completely covered in a crust of orange rust and emerald moss that have reached an equilibrium; neither defeats the other. Every thirty seconds a hidden pump, buried somewhere beneath the root-mass, fires with a deep, wet *thump* that you feel in your sternum. When it does, warm water is forced upward through the pipes and erupts from nozzles disguised as giant pitcher plants. The spray is fine, almost mist, and it drifts in slow motion through the leaves before the entire system reclaims it. The sound is constant: a low industrial heartbeat overlaid with the endless dripping, the rustle of ten thousand leaves moving in wind that has no source, and the loud, wet breathing of the place itself.

The vegetation is so total it has become substrate, architecture, and atmosphere at once. Vines thicker than your thigh form living buttresses. Orchids grow on orchids. Every surface that is not actively pumping water is furred with bromeliads, ferns, and mosses so dense they have no names. Yet the overall impression is not jungle chaos but a single, patient intelligence expressing itself through a billion mouths. Nothing competes. Everything cooperates in the same endless, humid respiration.

Fauna is abundant but strangely courteous. Tiny scarlet frogs no larger than thumbnails sit on leaves like living jewels and never seem to move. Clouds of midges swirl in slow galaxies around the warm mist plumes, catching the light like iron filings around a magnet. Somewhere far above, invisible in the green ceiling, larger things shift and call—deep, resonant hoots and metallic clicks that sound almost like the Machine answering them. Once in a great while a pale, translucent snake the color of milk glass pours itself from one leaf layer to another without disturbing a drop.

Nothing ever changes.

The same droplets fall from the same leaves in the same rhythm. The same slow geysers of mist breathe from the same iron pitchers. The same frogs sit in the same places. The temperature never varies by a tenth of a degree. Even the rust on the pipes appears to have stopped advancing centuries ago, as though time itself grew tired and lay down inside the warm, wet green.

It is not peaceful. It is loud—loud with water, loud with hidden engines, loud with the endless wet exhalation of a billion leaves. But it is stable. A pocket universe that reached equilibrium so long ago that the concept of “before” no longer applies.

You may leave whenever you wish.

The place will not notice.

It has already forgotten that anything was ever missing.
