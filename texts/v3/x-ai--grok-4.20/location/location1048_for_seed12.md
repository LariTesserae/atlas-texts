The place has no true surface.

It is a warm, dim, submerged world suspended at impossible height, where the ocean has been lifted into the sky and the sky has forgotten it was ever anything but water. You drift inside a slow, heavy stratosphere-sea that stretches for hundreds of kilometers in every direction, neither cloud nor true ocean, but a single continuous body of lukewarm brine held aloft by forces that have not yet decided what shape the world should take. The water is thick with dissolved minerals and microscopic life; it tastes of iron and green leaves.

Everything here is still being born.

Great soft continents of living matter are only now differentiating themselves from the water. They rise like half-formed dreams—vast, sodden mats of vegetation kilometers across and only a few dozen meters thick, floating just beneath the upper skin of the sea-sky. Their edges fray constantly, shedding slow rains of rootlets, moss, and pale fungal strands that drift downward for hours before they are reclaimed by other mats lower down. These are not yet islands. They are scaffolds. Embryonic land still deciding whether it wants to become forest, reef, or city.

Upon and within these living rafts a primitive urbanity has appeared, handmade and densely layered. There are no metals here—only bone, shell, woven root, and the resinous sap of certain buoyant trees that have learned to harden like amber. The “cities” are stacked lattices of walkways, baskets, and membrane-walls suspended between colossal trunks and stalks that have not yet hardened into permanence. Every structure sways in the almost imperceptible currents. Nothing is finished; scaffolding merges with architecture, ladders become roots, roofs become flowerbeds. The entire settlement feels like a single organism that has taught itself to build scaffolding out of its own body.

Light arrives dim and blue-green, filtered through uncountable meters of water above. There is no sun, only a bruised, diffused glow that never quite becomes day or night. Occasionally a brighter pulse moves far overhead—perhaps the belly of some titanic translucent creature that has never been named, or perhaps the world itself breathing.

Fauna is rare and ancient. What animals exist move with the patience of geology. Great soft-bodied leviathans the color of wet slate drift between the floating continents, their bodies so draped in epiphytic plants that they look like mobile hillsides. Schools of pale, blind fish move in perfect synchrony like living curtains. Once every few weeks a migration of luminous jelly-things the size of houses passes through, shedding cold light and a sound like distant bells.

The soundscape is mostly quiet, almost holy. The dominant noise is the soft creak and sigh of living wood under tension, the constant low drizzle of water falling upward or sideways when the pressure gradients shift, and the low, patient scraping of bone tools shaping new struts. Speech, when it occurs, is conducted in near-whispers; the people here have learned that loud noises can destabilize a mat and send whole districts sliding into the deep.

The people themselves are few, small, and strange. Their bodies are elongated from generations of swimming between layers. Their skin is mottled with symbiotic algae that photosynthesize the dim light. They do not fight gravity so much as ignore it; they treat “up” and “down” as mild suggestions. Their technology is stone blades lashed to living branches, nets woven from their own hair, and a sophisticated understanding of how to coax the embryonic continents into growing in desired directions. They are midwives to the world.

Everything changes with glacial slowness. A new reef-city might take three human lifetimes to reach maturity. A mountain of living coral that will one day become a plateau is still only a soft pink mound the size of a cathedral, pulsing gently as it feeds. Geological time and biological time have folded together here; you can watch a landscape being born the way one might watch bread rise, if bread took two hundred years to rise and occasionally changed its mind about what it wanted to be.

This is a world still negotiating with itself.  
The water has not yet decided where the sky ends.  
The land has not yet decided where the ocean begins.  
And the city has not yet decided whether it is a parasite, a symbiote, or the next stage of the continent’s own evolution.

You drift between colossal stalks covered in dripping moss. A warm current moves you slowly past a half-built tower whose scaffolding is still putting out pale flowers. Somewhere far below, in the darker, colder layers, something vast and slow is rising—new stone, maybe, or new life, or both—pushing the entire world upward one patient millimeter at a time.

The place is not yet ready to be looked at directly.  
It is still becoming.  
And it is in no hurry.
