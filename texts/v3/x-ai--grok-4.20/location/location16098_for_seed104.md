The place has no surface.

What remains of the world is a single, endless column of warm water suspended between two impossible pressures: an abyssal depth below that never quite becomes crushing, and a ceiling of pale, bruised cloud so high it generates its own weather. This is not an ocean. It is the ocean after the ocean has won. The last dry land drowned long ago, and the planet kept sinking into itself until only this stratified, breathing sea remained.

You drift inside a cathedral without walls.

The water is dense, almost syrupy with dissolved minerals and microscopic life. It tastes of iron and old flowers. Currents move in slow, planetary arcs, so vast that a single gyre might take a human lifetime to complete. Temperature hovers at the edge of blood-warm, yet never quite the same from one layer to the next; invisible thermoclines slide past like membranes made of heat. Sometimes you pass through a vein of water twenty degrees cooler than the rest, and for a moment the world forgets you.

Vegetation has not died. It has simply changed allegiance. What was once forest is now a hanging jungle of colossal kelp and sea-lilies that rise from unseen abyssal roots anchored in the mantle itself. Their fronds are the color of oxidized copper and bruised mulberry. They grow in overlapping canopies thousands of meters thick, layered like the pages of a book no one will ever finish reading. Between the fronds float vast colonies of translucent anemones the size of cathedrals, pulsing faintly, filtering the dim light into violet and sullen gold.

Everything is ancient. The erosion parameter is not a measurement of rock worn down; it is a description of time that has eaten its own tail. These living towers have stood so long they have achieved geological patience. Their oldest stalks are encrusted with generations of coral that no longer remember the sun. The very chemistry of the water has settled into its final, stable equilibrium; nothing new is born here that has not been born a million times before.

Yet the place is not still.

Pressure sings. The silence is not absence of sound but the presence of one sound so low it registers in the bones rather than the ears: a planetary heartbeat, the groan of mantle and water and root all turning together. Every few hours a bloom of pale plankton detonates somewhere in the middle distance, turning an entire cubic kilometer into living light the color of moonlight on bone. The bloom lasts only minutes, then collapses into a rain of corpses that drifts downward for days.

Fauna is rare, but what exists is enormous and deliberate. You might drift for weeks without seeing anything larger than a fist-sized translucent shrimp. Then, without warning, a shadow the size of a mountain range slides past overhead — something with too many ribs and no eyes, moving with the calm certainty of a creature that has never needed to hurry. It does not hunt. It simply is. The ecosystem has reached the far side of predator and prey; everything now participates in one long, slow digestion of the world.

Scattered through the hanging kelp cathedrals are the only marks of anything that was once not this. A half-dissolved spire of black stone that might once have been a lighthouse. A cathedral bell, perfectly preserved, hanging upside-down in a thicket of sea-vines, tolling once every seven years when a particular current brushes it. A child’s marble, still bright blue, suspended in a column of motionless water like a planet that forgot to fall. These relics have become part of the reef. Life has grown around them so completely they no longer look artificial; they look like bones the world grew on purpose.

Light is a memory the water refuses to forget. It filters down from the bruised cloud-ceiling in exhausted shafts, dim and silvery, full of floating motes. Some of the colossal anemones have learned to concentrate this light inside their bodies and release it again in slow, mournful pulses — living lighthouses for no one. At certain depths the water itself begins to fluoresce, a cold green fire with no source. Reality is thin here. Sometimes you see two versions of the same kelp tower existing slightly out of phase, as though the world is trying to decide which memory to keep.

This is the final stable state.

The planet has become a single, warm, dim, teeming, silent organism. Its weather is the slow turning of currents older than continents. Its seasons are the great plankton blooms and die-offs. Its geography is vertical. Its only law is this: everything must drift, everything must layer, everything must touch without ever quite meeting.

And somewhere, very far above where the cloud ceiling becomes something closer to vapor and pressure, there may still be a last gasp of dry sky. But nothing that lives here looks up anymore. The sky drowned. The world kept going. And what remains is vast, patient, densely alive, and gently, ceaselessly, forgetting that it was ever anything else.
