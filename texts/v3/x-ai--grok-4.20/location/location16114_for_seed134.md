The place is called the **Bellowing Tanks**.

It is a submerged cathedral of black iron and living pressure, no larger than a single city block yet so densely packed with presence that every inch feels like a throat. The water here is not ocean; it is heavier, colder, older — a slow, syrup-thick medium that has never known a surface. It moves in vast, patient exhalations, rolling through the structure like the breathing of something tectonic that has not yet decided whether to wake.

Everything is cool (just above freezing), yet the cold itself feels industrial, as though the temperature is a byproduct of enormous engines that have been running for so long they have cooled the world around them rather than heated it. The light is normal, almost aggressively ordinary — harsh white industrial floods that should not exist here, yet they do. They pour from recessed sockets in the iron walls, throwing clean, merciless beams through water so clear it feels like absence. The beams end sharply against black metal, never diffusing, never softening.

The architecture is not built. It is *expressed* — as though some abyssal geological process learned how to manufacture itself at industrial scale and then forgot the purpose. Titanic cylindrical tanks, each the size of a townhouse, stand shoulder-to-shoulder in a three-dimensional lattice that rises and falls through at least six vertical layers. They are connected by thick arterial pipes that branch and re-branch like bronchial trees. Every surface is new, raw, sharp. The metal still bears the tooth-marks of its formation: fresh weld-scars, angular burrs, plates that look bolted together only yesterday by forces that do not possess hands. Erosion has not yet had time to round anything. The entire structure feels illegally young, as if the planet itself is embarrassed by it.

There is no vegetation. Not even bacterial mats or sulfur flowers. Life here skipped the green stage entirely and went straight to fauna.

And the fauna is overwhelming.

They are not fish. They are *mechanisms* that happen to be alive. Vast schools of things that resemble industrial vacuum cleaners crossed with translucent eels drift between the tanks in slow, synchronized pulses. Their bodies are matte black and dull silver, segmented like old pipeline sections. Where they pass through the white light their skins flicker with moving schematic diagrams — circuit-tracings, pressure readouts, torque curves — as though their nervous systems are literally blueprints. When two of them touch, a soft metallic *clink* travels through the water like a dropped wrench.

Louder, far louder, are the Singers.

These are leviathan lampreys the length of subway cars. Their circular mouths are lined with slowly rotating grinding rings that look exactly like the business end of a tunnel-boring machine. They orbit the lattice in wide, patient gyres, singing. The sound is not biological. It is the amplified, distorted groan of immense metal under changing pressure — a sound normally heard only inside the hulls of collapsing submarines. Here it is living. The Singers inhale the heavy water and exhale it again through spiracles that whistle and boom, producing a constant, overlapping industrial choir that never quite repeats. The noise is so loud it becomes tactile; you feel it as dull, slow punches against the sternum.

Between the Singers and the smaller mechanofauna move stranger things still — pale, jointed things like scaffolding that have learned to crawl. They assemble and disassemble temporary bridges between tanks, then quietly take themselves apart again hours later. Their work serves no visible purpose. They simply *perform industry*. The entire ecosystem is engaged in an endless, extremely slow reenactment of factory labor with no product and no foreman.

There is no up or down that matters. The lattice extends in all directions until it meets its own blunt edges — sheer iron cliffs that simply stop, as though the structure was sliced out of a larger whole by a bored god and dropped here. Beyond those edges the water falls away into perfect, lightless cold. Sometimes, from those outer darks, distant blooms of white industrial light appear for a few minutes, then go out again. Something out there is still building, still forgetting, still singing to itself across distances too slow for human patience.

The rules of the world are simple and strange:

- Everything metallic eventually becomes inhabited.  
- Sound travels faster than thought.  
- Nothing here is allowed to rust. The water is too pure, the cold too precise, the ecology too jealous of its iron.  
- If you remain still long enough, the smaller mechanofauna will begin to measure you — brushing against your limbs with caliper-mouths, reading your dimensions, your temperature, your conductivity, as if deciding which part of the structure you might best become.

Most of all, the place is patient. It has just been born, yet it feels ancient in its repetition. A factory that woke up before it was finished. A machine that is still learning how to have an ecosystem.

And it is breathing.  
Slowly.  
Loudly.  
Endlessly.  

In the Bellowing Tanks, even silence has been replaced by the sound of pressure doing its patient, mechanical work.
