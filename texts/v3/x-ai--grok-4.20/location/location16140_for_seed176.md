The place is called the **Weeping Sump**.

It is a single, self-contained cathedral-sized basin perched near the absolute summit of a young knife-edge ridge. The ridge itself rose from the earth only a few centuries ago; its rock is still so new that many surfaces glisten like wet obsidian and fracture with glassy, almost metallic edges. The basin is no more than two hundred paces across, yet its walls rise three hundred meters on every side, forming a perfect, steep-sided crucible. Nothing larger than a hamlet could ever fit here. Nothing smaller than a god could have carved it.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not fall as rain so much as it simply *arrives*: a slow, warm exhalation that beads on every surface, threads down black rock faces in constant silver braids, and collects in trembling lenses on leaves. The air itself is saturated; breath condenses the moment it leaves your mouth. From the basin’s floor rises a shallow, tea-colored lake no deeper than a man’s thigh, its surface never still. Warm updrafts push faint ripples outward in perfect, patient circles that take minutes to reach the edge. Every few hours a hidden spring somewhere in the walls gives a low, seismic belch and the water level rises three fingers, then settles again over the course of a day.

The vegetation is almost violent in its lushness. Because the basin is so small and the air so wet, every vertical surface has been claimed. Mosses thick as folded velvet drape the rock. Ferns the size of small trees erupt from crevices. Vines as thick as a forearm braid themselves into living nets that sag under their own weight and the weight of the water they carry. Orchids, bromeliads, and fleshy pink pitchers grow so densely they form a second, trembling ceiling ten meters above the ground. Their roots dangle like pale tentacles into the mist below. The green is so total it hurts the eyes; every shade from acid chartreuse to almost-black emerald exists in layered strata.

Yet the green is pierced through, everywhere, by industrial relics.

A ruined funicular track crawls up one wall like a broken spine, its cables long since snapped and hanging in rusting loops that drip water in steady metronome plinks. Half-buried in the lake lies the corroded flank of a single narrow-gauge locomotive, its boiler split open so that water lilies now grow from the firebox. On the eastern cliff, a cluster of six enormous piston engines—each the height of a house—stands half-swallowed by vines. They have not moved in decades, yet once every few weeks the warm, wet air finds its way into their ancient cylinders and they give a single, groaning *huff* in perfect unison. The sound is deafening in such a small space; it rolls around the basin like thunder trapped in a bottle. Then silence returns for another fortnight.

Fauna is rare. A few pale salamanders the color of bone slip between the engine blocks. High above, a single species of flightless rail—black, red-eyed, the size of a small dog—picks through the orchids with a curved beak that looks almost like a wrench. That is almost all. Life here prefers to remain vegetable and mineral.

Light arrives normally but strangely. Because the basin is so deep and narrow, direct sunlight only reaches the floor in a single slow-moving blade that crawls across the water from late morning until mid-afternoon. The rest of the day the space is lit by a diffuse, warm, pearl-colored glow that seems to emanate from the wet rock itself. At night the condensation catches starlight and turns the entire basin into a dim cathedral of trembling reflections.

The dominant sound is layered and loud: the constant soft roar of a thousand trickles and drips, the wet *shush* of leaves shedding water, the low metallic groaning of the engines as they slowly oxidize, and, beneath it all, the deep sub-audible pulse of water moving through unseen faults in the young rock. When the pistons fire their single coordinated breath, every other sound is briefly obliterated.

There is a rule here, though no one alive remembers who wrote it on the rusted plaque bolted to the locomotive’s boiler:

**What is dropped must be allowed to rust.**  
**What is planted must be allowed to drink.**

The basin obeys. Anything mechanical left here slowly becomes part of the green. Anything biological is permitted to grow until it touches the water or the sky, then it is gently drowned or starved by its neighbors. The process is so slow it can barely be seen, yet the change is relentless. In another two hundred years the six great engines will be completely buried beneath a living wall of moss and strangler figs, and the only sound will be the soft, patient weeping of the mountain itself.

Until then, the Weeping Sump remains a small, impossibly dense, warm, loud, and slowly dreaming machine-temple at the edge of heaven, where water, iron, and green things negotiate an endless, gradual truce.
