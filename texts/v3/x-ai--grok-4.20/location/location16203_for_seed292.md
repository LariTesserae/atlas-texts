The world is called the Thalassic Shelf.

It is not an ocean. It is a single, planetary-scale body of cold, oxygen-rich water that has swallowed everything down to a depth of roughly nine kilometers, yet never quite reaches a true abyssal plain. The floor is an ancient, almost perfectly flattened seabed that has been eroding for so long that it has achieved a kind of geological serenity; every conceivable irregularity has been sanded away by three billion years of current and pressure until only vast, subtle undulations remain, like the breathing of something that has already died.

The water itself is in permanent, violent motion.

Immense columns of upwelling and downwelling churn between the abyssal floor and the unseen surface far above. These columns are kilometers wide and spin with slow, house-sized vortices at their edges. The noise is constant and overwhelming: a deep, organ-like roar of water shearing against water, punctuated by the metallic clangs and hydraulic shrieks of colossal machines that still somehow function down here. The sound never drops below the level of a foundry in full cry; it is simply the baseline of existence.

Vegetation is not planted on the bottom. It hangs in the water column itself. Vast, kelp-like fronds the color of oxidized bronze and bruised indigo rise in layered curtains hundreds of meters tall, their roots anchored to the ancient seafloor in dense, braided mats. Between the fronds grow floating colonies of translucent, barrel-shaped creatures that function as both plant and animal; they photosynthesize with faint blue-green luminescence and filter-feed on the constant rain of organic particles. The result is a three-dimensional forest so saturated that in places you cannot see more than ten meters. Every cubic meter is claimed.

The temperature hovers around 4 °C, the cold that water reaches when it is densest. Your breath would freeze instantly if you tried to surface; here it simply condenses into milky clouds that are immediately torn apart by the turbulence.

Light arrives from two sources. First, a cold, even illumination filtering down from the sky-ocean far above, filtered through so many kilometers of water and suspended life that it arrives as a uniform, sourceless silver. Second, the machines.

They are everywhere, but never clustered. A single half-sunken foundry the size of a small city might appear once every forty kilometers, its rust-red walls encrusted with generations of bivalves and tubeworms. Elsewhere you find lone smokestacks rising from the plain like iron redwoods, still coughing sluggish pillars of superheated brine. Abandoned drone rigs drift in slow circles, tethered by cables thick as ancient redwoods, their floodlights cutting pale tunnels through the kelp. All of it is industrial, heavy, nineteenth-century in aesthetic but built at a scale that suggests an entire civilization once tried to industrialize the seabed and then simply walked away, or drowned.

Fauna is absurdly abundant. Schools of armored fish the size of buses move like living iron curtains between the kelp pillars. Clouds of shrimp no larger than rice grains perform synchronized dances that look like silver blizzards. Occasionally a pale, cathedral-sized whale-thing glides past, its bellow so low it vibrates your bones more than your ears. Everything is loud. The snapping of pistol shrimp sounds like rifle fire. The hunting clicks of toothed whales arrive like hammer blows. The entire world sounds as though it is being dismantled and rebuilt at the same time.

Elevation is extreme in the vertical sense only. The seafloor itself is almost table-flat, worn down to its final geological form. Yet because the water column is so deep, and because the hanging forests create false “horizons” every few hundred meters, the sense of verticality is crushing. You can fall for twenty minutes and still not reach bottom. You can rise for an hour and still not breach the surface that may or may not still exist.

Scattered across the plain are the marks of whoever came before. A half-buried locomotive overgrown with tube worms. A cathedral-sized pressure door standing open on a cliff that was never a cliff. A single cast-iron bench sitting perfectly upright on a perfectly flat plain, as though someone just stood up and left it there three hundred million years ago. These artifacts never cluster. They appear like thoughts a dying mind forgot to finish.

The dominant feeling is not mystery. It is saturation.

Everything is too much. Too much life, too much noise, too much cold, too much pressure, too much rust, too much time. The world has reached its oldest stable state and that state is loud, turbulent, overcrowded, and still running the engines someone forgot to turn off.

This is not post-flood.  
This is the flood’s final, exhausted equilibrium: a cold, roaring, metallic kelp forest that fills the sky and the ground at once, where the last machines of a forgotten industrial age keep clanging away beneath an ocean that has already won.
