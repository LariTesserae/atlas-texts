The place has no name because nothing here has ever needed one.

It stretches so far that its own curvature is visible on the horizon, a single continental shelf of warm, fleshy stone that has never known rain. Where water should be there is only absence: a dry, mineral hunger that has shaped everything. The ground itself is a living crust, pale ochre and rose and bone-white, riddled with pores and fissures like enormous skin. It breathes. Slowly. Warmly.

From these pores the vegetation emerges in impossible density. It is not planted; it is extruded. Thick, succulent cables of deep green and oxblood push upward in tangled billions, forming a single continuous mat that covers the land from one edge of sight to the other. Their surfaces are glossy, almost sweaty. They pulse. Some are thick as ancient redwoods laid on their sides; others are delicate as veins. All are braided together so tightly that there is no soil visible, only this vegetal architecture rising from the breathing stone. They have no leaves in the ordinary sense. Instead, translucent fronds the color of young liver open and close like gills, drinking light and the faint chemical breath that rises from below.

The entire landscape is still in the act of forming. Nothing is finished. Towering ridges that will one day become mountain ranges are only half-extruded, frozen mid-birth like vast ribs pushing through the crust. Between them lie embryonic valleys still wrinkled with the folds of their own creation. New stalks of the vegetation burst through the stone even as you watch, splitting the rock with soft wet sounds, growing meters in a single hour. The ground is littered with the husks of yesterday’s growth—shriveled tubes already being reabsorbed by the mat. Everything is scaffold and meat at once.

It is warm. A thick, mammalian warmth that feels generated from below rather than above. The air smells of iron, chlorophyll, and something like breast milk left too long in the sun.

There are cities here, but they are not built. They have grown.

Immense organic lattices rise in dense clusters—some reaching hundreds of meters—formed by the vegetation itself learning to knot and reinforce. These living towers are pierced by spiraling galleries, bridges, and chambers where the stalks have parted and hardened into smooth floors and walls the color of tendon. The “streets” are narrow channels between colossal braided trunks, so packed with growth that the sky is reduced to jagged slits of normal daylight far overhead. Every surface crawls with slow, deliberate motion: tendrils thickening, pores opening, new buds forming into tomorrow’s architecture. The cities are embryonic metropolises that will never stop being born.

Fauna throngs inside this living infrastructure in absolute silence.

There are no footsteps, no wingbeats, no voices. Only the faint rustle of flesh against flesh. Vast herds of pale, soft-bodied creatures the size of horses move like blood cells through arterial passages in the vegetation. Their skins are translucent; you can watch their dark organs working. They feed by pressing flat mouths against the stalks and drinking the sweet sap that wells up in response. Above them, in the woven canopy, things that are mostly neck and eye drift between beams of light, filtering airborne spores. Smaller creatures—some no larger than a hand—scuttle through the microscopic gaps between braided stalks, cleaning, pruning, seeding.

Everything eats everything else in a slow, courteous rhythm. A predator the color of wet clay will envelop its prey like a living cloak; the prey relaxes, sighs, and is gently dissolved over the course of days. There is no screaming. The only sounds are low biological noises: the wet pop of a new stalk breaking through stone, the soft creak of living buttresses taking new weight, the rhythmic sigh of millions of gill-fronds breathing in unison.

And beneath it all, the stone itself is humming at a frequency just below hearing. It is not rock. It is the placenta and the infant in one body—still growing the world that will eventually stand upon it. The vegetation is its nervous system. The cities are its thoughts solidifying. The animals are the antibodies and messengers keeping the newborn continent alive while it dreams itself into permanence.

You can walk for weeks and never find an edge. Every direction simply continues: more breathing stone, more braided towers, more silent life unfolding in warm, packed density beneath ordinary sunlight.

This is not a planet that has finished forming.

This is a planet that is still deciding what kind of life it wants to be when it grows up, and it is doing so all at once, everywhere, in silence.
