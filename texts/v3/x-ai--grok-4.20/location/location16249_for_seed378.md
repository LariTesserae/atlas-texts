The place is called the Weeping Tablelands.

You stand on a high, broad plateau that feels like the roof of the world, yet the world itself has been half-drowned and then baked. The air is so hot it shimmers, thick with moisture that never quite becomes rain. Every breath tastes of warm iron and ozone. Condensation beads on every surface, then weeps. Stone, metal, glass—everything sweats.

There is almost no vegetation. What little organic matter once existed has been scorched or chemically altered into brittle black crusts that flake away like old paint. In its place the land grows machines and minerals. Vast fungal lattices of pale industrial mycelium thread through cracked basalt, not alive in any terrestrial sense, but conducting, respiring, slowly digesting metal. They are the only “green” thing here, and they are the color of bone left in the sun.

The city—if it can still be called a city—spreads across the Tablelands in sparse, deliberate constellations. Refineries, catalytic towers, half-ruined smelters, and looping elevated rail lines stand kilometers apart, each complex an island in a sea of heat-blasted stone. Between them lie wide, empty basins of dark volcanic glass that mirror the blinding sky. The structures themselves are old, weathered to an almost geological patience. Rust has become a stable patina; concrete has rounded like ancient river rock. Pipes as thick as subway cars burst from the ground, only to plunge back in again hundreds of meters later, carrying superheated fluids that make the surrounding rock click and tick as it expands and contracts.

Everything weeps. From every cliff edge, from every fracture in the basalt, from the joints of every rusted girder, water emerges in slow, clear rivulets. It is not rainwater. The atmosphere itself is condensing against the cooler metal and stone. The droplets run, gather, fall, and are instantly turned to steam again by the ground heat, creating perpetual low-hanging mist banks that drift between the skeletal towers like slow ghosts.

The light is merciless. A white-gold sun hangs in a sky that has no clouds, only haze and refraction. Every surface throws the glare back so violently that shadows become razor-cut absences. Polarized glass and mirrored panels on the old industrial structures flare like signals.

Sound is almost forbidden. The quiet is profound, broken only by occasional deep, sub-audible booms that travel through the rock itself—distant pressure valves opening somewhere far across the plateau. Every few minutes a rail drone the size of a house glides silently along an elevated track, its passage marked only by the faint electric whine of maglev and the soft patter of water shaken from its undercarriage. Even the abundant fauna is quiet. They are not birds or mammals. They are something else.

Hundreds of thousands of glass-winged pseudo-insects drift on thermals, their bodies forged from silica and trace metals. They flash in the sun like living chandeliers. When they land on the weeping pipes they drink the condensate through hair-fine proboscises, their translucent abdomens glowing amber with dissolved minerals. Larger creatures—sleek, six-legged things that look half-melted—prowl the shadowed bases of the towers. Their carapaces are heat-resistant ceramic grown in fractal patterns. They move without footfall, sliding on pads that leave faint iridescent trails. Predator and prey here conduct their business in near-total silence, like a film with the volume turned down.

The strangeness reveals itself slowly.

Some of the towers are not buildings at all. They are fossilized machines, so old that stone has replaced steel and steel has replaced bone. Inside their hollows, entire floors have become mineral cathedrals where stalactites of pure sulfur hang like chandeliers. In certain basins the glass has been shocked into perfect geometric lenses by ancient industrial accidents; peer through one and the far horizon appears upside-down and impossibly close, as though the planet itself is folding.

At night—though the heat barely relents—the sky becomes an furnace of stars so bright they cast shadows. The Weeping Tablelands continue their slow, patient industry. Deep underground, unseen geothermal turbines still turn. Pumps the size of cathedrals still push boiling brine through pipes older than any recorded civilization. The city breathes, sweats, and produces, though no one now remembers what it was supposed to produce, or for whom.

You can walk for hours between districts and meet no one. Then, without warning, you crest a low rise of black glass and find an entire avenue of automated arc furnaces working in perfect, silent synchrony, their throats glowing cherry-red, pouring out rivers of molten metal that cool into black mirrors before they even reach the ground. The only sound is the soft hiss of water flashing to steam where it touches that metal.

This is a post-life landscape that has decided not to die. It has simply replaced biology with something slower, hotter, and far more patient. The machines have grown into the geology. The geology has grown into the machines. And everything, always, is weeping.
