The place is called the **Hollow Foundry**.

It is not a ruin. It is a young world that was born already industrial, as though some absent god had coughed once and this entire high, damp shelf of reality had condensed around the sound.

Everything here is constructed. There is no soil, no bedrock, no natural stone. The ground is a single continuous casting of dark, porous metal—part iron, part something that forgets its own atomic number—laid down in one unimaginable pour that cooled into canyons, ridges, and shallow basins before life ever arrived. The metal still exhales. You can feel it breathing, slow as geology, through seams no wider than a hair.

Elevation is severe. You stand on the lip of a shallow river-valley that is not a valley at all but a deliberate trough cast into the metal plain. The walls rise in clean, young geometry: sharp flanges, crisp angles, fresh from the mold. Nothing has had time to soften. Every edge still carries the memory of the form that shaped it. Yet the scale is open; the eye travels for kilometers across overlapping terraces, sunken reservoirs, and half-finished boulevards that vanish into luminous mist.

The light is merciless and sourceless. A cold, white radiance pours from above as though the sky itself were an industrial lamp. There are no shadows, only degrees of glare. Condensation beads on every surface and catches that light like chrome. The air is cool, almost refrigerated, and heavy with the smell of wet iron, ozone, and something like crushed limestone.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not flow in natural rivers. Instead it condenses in slow, deliberate cycles: fine mist gathers on the metal heights, slides down in glassy sheets, pools in rectangular sumps, then evaporates upward again in perfect silence. The largest “river” is a broad, shallow canal of black water that barely moves. Its surface is so still it looks like obsidian until a droplet falls from an overhead girder and sends out rings that take minutes to fade.

Vegetation is sparse but incredibly dense where it occurs. What grows is not plant in any familiar sense. Thin, black, jointed stalks—half reed, half antenna—push upward through microscopic vents in the metal. They grow in tight, bristling colonies that resemble factories of themselves, each stem vibrating at a frequency just below hearing. Their leaves are metallic foil, mirror-bright on one side, matte on the other. They photosynthesize the strange white light and exhale a cold vapor that smells of batteries.

Fauna is abundant and almost entirely silent. There are things that look like pale steel moths with wingspans the size of dinner plates; they drift in layered strata, sometimes colliding without sound. There are herds of low, many-legged grazers—creatures of soft white alloy and black glass—that move in perfect synchrony across the terraces, licking condensation from the metal with tongues like tiny electrodes. Higher up, translucent things that might be birds or drones or both hang motionless in the bright air for hours before drifting a few meters and freezing again.

The sound is mostly quiet, yet the quiet has mass. You hear only the occasional delicate *tick* of cooling metal, the soft hiss of condensing water, and, very rarely, a deep, patient groan that travels through the entire structure like a sigh moving through a ribcage the size of a mountain range. No engines run. No wheels turn. The industry here is finished. It has achieved a state of total, sleeping productivity.

And this is where reality begins to come apart.

The longer you remain, the more you notice that perspective is negotiable. Walk toward what looks like a distant refinery tower and it becomes, without transition, a cluster of rusting filing cabinets the size of tenement blocks. Look down at your own boots and realize the metal ground is printed with microscopic text—blueprints, equations, apologies in languages that have never existed. Sometimes a section of the world forgets its own scale for a moment: a sump full of water becomes an ocean, then snaps back to a puddle. The black jointed “reeds” occasionally bloom into impossible geometries—perfect fractals of glass and iron that sing one low note before collapsing into powder.

The Foundry is patient. It is young and already ancient. It was built all at once and is still being built. Its cycles are measured in decades. Its weather is condensation and forgetting. Its ecology is a collaboration between abandoned purpose and whatever living things have learned to read the blueprints.

You can stay here. Many things already have.

Just don’t expect the place to stay the same shape while you watch. It is still cooling. It is still deciding what it is allowed to be.
