The place is called the Shattering Veil.

It is a high, bright, and mercilessly loud world that has never been young. Every surface here has the settled, almost smug patience of stone that has outlived continents. The elevation is punishing—thin air, blue-white light—yet the atmosphere itself is heavy with moisture. Water does not fall here so much as it is *always present*: it condenses on every cold edge, beads along fracture lines, and drips in slow, metronomic strings from overhangs that have been weeping for ten thousand years without ever running dry. The sound of that endless dripping is not delicate. It is a constant, overlapping percussion—thousands of separate trickles striking stone at different pitches—amplified by the strange acoustics of the place until the entire region roars like a stone cathedral filled with shattering glass.

There is no soil. There are no plants. Not a blade of moss, not a lichen, not even the expected high-altitude crusts. The ground is a riot of fractured mineral and volcanic glass, packed so densely that there is almost no flat place larger than a handspan. Razor ridges of obsidian interlock with pale granite and milky quartz like the teeth of two mountain ranges that have been slammed together and then frozen mid-bite. Every crevice is filled with smaller shards; every shard is coated with a thin, ever-renewing skin of water that catches the light and throws it back in blinding sheets. The brightness is overwhelming. The sky itself is a searing, cloudless cyan that seems to press down rather than float above.

Movement is almost impossible. The density is saturating. You cannot take three steps without having to twist your entire body between blades of stone that rise higher than your head. Yet the landscape has achieved a perfect, time-invariant equilibrium. Nothing erodes further. Nothing grows. The water condenses, drips, collects in trembling mirrored pools no wider than a palm, then evaporates upward again in slow spirals that never quite become mist. The cycle is closed, eternal, and deafening.

The only living things are rare. Every few hundred meters you might encounter a single translucent creature the size of a dog, something between a crystal shrimp and a mantis made of frozen fog. They move with agonizing slowness, licking condensation from the rocks with tongues of flexible quartz. Their bodies ring like bells when they strike one another—which they do, rarely, in slow-motion territorial disputes that can last days. Their exoskeletons are so clear that you can watch their pale organs working inside them like tiny galaxies. When the sun hits them at the right angle they vanish entirely, becoming only a faint refractive distortion in the air.

The weirdness is subtle but cumulative. Gravity feels slightly wrong in certain pockets, as though the stone itself is gently exhaling. Echoes arrive before the sounds that made them. Shadows sometimes fall upward for a moment, then remember themselves. Most unnerving of all is the sense that the entire place is *listening*—not with ears, but with the million wet, reflective surfaces that turn every noise into a thousand answering noises. The roar of dripping water is not random. After long exposure you begin to hear, buried inside the chaos, something like a chord. It never resolves. It never changes. It has been singing the same unresolved harmony since before anything alive remembers.

There are no ruins here. No tools, no footprints that last longer than the next condensation cycle. The Shattering Veil tolerates no history except its own. It is packed, bright, cool, and violently loud, yet perfectly still. A mineral scream held in equilibrium for so long that the scream itself has become the landscape.

If you stand motionless between two blades of wet obsidian and let the water run down your neck, you can feel the place deciding whether or not to notice you. Most of the time it decides you are simply another temporary wet surface, no different from the rocks.

And the dripping never stops.  
It never has.  
It never will.
