The place has no name because nothing here lasts long enough to need one.

You stand inside a colossal subterranean cathedral of living meat and fiber. The ceiling is lost somewhere far above, hidden behind overlapping layers of colossal leaves the color of raw liver and oxidized copper. Every surface is plant, yet none of it is still. The vegetation does not grow so much as it *seethes*. Great ribbed stems thicker than redwoods surge upward only to split, rupture, and collapse back into the writhing mass moments later. Vines as thick as your torso lash like whips, slapping wetly against one another. Fleshy pads bloom, rot, and are immediately reabsorbed by neighboring tissues in a continuous, obscene metabolism.

There is almost no water. What little moisture exists is thick, syrupy, and alkaline, sweated directly from the pores of the megaplants themselves. It beads on waxy surfaces, runs in slow amber rivulets, and evaporates almost at once in the warm, mineral-scented air. The chemistry here is not aqueous but saccharine-fermentative; everything smells of overripe fruit, hot compost, and something like crushed ants.

The heat is oppressive but not scorching—more like the inside of a living body. A constant 34 °C that makes the air feel fleshy. You can taste it on the back of your tongue.

Sound is violence. The roar is deafening and never constant. It is a layered, wet cacophony: the wet tearing of new stems punching through older ones, the bass drumming of colossal seed pods exploding open, the high metallic shrieking of millions of insectoid fauna scraping, sawing, and singing in unison. Every few minutes a new tone emerges as some fresh structure achieves resonance and begins to howl like a broken pipe organ before it is overgrown and silenced. The noise is so total it becomes architectural; you feel it in your teeth, your sinuses, the marrow of your bones.

Fauna is overwhelming in the most literal sense. There are no discrete animals here. Instead the vegetation itself is crawling, twitching, and *wearing* life. Legions of translucent, crab-like creatures the size of dinner plates scuttle across every surface, their bodies made of the same fibrous matter as the plants. Larger things—some like six-legged sloths stitched together from bark and wet membrane, others like vast jellyfish made of petals—drift through the vertical shafts between trunks. They are born, mate, fight, and dissolve back into the substrate within hours. Nothing is permanent. The entire ecology is one continuous act of self-cannibalism and resurrection.

The terrain is still being born. You are walking across what might one day be called ground, but right now it is only a slowly rising crust of compacted root felt and calcified sap. In places it splits open and new pale shoots erupt like fingers from a grave. Whole cliffs of living tissue are currently forming to your left; you can see them swelling, vein by vein, as fluids pump beneath translucent skins. Elsewhere the floor simply gives way into soft pits of warm mulch that sigh and close behind you. Nothing is finished. The world is a half-born fetus the size of a mountain range, still twitching in its womb of stone.

Scattered through this churning green-and-crimson hell are the only signs of anything that might be called “built.” Primitive things. A skeletal tower of lashed bone-vines and chitin plates standing at a crooked angle, half swallowed already. A ring of standing stones carved with spiraling glyphs that the plants have not yet dared to overgrow. A single low dome of fired clay, its surface covered in hundreds of handprints pressed into the surface before it hardened. Whoever made these marks is long gone—either absorbed or simply out-evolved. The things feel like messages left by previous versions of intelligence that the ecosystem has already forgotten.

Light comes from above in dusty, cathedral shafts. It is not sunlight. The roof of the cavern, kilometers up, is encrusted with colonies of bioluminescent lichen and strange glowing fruiting bodies that pulse in slow, hypnotic rhythms. The light they cast is the color of old brass and dried blood. It moves when the plants move, so the entire chamber seems to breathe.

Weirdness is not an exception here; it is the substrate. Every few minutes the logic of the place changes. You round what you think is a hill only to realize it is a lung. A vast sac the size of a cathedral nave inflates, then exhales a cloud of golden spores that scream as they disperse. A nearby thicket of what looked like bamboo suddenly blooms into hundreds of perfectly geometric crystal flowers, rings like bells for nine seconds, then melts back into sap. The ecosystem is not trying to kill you. It is not even aware of you. You are simply one more stimulus in an endless argument the world is having with itself.

This is not a forest. It is not even a single organism. It is a geological event that happens to be alive, still in the furious process of deciding what shape it will take when it finishes being born. And it shows no sign of ever finishing.

You can walk for days and the roar never lessens. The heat never breaks. New species will have invented and discarded themselves by the time you stop to rest. The only permanent thing here is turbulence.

Welcome to the Everbirth.  
Try not to become architecture.
