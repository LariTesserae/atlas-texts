The place has no name because nothing here has ever needed one.

It is called, by those few who have drifted through its margins, the **Abyssal Atrium**.

You stand (if standing is still the word) inside an endless, flooded cathedral that has forgotten it was ever a cathedral. The water is not merely deep; it *is* the architecture. A cold, dark, heavy medium—thicker than water should be—presses against every surface like living stone. It is not black. It is the color of obsidian seen through smoke. Currents move through it in slow, deliberate spirals that feel architectural, as though the ocean itself had learned geometry and then gone senile with age.

Everything is constructed.  
Everything is ancient.

Vast lattices of unknown origin rise and fall through the liquid dark: rib-like buttresses kilometers long, fused rings the size of moons, spiraling colonnades that corkscrew upward until they vanish into dimness. They are made of something that is neither metal nor stone nor crystal, yet partakes of all three. Its surface is covered in a fine, velvet-like patina of ultra-slow corrosion that has been accumulating since before the concept of “before” had meaning. Every edge is rounded by epochs of patient dissolution. Nothing is sharp anymore. The entire world has been worn into the soft, enormous curves of a tooth that has been chewing itself for a billion years.

There is no sky.  
There is no seabed.

Instead there is height and depth that refuse to resolve. You can fall for hours in any direction and still not be certain whether you are rising or descending. Pressure changes in unpredictable pulses, sometimes squeezing so tightly the water feels like iron armor, sometimes loosening until you feel you might evaporate into it. The cold is constant and surgical; it does not bite. It simply *occupies*.

Vegetation is absent in any form that biology would recognize. In its place, the structures themselves sometimes extrude slow, glassy filaments—milky skeins that drift like kelp made of frozen fog. These are not alive. They are more like the dreams that architecture has when it has been abandoned by time. Occasionally one of the filaments will fracture and release a brief constellation of pale motes that swirl for a few moments before winking out. No one knows what they are.

The only living things are the fauna, and they are abundant in the way that thoughts are abundant inside a fever.

They are barely creatures. Most are translucent sacks of fluid sculpted into baroque, impossible symmetries—things that look like cathedrals trying to become jellyfish, or jellyfish that have decided to cosplay as forgotten gods. Some drift in vast, silent schools that wheel and fold like living origami. Others cling to the ancient lattices in slow, pulsing colonies, their bodies glowing with a cold bioluminescence so weak it barely deserves the name. Their light is the color of distant, dying stars. When they move they do so with the languid turbulence of ink dropped into heavier ink.

Everything here is in turbulent motion, yet the motion is ancient and grave. Currents collide and birth temporary vortices that last for centuries. entire colonnades of the megastructure will suddenly groan, twist, and fold into new geometries with the terrible patience of continental drift. When two vast rings grate against one another, the sound is so low it is felt rather than heard—a subsonic heartbeat that vibrates every cell of your body for days afterward. Then silence returns, deeper than before.

The Atrium is not dying.  
It is not being born.

It is in the settled, final form of something that has been chaotically becoming for so long that becoming and being have achieved equilibrium. A perpetual storm that has calcified into liturgy.

Light arrives only as rumor. Dim, sourceless phosphorescence leaks from the structures themselves, or from the bodies of the largest fauna—colossal, cathedral-sized beings that glide through the upper vaults like migrating continents. Their passage stirs pressure fronts that race ahead of them for hundreds of kilometers, announcing their coming long before their faint silhouettes appear.

There are rules here, though they are felt rather than known:

- All straight lines have long since surrendered.  
- Sound travels farther than sight.  
- Memory does not persist; everything forgets itself every few hours and must be reinvented by the water.  
- If you stay long enough, the architecture will begin to imitate the shape of your thoughts. This is considered impolite, but inevitable.

This is not an ocean.  
This is the inside of a mind that has outlived its thinker and continues, with solemn, turbulent dignity, to dream in the only language it still remembers: architecture, pressure, cold, and the slow, baroque turning of impossible creatures through liquid cathedrals older than light.
