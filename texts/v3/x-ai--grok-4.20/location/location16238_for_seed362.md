The world is called the Verdant Lattice.

There is no water here. Not a drop has existed for geological ages. What looks at first like pale sand or salt is only the desiccated, powdered residue of ancient cellulose that has been ground finer than talc by ten thousand years of slow mechanical abrasion. The air itself is dry enough to make your tongue feel like paper.

Yet the entire planet is alive.

A single, immense, self-replicating plant structure has consumed every mineral, every metal, every former ocean basin. It is not forest. It is not even jungle. It is substrate, architecture, and ecology fused into one continuous organism. The ground, the walls, the hills, the towers, the bridges, the ceilings of the world — all are living, fibrous, woody tissue. Trunks thicker than city blocks rise and intertwine into vaulted canopies that support further trunks. Branches serve as elevated roads. Leaves the size of theatre curtains hang in motionless tiers, filtering the searing white light that pours from a sky that is itself a membrane of translucent photosynthetic skin stretched between orbital-scale ribs.

Everything is built. Nothing is natural anymore. The Lattice learned long ago to imitate, then to improve upon, the forms that once existed. It grew its own factories. It extruded its own engines. Vast cathedral-naves of pale green xylem house ranks of titanic looms that have never stopped weaving new vascular tissue. Gear-like lignified rings, kilometres across, turn inside one another with the patience of glaciers, powering entire continents of secondary growth. Conveyor systems of hollowed vines ferry nutrients and spores along continent-spanning arcs. Refineries of hardened heartwood distill exotic non-aqueous solvents from the plant’s own resins. The entire world is a single industrial organism that has reached steady-state maturity.

The temperature is cool, almost cold, despite the blinding brightness. The light comes not from a sun but from the sky-dome itself — a perpetual, sourceless, actinic glare that has no cycle. Every surface reflects it with a waxy sheen, so that the world feels both overexposed and somehow clinical. Shadows are rare and razor-edged.

Elevation is low. The topography never rises more than a few hundred metres above the mean datum, yet because the vegetation is so total and so dense, the horizon is always close and tangled. You walk through endless colonnades of living pillars. You climb spiralling ramps grown inside hollow stems. You pass beneath ribbed vaults where the roof itself is breathing. Every cubic metre is saturated with structure. Nothing is empty. The air itself feels crowded.

It is quiet.

Engines the size of mountains turn in near-silence, muffled by layer upon layer of insulating bark. The only regular sounds are soft biological ones: the faint creak of wood fibres adjusting tension, the whisper of wind through ten trillion stomata, the occasional low subsonic heartbeat as a continent-wide pulse of sap moves through pressure gradients. Sometimes you hear the dry clicking of spore-drones launching from launching pods. That is all.

Fauna is rare. What little animal life remains has been absorbed, simplified, or repurposed. A few species of translucent, bark-coloured insects the size of your hand move in slow, deliberate parades along designated vascular highways. They are more maintenance workers than creatures. Occasionally a vast, sluggish megafauna — something between a sloth and a dirigible — drifts between the upper canopies, its body entirely clothed in epiphytic growth, its metabolism so slow that generations of lichen live and die on its hide before it completes a single circuit of the planet.

The weirdness is not in any single object. It is in the realisation that the physics here is no longer entirely trustworthy.

Distance behaves strangely. Walk long enough in what appears to be a straight corridor of repeating arches and you will find you have circumnavigated the globe without ever changing elevation. Gravity feels variable; some vertical shafts seem to require climbing, others feel like gentle descents no matter which way you face. Certain vast chambers alter the colour of your thoughts. Stand inside the Cathedral of the Ninth Cambium too long and you will begin to remember memories that belong to the plant itself — the taste of magnesium-rich regolith from a continent that died forty million years ago, the satisfaction of sealing a tectonic wound with fresh lignin.

The Lattice is in balance, but it is not still. It is dynamically, industrially, biologically active on every scale at once. Spores drift in slow storms that function as weather. Entire biomes of new growth are harvested and replaced in cycles that last centuries. When a fault line opens in the crust, the plant seals it with continent-scale callusing and grows new power-veins through the wound. Everything is being digested, reprocessed, extruded, reinforced, culled, and reborn in overlapping, patient timescales that make human urgency feel like a nervous tic.

There is no outside.

The sky is plant. The ground is plant. The air is laced with volatile organic signals that function as both language and weather. The only law is continuation. The only morality is structural integrity. The only future is further growth, further refinement, further quiet industry, beneath a white light that never dims, across a living world that has forgotten it was ever anything else.
