The place is called the Underloom.

It is a low, cathedral-sized hollow beneath a long-collapsed roof of black volcanic glass. The ceiling, what remains of it, hangs only thirty meters above the floor in fractured plates that weep steadily; each droplet falls with a soft, clockwork *plick* that never quite synchronizes. The air itself is saturated, cool, and heavy with condensation. Mist condenses on every surface then slides in slow silver sheets down pillars, down walls, down the flanks of half-buried machines. Nothing here is dry for longer than a breath.

There is almost no vegetation. What little grows is not plant life. Pale, vein-like lattices of luminous fungus stretch across wet stone and rusted iron in delicate, nervous geometries, pulsing faintly in time with something that might be a distant heartbeat or a failing generator. Their light is the color of moonlight on bone and gives no warmth.

Everything else is mineral, metal, and memory.

The floor is a dense confusion of settled ruin. Old industrial mechanisms have sunk into one another over centuries of gentle erosion until they have become a single topographic body: boiler chests fused with cooling fins, conveyor spines braided into arched buttresses, tangles of corroded pipework that have grown barnacles of iron oxide and quartz. Nothing stands alone. Every object touches at least three others. The density is almost claustrophobic; you cannot walk ten paces without brushing cold, damp steel or glass. Yet the forms have achieved a kind of weathered equilibrium, an established melancholy. The place has been dying for so long that dying has become its stable state.

Scattered among the fused machinery are the built remnants: a single watchman’s shack of riveted plate, listing drunkenly on three legs; a half-collapsed gantry that still carries one lonely electric lamp whose filament has not burned in living memory; a row of six ceramic insulators the height of a man, now used as nesting pillars by something else.

The fauna is abundant and strange.

Pale, translucent creatures the size of cats move between the dripping surfaces on jointed glass legs. Their bodies are mostly water held together by skeins of pale fungus; when they pause, you can see their slow internal currents. They click softly to one another in frequencies just below human hearing. In the deeper folds of the ruin live the Choir — fist-sized beetles whose wing casings are perfect parabolic mirrors. They do not fly. Instead they crawl in slow, deliberate circuits, reflecting and concentrating the scant ambient light into bright pinpricks that dance across the wet walls like will-o’-the-wisps. At irregular intervals the Choir synchronize and emit a single, ringing note that travels through the metal bones of the place and makes every droplet tremble at once. Then the Underloom falls back into its mostly-quiet murmuring: the *plick* of falling water, the low creak of cooling iron, the wet sigh of mist moving through broken ducts.

The air smells of ozone, cold stone, and something faintly like crushed almonds.

Light is dim but never absent. It comes from the fungal veins, from the mirrored beetles, from occasional phosphorescent seepage that leaks out of fractured conduits like slow green blood. Shadows pool thickly between the layered wreckage, yet nothing is truly dark; every surface glistens and reflects.

The world here is active in slow, patient cycles. Water condenses, falls, pools, evaporates into mist, condenses again. The glass-legged things migrate along invisible moisture gradients. The Choir circuit their ancient routes. Every few hours a deep subterranean valve, still functioning after all this time, opens with a bronchial sigh and releases a new gout of lukewarm water somewhere in the dark. The entire hollow breathes.

There are rules, though they are not written.

Nothing here may be taken out dry.  
Anything removed will begin to rust or bloom with fungus within hours of leaving the saturated air.  
The Choir must never be touched; if one is crushed the rest will gather and sing a different note, and the ceiling will answer by shedding one of its remaining glass plates.  
The watchman’s shack must be left exactly as found. Its door is always open. Something still sits inside, but only in peripheral vision.

Most of all, the Underloom does not like silence. When the dripping slows or the Choir fall quiet, the place begins to manufacture new sound: a soft metallic groaning that rises until the water falls faster and the beetles resume their mirrored procession.

It has been maintaining this balance for a very long time.

You are not the first to find it.  
You will not be the last.  
The weeping never stops.
