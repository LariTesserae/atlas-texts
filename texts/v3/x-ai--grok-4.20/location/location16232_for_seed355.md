The place has no name anymore. Names require memory, and memory here curdles.

It is a low, wide basin that once might have been a coastal plain or the floor of an ancient lake. Now it is half-drowned and half-baked under a heat that feels geological. The air itself sweats. Moisture condenses on nothing and falls upward in slow, trembling beads that drift like mercury before they burst against the ground. Every surface weeps. Stone, mud, the few remaining stalks of vegetation — all of them bleed slow threads of water that never quite reach the same destination twice.

The city did not grow here. It arrived, long ago, and then the world forgot how to stay consistent.

Ruins of what must have been a substantial urban complex spread across kilometres in every direction, yet “ruins” is not quite right. The structures have settled into the wet ground as though they were always meant to be half-submerged, half-melted. Towers lean at impossible angles without falling. Streets ripple like ligaments. Entire districts have been folded into themselves the way wet paper folds, creating layered pockets where yesterday’s alley is tomorrow’s ceiling. The masonry is not crumbling; it is *recomposing*. Bricks sweat and slide past one another with the lazy patience of deep-sea creatures. Concrete exhales warm mist that smells of iron and lilies.

There is almost no vegetation. What little exists looks like it is trying to leave. Sparse clusters of pale, jointed reeds grow in the wettest seams between buildings, but their leaves point downward, as if gravity is a suggestion they have politely declined. Their roots dangle in mid-air above black ponds that have no bottoms.

Light is a memory the world keeps forgetting to honour. A thin, bruised glow leaks from the sky — not day, not night, but the colour of an old bruise — yet it never quite arrives. Most of the basin lies in a warm, oily darkness that clings to the skin. Every so often a pocket of reality forgets to be dark and a column of sourceless radiance drops like a spotlight, illuminating a single plaza or the interior of a half-collapsed atrium for no discernible reason, then switches off again. In those brief illuminations you can see that the buildings are covered in slow, glistening calligraphy that was never carved by hands. The marks move when unobserved.

Sound is sparse, but not absent. Mostly there is the constant, low-level dripping of a world that cannot keep its water inside itself. Sometimes a structure exhales — a deep, wet sigh like a whale surfacing — and a new corridor opens where none was before. Occasionally the quiet is broken by the soft *crack* of reality resealing itself somewhere far off, the sound of two incompatible versions of a street deciding which one will remain.

There is no animal life. Not truly. What moves here does so without biology. Long translucent membranes drift between the towers like jellyfish made of half-remembered architecture, carrying faint reflections of streets that no longer exist. They pulse gently, and where they pass, erosion runs backward: fallen stones rise and click themselves into walls again. These membranes are the only visible “fauna,” and they are not alive in any way a mind can accept. They are wounds healing in the wrong direction.

The heat is oppressive but wet — a tropical fever heat that makes metal sweat and stone breathe. It presses down on the low basin like a warm palm. Nothing dries. Everything loosens. The ground itself is soft in places, as though the planet has not yet decided whether this region should be solid or liquid. You can push your hand into what looks like stone and feel it close around your wrist with the slow, thoughtful pressure of warm clay. After a while it lets go. It always lets go. The place is not cruel. It is simply no longer obliged to remain what it was when you last looked at it.

This is an old place. The erosion parameter is high because the landscape has had centuries — perhaps longer — to negotiate with its own contradictions. It has reached a kind of balance, the way a fever dream eventually learns its own recurring symbols. The city and the marsh and the heat and the darkness have all compromised. They have become one another.

Every few hours the world exhales.

When it does, the temperature drops by a fraction, the light remembers itself for a moment, and every droplet of water suspended in the air reverses direction at once. For three or four heartbeats the rain falls upward in perfect synchrony, glittering like glass beads rising toward a sky that is not there. Then the moment passes. The droplets lose interest and resume weeping sideways, downward, or into the stone itself.

If you stay long enough, you begin to feel the place learning you. Not watching. *Learning*. The way a estuary learns the shape of a new river.

There are no roads out that remain roads for long. The only constant is the slow, warm, dripping negotiation between what used to be a city and what is forgetting how to be a world.
