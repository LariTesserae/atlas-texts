The place is called the Weeping Sump.

It is a low, bowl-shaped basin no larger than a modest cathedral, yet it feels far older than any building. Its edges are ringed by low, rounded ridges of black basalt that have been worn so smooth by centuries of water they look like the backs of sleeping whales. Everything inside the basin is saturated. Water does not fall here so much as it *persists*: it beads on every surface, sweats from porous stone, drips in slow silver strings from overhangs, and condenses out of the air itself in cold, oily droplets that never quite become rain.

The vegetation is almost apologetic. A few stunted reeds and liverworts stain the margins green-grey, but most of the ground is bare or covered in a thin, living slick of microbial felt that shifts between rust, bruise-purple, and sickly chartreuse depending on the angle of the flat, even light. Nothing tall grows. The only vertical accents are the ruins of what were once small industrial structures: a collapsed pump house whose bricks are now so softened by moisture they resemble wet gingerbread; three corroded distillation columns leaning against one another like drunken iron giants; a rusted lattice of pipework that still, impossibly, carries water in slow, gurgling pulses from nowhere to nowhere.

The atmosphere is cool and heavy, like the inside of a mouth. Every breath feels slightly too wet. Condensation runs down your face the moment you stop moving, as if the air itself is trying to drink you.

The true strangeness is not in the ruins but in the ground. The basin floor is not solid. It is a dense, turbulent mat of overlapping things: thousands upon thousands of small, fist-sized mechanisms half-buried in black peat and microbial ooze. Some are ancient pumps, others appear to be parts of engines that were never meant to exist. They do not rust in the ordinary way. Instead they *change*. While you watch, a corroded valve may slowly bloom new copper petals; a cracked gearbox might sprout delicate glass tubing that was not there a minute earlier. The entire floor seethes with this patient, relentless industrial metabolism. Metal grows, fuses, dissolves, and regrows in slow, blind tides. The sound of it is almost nothing: a constant wet clicking, the soft hiss of vapor, the occasional deep metallic groan as something underground shifts its weight.

Fauna is rare but not absent. Tiny translucent isopods the color of old bone scuttle between the mechanisms. Once or twice an hour a pale, lamprey-like thing the length of your forearm will surface in one of the deeper pools, regard you with milky eyes, and sink again. They do not seem to eat. They seem to *wait*.

The light is normal, even, sourceless. There is no sun, no visible sky, only a pearl-grey ceiling that presses low. Every surface glistens. Every edge is rounded by centuries of chemical caress. The basin is in balance, and yet it is never still. The Weeping Sump is a single slow organ made of stone, water, and abandoned industry, breathing, sweating, rebuilding itself in the dark between one dripping second and the next.

If you stand in the exact center, where four half-buried pipelines cross like the spokes of a wheel, you can feel the whole place gently rocking, as though the basin itself is a cradle and something beneath it is turning in its sleep. The water weeps. The metal dreams. The silence is absolute except for the soft, wet sounds of perpetual, turbulent becoming.
