The place is called the Weeping Threshold.

It is a low, wide basin that has not yet learned how to be old. Everything here feels freshly cracked open, as though the land surfaced only a few centuries ago and is still deciding what shape it wants to keep. Sharp ridges of pale, glassy mineral rise in dense, near-vertical ranks, leaning against one another like the teeth of an unfinished jaw. Between them run countless seams and fissures no wider than a handspan, and from every seam water moves.

The water does not flow so much as sweat. It beads, condenses, and slides in heavy, slow pearls down every inclined surface. The rock itself seems to exhale moisture; the air is saturated enough that breath condenses on the skin and on the black, vitreous walls. Where two ridges almost touch, the space between them becomes a capillary, and water climbs upward in thin trembling films before gravity remembers itself and drags the films back down in viscous ropes. The sound is everywhere: a constant, overlapping percussion of drips, splashes, and hollow gurgles, all slightly delayed by the dense, humid air so that the entire basin feels like the inside of a mouth that has not yet swallowed.

There is almost no vegetation. What little grows is not plant life in any familiar register. Here and there, on ledges that catch a fraction more light than the rest, translucent fungal sheets the color of oyster flesh spread in perfect geometric lattices, pulsing faintly in time with distant, sub-audible harmonics. They are so thin the rock shows through them like bone beneath skin. Occasionally a single droplet will strike one of these lattices and the whole sheet will ring like struck crystal, a high, cold note that cuts cleanly through the wet percussion before dying.

The light is dim, blue-grey, and sourceless. It arrives already exhausted, filtered through perpetual low cloud that never quite becomes rain. The cloud clings so tightly to the ridges that the upper edges of the formations disappear into it, giving the false impression that the world ends thirty meters above your head. In truth the ridges march on for kilometers in every direction, a dense forest of stone blades and leaning monoliths packed so closely that passage between them is sometimes only possible by turning sideways and letting the weeping walls brush your shoulders.

Scattered among the press of mineral are the Built remnants: the only evidence that anything ever tried to live here on a scale larger than spore or droplet. A half-buried arch of black glass, its lintel still bearing the faint negative imprint of enormous hinges that no longer exist. A solitary pillar, perfectly circular, rising from a pool of its own condensed runoff; someone long ago carved a spiral stair around it that now leads only into cloud. These artifacts are few and far between, but each one feels recent, as though its makers walked away yesterday and the water has not yet had time to erase them.

The fauna is rare and strange. Small, blind things the color of wet hematite move across the rock in slow, tidal pulses. They look like living clots of rust until they unfold delicate, dripping membranes and glide from one wall to the next on cushions of surface tension. Their movement is almost invisible; you only notice them when the dripping suddenly changes rhythm because one of them is drinking. Deeper in the maze, something much larger sometimes passes. You never see it. You hear only a deep, patient slithering of something huge and soft dragging itself through a corridor too narrow for human shoulders, accompanied by a sound like wet leather being slowly wrung out. It never comes close. It is simply part of the long, slow metabolism of the place.

Everything here is young and saturated and loud and slow. The ridges are still sharp enough to cut. The water is still discovering where it wants to go. The cloud has not yet decided whether it will remain cloud or become a sea. In a million years this basin may calm into something gentler, but right now it is still in the first violent flush of becoming: a dense, dripping, half-finished world that rings and weeps and refuses to settle.

If you stand still long enough, you can feel the land deciding, one bead of water at a time, what kind of memory it will keep.
