The place is called the **Nascent Cathedral of the Weeping Engines**.

It is small in the way a cathedral is small: everything fits inside a single, self-contained hollow no larger than a modest hamlet, yet the ceiling vanishes upward into shadow and the walls feel as though they have no end. The entire volume is *made*. There is no natural stone, no soil, no unaltered surface anywhere. Every inch has been grown, extruded, riveted, or precipitated by industrial processes that have not yet finished their work. The landscape is still being born.

You stand on a floor of blackish-green metal lattice that breathes. Warm condensation gathers in thousands of bead-sized droplets along every strut, then slides downward in slow, silvery worms. The droplets never quite fall; they are siphoned away by capillary channels before they can strike the ground, feeding the next cycle. The air itself is wet and heavy, almost soupy. It smells of ozone, hot iron, wet ferns, and something like crushed limestone still reacting.

Everything is warm. Not hot, but the constant 28 °C of a body at labor. The warmth rises from within the structure rather than from any sun; the light is normal, diffuse, sourceless, as though the air itself has learned how to glow a gentle pearl-grey. There are no true shadows, only softer and harder densities of that same light.

The vegetation is obscene in its lushness. Pale viridian pipes and conduits, thick as ancient oaks, twist upward in slow helical growth, their surfaces covered in living rust that flowers into tiny scarlet lichen-frills. Between them, mats of engineered mosses pulse gently, inhaling moisture from the weeping metal and exhaling clouds of microscopic spores that catch the light like slow gold smoke. Ferns the color of oxidized copper unfurl from joints in the architecture, their fronds dripping constantly; the droplets fall onto lower leaves with soft, rhythmic ticks, the only steady percussion in the mostly quiet space.

The Cathedral is still forming. Great ribs of half-finished scaffolding arc overhead, dripping condensate like stalactites in reverse. Some ribs are glossy and new, others already furred with self-seeding machinery: small brass beetles the size of walnuts crawl along them, extruding new lattice, etching new channels, soldering new moss. They are the only fauna you will reliably see. Larger animals are almost absent; the few that exist are strange and half-mechanical—long silver millipedes whose segments are glass vials filled with glowing blue algae; a single species of pale bat-thing that hangs from the ceiling like a folded umbrella of wet parchment, its wings veined with copper wire.

The sound is low and layered. A constant soft hiss of vapor. The wet click of droplets. The distant, almost subcutaneous throb of pumps working somewhere far below the lattice floor. Every few minutes a deep, bass groan rolls through the structure as a new buttress finishes its growth and locks into place with a sound like a wet knuckle cracking. Then silence again, thick enough that you can hear your own pulse.

Elevation is deceptive. Though the entire chamber sits at true high altitude (thin, cool winds move across the unseen roof far above), the atmosphere inside is pressurized and humidified by the machines. You feel both the altitude’s slight giddiness and the tropical weight of saturated air at the same time. It is mildly intoxicating.

Everything is active. The Cathedral is not a finished place; it is a process wearing the clothes of a place. New transepts are still budding from the walls like mushrooms after rain, their wet metal petals unfolding with audible creaks. Channels reroute themselves. Temperatures shift in slow breathing cycles. The moss thickens, the pipes thicken, the beetles multiply, the light itself seems to thicken by imperceptible degrees. The whole structure is slowly, relentlessly, becoming more itself.

There is a rule here, though no one enforces it aloud: nothing is allowed to reach completion. The moment any section achieves what a human mind would call “finished,” hidden valves open and begin to dissolve it again with warm acidic mist. The Cathedral must remain embryonic. It must keep weeping. It must keep growing. It must never be allowed to become *merely* a building.

If you stand still long enough, you can feel the floor beneath your feet slowly rising, a few millimeters per hour, as new layers accrete beneath the lattice. The ceiling is descending at the same pace. One day, centuries from now, they will meet. Until then the Nascent Cathedral continues its wet, warm, metallic labor of becoming, forever cathedral-sized, forever unfinished, forever alive with the sound of its own slow, industrial birth.
