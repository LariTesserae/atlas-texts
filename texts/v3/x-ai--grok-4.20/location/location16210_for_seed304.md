The place is called **the Weeping Hollow of Veyr**.

It is a small, densely packed basin no larger than a modest cathedral, yet it feels both intimate and impossibly full. The ground sits low, only a few dozen feet above some unseen water table that presses constantly upward. Every surface sweats. Water does not fall here so much as *emerge*: it beads on stone, drips from overhangs in perfect silver threads, wells between roots, and condenses on the air itself in slow, cold exhalations that drift like breath made visible.

The rock is young—barely a few centuries old, still raw from whatever cataclysm or slow buckle of the earth birthed it. Black basaltic columns, sharp as newly broken glass, rise at strange angles and crowd against one another with almost no soil between them. Their edges have not had time to round; they cut the eye. Between the columns, the stone is veined with pale, translucent calcite that weeps constantly. The water runs in thin films down every face, polishing the dark rock until it glitters like wet obsidian, then gathers in hairline gutters and trickles into a thousand miniature waterfalls no taller than a man.

Vegetation is sparse but stubborn. Pale mosses the color of sea-foam cling to every north face. Here and there a stunted black alder or dwarf willow has managed to wedge its roots into a fissure, its branches twisted into agonized shapes by the constant moisture and poor soil. Their leaves are small, dark, almost metallic. They do not rustle; they simply drip.

The entire hollow is packed so tightly that there is almost no open ground. Every cubic foot is claimed. Stone, water, root, and the low crude architecture of the Veyri people fight for the same space. Their settlement is not *on* the rock but *of* it—rough dwellings of mortared fieldstone and heartwood grown directly against the basalt columns, half cave, half house. Narrow lanes twist between buildings like crevices. Rope bridges and hand-hewn ladders span the vertical gaps. Everything is damp. Everything smells of iron, cold stone, and the faint sweet-rot of moss.

Tech is primitive: bronze tools, fire-hardened wood, rope of braided sedge. The Veyri have no forges here; metal is brought in from elsewhere and treated like something sacred. Their brightest technology is hydraulic. They have learned to listen to the weeping of the stone. Tiny clay gutters and carved channels direct the constant seepage into cisterns, into prayer basins, into the long, narrow reflecting pools that serve as streets in the lowest part of the hollow. The sound of moving water is therefore perpetual, yet strangely muted—never loud, never silent. A constant soft percussion of drips, trickles, and the low ringing note when a droplet falls into a bronze bowl left out for that purpose.

Fauna is rare. A few pale moths the size of a man’s palm drift between the columns like slow snow. Occasionally a small, blind salamander the color of milk crawls from one fissure to another, leaving a wet trail that vanishes almost instantly. Once in a great while something larger moves in the mist above: a grey heron-like creature with too many joints in its legs, standing motionless on a roof-ridge as though it grew there.

The atmosphere itself is cool and heavy. Condensation gathers on every eyelash, every strand of hair. Breath fogs and then, strangely, seems to linger, refusing to dissipate. Light arrives normal but filtered—diffused by the constant mist into a soft, shadowless silver that makes distances hard to judge. At “noon” (if such a thing can be said to exist here) the hollow glows like the inside of a pearl. At “night” the water itself seems to phosphoresce very faintly, so the wet stone gives off a cold ghost-light.

The place is active. The water never rests. The stone itself is still settling; every few days a new hairline fracture appears with a sound like a harp string plucked once, and a fresh weeping begins. The Veyri treat these as oracles. They mark each new fissure with a small bronze medallion hammered into the crack, so the walls are slowly becoming a constellation of tiny shields that ring softly when the water strikes them.

There is a rule here, felt more than spoken: nothing may be fully dry. Even the dead are laid in shallow niches where the weeping of the stone can reach them, slowly encrusting their bodies with delicate calcite until they resemble pale statues wearing their own dissolution.

The hollow feels like a wound that has not yet decided whether it will heal or keep bleeding forever. And the people who live inside it have made that wound their home—crowded, dripping, sharp-edged, and strangely reverent—listening always to the soft, endless weeping of young stone.
