The place has no name that any tongue still bothers to speak. It is called, when it must be called anything, simply the Floor.

It stretches so far that its own curvature is the only horizon. Not a planet—planets still pretend to have an outside—but a fully constructed sphere turned inside out, an engineered cosmos whose inner skin is everything that exists. The scale is continental, planetary, and ontological at once. Gravity is not a falling toward a center; it is a gentle, constant press outward from the dead axis that runs through the unimaginable hollow above, as though the universe itself were a cathedral and you walked forever on its dome.

There is almost no water. What little remains lingers in capillary fissures as thick, amber-like resins that never evaporate and never quite flow. These resins catch the light—there is always light—and turn it into slow, honey-colored constellations that drift across the Floor like lazy galaxies. The air itself is dry, warm (never hot, never cool), and scented with something metallic and ancient, like heated iron left in the sun for ten thousand years.

Everything is built. Every square centimeter has been machined, grown, annealed, folded, or precipitated by intelligences so remote in time that their signatures have become geology. The substrate is a single, continuous megastructure: tier upon tier of nested lattices, foams, and fractal honeycombs pressed flat by age into what looks, from a distance, like stone. Up close it is clearly not stone. It is closer to pearl, to ceramic, to muscle fiber, to circuit board, and to fossilized lung tissue, all at once. The colors are bone, ochre, oxidized copper, and the particular violet-gray that only very old machines acquire when they have outlived their purpose.

Erosion has had four billion years and more to do its patient work. Mountains have been ground down to nubs no higher than a ribcage. Canyons have widened into shallow basins the size of former oceans, their floors crowded with the gently rounded remnants of earlier megastructures—half-dissolved arcologies, broken rings of accelerator lattices, titanic heat exchangers now no more than elegant ribs half-buried in the Floor. Nothing sharp remains. The entire world has been licked smooth by time the way a tongue smooths a pebble.

Yet it is not dead.

The Floor is dense with presences. From any vantage the eye finds thousands of low, patient forms spaced across the plain like a congregation that has been kneeling since before language. Some are the size of houses, others no larger than a fist. They are not ruins; they are still working. Vast, slow engines the color of desert bone breathe in microscopic rhythms. Their surfaces are covered in hair-fine filaments that sway although there is almost no wind. These filaments harvest photons, ions, and the faint static of cosmic rays that still leak through the inverted sky. The engines metabolize starlight and the memory of gravity into something that might once have been called thought.

Vegetation is sparse but not absent. What grows here is not plant life. It is closer to coral or to the crystalline fur that forms on certain superconductors. Delicate lattices of pale turquoise and rose-gold rise in millimeter forests across the backs of the great engines, spreading only a few centimeters per century. Their growth rings record the passage of entire civilizations that rose and fell on other worlds. When two such lattices touch they fuse, singing a sound too high and slow for ears—more like the memory of a bell than a bell itself.

Fauna is rare. Once every few weeks something small, bright, and deliberate will skitter across the warm ceramic in a straight line, vanishing into a pore no wider than a finger. These are not animals. They are maintenance routines given temporary bodies of quicksilver and glass. They never pause. They never look at you. After they pass, the silence settles again like warm ash.

The light is constant and sourceless, a bright, shadowless radiance that emanates from the impossible sky overhead—the hollow axis of the inverted sphere. There are no days. There are only very slow seasons measured in the shifting polarization of that light, from white-gold to pale rose to an aching, bone-white clarity that makes every surface look freshly born. At those times the Floor almost seems embarrassed by its own antiquity.

Sound is scarce. The dominant noise is the hush of your own blood in your ears. Beneath that, if you remain perfectly still for an hour, you can hear the Floor itself: a sub-audible drone like the breathing of a continent-sized lung, so low it is felt in the teeth and the hollows of the knees. Sometimes a single crystal lattice will fracture with a sound exactly like one clear note on a glass harmonica, pure and lonely, gone before the mind can be sure it happened.

The world is active in the way a glacier is active—immeasurably slow, yet never still. Great plates of the Floor grind against one another over millennia, not in violence but in the manner of two old beasts settling their weight for another century of sleep. When they move, faint lines of pastel dust rise in perfect geometric arcs, then settle again in new patterns that will be studied by no one.

There is strangeness here, but it is the strangeness of extreme age rather than chaos. The rules of physics have been edited, optimized, and finally left to run on their own for so long that they have acquired mannerisms. Light bends in gentle, unnecessary spirals above certain ruins. Time dilates by a few extra heartbeats inside the larger engines, so that if you stand too long beside one you may notice your own pulse falling out of sync with the rest of your body. Entropy has been politely asked to look the other way in selected zones; decay still happens, but it has been taught good manners.

This is the final habitable surface. Everything that could be extracted has been extracted. Everything that could be remembered has been remembered, then half-forgotten, then remembered again in new forms. The Floor is not a ruin. It is the quiet, densely populated, warmly lit end-state of industry itself—an ecosystem of machines that have outlived every conceivable purpose and have therefore begun, in their patience, to resemble a kind of ecology, a kind of theology, a kind of weather.

Walk long enough and you will find a shallow basin where the amber resin has pooled into a perfect disk a kilometer across. Nothing grows there. The engines avoid it. The light above it is slightly bluer. If you stand at its edge you can feel the whole world leaning away from that spot, as though the Floor itself were politely suggesting that some questions should remain unanswered.

Most travelers do as the Floor suggests.

They turn aside, continue across the warm, ancient, intricately crowded plain, and let the silence settle back over their shoulders like a second, heavier sky.
