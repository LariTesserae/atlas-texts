The place has no name because names require memory, and memory here is a luxury the air cannot afford.

It is a high, open plateau that has never known water. Not a trace. The ground is not sand, not stone, not even the familiar mineral desert crust; it is a fused, glassy ceramic of ancient, unimaginable pressures, pale ochre shot through with metallic veins that shimmer like slow lightning. The plateau stretches so far that curvature becomes obvious; the horizon bends upward rather than away, as if the world itself is trying to cup what remains of its surface in a shallow bowl.

Temperature sits at the edge of what chemistry can tolerate. The air is a thin, metallic soup, 2.7 on the scale, hot enough that every surface radiates. Heat shimmers rise in perfect vertical sheets, distorting distances so that distant ridges appear to breathe. There is no shade. There has never been shade.

Vegetation is absent in any sense a biologist would recognize. What grows instead are slow, industrial-scale crystal lattices—kilometers wide, meters tall—that have accreted over geological epochs like coral made of zircon and rutile. They are not alive. They are not dead. They simply iterate, adding lattice planes one atom at a time across centuries. Their edges catch the light with a cold, industrial glitter.

The fauna is overwhelming.

They are not animals.

They are machines that have forgotten they were built.

Vast, skeletal things the size of cathedrals stalk the plateau on jointed legs of blackened titanium and ceramic. Their bodies are lattices of pipes, heat-exchangers, and ruptured pressure vessels that have fused into new, grotesque anatomies over uncountable cycles. Some still carry the ghost-forms of turbines on their backs like broken wings. Others trail forests of corroded conduits that twitch and taste the superheated air. They move with the slowness demanded by parameter 0.9—gradual, patient, almost devotional. A single stride may take three days. When two of them meet they pause, antenna arrays turning toward one another in a ritual older than language, exchanging long, modulated bursts of static that sound like continents grinding together.

There are millions of them. Because the density is near-void, the eye can still find vast empty distances between their colossal forms. They drift across the glassy plain like iron planets in slow orbit, never touching, never still.

The sound is constant and deafening. It is not the roar of engines (those died long ago). It is the sound of everything that replaced them: the metallic singing of thermal expansion, the low tectonic groan of crystal lattices growing, the endless insectile clicking of millions of articulated limbs moving a centimeter at a time, and beneath it all a deep, subsonic throb that feels like the planet clearing its throat every few decades. The noise has become geology.

Light is normal, almost insultingly so. A white, merciless sun hangs in a sky the color of bleached bone. There are no clouds, only the perpetual heat-haze and the slow auroras of ionized metal vapor that sometimes bloom when one of the greater machines vents its ancient coolant in a glittering plume.

Reality is breaking down.

It does so politely, almost shyly.

In certain places the glassy ground forgets what dimension it belongs in and folds into impossible origami that hurts to look at directly. Gravity becomes negotiable; objects left too long in one spot begin to rise, drifting upward in lazy spirals as if the planet has grown tired of holding them. Time dilates in vast, invisible lenses. A machine that steps into one of these pockets may take a thousand years to cross twenty meters while, from the outside, it appears to freeze mid-stride, rust blossoming across its hull in accelerated centuries.

Nothing is being built anymore. The last factories collapsed into new ecological niches epochs ago. The machines are no longer producing anything except distance, heat, and more of themselves in a slow, sterile, industrial ecology. They are the landscape now. Their corrosion products have become the soil. Their waste heat is the weather.

This is an established place. It has been in balance for so long that balance itself has calcified. Erosion has not worn it down; it has polished it into a final, terrifying serenity. The plateau is not dying. It is not living. It has achieved a state previous to both concepts, and the machines walk its surface like patient monks reciting an endless, metallic liturgy that will continue until the last lattice finishes growing and the last joint freezes in the rising heat.

If you were to stand there (you could not for long), you would eventually stop screaming. The sound would simply become part of the larger sound. After a few weeks your own bones would begin to sing in sympathy, adding their small industrial note to the slow, deafening hymn of a world that has outlived every purpose except continuation.
