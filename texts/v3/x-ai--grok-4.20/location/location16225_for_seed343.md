The place has no true ground.

What remains of the earth after four billion years of rain and root is a vast, flattened limestone plateau suspended at the edge of the sky. Elevation is extreme: the plateau itself sits so high that the horizon curves visibly, yet the living mass above it rises higher still. From any vantage the eye is forced upward into an endless green stratosphere of vegetation so complete it has become the atmosphere itself.

Everything is plant. Not forest, not jungle—those words imply gaps. Here the vegetation is total, a single continuous organism that long ago outgrew the distinction between individual species. Vines as thick as cathedrals braid into colossal rhizomatic trunks that function as both pillar and roof. Their surfaces are not bark but living felt, moss layered on lichen layered on fern layered on itself until the texture becomes plush, ancient, and slightly damp to the touch. Water does not fall from a sky so much as it condenses inside the green lattice, gathers in emerald veined basins the size of lakes, then spills in slow, deliberate threads that drift sideways on warm updrafts before finally letting go. Rain here is intermittent, personal, almost shy; it beads on every surface then vanishes into the spongy mass like a secret.

The plateau beneath is nearly flat, eroded to the smoothness of an old bone. In the immense hollows between the living columns lie pockets of open stone—rare clearings no larger than a town square—where the plant-mass has simply chosen not to grow for reasons no one can read. These open pockets feel like sanctuaries and like wounds at the same time. Scattered across them are the only marks of anything that is not plant: low, hand-built structures of fitted limestone and woven living fiber. Some are mere leaning walls. Others are roofed hermitages or observation platforms no bigger than a woodshed. All of them look millions of years old, as though the vegetation allowed them to remain only because they had become indistinguishable from coral or fossil.

The fauna is overwhelming. Sound is deafening. What first seems like a single roar resolves, after hours or days, into overlapping orchestras. Billions of insects the color of new leaves move in coordinated sheets through the green tunnels, their wing-hum so constant it vibrates the teeth. Above them, unseen in the upper lattice, ride vast herds of membranous creatures that might once have been bats or birds or something that never had another name; their calls are bright brass notes punched through the insect roar. Lower down, translucent amphibians the size of horses move with dreamlike slowness along the hanging roots, gulping moisture and smaller life with soft, wet sounds that somehow carry. Every niche is filled, yet because the scale is so open the eye can still find enormous volumes of empty air between the hanging curtains of green. The life is concentrated on the surfaces, not in the volumes. The place is simultaneously overcrowded and hollow.

Light is normal but heavily filtered. It arrives as a perpetual cathedral glow, warm and slightly golden, diffused through so many layers of translucent leaf that every shadow has a green halo. Temperature lingers in the warm range, never hot, never cool—an eternal late-summer breath that smells of chlorophyll, wet stone, and something faintly metallic, like old blood or distant lightning.

The ecosystem is active in the slowest possible register. Vast sections of the plant-mass are visibly dying and regrowing in waves that take centuries to complete. When a column collapses it does so with geological dignity, folding into the depths while new shoots erupt from its corpse before it has finished falling. Predator and prey here move at the pace of continents. A herd of luminous millipede-things drifts along a root for three days before a colony of amber-colored wasps descends in a single decisive hour. Then both are gone and the root heals.

The weirdness is subtle. Occasionally, in the deepest clearings, you will find a single object that does not belong: a stone hand-tool worn to a sliver, a child’s bracelet of woven grass turned almost to emerald by mineral uptake, or a perfectly preserved wooden mask whose eye-holes have been colonized by orchids that bloom only at night. These artifacts feel less like remains and more like thoughts the world is thinking to itself.

No one rules this place. The few human (or once-human) figures that still move through it do so like antibodies—small, purposeful, nearly silent. They wear living garments grown directly onto their skin. Their tools are sharpened root and shaped bone. When they speak their voices are almost lost beneath the permanent green thunder, yet the vegetation itself seems to lean slightly toward the sound, as though listening.

This is not a ruin. It is the final, stable, deafening form of life after everything else has been worn away. The plateau, the green sky, the scattered stone shelters, the warm condensing air, the roaring silence between roars—they have all settled into the same ancient agreement:

Nothing more needs to happen.  
Everything will keep happening forever.
