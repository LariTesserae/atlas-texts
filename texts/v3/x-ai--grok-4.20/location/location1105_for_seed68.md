The place is called the **Mossfoundries**.

It is a low, folded basin no larger than a modest hamlet, yet it feels both intimate and impossibly old-new at once. The ground is young—barely a few centuries since the land itself heaved upward in some quiet geological shrug—and the stone still wears the sharp, raw edges of recent birth. Black basalt ribs break through the soil like the half-buried hulls of sunken ships. Between these ribs, water moves in hesitant, intermittent ways: it does not flow so much as appear. Condensation beads on every cold surface and gathers in glassy lenses inside hollows of rock. Sudden, brief rains arrive like afterthoughts, drumming for ten minutes then vanishing, leaving the air damp and smelling of iron and chlorophyll.

The vegetation is lush but strangely spaced. Great shelves of moss, thick as horse blankets and colored in impossible greens and bruise-purples, drape the basalt. Between them rise scattered stands of white-barked trees whose leaves are broad, glossy, and perpetually wet. The trees do not form a forest; each stands alone, as though each had claimed its own small kingdom of ten or twelve meters. Their roots crawl over the stone in thick cables, drinking from the condensation pockets. Where the moss has not yet conquered, ferns taller than a man uncurl with almost visible slowness.

The air is cool, the light is normal and slightly milky, as if the sky itself were a porcelain bowl. There is no true sun, only a soft, directionless brightness that never quite warms anything. Sound, however, is constant and loud. The entire basin resonates with the slow, patient heartbeat of industry. Deep beneath the moss and roots, ancient-looking machines—half cathedral organ, half forge—turn in deliberate, geological rhythms. Their flywheels are the size of ox-carts; their pistons move once every forty or fifty seconds. Each stroke sends a low *thoom* through the stone that you feel in your teeth. Pipes as thick as a man’s torso surface here and there, wrapped in living moss, venting warm mist that smells of ozone and wet stone. The machines are not new. They look as though they grew here, or the land grew around them.

This is an urban place, but the city is only the size of a single neighborhood. A loose constellation of brick-and-iron structures crouches between the trees and the basalt outcrops. Their roofs are steeply pitched to shed the frequent condensation; their walls are half-swallowed by moss and climbing vines whose flowers open only when the machines exhale. Narrow elevated walkways of riveted iron lace the buildings together, never straight, always following the natural curves of the land. There are perhaps two dozen structures in total: a foundry hall with doors tall enough for locomotives, a scriptorium where pale archivists record the changing songs of the engines, a cluster of dormitories whose windows glow soft amber at all hours. Everything is connected by a slow circulatory system of pipes, belts, and dripping gutters.

Fauna is abundant and strangely calm. White herons with iron-grey wingtips stand motionless on the warm vent pipes, warming their bellies. Colonies of small moss-colored mammals—something between otter and monkey—move unhurriedly along the branches, licking condensation from the leaves. In the deeper hollows, translucent salamanders the length of your arm drift in the cold pools, their gills pulsing in time with the machines. None of the animals seem afraid of the noise or the people. They treat the industrial rhythm as weather.

The weirdness is quiet but persistent. Occasionally a tree will sigh and release a cloud of spores that briefly form perfect geometric patterns before dissolving. Certain pipes, when struck, ring with notes that linger for nearly an hour. At the exact center of the basin stands a single basalt pillar that has never been claimed by moss; on its flat top sits an iron bowl that fills itself with clear water no matter how much is drunk from it. The water tastes faintly of distant thunder.

Everything changes with excruciating patience. A new pipe may surface over the course of a decade. A building may be slowly engulfed by moss and quietly repurposed as a greenhouse. The machines themselves are migrating—moving perhaps a handspan every century—grinding new grooves into the young stone as they go. The people who live here have adapted to this slowness. They speak softly. They measure time in moss-growth and piston-strokes. They wear clothes the color of wet iron and damp bark.

This is the Mossfoundries: a small, damp, loud, cool, slowly breathing machine-grove where industry and rainforest have decided, over long centuries, not to fight but to marry. The wedding is still young. The stone is still sharp. The pipes still sweat. The engines still turn, one deliberate heartbeat at a time, while the white herons wait on the warmth and the moss keeps growing, patient as geology, loud as prayer.
