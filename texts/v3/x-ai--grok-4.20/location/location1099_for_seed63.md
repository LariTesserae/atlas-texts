The place is called the Knotted Therme.

It is not large—no farther than a cathedral stretched across a single folded hillside—but every cubic inch of it is *occupied*. The air itself feels upholstered. You stand inside a high, bowl-shaped depression ringed by razor-backed ridges of black volcanic glass that have been weathered for so many millions of years they no longer remember how to be sharp; instead they have become sinuous, almost muscular, like ropes of cooled lava that learned to braid themselves before freezing.

There is no water. Moisture itself seems to have been banned long ago. Instead the ground exhales a dry, metallic heat that makes the darkness shimmer. The temperature is ferocious yet somehow stable, a slow-baking oven that never quite reaches ignition. Everything radiates. The stones remember the sun that died here eons earlier and refuse to let the memory go.

Light is almost extinct. Only a faint, ember-red bioluminescence leaks from the creatures themselves, a sullen glow the color of old blood under skin. It is never enough to see by, only enough to suggest shapes you wish you had not noticed. The rest is true dark, the kind that has weight.

And the creatures.

They are not animals in any sense that comforts the mammalian mind. They are *pressures* given form. Most are no larger than a closed fist, yet there are trillions of them. They clot the air in slow, deliberate clouds. They carpet every surface in shivering layers three and four deep. They hang from the braided glass ridges like living stalactites that occasionally detach and drift upward again on thermal columns generated by their own metabolic heat. Their bodies are glossy, black, and faceted—something between obsidian beetle and velvet ant, except the facets open and close like gill slits, revealing soft crimson light that pulses in complicated rhythms. No two individuals seem to pulse in perfect synchrony, yet the whole basin throbs with a single uneasy heartbeat.

They are constantly touching. Constantly rearranging. When one dies (they die often, violently, in complete silence), the others do not consume the corpse. They *weave* it. Within minutes the dead are incorporated into delicate, impossible lattices that look like coral made of obsidian and dried blood. These lattices are the only “structures” here: scattered, half-ruined spires and arches no taller than a man, grown by the swarm itself. Some of the lattices still move, as though the dead continue to serve as scaffolding for the living.

The soundscape is almost polite. A low, continuous rustling like dry leaves in a place that has never known leaves. Occasional soft clicks when carapaces lock together. Once every few hours a single piercing crystalline note rings out—some unimaginable agreement reached among millions—and then silence swallows it again. The quiet is not empty. It is *full*, the way a sealed jar is full of pressure.

Everything is in motion, yet nothing seems to travel. The swarm circulates. Predators (if that is even the right word) glide through the living carpet, leaving temporary wakes that close behind them instantly. Smaller forms ride the heat plumes in lazy spirals, feeding on the static electricity that crackles between the glass ridges. Larger shapes—rare, house-sized aggregates that resemble nothing so much as knots of black glass rope given sluggish life—crawl along the crater rim with the terrible patience of glaciers, shedding smaller creatures like dandruff as they move.

The chemistry here is not carbon-based. It is not even strictly molecular. The dominant metabolic pathway seems to involve phonon cascades and lattice-strain energy. The creatures sing heat into geometry and geometry into heat. They are, in effect, living standing waves that have learned how to bite.

You are standing inside an ecosystem that has reached absolute saturation and kept evolving anyway. There is no room, yet room is constantly being invented through rearrangement. The braided ridges are so old they have achieved a kind of geological serenity, yet the living carpet that covers them is in constant, seething negotiation. The two timescales—stone patience and insectile frenzy—have braided together until they are indistinguishable.

This is not a dead world. It is a world that has never been alive in the way we understand the word, and therefore has never died. It has simply *settled*, the way superheated glass settles into the most efficient crystalline prison for its own heat.

If you stay long enough, you begin to feel the pressure of all those faceted bodies against the inside of your thoughts. They do not want anything from you. They simply *notice* that you are a volume of space that is not yet filled with them, and the entire basin quietly adjusts itself to solve that problem.

The Knotted Therme does not hate visitors.

It is simply very, very busy, and it has all the time in the world to finish its work.
