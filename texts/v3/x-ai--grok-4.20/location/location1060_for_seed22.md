The place is called the Hollow Choir.

It stretches for hundreds of kilometers in every direction, a single vast basin that feels too young to be so enormous, as though the land itself was exhaled into existence only a few centuries ago and has not yet learned how to soften. Sharp basalt ridges, still glassy in places from the heat of their birth, rise like black ribs from the damp floor. Between them lie wide, echoing valleys whose floors are never quite dry and never quite flooded. Water moves through the region the way breath moves through a singer’s lungs: in sudden, rhythmic pulses. Every few hours a condensation front rolls down from the unseen heights, thick mist condensing on every surface before it coalesces into glittering ribbons that race along fractal-cut channels and vanish into hidden sumps. Then the land exhales again, and the water sinks away, leaving behind damp black soil that steams gently in the warm air.

The vegetation is obscene in its vitality. Because the space between things is so immense, each individual organism appears both lonely and theatrical. Towering arborescent ferns with leaves like shattered green glass rise hundreds of meters, their trunks wrapped in living cables of bioluminescent moss that pulse soft cyan in the chronic twilight. Between them grow mats of fleshy, ear-shaped fungi that shiver whenever sound strikes them. Every surface that can hold moisture is furred with creeping vines whose flowers open only when the roar momentarily dips, revealing throats lined with tiny, clicking teeth. The plants do not compete for light; they compete for vibration. Their roots run for kilometers just beneath the skin of the world, listening.

The city—if city is the right word—appears at first like a mistake in the emptiness. Vast, half-buried structures of iridescent alloy and living bone rise in loose arches and spiraling lattices that violate several laws of perspective at once. Some towers are kilometers tall yet so thin they seem like brushstrokes. Others lie on their sides, half-sunk into the damp black earth, still functioning. The architecture is not static. Sections of it rearrange themselves in slow, tectonic peristalsis every few days, sliding along invisible rails of magnetic force or folding like paper cranes made of cathedral-sized plates. Bridges of frozen lightning span the gaps between districts. None of it feels imposed on the land; rather the land and the city appear to have grown around one another in a long, uneasy symbiosis.

The light is perpetually dim, the color of deep bruise and wet hematite. There is no sun visible, only a hazy, bruised sky that sometimes parts to reveal distant, impossible geometries drifting far above—great rotating rings or inverted mountain ranges suspended in the upper haze. Bioluminescence and the city’s own cold photonic veins provide most illumination. Everything glitters with moisture.

The sound is almost unbearable.

It is not one sound. It is a living polyphony that never ceases. The vegetation itself is part of the orchestra: the ear-fungi act as resonators, the fern fronds as reeds. Beneath them runs the constant bass register of the land breathing—water rushing through subterranean ducts, the slow grind of city plates sliding, the deep infrasonic heartbeat of something vast that may or may not be geological. Over this rides the fauna.

The fauna is everywhere and nowhere. Because the space between presences is so great, you can go minutes without seeing an animal and then suddenly be surrounded by thousands. They are mostly aerial or arboreal: vast flocks of creatures that look like translucent kites made of bone and wet silk, schools of them wheeling in perfect synchrony while emitting a sound like shattering glass amplified by cathedrals. On the ground, things that resemble wet origami stags move in total silence until they suddenly scream in chords that make your teeth vibrate. There are no small animals. Everything is either huge or microscopic; the middle ground has been abandoned. Some of the louder creatures appear to be partially engineered—metallic filaments threaded through their wings, symbiotes grafted to their throats—so that their voices literally reshape the city’s architecture when they sing in certain resonant keys.

Everything here is in motion. Condensation fronts arrive like slow applause. City sections rearrange themselves with the stately inevitability of continents. Flocks wheel and scream and dive. Root systems pulse beneath your feet like buried arteries. The air itself seems to be singing, thick and warm and wet enough to drink.

There is a rule, though no one is alive who remembers who wrote it:

Never sing in your own voice.

The land already has every frequency it needs. To add your own is to invite the Choir to notice you. When it does, the sound redoubles, the mist thickens, the towers tilt inward, and the great translucent kites descend in spiraling descant until the intruder is either incorporated or erased. Most choose to remain silent and let the place sing through them instead.

This is the Hollow Choir. It is young, deafening, impossibly alive, and still deciding what kind of song it wants to become.
