The place is called the Ascent of the Hollow Choir.

It is a high, cold, dripping darkness that has never known a sun. The air itself is wet and heavy, not with rain but with slow, perpetual condensation that beads on every surface and falls in perfect, metronomic drops the size of marbles. You hear them before you see anything: a vast, slow, overlapping percussion, thousands of drips arriving at different depths and densities, ringing from stone to stone like a cathedral of wet bells.

There is almost no vegetation. What little biological presence exists is not plant nor fungus but something older, something that perhaps predates both: pale, translucent lattices of mineralized mycoid threads that grow only along the seams where two different kinds of stone were forced together by unimaginable pressure long ago. They glow faintly, the color of moonlight on bone, but so dimly that they serve only to emphasize the blackness around them.

The city—if it can be called a city—is not built upon the mountain. The mountain is being grown, or perhaps remembered, into a city.

Every surface is raw geology still in the act of deciding what it will become. Cliff faces bulge and slump with tectonic patience, forming balconies, staircases, and colonnades that will not be finished for another ten thousand years. Vast towers of black basalt and pale quartzite extrude themselves upward in slow, deliberate pulses, millimeters per century, their surfaces still molten at the core. You can feel the heat of their becoming through the soles of your boots even as the outer air remains near-freezing. The city is embryonic and already enormous, a half-born metropolis the size of a mountain range.

And it is dense beyond reason.

Structures press against one another like coral in accelerated time. Bridges span only meters yet are layered thirty deep, each new span forming beneath the previous one as the rock itself folds and re-folds. Tunnels open and close again over decades. Entire districts are still inside the stone, visible only as darker veins or faint geometric shadows within translucent cliffs. You walk through what feels like a three-dimensional street grid that has been crumpled and refolded by an indifferent god.

The fauna is overwhelming.

They are not animals in any sense we would recognize. They are living densities—vast, slow-moving congregations of something that exists between sound and matter. You experience them first as pressure changes in the ears and chest. Then the dripping stops for three heartbeats as something passes through the volume you occupy. They have no fixed shape. Some are miles across, vast curtains of condensing vapor that drift between the half-formed towers, singing in frequencies so low the stone itself vibrates. Others are small, fist-sized, and travel in glittering swarms that sound like breaking glass when they move. All of them are singing. The sound is constant, oceanic, layered—a slow thunder of overlapping chords that never resolves. It is not communication. It is metabolism.

The darkness is not absolute. There is light, but it is generated by the place itself in ways current physics would struggle to explain. Occasional blooms of cold plasma drift between the towers like ghostly lanterns, shedding a sick violet that reveals nothing clearly. Some of the condensing water droplets fall through invisible fields and ignite for half a second, becoming tiny falling stars that illuminate only the surfaces they strike before dying. The overall effect is that you are forever on the edge of being able to see, forever denied.

Everything moves at the pace of geology.

A tower that began rising while the first multicellular life was forming on Earth is still only halfway finished. A district that will one day house millions is currently a honeycomb of embryonic chambers the size of cathedrals, their ceilings dripping with expectant water. The great singers glide between the half-formed structures over centuries, their songs slowly carving new avenues and plazas with vibration alone.

This is not a ruin. It is not abandoned.

It is a city that is still being dreamed by something vast and cold and patient, and you are standing inside the dream while it is still wet.

Every footstep echoes for a very long time. The sound of your own breathing feels like an intrusion, a mayfly heartbeat inside a cathedral built for epochs. The condensation gathers on your eyelashes. The towers keep rising, millimeter by millimeter, while the great slow singers pass overhead like continents of sound, and the dripping never, ever stops.
