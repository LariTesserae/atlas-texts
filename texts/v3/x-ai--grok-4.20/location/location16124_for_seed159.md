The place is called the Underslip.

You stand inside a cavern system so vast that the ceiling vanishes into luminous haze two hundred meters above. Normal daylight somehow reaches here—thick, warm, buttery shafts that angle down through unseen fissures and fracture into a thousand smaller beams. The light is alive; it trembles, shifts, and sometimes reverses direction as though the sun above were breathing.

Everything is damp but never quite wet. Moisture gathers in glassy beads on every surface, condenses into trembling lenses, then slides away in slow silver threads that vanish into hairline cracks. The air itself feels spongy, saturated, warm as breath. Occasional fat drops fall from nowhere, striking stone with soft, deliberate plaps that echo like struck bells.

There is almost no vegetation. What grows instead are mineral gardens in accelerated infancy. The rock is not finished forming. Great ribs and plates of raw crystal surge upward in real time—clear quartz, milky feldspar, veins of tourmaline—growing like coral in fast-forward. Some formations bloom outward in feathery sprays only to collapse minutes later with glassy chimes, their fragments immediately re-absorbed by the living stone beneath. The ground is a slow, churning hull of emerging geology, still soft in places, still deciding what it wants to be.

And everywhere, in every cubic meter, there is fauna.

They are not animals in any familiar sense. They are half-made things, embryonic, wet with the same condensation that drips from the ceiling. Pale, translucent insectoids the size of wolves scuttle on too many joints; their bodies look poured from molten glass and only half-cooled. Cloud-like colonies of luminous worms drift between the crystal lattices like living nebulae, pulsing in slow codes. things that might become birds one day cling to vertical faces with sucker-feet, singing in wet, gurgling chords that overlap into a constant, deafening chorus. The sound is overwhelming—clicking, humming, scraping, warbling, the wet pop of new bodies splitting open, the ringing of crystal births, the low thunder of collapsing formations—all of it layered so densely that the noise itself feels solid.

The cavern stretches for kilometers in every direction, opening into further halls, vaults, and plunging galleries. Nothing is stable. New arches burst through older ceilings only to be swallowed again. Whole districts of the Underslip are born, reach maturity, and die in the span of a single hour. In the middle distance you can see a fresh range of rose-quartz mountains lifting skyward with audible grinding, shedding newborn dust that sparkles in the slanted light.

Scattered among this frenzy are the works of something primitive. Crude hand-hewn pillars of black basalt stand at irregular intervals, each one carved with the same looping sigil. Some are half-buried by new crystal, others have fresh growths budding from their tops like grotesque flowers. Occasionally you pass a low wall built from fitted river stones—clearly placed by hands, clearly ancient—now used as a causeway by a migrating column of glass-bodied millipedes. A single toppled monolith lies half-submerged in a warm, milky pool; something is using its carved surface as an incubator, thousands of translucent eggs trembling in time with the rock’s own pulse.

The rules here are simple and merciless:

- Nothing is allowed to finish.  
- Everything is allowed to try.  
- The stone itself is hungry, curious, and in labor.  
- Light arrives whether it is wanted or not.  
- The noise never drops below a roar.  
- If you stand still too long, mineral filaments will begin to taste your ankles.

This is not a cave. It is a womb the size of a mountain range, and it is having a very loud, very bright, very crowded dream. The dream has been going on for several million years and shows no sign of waking.
