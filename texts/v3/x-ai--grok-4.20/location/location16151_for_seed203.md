The world is called the Undersward.

It is not a cave. It is a single, continent-sized hollow beneath a crust that never quite finished forming. The ceiling — if it can be called that — hangs so high that its pale vaults are lost in their own haze, yet the light is everywhere: a cold, sourceless brilliance like sunlight filtered through ten meters of milk-white ice. Nothing casts a true shadow. Every surface glows with its own borrowed radiance.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It condenses in slow silver beads on the undersides of colossal leaves, trembles there for hours, then drops hundreds of meters into darkness. Shallow braided rivers wander across the tops of titanic leaves before vanishing again into pores the size of cathedrals. Mist hangs in motionless curtains between vertical stems. The air itself feels recently rained upon, perpetually damp, yet never quite wet enough to pool for long.

And the plants have eaten the planet.

They are not trees. They are not even coherent organisms in the way we understand them. What grows here is a single, turbulent super-organism that has learned to wear the shapes of an entire biosphere. Vast pale blades, each wider than a city boulevard, rise like frozen waves, their surfaces rippled with veins thick as aqueducts. Between them hang curtains of moss so dense they have become cliffs. Tendrils thicker than redwoods corkscrew upward for kilometers, then abruptly abort and fall as fresh, living wreckage. Every surface is either smooth living skin or furred with minute translucent hairs that breathe.

The vegetation is total, yet the density of the place is almost nothing. The scale is so immense that the living mass is spread thin across an emptiness that feels like the inside of a bell. You can stand on the broad back of a leaf the size of a prairie and see for twenty kilometers in every direction before the next colossal rib or hanging root-system interrupts the view. The air between is cold, bright, and almost sterile.

It is young.

The Undersward is still forming. Fresh growth bursts from older tissue in violent, audible spasms; you can hear the wet crack of new stalks punching through older ones like green bone breaking. Whole districts of the living architecture collapse under their own weight every few days, only for new shoots to erupt from the rotting debris within hours. The ground — where there is ground — is a spongy mulch of fresh death and fresher birth. Nothing here has had time to round its edges. Every leaf-margin is razor-sharp, every fracture glistens with sap the color of quicksilver.

Sound is rare and therefore startling. Mostly there is only the slow drip of condensate, the distant creak of titanic stems adjusting their posture, and the low, constant rustle of leaves the size of ship-sails breathing. Every few hours a new tower of growth tears free somewhere in the haze with a sound like a forest being born in a single scream. Then silence returns, embarrassed.

Fauna is vanishingly rare. What little there is seems embarrassed to exist at all. Pale, translucent things like glass shrimp move along the veins of the great leaves. Once in a great while something enormous and slow turns in the upper vaults — a silhouette like a whale made of moss — and is gone again before the eye can be sure. Most creatures here are no larger than a fingernail and move only when the plant moves them.

Scattered across this turbulent green immensity are the remnants of something that tried to build.

Primitive things. Hand-hewn platforms of pale wood lashed to living stems with braided root-fiber. A spiral stair carved into the side of a single colossal vein, rising three hundred meters before it simply stops, its last step still weeping sap. Rope bridges that lead nowhere. Small shrines grown from living bonsai-like nodes, their tiny altars covered in dew that never evaporates. These artifacts look as though they were made yesterday, or ten thousand years ago; time behaves strangely when nothing ever truly dies.

The rules of the world are simple and merciless:

- Everything grows.
- Everything falls.
- Light is constant.
- Warmth is forbidden.
- Nothing is ever finished.

This is not an ecosystem. It is an event. A continental-scale spasm of becoming that has forgotten how to stop. The Undersward does not dream of balance. It dreams only of more.

And in the bright, cold, dripping quiet between its violent episodes of growth, you can sometimes hear the soft, wet sound of the world digesting its own yesterday so it can become tomorrow.
