The world is called Veyr-Keth, the Hollow Throat.

It is a single, continent-sized canyon carved so recently and so violently into the crust of a cold young planet that its walls still gleam with fracture planes. The rift is so deep and so wide that it has its own weather: a perpetual slow cyclone of freezing mist that spirals down from the unseen lip thousands of meters above, feeding thin, icy rivers that appear and vanish among the vegetation like veins.

Everything else is plant.

There is almost no soil. The living mass itself is the substrate. A single species—perhaps a single individual—has conquered the entire volume. It is not forest. It is not even jungle. It is architecture and body at once. Colossal pale-violet stems, some thicker than cathedrals, rise in spiraling lattices that interlock without touching, forming vast airy halls hundreds of meters high and kilometers across. Between them hang curtains of feathery black moss that drink the condensing mist. Every surface is furred, fronded, or bearded with secondary growth; every column is itself a vertical ecosystem. The plants breathe audibly. At night you can hear the wet clicking of stomatal mouths opening and closing in slow tides.

Because the density parameter is so low, these living megastructures stand apart from one another by distances that feel cathedral-like. Between them lie immense volumes of near-empty cold air, so clear that sound travels terrifying distances. That is why the place is loud. The vegetation generates its own noise: creaking as stems expand in rare pulses of warmth, the constant hiss of mist condensing on billions of leaves, the low organ-deep rumble of sap moving through vascular highways the size of subway tunnels. Wind, funneled down the canyon, plays the lattice like a harp. Every few hours a distant “branch” (really a secondary trunk fifty meters thick) tears free under its own weight and falls for a full minute, crashing through other structures in a slow-motion avalanche of wet green thunder that can be heard for a hundred kilometers.

The cold is constant and wet. Temperature rarely rises above freezing; most precipitation arrives as sleet or heavy, supercooled mist that clings and grows into strange ice-ferns before melting again. Rivers are born high on the living walls where condensation gathers into silver threads, trickle downward through moss chutes, pool in vast cupped leaves the size of lakes, then overflow and fall as cold white curtains into the abyss below. Some of these waterfalls never reach the bottom; they are caught and drunk by lower tiers of the plant.

Fauna is abundant but eerily sparse per cubic kilometer. The animals are almost all aerial or arboreal. Vast flocks of membranous things that look like manta rays crossed with kelp drift on the internal thermals, singing in minor chords that echo for hours. Packs of glass-shelled herbivores the size of horses crawl along the stems, their feet making a sound like a thousand chandeliers being gently tapped. Predators are few but enormous: pale, many-winged things that fold themselves into crevices of the lattice and wait, perfectly camouflaged as clumps of frost-covered moss until something warm-blooded passes beneath.

Light is almost nonexistent. The sky—if it can still be called that—is lost beyond the upper reaches of the living architecture, and what faint illumination exists is bioluminescent. Most of it comes from the plants themselves: soft cyan nodules that pulse in slow sympathetic waves, like enormous neurons firing across the canyon. Occasionally a vein of reflective crystal buried in a stem catches this glow and throws it across the void in sudden, silent searchlight beams that last only seconds before the geometry shifts and the light vanishes again.

Scattered through this living immensity are the marks of something older and smaller. Primitive. A single basalt ziggurat, half-swallowed by violet stems, its steps now used as nesting ledges by the manta-things. Rope bridges of braided lichen strung between distant columns, maintained by beings who have never been seen clearly. A ring of standing stones balanced impossibly on a leaf the size of a town square, each stone carved with spiral petroglyphs that seem to move when the light pulses. These traces are rare, widely separated, and look both reverent and afraid.

The world is young, active, and hungry. Every few years the entire lattice undergoes a synchronized growth spasm. Stems thicken overnight, new vaults form, old ones are crushed. Rivers are rerouted. Whole ecosystems are born and drowned in a single season. The noise during these events is deafening: a wet, groaning, orchestral roar that vibrates the ribs of anything with bones.

And yet the emptiness between the living megastructures remains. You can stand on a ledge of moss the size of a city block, look out across kilometers of cold violet air, and feel that the place is still forming itself, still deciding what it will become. The plants seem to be listening to that emptiness, and the emptiness is answering back in wind and water and the slow, patient ticking of ice.

This is not a forest you walk through.

This is a cathedral that is growing you.
