The place is called the Black Veins.

It is a high, warm plateau the size of a small continent, lifted so far above the world that its own weather systems have evolved in isolation. The air is thin but never cold; instead it holds a dry, furnace-like heat that radiates upward from the stone itself. Night, however, is absolute. There is no moon here worth mentioning and the stars are faint, as though seen through soot-stained glass. For most of the cycle the plateau lies under a thick, motionless darkness that feels almost mineral.

The land itself is ancient erosion perfected. Red-black basalt and ochre sandstone have been worked by wind and rare water for so long that every slope and canyon has settled into its final, inevitable shape. Edges are rounded like old bones. Cliffs wear long, vertical striations that look like the claw-marks of something planetary. Nothing here is still forming; everything already *is*.

Water reveals itself only in disciplined, miserly ways. Deep fractures in the stone hold narrow, ink-dark rivers that appear and vanish without warning. Condensation gathers on the undersides of overhangs at the coldest hour before dawn, forming trembling beads that fall into moss-lined cups. Every few weeks a slow, warm rain drifts across the plateau like a sigh, barely enough to darken the dust, yet the stone drinks it so completely that within an hour the ground is dry again. The rivers never reach the edge; they sink instead into hidden aquifers that feed groves of pale, rubbery vegetation growing in the deepest cracks.

Vegetation is sparse but not absent. It grows in disciplined archipelagoes: low, iron-hard shrubs with leaves like flakes of rust, stands of bulbous succulents the color of bruises, and, strangest of all, the lamp-moss. This last is the only thing that produces its own light (a weak, cold bioluminescence the color of moonlight on steel). Where the lamp-moss grows thick it creates faint, floating galaxies along the canyon floors, visible only from above. From the ground they are invisible until you are almost standing in them.

Life here is loud in its silence. The fauna is abundant and almost entirely nocturnal or crepuscular. Vast herds of blind, six-legged grazers the size of cattle move through the dark in near-total quiet, their soft footfalls and the wet click of their mouthparts the only sounds. Above them glide things that are neither birds nor bats but something older, leather kites with luminous veins in their wings that pulse in slow code. In the deeper ravines live colonies of something that sounds like wet coughing laughter; no one has ever seen them clearly. The predator-prey cycles are stately, almost geological. A kill might take three nights to finish. Nothing here is in a hurry.

The weirdness is subtle but constant. The stone sometimes sings (low, sub-audible notes that make teeth ache). Compass needles spin in lazy circles for no reason. Certain ridges cause echo delay; you speak and your voice returns half a minute later, deeper, as though the land had chewed on it. Most unsettling are the engines.

No one built them.

Across the plateau, half-buried in canyon walls or rising from the desert floor like broken teeth, are titanic industrial mechanisms that predate any conceivable civilization. Some are clearly pumps, rust-red and motionless, their intake pipes reaching down into aquifers no living thing has ever mapped. Others resemble half-excavated factories, vast halls of corroded iron and brass standing open to the sky, their purpose forgotten even by the stone that has grown around them. They do not rust further. They do not move (except once every few years, when one will cough to life for thirty heartbeats, release a gout of warm, mineral-scented steam, and fall silent again). The animals treat them as just another kind of cliff.

The whole plateau feels like a machine that has achieved ecology. Industry and wilderness have bled into one another so completely that the difference no longer matters. The darkness is not empty; it is full of slow, patient motion. The quiet is not absence of sound but the sound of something thinking.

If you stand on the highest weathered promontory at the hour when the lamp-moss is brightest and the warm wind dies, you can sometimes hear the land breathing: a low, rhythmic hiss from a thousand hidden vents, the sigh of ancient pumps still dutifully moving water beneath a continent of stone and starless night.

This is a place that has already ended and is still, stubbornly, continuing.  
It does not need you.  
It has its own work to do.
