The place is called the Weeping Grove of Veyr, though nothing here has ever been named by a human tongue.

It sits in a high, folded crease of the world where the mountains forget to keep climbing and instead settle into a shallow, secret bowl no wider than a modest cathedral. Everything here feels both intimate and ancient, as though the land itself has been holding its breath for ten thousand years and has only just begun to exhale.

Water is everywhere, but never in a hurry. It emerges as slow, glassy beads from the porous bones of the pale cliffs that ring the grove. The rock itself weeps—thick, mineral tears that slide down moss-blackened faces in perpetual silent lines, gathering in emerald pools before slipping underground again. The air is so saturated that droplets condense on every leaf and every strand of your hair the moment you stand still. Breathing feels like drinking.

The vegetation is outrageously lush for such a high place. Great ferns taller than a man unfurl like green sails. Moss grows in velvet shelves thick enough to sleep on. Trees—stunted, ancient, bonsai-like giants—grow only in sparse, deliberate company. Each one stands ten or twelve paces from the next, as though they have long ago agreed on the proper distance for conversation. Their bark is the color of wet slate and drips with slow amber resin that smells of crushed thyme and iron. Between them, the ground is mostly bare black soil and pale stones, giving the eye long, breathing corridors of emptiness.

The light is normal, even generous—high, diffuse daylight filtered through a perpetual low mist that drifts in slow, hypnotic currents. It makes every droplet on every leaf flash like a tiny lantern. Shadows are soft and generous.

Sound is strangely, almost violently loud. The constant dripping from the weeping cliffs becomes a scattered percussion that echoes inside the bowl of stone until the grove rings like a wet cathedral. Every droplet that falls from a high leaf into a still pool produces a clear, bell-like note that hangs in the air far longer than physics should allow. When the wind moves (which it does only once or twice a day, and then only gently), it sets the giant ferns clapping against one another with a sound like distant applause. The acoustics of the place are uncanny; a single footstep on wet moss can sound like a door closing in a stone hall.

Fauna is rare, but what exists feels important. Tiny iridescent frogs the color of fresh blood sit motionless on the weeping rock, drinking the mineral tears. Once every few hours a solitary white bird, wingspan no wider than your outstretched arms, glides silently between the trees without seeming to flap. Its eyes are milky and blind. It navigates by the sound of the dripping.

There is a strangeness here that settles into the bones. The trees are not quite trees. Their trunks are faintly ribbed, like the throats of enormous reptiles. Sometimes, if you watch one long enough, you can see the bark breathing—slow, patient inhalations that take minutes to complete. The moss, when stepped on, remembers the shape of your foot for half a day before slowly forgetting. And every so often the entire grove seems to sigh at once; every droplet pauses mid-fall for half a heartbeat before continuing, as though the place itself just remembered something ancient and sad.

Nothing here has been built. There are no paths, no ruins, no carvings, no tools, no evidence that anything with hands has ever stood here longer than it takes for the moss to forget a footprint. The land is in perfect, weathered balance with itself—established, patient, and deeply, quietly alive.

If you stay long enough (and the place encourages you to stay), you begin to feel the slow gradient of its thoughts. The trees are considering something. The water is remembering something. The mist is waiting for something.

And the dripping never stops.
