The place is called the Hollow Foundry.

It stretches across a low, wide basin that once might have been a vast inland delta. Now the ground is entirely mineral: black and rust-red basalt, obsidian-slick in places, porous volcanic tuff in others. Water is everywhere without ever becoming a lake or river. It beads on every surface, drips from overhangs, seeps upward through capillary cracks, and hangs in the air as warm, metallic mist. The humidity is so high it feels like breathing soup. Every few hours the condensing atmosphere releases slow, fat drops that fall straight down through the dimness like warm oil.

There is no visible plant life. Not a blade of grass, not a patch of moss. The only living things are the machines and the things that live inside them.

The city is immense yet strangely sparse. Great half-ruined industrial complexes stand kilometers apart, connected by elevated rail lines that have not carried a train in centuries. Between them lie wide, empty tracts of cracked paving and wind-scoured slag. The scale is regional: you can stand on the roof of one derelict smelter and see the silhouettes of three others on the hazy horizon, yet nothing feels crowded. The emptiness between structures is absolute.

The heat is oppressive. The air shimmers at ground level. Every metal surface is warm to the touch; some pipes and vats are hot enough to blister skin. Steam rises in lazy pillars where groundwater meets buried coolant lines or ancient forges still glowing faintly under layers of scale.

It is an industrial city that has been left to run on its own for so long it has achieved a kind of geological equilibrium. Pipes have burst and healed over with mineral crusts. Conveyor belts have seized and become terraces where condensate gathers in perfect rows of shallow pools. Cranes have frozen mid-swing and now serve as nesting spines for something else.

The fauna is overwhelming in both number and presence, yet the place remains mostly quiet.

They are not animals in any familiar sense. They are thermophilic megafauna that have evolved to live inside the machines. Vast, pale, soft-bodied things the size of buses that breathe superheated air and drink liquid rust. They move with the slow dignity of continents. You see them as dim, glistening shapes sliding along elevated ducts, or coiled inside the skeletal remains of blast furnaces like enormous larvae. Their bodies make almost no sound; only the wet, rhythmic suck of respiration and the faint metallic ticking of their cilia against iron.

Thousands of smaller creatures—some no larger than a hand, others the size of horses—scuttle, drip, and glide through the infrastructure. They are all variations on the same wet, heat-loving biology: translucent skin, no eyes, organs visible as slow pulses of darker fluid. They feed on the endless chemical gradients produced by the decaying industry: sulfur, iron, arsenic, heat itself. Their combined breathing and movement produces a constant background of soft organic dripping, the sound of wet flesh sliding over wet metal. It is never loud. The basin swallows noise.

The light is perpetually dim, the color of old brass and dried blood. A thick haze filters everything. There is no sun visible, only a uniform, heat-bleached sky that glows dully like the inside of a kiln. At “night” the temperature drops by perhaps eight degrees and the mist thickens until the city becomes a maze of silhouettes and dripping echoes.

Everything feels established, ancient, in balance. The machines have become reefs. The creatures have become the new ecology. Pipes that once carried molten steel now carry slow rivers of living fluid. Ventilation shafts exhale warm, sweet-smelling vapor that the creatures follow like migrating birds. Entire districts have collapsed into gentle rubble that has been colonized by layered generations of translucent nests.

There are rules here, though they are not written.

Nothing dry is allowed to remain. Anything that does not conduct heat or moisture eventually dissolves or is broken down by secretions. Movement must be slow and deliberate; sudden motion draws the attention of the largest thermovores, who will investigate with the lazy curiosity of something that has never known real predators. Sound is treated as waste heat; the quieter you are, the less of your energy you broadcast. The city itself seems to prefer silence. Even the wind is sluggish and damp.

You can walk for hours along the old service roads without seeing another human-shaped thing, only the slow, glistening passage of something vast sliding between towers, the steady drip of warm water from every cornice, and the distant, almost tender sound of a thousand soft bodies breathing inside the iron. The Hollow Foundry does not hate you. It simply has no need of you. It has achieved its own wet, hot, eternal equilibrium, and it will continue its slow industrial digestion of the world long after anything that remembers its original purpose has turned to condensate and memory.
