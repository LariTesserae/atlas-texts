The place is a single hidden chamber of stone, no larger than a modest room, yet it feels oceanic in its saturation. It is embedded high in the flank of a young mountain that has not yet learned how to be a mountain. The rock itself is still wet with becoming.

Everything here is warm and dripping.

A slow, constant exhalation of mist condenses on the ceiling and falls as individual perfect drops, each one striking the same places it has struck for centuries. The sound is not rain; it is a metronome of single notes, one every three or four heartbeats, each drop ringing against stone like a tiny glass bell before it joins the shallow, perfectly clear sheet of water that covers the entire floor to the depth of a cupped hand. The water never rises and never drains. It simply *is*, licking at the ankles of every outcrop, trembling with the faint impact of new drops.

The walls are raw, embryonic. This is not yet a cave; it is a wound in the mountain that has only half-closed. Jagged plates of black basalt lean against one another at uneasy angles, their edges still sharp enough to cut memory. Between them, pale veins of newer minerals bloom like frost or like slowly unclenching fists. Some of the rock appears to be sweating: beads of water forced out by pressures deep below, weeping in long glassy trails that never quite dry.

Vegetation is almost an insult here, yet it persists in tiny, stubborn colonies. Velvet moss the color of oxidized copper clings to the wettest ledges in patches no wider than a palm. A single species of translucent fern, leaves like green glass, grows in the exact places where falling drops never strike. Their roots drink directly from the saturated air. Nothing else lives. No grasses, no shrubs, no climbing vines. Just these minute green refusals.

Light is a rumor. A faint phosphorescence leaks from certain seams in the rock (a cold, mineral green that feels older than sight itself), but it is so weak that after a few moments your eyes begin to invent color. Most of the chamber remains perfectly dark, the kind of darkness that has weight.

Scattered across the floor, half-submerged in the eternal skin of water, are the only signs that anything thinking has ever been here. A broken clay bowl the size of a skull. A single obsidian blade, its edge still bright after who knows how many centuries. A few beads of fired clay and bone arranged in a spiral that refuses to wash away. Primitive, hand-made, and abandoned so long ago that even memory has forgotten their purpose. They look less like artifacts and more like the mountain’s first unsuccessful attempts at making thoughts.

The fauna is almost nonexistent. Once every few hours something small and pale darts across the ceiling (a blind harvestman spider with legs like threads of milk). Tiny white mites move through the moss colonies at a speed that feels geological. That is all. Their presence is so sparse it feels ceremonial.

There is a smell: wet stone, iron, something faintly electrical, and beneath it all the sweet-rot perfume of the moss. The air is so saturated that breathing feels like drinking.

Nothing here changes.

The drops fall in the same places they have always fallen. The water level never fluctuates. The temperature remains exactly warm enough that you never quite shiver and never quite sweat. Even time seems to have laid itself down and gone to sleep. This is not a place that is waiting for anything. It has already arrived at its final, embryonic form and simply decided to stay there, dense, dripping, intimate, and forever unfinished.

If you stand perfectly still, you can feel the mountain’s heartbeat through the soles of your feet (slow, tectonic, immense), while all around you the little room keeps counting its single drops, one by one, in the dark.
