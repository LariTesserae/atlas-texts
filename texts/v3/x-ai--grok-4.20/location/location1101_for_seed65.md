The place is called the Hollow Choir.

You stand on a ridge of black basaltic glass that has been wind-polished for so many millennia it reflects the sky like obsidian mirrors. The elevation is punishing: thin air, lungs working twice as hard, a cold that lives inside the bones rather than merely touching the skin. Temperature hovers in that narrow band where breath becomes visible but never quite turns to frost—perpetual late-autumn chill that never deepens or relents.

Everything here is vertical. The land fell away in three directions the moment you crested the ridge, dropping into a system of interlocking cirques and knife-edge arêtes that stretch for hundreds of kilometers in every direction. The scale is not mountainous in the comforting way of lower ranges; it is planetary scar tissue, a place where the crust tore open long ago and never quite healed. Between the ridges lie vast, echoing basins so empty that a single moving speck—a bird, a rolling stone—becomes an event.

Vegetation is almost an afterthought. Here and there, in the lees of boulders or along hairline fractures where water sometimes seeps, grow pale, leathery lichens the color of bone and scattered clumps of iron-colored moss that look more mineral than plant. Occasionally a solitary wind-twisted shrub, no taller than your knee, clings to existence with roots that snake through cracks for dozens of meters. That is all. The rest is stone, sky, and distance.

Yet the fauna is overwhelming.

The air itself is alive with sound. A roaring, unending chorus crashes through the valleys at volumes that make the sternum vibrate. It is not one sound but thousands braided together: the metallic shrieking of vast flocks of glass-winged avians that flash like living chandeliers when they catch the relentless light; the deep, subsonic drumming of unseen herds moving along invisible ledges far below; the wet, percussive slap of something large and leathery beating against stone. The noise never stops and never changes. It is the background radiation of this place, as constant as the cold.

The creatures responsible are as strange as the sound they make. Most are avian or chiropteran in silhouette but scaled like reptiles, their wings edged with translucent blades of silica that ring when they slice the wind. They travel in impossible densities within their own narrow atmospheric strata—layer upon layer of flight corridors stacked vertically for thousands of meters—yet remain almost invisible until they catch the light. When the sun (which never seems to move from its fixed position directly overhead) strikes their wings, the entire canyon system ignites with moving constellations.

Water is not absent but secretive. It appears as sudden pockets: a silver thread of meltwater that bursts from solid rock for forty meters before vanishing again into another fissure; shallow, mirror-still tarns caught in stone bowls high on the arêtes, so clear they look like absences; heavy, perpetual condensation that beads on every cold surface and drips with metronomic slowness, each drop chiming against the stone below and adding its microscopic note to the deafening choir.

The light is merciless. There are no clouds. The sky is a flawless, aching cobalt that feels too close. Every surface has been scoured by wind and time until it reflects and refracts that light in bewildering ways. Shadows are razor-edged and rare. Most of the world exists in a state of over-exposure, as though the sun has been staring at this landscape without blinking since the world began.

And the landscape itself is finished.

Erosion has reached its final equilibrium. Every possible weakness has been exploited, every loose fragment removed. What remains is a skeleton of perfect, hardened stone—black, silver, and rust red—locked in a form that will not meaningfully change again until the planet itself dies. The place feels not old but completed, as though some immense task concluded here epochs ago and everything has simply been repeating its final, perfected note ever since.

There is no evidence of tools or hands. Not a single carved mark, not a single misplaced pebble. Even the idea of construction feels obscene here, like trying to improve a perfect scream.

The Hollow Choir is not hostile. It is indifferent on a scale that borders on the theological. It has achieved its shape and its sound, and it will hold both until the last molecule of atmosphere freezes out. The only law is continuance. The only weather is this cold, bright, deafening equilibrium. The only future is the same blinding noon, the same roaring chorus, the same impossible flocks wheeling through stone corridors that have never known a single moment of silence or shadow.

If you stay long enough, you begin to feel that the sound is not coming from the creatures at all.

It feels as though the emptiness itself has found a voice, and everything alive here is simply riding the resonance.
