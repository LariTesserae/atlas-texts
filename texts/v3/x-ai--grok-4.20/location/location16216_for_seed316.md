The place has long forgotten it was ever a planet.

It calls itself the Sump, though nothing here speaks in words. What remains is a single, low, continent-wide wetland that has been eroding for so long that its original geology has collapsed into a single, saturated, primordial skin. Elevation barely exists; the entire world lies within a few meters of the same sluggish datum, as though some ancient catastrophe pressed it flat and then abandoned it.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not flow so much as pulse. The ground is not ground but a warm, spongy membrane of peat, microbial mats, and living stone that sweats. Every few paces the surface splits and clear, blood-warm water wells up like tears from pores in the world’s oldest flesh. In other places the water simply condenses out of the air itself, forming slow silver beads that slide upward along stalks of half-rotted vegetation before falling again in the opposite direction. Rivers here are not channels; they are temporary agreements between moisture and gravity that last minutes before dissolving back into mist.

Vegetation is sparse yet violently dense where it occurs. What grows does so in isolated, jealous clumps—enormous black reeds with translucent leaves like jellyfish bells, squat fungal towers that breathe visibly, and low carpets of liverwort so thick they form knee-high continents of their own. Between these living islands stretches bare, glistening mud the color of old bronze, etched with fractal patterns left by evaporating water over ten million years. Every clump of life is overcrowded with itself; roots, stems, spores, and slime all fighting for the same few cubic centimeters of saturated matter. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is thin.

The air is warm, heavy, and dim. Light arrives bruised—filtered through perpetual low cloud and the exhalations of the marsh itself—giving everything the quality of a photograph left too long in developer. There is no true sky, only layered atmospheres that argue with one another. Sometimes the upper haze rains upward. Sometimes the lower fog ignites with slow, silent lightning that never reaches the ground.

Sound is apocalyptic.

The Sump roars. It is not one roar but ten thousand braided together: the wet thunder of collapsing peat domes, the ceaseless hiss of condensing vapor, the deep organ-note of gas escaping from thousand-meter-deep microbial tombs, the wet slapping of vast unseen membranes flexing beneath the crust. Every few minutes something immense gives way far off—perhaps an entire living hill slumping into a newly opened cavity—and the concussion rolls across the flatness like the death-cry of a continent. The noise never drops below the level of pain. After days (if days can be said to exist), visitors stop hearing it and begin to feel it as pressure on the bones.

Fauna is rare but colossal when it appears. Most creatures are no longer recognizably animal. They are extensions of the marsh: translucent, many-chambered things that drift through the warm water like living zeppelins, filter-feeding on the constant bacterial soup; things that look like mobile forests until they rear up on jointed root-legs and scream with the voice of tearing turf; things that might once have been birds but now exist as clouds of identical pale moths that travel as a single superorganism, blotting out what little light there is before dispersing again into nothing.

Reality itself is exhausted here.

The longer one remains, the more the world begins to edit. Horizons fold. Distance becomes negotiable. A clump of reeds you have been walking toward for six hours may suddenly be behind you, having apparently migrated while your back was turned. Cause and effect grow bored with each other. Sometimes the deafening sound stops for three full seconds and in that vacuum you can hear your own memories playing at the edge of audibility, slightly out of sync. Then the roar returns, louder, as though punishing the silence.

Scattered across this turbulent, packed, ancient flatness are the Built—pathetic, almost apologetic remnants. A single basalt pillar half-swallowed by peat, its carvings worn to nothing. The broken spine of a bridge that ends in mid-air above a perfectly circular pool that was never a lake. The fossilized skeleton of something that might have been a city, now indistinguishable from mineral formations except that every few centuries it still tries to complete one final arch before the marsh pulls it down again. These fragments are so rare that finding one feels like a personal event in the life of the planet.

There are no tools. There never were. Technology cannot survive the Sump; metal rusts to lace in minutes, plastics deliquesce into nutrient slurry, thought itself begins to decompose. Only biology—raw, endless, shameless—remains valid.

This is not a dying world. It is a world that has outlived death and kept going, a warm, wet, deafening, overcrowded ossuary where the boundary between living and fossil has rotted away. Every inch is occupied, every moment is turbulent, and the only constant is the slow, patient, primordial pulse of something that was never born and will never, under any circumstances, be allowed to end.
