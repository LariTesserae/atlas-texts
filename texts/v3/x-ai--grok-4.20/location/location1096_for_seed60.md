The place is called the Bloomworks.

It is a low, sunken basin no larger than a modest cathedral, yet so densely woven with vegetation that the eye cannot find a single straight line longer than a forearm. Every surface, every volume, is claimed by living plant matter. There is no soil visible; the ground itself is a thick, breathing mat of interlaced rhizomes, mosses, liverworts, and pale fungal sheets that have grown together for so many centuries they function as one continuous organism. Vines as thick as ship’s cables loop overhead in slow arches, their surfaces beaded with perpetual condensation. From these cables hang curtains of finer creeper, each tendril tipped with translucent bladders that swell and shrink like lungs.

The air is warm, heavy, and wet. It does not so much rain as remember rain; droplets form on every leaf and root and fall in slow, deliberate intervals. The atmosphere itself seems to be sweating. Moisture condenses on the skin within moments of entry. The smell is overwhelming: green, mineral, faintly electrical, like crushed stems mixed with hot transformer oil.

Scattered among the vegetable architecture are the remnants of an old industrial plant that has been swallowed whole. A rusted boiler the size of a carriage lies half-dissolved into the root mass, its iron surface now a trellis for liver-colored orchids. Overhead, corroded pipework weaves through the canopy like metal arteries. Some of the larger stems have grown *through* abandoned pressure valves, splitting the brass open in slow motion over decades until the metal petals curl outward like grotesque bronze flowers. A single brick smokestack, only twenty feet tall, rises near the center; its top is crowned with a living sphere of bromeliads that have fused into a single dripping mass.

The sound is constant and loud, yet never hurried. It is the sound of a thousand small hydraulic events happening at once: the wet click of opening seed pods, the low gurgle of sap moving through vessels thicker than a man’s thigh, the steady drip-drip-drip from ten thousand leaf-tips, the distant mechanical heartbeat of something still alive deep inside the root-ball — perhaps an old pump, perhaps a colony of specialized insects that have learned to mimic the rhythm of forgotten engines. Every few minutes a low, metallic groan travels through the structure as some buried girder finally yields another inch to the patient pressure of expanding wood.

Fauna is rare. Occasionally a pale, translucent millipede the length of a forearm ripples across a leaf the size of a dinner table, or a cloud of minute white moths drifts between shafts of normal, slightly greenish light. Mostly the place feels watched by the vegetation itself. The leaves turn toward movement with a deliberation that suggests awareness.

Everything is in balance, but the balance is strange. The industrial relics have not been destroyed; they have been *incorporated*. Copper wiring has become trellises for parasitic vines that glow faint blue at night. Old turbine housings serve as water cisterns where tiny ecosystems have reached climax inside the dark iron bells. The entire basin is one slow, cathedral-sized digestive tract: plant eating rust, rust feeding minerals to plant, plant exhaling moisture that rusts new metal, and so on in a cycle that has lasted long enough for the distinction between machine and organism to lose all meaning.

The boundaries are clear only because the density beyond the basin drops away. Step twenty paces past the outermost curtain of hanging roots and the world becomes ordinary again. Inside the Bloomworks, however, nothing is ordinary. The air itself feels pregnant. Time moves at the pace of sap. The longer one remains, the more one begins to suspect that the plants are not simply growing *around* the old factory.

They are remembering how to be a factory.  
And the factory is learning, very slowly, how to bloom.
