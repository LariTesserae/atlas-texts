The place has no name, and it has never needed one.

It is a single, breathing continent of plant-flesh that has only just begun to cool. The land is young—geologically adolescent—still raw from whatever cataclysm or extrusion first forced it upward from the black water beneath. Its scale is wrong; it feels like an entire world that has been folded into the shape of a marsh the size of a small planet. You can walk for weeks and never leave the same slow, humid thought.

Everything is warm, but not hot. A steady 28 °C that never varies, held in perfect equilibrium by the metabolism of the single organism that is the land itself. The air is so saturated that water does not fall as rain; it simply decides, here and there, to become liquid and slide down the surfaces of things. Every leaf, every trunk, every spongy shelf exhales moisture back into the dark, so the atmosphere is perpetually condensing on itself. Fat droplets bead, tremble, and crawl like slow silver insects before they merge into the shallow, warm sheets of water that cover nearly everything. The ground is never dry. The ground is never solid.

There is no soil. There are only plants growing on plants growing on plants. The substrate is living. What looks like black earth is a dense mat of root-hairs and mycorrhizal felt, kilometers thick, pulsing with slow peristalsis. Trees do not rise from the ground; they erupt laterally from the sides of other trees, or hang downward in vast curtains that root themselves again in the canopy below. Every surface is colonized. Lianas the thickness of tram cars braid into one another until they become bridges, balconies, entire districts of braided vegetable architecture. Orchids the size of houses bloom only once every few centuries; when they do, their petals open with the soft sigh of releasing trapped air, and then remain motionless for another lifetime.

It is almost completely dark.

A dim, bioluminescent mucus coats the undersides of the upper canopy layers, giving off the color of old pearls and dying embers—enough to see shapes, never enough to see distance. The light feels guilty, as though it is embarrassed to exist. Most of the world therefore lives in a perpetual heavy twilight that thickens into true darkness only a few hundred meters down through the stratified levels of leaves. There, in the deep galleries, the only illumination comes from occasional colonies of pale fungi that glow with the cold blue of television static.

Technology has arrived, but it did not arrive as visitors. It arrived as infrastructure.

Somewhere in the early formation of this place—perhaps only decades ago, perhaps only days in its own frozen time—vast industrial organs were extruded from the living mass itself. No one built them. The plant learned them. Enormous iron-black pipes, sweating with condensation, rise like veins through the braided trunks. Somewhere far below, in chambers no eye has ever seen, peristaltic pumps the size of cathedrals thump with geological slowness, moving warm nutrient slurry from one layer of the organism to another. Dynamo-fungi spin inside hollowed-out gourds, generating faint electric fields that make the air smell of ozone and wet iron. Every few kilometers a colossal fleshy engine-room beats like a heart the size of a mountain, driving incomprehensible chemical factories that have no purpose except to keep the equilibrium perfect. Their exhaust is clean water and more plant.

There are no animals. Not one.

The silence is total except for the sounds the plant makes to itself: the soft creak of cellulose under tension, the distant wet thud of a pump valve the size of a house opening and closing once every six hours, the occasional sigh of gas released from fermentation chambers deep in the root mat. These noises arrive without echo, swallowed instantly by the endless layers of living felt.

Nothing ever changes.

The same droplet has been sliding down the same leaf for seventeen years. The same pale orchid has been half-open since before your grandparents were born. The same iron pipe has been dripping at the exact same rate, in the exact same place, since it first hardened out of vegetable resin and rust. The continent is in perfect dynamic equilibrium—an organism that reached maturity and then chose to stop. It has become a closed loop so complete that even time has been digested and turned into more plant.

Yet the landscape still feels newborn. Its edges are sharp, almost violent. Newly uplifted cliffs of living wood rise in clean geometric fractures, their wounds still weeping clear sap that never quite clots. Enormous shelves of bracket fungus hang over black lagoons like the roofs of half-finished cathedrals. Everything looks as though it finished forming only yesterday and then immediately decided never to move again.

You can stand in one place for a thousand years and the only thing that will change is the slow, guilty glow of the mucus above you, pulsing once every few days like the breath of something that dreams but never wakes.

This is not a jungle.  
This is a single vast creature that has industrialized its own body, wrapped itself in perfect darkness, and chosen to remain young forever while never changing again.

And it is still growing, very, very slowly, outward into the warm black water that has no shore.
