The world is called the **Drowned Lattice**.

It is not an ocean. It is a warm, black, infinitely patient liquid that has long ago swallowed every valley and plain, leaving only a single, low-lying region the size of a small continent still partially emergent. Here the water does not lie flat; it hangs. Great curtains and veils of it drift between half-submerged towers of living bone and stone, forming slow-moving walls that breathe. Rain falls upward in certain districts. In others the liquid simply forgets gravity for hours at a time and becomes a trembling, silver-black fog that tastes of iron and old flowers.

Everything is warm—blood-warm, breath-warm. The temperature barely fluctuates between what would be midnight and what would be noon, because there is no sun and no sky, only an endless stratified darkness above and the faint bioluminescent pulse of the world below.

The city—if it can still be called a city—has been underwater so long that it has stopped decaying and has begun to negotiate. Towers of forgotten civilizations lean against one another like old men, their stone and coral and chitinous exoskeletons fused by millennia of slow chemical marriage. Bridges of calcified tube-worm colonies span the liquid gaps. Streets have become canyons of sediment and memory. Every surface is densely colonized: not by plants (there is almost no true vegetation, only a thin black lichen that grows in perfect spirals), but by layered, overlapping life. Barnacle-like colonies the color of old bruises. Veils of translucent polyps that open and close in perfect synchrony. Mats of pale anemones that function as lungs for the stone itself.

The fauna is everywhere at once. Schools of blind, luminous fish move like slow nebulae between the towers. Things that are half eel, half ribbon of living night slide along the walls, tasting the memories that have soaked into the masonry. Vast, cathedral-sized jellyforms drift overhead, their bells pulsing with soft infrasound that you feel in your teeth more than hear. Predators the length of freight trains hunt in packs, yet the ecosystem is so ancient and balanced that nothing ever truly dies of violence—only of agreement. When something is eaten, the water itself seems to sigh in relief.

It is never loud. The dominant sound is a low, wet hush, like listening to the inside of a seashell the size of a mountain. Occasionally a pillar of coral collapses under its own accumulated weight and the sound is almost tender, a muffled boom followed by a glittering rain of debris that takes hours to settle. Bioluminescent plankton flare in slow-motion constellations whenever something large moves through them, then fade again into velvet dark.

The architecture is urban in the way a reef is urban: organized, hierarchical, ancient, and utterly biological. There are districts. There are boulevards lined with what were once streetlamps but are now petrified trees of glass sponge. There are amphitheaters where the seats are giant clams that have never closed. Whole neighborhoods have been claimed by a single organism—an immense, sponge-like intelligence that has worn the shape of tenement blocks so perfectly that you can still see phantom windows and balconies in its porous flesh.

Elevation is low everywhere. The highest points are the crests of submerged hills that break the surface of the liquid sky like the backs of drowned whales. From those rare ridges you can look out across a midnight panorama of hanging oceans, drifting curtains of water, and the soft constellation of living city-lights moving slowly below—each light a creature, each creature part of the slow, deliberate metabolism of a place that has achieved a state of perfect, warm, dark equilibrium.

Nothing here is new. Nothing here is in a hurry. The Drowned Lattice has been negotiating with itself for so long that erosion, construction, and biology have become the same continuous process. The city is not ruined. It is not alive. It is simply in balance, and that balance is vast, dense, warm, and quietly, impossibly strange.

If you stay long enough, the water begins to learn the shape of your breathing. And eventually, with great patience and no cruelty at all, the Lattice will ask you whether you would like to become infrastructure. Most things eventually say yes.
