The place is called the **Hollow Basilica of Perpetual Condensation**.

It is not large in the way a mountain or a city is large; it is cathedral-scale, perhaps the size of three football fields laid end to end, yet its ceiling vanishes so high above you that the eye refuses the distance. The entire structure is entirely built, every surface machined or cast or riveted. There is no natural stone or soil. Even the air feels engineered.

You stand on a grilled iron floor that stretches away in every direction, punctuated by vast circular pits where water has collected into perfectly round, black mirrors. These pools never overflow and never dry; they simply breathe. Condensation gathers on the underside of the distant vaults in glassy beads the size of fists, then falls in slow, deliberate plinks that echo for seconds before they strike the iron. The rain is not weather. It is the building sweating.

The temperature is warm in the way a running turbine is warm—thick, oily, alive with low-grade heat. Steam drifts in lazy horizontal sheets, catching the normal, sourceless industrial light that falls from hidden skylights far overhead. That light is the colour of old brass.

Everything here has been weathered for so long that erosion and construction have become the same process. Massive pipelines, thick as train carriages, twist upward in helical spirals that have sagged and settled over centuries into elegant, almost organic curves. Their rivets have grown collars of ferrous crust; their flanges have become balconies for mosses that were never planted but simply arrived, dark green and impossibly lush. Ferns the colour of oxidized copper spill from every joint. Vines thick as a man’s thigh have armoured themselves in calcium scale leached from the concrete that once lined the ducts; now they look like living pipes themselves.

The vegetation is almost insolent in its abundance. It grows only where metal and moisture meet, which is everywhere. Epiphytic orchids cling to the undersides of catwalks. Banks of elephant-ear leaves, veined with rust, tremble above abandoned control panels. Yet between these sudden jungles lie immense volumes of emptiness. You can stand in a cathedral-sized nave of pure air, nothing but distant dripping and the faint smell of ozone and warm iron, before the next thicket of growth appears fifty metres away like an island.

The sound is nearly unbearable. It is not one sound but a stacked, slow orchestra of industrial processes operating at the edge of hearing and far beyond it. Deep beneath the iron floor, planetary-scale gearboxes turn a single degree every hour. You feel the subsonic grind in your teeth. Overhead, enormous ventilation fans the size of houses rotate so gradually that each blade takes twelve minutes to complete its arc; their bearings howl a constant, mournful chord that never resolves. Every few minutes a pressure valve somewhere in the vaults releases a cannon-loud hiss of steam that rolls across the hollow like divine judgment, then fades back into the dripping and the grinding and the insectile ticking of cooling metal.

Fauna is abundant but never hurried. Vast, pale moths with wingspans wider than dinner plates drift between the hanging gardens, their wingbeats audible as soft applause. Things that might once have been lizards have become sinuous, scale-armoured tubes that live inside the steam pipes, emerging only to drink from the condensation pools. There are beetles whose carapaces are perfectly reflective chrome; they scuttle across the floor in small glittering armies, mirroring the rust and the green until they vanish against their own background. None of them seem afraid of you. None of them move quickly. The entire ecology operates on geological patience.

This is a made environment that has forgotten it was ever finished. The original purpose—some titanic atmospheric processor, perhaps, or a continent-scale humidifier—has long become irrelevant. The building now simply *is*, a self-sustaining machine-organism whose only remaining function is to slowly rust, drip, grow, and sing to itself in frequencies that make the bones ache.

If you stay long enough, you begin to notice the strangest rule of the place: nothing here is allowed to finish its gesture. The gears turn but never complete a rotation. The vines climb but never reach the unreachable ceiling. The water falls but the pools never rise. The sound builds and builds and is forever on the verge of becoming language, yet never quite arrives.

It is a cathedral built for condensation, consecrated to rust, and it will still be dripping, still turning, still growing, still deafening itself, long after every living thing that ever walked its iron floor has turned to moss and mineral and memory.
