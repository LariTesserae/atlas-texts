The Plateau of the Hollow Sun is not a desert, though it holds no water. It is not a mountain range in any ordinary sense. It is a continent-wide shelf of living mineral that has forgotten how to need rivers.

At this altitude the air is thin and cold enough that breath becomes a visible thing, a brief silver ghost that vanishes before it can fall. The temperature never quite reaches freezing, yet the cold feels surgical; it pares sensation down to its clearest edges. Light, however, is merciless. A single white sun, far larger and closer than any sun ought to be, hangs in a sky the color of pale violet glass. There are no clouds. There never have been clouds.

The ground itself is the astonishment.

It is not rock. It is not plant. It is both, and has been both for so long that the distinction has worn away. Vast plates of litho-coral rise in gentle, undulating waves, pale bone-white shot through with veins of translucent aquamarine and dusty rose. Their surfaces are not smooth; every square meter is covered in a low, dense fur of crystalline fronds no taller than a knuckle. They shimmer like frost but are warm to the touch, faintly, as though each holds a slow internal pulse. These are the “leaves.” They photosynthesize starlight and the strange radiation that leaks from the world’s own bones.

Between the living reefs stretch kilometers of open bone pavement, so sparsely colonized that the eye keeps mistaking distance for emptiness. The scale is planetary. A single shallow basin visible from any modest rise might be the size of a small earthly country, yet it is only one dimple in a surface that curves away for weeks of walking in every direction. The horizon itself feels wrong; it bends upward, as though the plateau is the skin of something vastly larger that has not yet decided to wake.

Erosion here reached equilibrium long ago. Wind, thin and ceaseless, has polished every exposed edge into soft, almost sensual curves. What little dust exists is not dead mineral but the powdered husks of ancient fronds, so fine it behaves like pollen. It drifts in slow golden sheets that catch the light and turn the air into cathedral shafts even when there are no cathedrals.

Sounds are rare and precious. The wind is too high and thin to roar; it merely sighs across the litho-coral, producing a constant, barely audible chord that shifts in microtonal waves. Every few hours a deeper note appears, like someone running a wet finger along the rim of an enormous glass bowl many kilometers away. These are the breathing vents—natural chimneys where the living crust exhales cold, dry gases from the world’s unfathomable interior chemistry. The vents never erupt. They only sing.

Fauna is vanishingly scarce. Once every few days a observer might witness a flock of glass-winged pseudo-birds, more membrane than flesh, riding the thermal columns on wings that refract the white sun into rainbows. They do not land. They do not seem to eat. They simply are, like thoughts the plateau is having about flight. Sometimes, in the quietest hours, something vast and slow moves beneath the crust. The surface trembles with a sub-audible heartbeat. The fronds ripple in unison for kilometers, then still again. No one has ever seen what causes it.

There is no water, yet the plateau is lush in its own idiom. Every living surface is furred, fringed, spined, or softly pulsing with color. The chemistry is anhydrous and exquisite; silicates and exotic carbonates perform the roles usually reserved for chlorophyll and cellulose. The color palette is gentle and mineral: ivory, frost blue, oxblood, gold, the occasional shocking thread of ultraviolet that human eyes perceive only as an ache behind the forehead.

Nothing here was built. Nothing here was left. The Plateau of the Hollow Sun has simply been becoming itself for longer than time has bothered to count. It is not wilderness. It is a mature, self-regulating physiology on a scale that makes the word “ecosystem” feel like a child’s toy.

And still it changes.

Great slow tides of color sweep across the living plains every equinox as different mineral species bloom in sequence. Dust-laden winds rearrange the pollen dunes into new geometries that last for centuries before the next wind forgets them. Every few thousand years a new species of litho-coral evolves, pushing up through its ancestors like a dream insisting on being remembered.

If you stand still long enough, you can feel the plateau considering you. Not with eyes. Not with mind. With the same patient, geological regard that stone gives to moss, or that moss gives to stone when both have been together since the world was young.

There is no trail. There is no ruin. There is only the bright, cold, singing vastness, breathing through its thousand crystal mouths, wearing its own powdered history like perfume, perfectly balanced between silence and song.
