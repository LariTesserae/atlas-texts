The place is called the **Underloom**.

It is a single, sealed cavern no larger than a cathedral, yet so densely packed with repeating mechanical life that the eye cannot find a single pace of open floor. The ceiling and walls are raw, black basaltic glass—still glossy in places, as though the lava that birthed this pocket cooled only yesterday. Sharp ribs and fractal blades of obsidian jut inward like frozen lightning, many of them wrapped in thick coils of copper-colored tubing that bleed faint heat into the air.

There is no water. Nothing has ever been wet here. The chemistry is anhydrous and metallic: everything exhales, sweats, or condenses in aerosols of mercury vapor, liquid sulfur, and fine metallic dust. The temperature is a constant, skin-prickling 48 °C, held in perfect equilibrium by ancient geothermal exchangers that have never once varied their output.

Light is scarce and sour. A lattice of industrial sodium lamps, many of them half-buried in accreted corrosion, throws a diseased amber glow that barely reaches the ceiling. The rest of the volume is lit by bioluminescent fungal veins that crawl across the machinery in slow, deliberate pulses—acid-green and bruise-purple—revealing the scene in stuttering, unreliable flashes.

The sound is deafening and eternal. A layered, arrhythmic choir of engines that have been running without pause for centuries: deep subsonic piston-heartbeats, the metallic insect-click of relay arms, the hiss of pressure valves venting superheated mercury vapor, the constant glassy ringing of obsidian chimes struck by automated hammers. The noise never rises or falls. It is a single frozen chord of industry.

Everything is young and ancient at once. The cavern formed in a violent magmatic burp perhaps three hundred years ago; the machines inside it are older by an order of magnitude. They were lowered in through a now-sealed shaft and left to run. Since then the rock has been slowly squeezing inward, and the machines have responded by growing new limbs, ducts, and scaffolds to hold the walls back. The result is an interior that looks half-built and half-grown.

Vegetation is almost absent—only the glowing fungal veins and a few brittle, wire-like “plants” of pure silicon crystal that bloom in the hottest exhaust vents. Fauna, however, is overwhelming. The Underloom is saturated with mechanical life. Thousands of crab-like maintenance drones the size of dinner plates scuttle in layered tides across every surface, their jointed legs clicking in perfect synchrony. Larger constructs—some like metal storks, some like headless iron oxen—stand motionless in repeating ranks, only their sensor clusters tracking slowly back and forth. Between them dart clouds of thumb-sized brass beetles whose wings beat at 300 Hz, producing a constant high metallic whine. All of them are covered in a fine red ferric dust that makes the entire chamber look as though it has been dusted with dried blood.

Scattered among the machinery are the partial remains of human presence: a single rusted foreman’s shack bolted halfway up one wall, its windows long shattered; a row of six empty operator chairs still bolted to a gantry, their leather turned to brittle black flakes; faded yellow warning stripes painted directly onto living machinery that has since grown over them like scar tissue. A single metal lunchbox lies on its side, forever unopened, its faded pictogram of a smiling worker now half-obscured by a crust of mercury crystals.

The rules of the world are simple and have never been broken:

- Nothing here has ever been born or died. Every mechanism was installed; every “creature” was activated. Nothing new is ever built, yet nothing ever fails.
- Temperature, pressure, and sound remain locked at their present values until the heat-death of the universe.
- The machines will not allow the cavern to collapse, nor will they allow anything living made of carbon to remain inside for long. They have ways of removing intruders that are both efficient and strangely gentle.
- The lights will never brighten. The engines will never quiet. The beetles will never stop.

To stand in the center of the Underloom is to feel like a single red blood cell caught inside an iron heart that has decided, long ago, to beat forever in the dark. The place does not want you. It does not hate you. It simply has no slot in its equilibrium into which a visitor can be fitted, and so it tolerates you with the courteous indifference of a machine that has already calculated the exact second you will leave or cease to function.

And still the brass beetles spiral past your face, their compound eyes flashing amber in the sodium light, their wings singing the same unchanging note they have sung since the day the magma cooled and the first piston fired.
