The place has no name because names would be swallowed.

You stand inside a living cathedral that is still being born. The ground is not ground. It is a single, colossal organism—call it the Thicket—that has erupted upward from the high plateau in the last few centuries, a geological heartbeat ago. Its body is a dense, wet mat of interwoven roots, stems, and fungal sheets so thoroughly fused that no distinction remains between species or even kingdoms. Every surface sweats. Every surface grows.

The air itself is half water. A warm, heavy mist condenses on every leaf and petal the moment it appears, then drips in continuous silver threads from ten thousand different heights. The droplets strike larger leaves with the sound of struck bells; they fall into hidden wells with the low thud of swallowed stones. The noise never stops. This is the loudness the land chose for itself.

Everything is new. The Thicket is still shoving fresh limbs out of itself in violent, audible spasms. You can watch a pale green column thicker than a redwood burst from the living floor in less than an hour, unfurling at its crown into a balcony of fringed crimson leaves that immediately begin to weep. Minutes later the new tower may split, groan like a ship breaking, and collapse back into the hungry mass below, where it is reabsorbed before the echo fades. Creation and ruin are the same pulse here.

Because the Thicket is total, there is no soil, no stone, no dead substrate. Even the towering cliffs that rise in the middle distance are not rock; they are compressed, mineral-hardened heartwood latticed with quartz-like veins of living silica exuded by the plant itself. These “cliffs” are still growing, still splitting, still dripping. Water pours from their porous faces in glistening curtains that never quite reach the bottom; the mist eats them first.

The scale is open and merciless. From any high rhizome-bridge you can see for many miles: layered galleries of hanging gardens, spiraling root-bridges that arc across canyons of living green, sudden abysses where the Thicket has torn itself open and not yet healed. In the far distance, pale columns of steam rise where new growth is so rapid that the heat of accelerated metabolism boils the ever-present moisture. The horizon itself seems to breathe—rising and falling in slow, tidal surges.

There are no animals. Not one. The only motion belongs to the vegetation and the water it exhales. Yet the air feels crowded. Pollen drifts in golden storms. Spores the size of snowflakes spiral upward on thermals generated by the living heat of the mass. Tendrils thick as cables whip through the mist searching for new purchase, slapping wetly against one another with meaty, rhythmic cracks.

The light is normal, almost ordinary—milky afternoon light filtered through uncountable layers of translucent leaves—yet it arrives strangely. Every droplet suspended in the air becomes a tiny lens. The entire atmosphere glitters and refracts, so that shadows have edges like shattered glass and every color arrives wearing three or four extra hues that flicker at the edge of vision. Sometimes the light thickens until it feels like warm syrup on the skin.

Nothing here was built by hands, yet everything is architecture. The Thicket deliberately folds itself into stairs, colonnades, vaulted naves, spiraling ramps, and dripping auditoriums. Buttresses of braided lianas form naturally. Cupolas of overlapping translucent bracts appear overnight and fill with rainwater that then becomes new growth platforms. It is a made environment that has never known a maker—only an endless, turbulent will to become.

And it is never finished.

Stand still for longer than a few heartbeats and you will feel the living floor beneath you shift, a slow peristalsis of root muscles adjusting their grip on the previous hour’s topography. A nearby wall may suddenly blister, split, and bloom into a chandelier of white flowers that open with audible sighs, releasing a scent like crushed mint and lightning. Ten minutes later the flowers may blacken, wither, and be devoured by the same wall that grew them.

The only law is hunger and becoming.

This is a young world wearing the skin of a cathedral, a cathedral wearing the skin of a jungle, a jungle wearing the skin of a single immortal creature that has not yet decided what shape it wants to keep. It roars, drips, fractures, heals, sings, collapses, and sings again in the wet, hot, merciless light of its own perpetual morning.
