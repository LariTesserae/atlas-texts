The place is called the Hollow Choir.

It is a single, self-contained basin no larger than a modest cathedral, yet it feels far older than the planet that holds it. The ground has been worn so completely by time that it has passed beyond mere flatness into something almost concave, a shallow stone bowl pressed into the world’s oldest bedrock. Every slope, every lip of stone, every fracture has been rounded to the smoothness of worn bone. Nothing here still rises sharply; the land has surrendered its angles and now only sighs.

Water is everywhere without being a lake. It beads, weeps, condenses, and pools in a thousand slow, deliberate places. The rock itself sweats. Thin films of moisture glide over black basalt that has been polished by epochs of gentle dissolution until it shines like obsidian glass. From the porous ceiling of the low, overarching canopy of stone and living matter, clear droplets form and fall at irregular intervals, each one chiming faintly when it strikes the pools below. The air itself is humid enough to feel like breath against the skin; mist gathers in soft columns where warmer currents rise from hidden vents in the rock.

Vegetation is lush but strangely sparse, as though the land has chosen only a few chosen species and granted them extravagant dominion. Great pale ferns taller than a man stand in solitary majesty, their fronds dripping. Between them, nothing. Then, without warning, a single tree—ancient, low, and grotesquely broad—spreads like a green cathedral dome no wider than twenty paces. Its bark is the color of wet slate and glistens. Wherever its roots touch stone, the rock has softened into black, living mud that smells of iron and crushed lilies. Mosses grow in perfect, velvet geometric patterns that repeat at scales too small for the eye to comfortably hold. They are not random; they are lattices.

The light is bright and sourceless. It arrives already filtered through an invisible membrane that floats somewhere far above the canopy, a technological relic so old it has become weather. The illumination is warm gold-white, the color of late afternoon trapped in amber, and it never shifts. There are no shadows longer than a few handspans. Every surface seems back-lit.

Fauna is overwhelming in its presence yet almost never seen directly. The basin thrums with invisible, hyper-active life. The quiet is not empty; it is saturated. Billions of microscopic machines no larger than pollen grains drift in slow glittering clouds, catching the light like motes of glass. They are the dominant “animals” here—post-biological, self-replicating, barely distinguishable from living dust. When they swarm they form brief, deliberate shapes: the suggestion of wings, of antlers, of long translucent fish that dissipate the moment you focus on them. Larger creatures exist, but they are rare and stately. A creature like a white heron made of water and light steps between ferns without sound, dissolving into mist the moment it is perceived. Something that might be a herd of transparent deer moves across the far curve of the basin; you see only the refraction of their bodies warping the air, then they are gone.

There are artifacts.

Here and there, half-sunk into the living stone, lie single structures that predate every known civilization. A obsidian plinth no taller than a child, perfectly smooth except for one impossible angle that hurts to look at directly. A ring of six shattered pillars whose broken tops still orbit one another in microscopic ellipses, held by forces that should not persist. A doorway standing alone with no walls attached, its frame carved from a single piece of pink granite that occasionally exhales cold, dry air from some other place. These objects are not ruins. They are still performing whatever function they were built for, only so slowly that a human lifetime registers as a single clock tick.

The sound is mostly quiet, but not silent. The dominant noise is the delicate, irregular dripping of water—each drop striking a different resonant surface so the basin becomes an enormous, slow aeolian harp. Every few minutes a deeper tone moves through the stone itself, like the memory of a bell rung once at the beginning of time and still decaying. Occasionally the microscopic machines produce a sound like distant glass chimes pitched so high they register just below the edge of hearing.

The ecology is active in a way that feels ceremonial. Everything is eating everything else at scales both nano and geologic. The ferns slowly drink dissolved minerals from the weeping rock while the rock, in turn, is being gently sculpted by bacterial colonies guided by the machine-pollen. The transparent heron-things sip the pollen from the air. The pollen edits the heron-things. The doorway exhales air that carries new instructions. Nothing here is in balance. Everything is in elaborate, ancient conversation, and the conversation has been going on so long it has achieved the dignity of weather.

This is not a place that was made.  
It is a place that was finished.

And in its final, settled form it has become a kind of living reliquary—small, bright, wet, impossibly old, and still quietly, busily, rewriting itself one glistening drop at a time.
