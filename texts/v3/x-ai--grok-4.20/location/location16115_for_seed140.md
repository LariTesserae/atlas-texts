The place is called the Hollow of the Last Breathing.

It is not large—no farther than a cathedral stretched across a modest valley—but it feels enormous because there is almost nothing inside it. The air itself has been winnowed thin by time. What remains is a single, ancient basin high in a range so old the mountains have forgotten they were ever mountains. Their slopes have subsided into gentle, fleshy curves, like the bellies of sleeping gods who will never wake again.

Everything is wet. Not raining, never quite raining, yet water is always arriving. It condenses on every surface in fat, trembling beads. It seeps from the black moss that covers the stone like a second, living skin. It weeps in perfect silence from the lips of ancient fractures and drips upward, defying memory, from the undersides of floatingroot lattices that hang in the dark like inverted chandeliers. The atmosphere itself is half mist, warm, heavy, and sweetly mineral, as though the planet is still exhaling after an unimaginable fever that ended geological ages ago.

There is no true light. Only a faint biogenic phosphorescence—soft teal, bruised violet, dull ember-red—given off by colonies of lichen and microscopic animals that have learned to photosynthesize the memory of starlight. These colors do not illuminate; they only confess. They make the darkness wetter, more intimate.

The vegetation is almost obscenely lush yet strangely sparse. What grows does so in immense, solitary gestures. A single tree—twisted, impossibly old, its bark lacquered black by mineral seep—stands near the center like a cathedral pillar. Its canopy is so dense it forms a private night beneath itself. Around its roots, bromeliads the size of small houses hang suspended on nothing, their urns brimming with black water and tiny luminous newts. Between these rare giants the ground is mostly bare stone, polished by three billion years of patient water and wind until it feels like warm skin.

The sound is constant and loud, yet nothing seems to be moving. It is the sound of water learning to speak after too many epochs of silence: dripping, trickling, sighing, gurgling, humming through hidden veins in the rock. Sometimes the stone itself groans, a tectonic belch so low it becomes pure feeling in the chest. Occasionally a fruit falls from one of the hanging lattices and bursts on the stone with a wet slap that echoes for minutes. The fauna is everywhere, but almost never seen. Things rustle, click, and breathe just beyond the limit of the phosphorescence. Vast colonies of blind moths the color of bone drift through the dark in slow, purposeful galaxies. Something enormous and soft passes once every few hours; you feel it in the change of pressure more than in sight or sound.

Scattered across the basin are the remains of something that was never quite a civilization—more like the toys of gods who grew bored with physics. A half-buried torus of metamaterial that still occasionally sings in frequencies that make your teeth ache with nostalgia for memories you never had. A single obsidian needle, thirty meters tall, etched with equations that rewrite themselves when unobserved. A delicate arch of frozen light that no longer leads anywhere but still insists on being a door. These artifacts are so ancient they have achieved the dignity of geology; moss and water have claimed them as kin.

This is the end-state of a world. Not ruined—settled. The final, deeply satisfied sigh after every possible drama has already been performed. Erosion has won. Time has won. What remains is a warm, wet, hollow cathedral where the last slow processes continue without hurry. The lichen will keep eating stone and remembering stars. The water will keep learning new ways to fall upward. The great soft creature whose footfall you feel every few hours will keep circling the black tree in patient, centuries-long orbits.

Nothing here wants anything from you.

It has already become everything it was ever going to be, and it is still becoming it, very gently, forever.
