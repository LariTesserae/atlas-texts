The place is called the **Sigh**.

It is a high, ancient plateau that has been worn so completely flat by time that its curvature follows the planet’s own like a second, tighter skin. No rivers, no lakes, no ice. Water is myth here. The only moisture is a faint, perpetual exhalation that condenses on the undersides of leaves and is immediately re-absorbed by the living architecture.

Everything else is plant.

Not plants growing on rock, but a single continuity of vegetable matter that has replaced geology. The ground is a dense, pale felt of root-felt thousands of meters thick, compressed by its own mass into something that behaves like stone yet still lives. From this living substrate rise colossal, buttressed trunks the color of old bone and oxidized copper. They do not form a forest in any familiar sense; they stand kilometers apart, each one a city-sized organism whose canopy spreads horizontally for leagues, fusing with its neighbors in slow, deliberate arches. The result is a vaulted ceiling of interwoven branches, leaves, and aerial roots that filters the already-dim sky into a perpetual twilight the color of tarnished silver.

The air is cool (never freezing, never warm), yet the sound is deafening. A constant, oceanic roar of wind moving through trillions of leaves, punctuated by deep, subsonic groans as the titanic stems flex and readjust their internal pressures. Every few minutes a sound like tearing sailcloth erupts as a kilometer-long strip of bark peels away and flutters downward in slow spirals, revealing fresh, luminous green tissue beneath. The peeled bark does not die; it drifts on thermals, reshapes itself mid-air into delicate, finned gliders, and seeks new purchase on distant trunks. These are the “ghost mantles,” and they are the closest thing the Sigh has to birds.

The built environment is not imposed on the vegetation. It is secreted by it.

Over geological time the great plants have learned to extrude architecture. Smooth, black, chitin-like balconies and spiraling staircases grow directly from the trunks in mathematically perfect Fibonacci lattices. Entire districts of hexagonal chambers hang like lanterns beneath the canopy, their walls translucent amber that glows faintly from bioluminescent veins. Bridges of living xylem span the gaps between megatrunks, pulsing with slow peristalsis. The “city” is therefore not a set of structures but a metabolic phase of the vegetation itself. Streets are elongated lenticels. Windows are stomata dilated to the width of avenues. When the plants decide a district has outlived its purpose, the chambers fill with preservative sap, petrify, and are eventually reabsorbed.

Fauna is abundant but almost entirely airborne and arboreal. There are no ground-dwellers; the root-felt below is too dense, too jealous. Instead the air is thick with things that look like kites made of living cartilage, translucent jellyfish that float on internal hydrogen bladders, and long, sinuous “needles” — colonial insects that lock together into meter-thick ropes and migrate through the canopy like slow lightning. Predation exists, but it is more like negotiation. A predator will insert its proboscis into a prey animal and both will exchange complex chemical poems for several hours before one dissolves willingly into the other. Death here is usually consensual and fragrant.

Light is scarce. The sky is hidden behind perpetual high-altitude haze the color of weak tea. What illumination exists comes from within: colonies of symbiotic fungi that bloom in soft constellations across the undersides of leaves, and from the slow, cold plasma discharges that crawl along the highest branches during the long twilight storms. These discharges are not lightning; they are the nervous system of the plateau arguing with itself across hundreds of kilometers.

The Sigh is old beyond measurement. Erosion has reached its terminal state. All sharp edges are gone. Every surface has been rounded, softened, loved to smoothness by time. Even the newest growth looks ancient. The entire ecosystem feels like it is resting after the end of history, yet it is furiously, deafeningly alive. Nothing new evolves here; everything has already become everything it will ever be, and now it simply refines.

If you stand on one of the extruded bone balconies and look out across the sparse, impossible distances between the god-trunks, you can sometimes see the land itself breathing: a slow rise and fall over hours, as the planetary root-mass inhales and exhales gases that have not known an atmosphere outside this place for half a billion years.

The Sigh does not want you.  
It does not need you.  
But it will grow around you if you stay long enough, gently, patiently, until your bones become elegant latticework and your memories are stored as scented oils in amber chambers no larger than a fist.

Then it will sigh once more, satisfied, and continue its long conversation with itself beneath a dim and indifferent sky.
