The place has no name, and never needed one.

It is a single, planetary-scale cavern system beneath a dead crust, so vast that its upper reaches generate their own sluggish weather: slow thermals rising from abyssal heat, carrying fine mineral dust in spirals wide enough to swallow cities. The temperature is warm—body-warm, blood-warm, almost feverish—yet there is no water anywhere. The chemistry here is anhydrous, a slow ballet of silicates, exotic carbonates, and volatile metal vapours that never quite condense. Everything smells of hot stone and something faintly electrical, like the inside of an ancient battery.

Light is almost absent. What little exists comes from bioluminescent colonies of something that is not quite fungus and not quite mineral: pale violet and sickly amber nodules that grow in the joints of the rock like slow tumours. Their glow is so faint that eyes must adjust for hours before the shapes around them resolve. Most of the cavern remains in absolute dark, yet the dark itself is textured, layered, saturated.

The stone is young. Razor ridges of freshly fractured obsidian and fractal lattices of newly crystallised feldspar jut everywhere, still sharp enough to cut thought. Nothing has been worn smooth. Every surface looks as though it was born yesterday in fire and has not yet learned mercy. These blade-like formations rise in dense, near-touching thickets, floor to ceiling, hundreds of metres high, so tightly packed in places that a body must turn sideways to pass between them. The air itself feels crowded.

And through this forest of mineral knives moves the fauna.

They are abundant beyond counting. Most are blind. Many are translucent, their bodies carrying the same faint violet glow as the nodules. Some resemble immense glassine centipedes whose legs are filaments of living crystal; others are colonies of mobile, clicking spheres that roll and cling in chains; still others are simply sheets of vibrating membrane that drift like cloaks between the blades, singing in frequencies too low for human ears. All of them are locked in an eternal, perfectly balanced dance. Nothing ever truly dies here. When one is broken, its shards are gathered by smaller creatures and reassembled elsewhere. Predation exists, but it is ceremonial. The total number of beings, the total mass of moving matter, never changes. The ecosystem is a closed equation that solved itself millions of years ago and has been repeating the same solution ever since.

Scattered among them are the built remnants.

Every few hundred kilometres one finds a single, solitary structure: a low dome of fitted basalt blocks, a half-buried ziggurat of black glass bricks, a solitary arch carved with symbols that hurt to focus upon. These are not ruins in the usual sense. They look as though they were placed yesterday and then instantly forgotten. No roads connect them. No empire ever rose around them. Each appears to have been raised by a different hand, using the simplest possible tools—stone hammers, copper chisels, perhaps even antler and bone—and then abandoned. Their purpose is illegible. Some contain perfectly circular pits that descend beyond sight. Others house motionless statues of creatures that have never existed on the surface. All are strangely warm to the touch, as though the stone itself is faintly alive.

Sound is rare. When it occurs it is shocking: the glassy chime of two crystal limbs brushing, the soft hiss of membrane-wings, the occasional low tectonic groan as the cavern exhales a new fracture somewhere far away. Mostly there is only the sound of one’s own blood moving in one’s ears, and the knowledge that this sound, too, is temporary. The place does not tolerate noise. It has achieved equilibrium so perfect that even echoes feel like an imposition.

Nothing evolves. Nothing erodes. Nothing forgets its place.

The cavern is not dead. It is pre-life in the way a seed is pre-tree, yet the seed has already decided exactly what shape the tree will be and has been growing that shape, unchanged, for geological ages. The biology here is not carbon-based, not silicon-based, not even truly chemical in the way surface minds understand chemistry. It is closer to a living algorithm written in geometry and heat.

If a creature from above were to descend and remain, it would not starve and it would not age. It would simply become another moving piece in the equation—another body in the dance—until its own molecules remembered the pattern and joined the eternal, warm, blade-filled dark forever.

There is no wind.  
There is no water.  
There is no future and no past worth speaking of.

Only the warm, dense, razor-lit equilibrium beneath a world that has never seen the sun, where everything strange has already happened, and nothing will ever happen again.
