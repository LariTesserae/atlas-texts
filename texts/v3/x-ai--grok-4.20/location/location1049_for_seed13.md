The place is called the **Hanging Valleys of Veyr**.

You stand on a wide shelf of crisp black basalt at the lip of a half-formed world. The air is cool and damp, the kind of cold that lives inside stone and only reluctantly releases itself. A thin, perpetual mist drifts between things rather than over them, born not from rainclouds but from the slow breathing of the rock itself. Every few hours a brief, soundless condensation gathers on every surface—leaf, roof-tile, iron blade—then slides away in perfect beads that never quite become rain.

The land is young in the way mountains are young when the gods have only just stopped hammering them. Sharp ridges of fresh basalt and obsidian cut the sky like the teeth of broken saws. Between these blades lie shallow, wide valleys that were never meant to hold cities, yet here the cities are anyway—scattered, stubborn, built by hands that still remember how to knap flint.

**Veyr** itself is not one city but a slow constellation of them. Six major settlements and perhaps thirty lesser hamlets are stitched across the high plateaus and hanging valleys by narrow stone roads and rope bridges that have hung so long they have grown moss like beards. The buildings are low and heavy, stacked schist and basalt blocks mortared with a pale lime that never quite dries. Rooflines bristle with turf and living sedge. Chimneys are rare; instead, long ceramic tubes snake across rooftops, carrying warmed air from communal hearth-fires that have not gone out in living memory. The fires do not flicker. Nothing flickers here.

Time has agreed to stand still.

Nothing grows older. Nothing decays. The same generation that raised the first lintel stones still walks the streets wearing the same wool and leather they wore three hundred years ago, though they no longer remember that this is unusual. Children are born, but they are born already carrying the adult faces they will wear forever. The rivers do not cut deeper; the mist does not thicken or thin. Even the wind has learned the same three sighing notes and repeats them at precise intervals.

Vegetation is lush yet strangely spaced. Between the basalt ridges grow dense pockets of pale-green fern, clubmoss, and dwarf cedar, but these pockets are separated by long stretches of naked stone where only crustose lichens the color of moonlight grow in perfect geometric rings. The ferns are twice a man’s height and so saturated with internal water that they ring like bells when struck. Their roots do not reach downward; instead they braid sideways through the mist, forming living bridges that the locals use as secondary pathways.

Fauna is abundant and eerily calm. Glossy black martens with silver faces flow along rooftops in small troops. Flightless birds the size of dogs, colored like wet hematite, stand motionless on single legs for hours, only moving when someone needs to pass. In the deeper gullies live herds of small, delicate deer whose antlers branch into perfect spirals and never shed. All animals move as though they have rehearsed their lives since the first morning and have no intention of changing the choreography.

The light is normal, almost aggressively so. The sun and two small moons pass overhead with mechanical regularity, yet their light never seems to warm anything. Shadows are crisp, blue-black, and strangely loyal; they do not lengthen or shorten with the hours because the hours themselves have agreed on a permanent angle of incidence.

There is a sound, but it is so low and constant that most inhabitants no longer register it: a deep, almost sub-audible ringing, as though the entire plateau were a tuning fork struck once at the dawn of the world and never allowed to stop. When the wind moves through the hanging valleys it carries this tone like a melody that has forgotten its own lyrics.

The weirdness is quiet, almost polite. Occasionally a new building will appear overnight between two older ones, perfectly integrated, as though it had always been there and the inhabitants simply forgot to notice it until now. Sometimes a citizen will pause mid-conversation, stare at their own hands, and whisper, “I think I was younger once,” before continuing as if nothing happened. At certain precise moments—always the same three moments each day—every bird, every person, every blade of grass will face the same direction for seven heartbeats, then resume.

The people call this phenomenon “the Alignment.” They speak of it the way others speak of the weather: inevitable, harmless, and not to be questioned.

This is a damp, cool, high, half-made world that has been caught in a single exhalation. A place of sparse grandness and hidden abundance. A city carved into the bones of a mountain that has not yet decided what kind of mountain it wants to become, and has therefore decided to become nothing else at all.

And it never will.
