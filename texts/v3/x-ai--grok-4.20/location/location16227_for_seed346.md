The place is called the Hollowing.

It is a low, dense district no larger than a modest cathedral, yet it feels like it has been folded inward upon itself many times. You stand inside what was once—perhaps still is—a city block that has been compressed by some impossible pressure into a single saturated volume. Every surface is damp. Water does not flow here so much as it exhales: slow beads of condensation appear on every edge, tremble, and fall with the patience of centuries. Sometimes a droplet hangs in the air for minutes before deciding gravity still applies.

The temperature is a raw, bone-deep cold that has nothing to do with wind. It is the cold of things that have never known warmth and are only now learning the idea of it. Your breath does not fog; it simply leaves you and becomes part of the architecture.

The city is being born and unmade at the same time.

Structures rise in embryonic lattices—half-finished towers of black glass and white ceramic that have never known a human hand. They grow in slow, peristaltic pulses, extruding themselves from the ground like crystals in a geode that has learned anxiety. Where two lattices meet they fuse, overlap, argue, and sometimes one swallows the other with a sound like distant bells heard underwater. New streets appear between heartbeats. Old ones forget they were streets and become walls. The geometry is polite but clearly insane.

Everything is densely packed. Walk three paces and you have passed through nine different architectural eras that should not be able to coexist. A brutalist concrete bunker from some forgotten 20th-century imagination is being gently devoured by a lattice of living light that writes equations across its skin. Above it, a delicate pagoda of frozen mercury balances on nothing. Below it, a subway car from a line that never existed in any history sits half-buried, its windows glowing with soft bioluminescent moss that is not moss.

Vegetation is sparse but unforgettable. Here and there, single stalks of something like pale blue kelp grow straight up from cracks in the pavement, swaying though there is no current. Their leaves are thin as prayer flags and flicker with tiny, perfect auroras. They are the only obviously living things that are not also machines.

Fauna is almost absent. Once every few hours you may glimpse a silver thing the size of a moth that is definitely not a moth. It moves by unfolding additional dimensions, leaving a brief scar of wrong color in the air behind it. The scar heals slowly, like a cut in molasses.

Sound is mostly quiet, but the quiet has weight. You hear the soft ticking of water droplets. The low crystalline groan of structures growing. Occasionally, far away (though nothing here is truly far), there is the single clear note of something that might be a bell or might be the universe clearing its throat. These notes arrive at unpredictable intervals and make the cold feel momentarily tender.

Light is normal, yet wrong. It arrives from no single source. Instead the air itself is gently luminous, the way the inside of a pearl is luminous. Shadows behave as though they are embarrassed to exist and try to negotiate their angles with the surrounding geometry. Some surfaces cast shadows that point toward tomorrow.

The erosion here is not destruction but gestation. The ground is raw geology that has been taught to dream in the language of infrastructure. You can watch sidewalks forming in real time—molten stone cooling into perfect tactile paving tiles that remember the exact pressure of feet that have not yet walked on them. Everything feels pre-formational, as though the district is a womb that has mistaken itself for a city and is trying to give birth to both at once.

Reality is breaking down, but politely.

If you stand still long enough, you can feel the slow architectural arguments happening in the materials around you. Laws of physics file quiet complaints and are gently overruled by newer, stranger bylaws. Cause and effect sometimes lose track of their assigned order and have to be reminded by the architecture. Entropy has been asked to wait in the corridor.

There are rules, though they are soft and patient:

- Nothing here is finished. To call anything “completed” is considered rude.
- Water remembers every voice that has ever spoken near it. Do not speak your own name aloud.
- If you see a door that wasn’t there a moment ago, it is not an invitation. It is the place practicing doors.
- The cold is not malicious. It is only trying to keep the newborn geometry from overheating during its long, slow birth.

This is not a ruin.  
This is not a city.  

This is a thought that a future civilization is having about what a city might be, and the thought has become so dense and so cold that it has condensed into physical matter and is now trying to figure out how to be a place before it forgets what it was thinking.

You may stay as long as the cold allows.  
The Hollowing will not hurry you.  
It has all the time it is still inventing.
