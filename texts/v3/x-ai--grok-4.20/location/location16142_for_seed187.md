The place is called the Hollow Basilica.

It is a single, vaulted chamber of living vegetation so vast that its ceiling vanishes into a soft green haze thirty meters overhead. Every surface — floor, walls, columns, buttresses, even the air itself — is entirely made of plant. There are no stones, no soil, no fungal felt; only layer upon layer of ancient, woody vines, braided lianas, and colossal fungal rhizomes that have grown, died, and been replaced by new growth for so long that the distinction between individual organisms has vanished. The entire structure is one continuous organism, or perhaps a committee of them locked in eternal, stable negotiation.

The scale is intimate yet cathedral-like: you could walk the length of the chamber in ten minutes, yet the space feels enormous because it is almost empty. Between the massive living pillars and arches there are great volumes of cool, still air. The density parameter has rendered the interior nearly hollow, so the eye travels huge distances before meeting another vertical mass of greenery. Each “column” is a fused bundle of trunks and stems thick as grain silos, their surfaces rippled and cable-like, dripping with slow beads of condensation. Where two pillars meet the ceiling they spread into ribbed vaults of interlaced branches that have flattened over epochs into smooth, leaf-green ogives.

The light is normal but indirect, as though the sun has been filtered through a thousand layers of translucent leaf. Everything is bathed in a quiet, even celadon glow. There are no sharp shadows.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not rain here; it simply condenses. Every few minutes a fat drop falls from somewhere high above, striking a leaf with an audible *plock* that echoes for seconds in the hollow space. Tiny rivulets run along the undersides of arches, gathering into glassy beads that hang like chandeliers before letting go. The air is damp enough that your skin never quite dries, yet the floor is never flooded; the living architecture drinks what it needs and exhales the rest back into the still air.

The temperature is cool — the steady twelve degrees of a deep cellar — and perfectly unchanging. Nothing decays here in the usual sense. The oldest wood has simply become stone-hard, polished by centuries of condensation and the slow polishing tongues of creatures that have never known wind or sun. This is the end-state of a forest that has eaten its own ruins so completely that erosion has nothing left to gnaw. The Basilica has reached its final, equilibrated form. It will look exactly like this for the next ten million years.

Sound is shockingly loud because the space is so resonant. The endless dripping becomes a slow, arrhythmic percussion. Beneath that is the low, industrial throb of the place itself. The plants here do not merely grow; they *pump*. Vast internal channels, thick as subway tunnels, carry sap under pressure. You can hear the distant, rhythmic shudder of colossal valves opening and closing somewhere inside the living walls — a slow, mechanical heartbeat at industrial scale. The sound is not metaphorical. The Basilica is, in its own way, a biological factory running at steady state for geological time. It produces nothing a human would recognize as a product, only more of itself, perfectly balanced.

Fauna is abundant yet strangely unobtrusive. Tiny translucent frogs no larger than thumbnails cling to the undersides of leaves, their throats pulsing in exact synchrony with the internal pumps. Clouds of minute white moths drift in the open volumes like slow galaxies, feeding on the perpetual condensation. Every few minutes a larger shape passes — a six-legged sloth-like thing the color of wet moss — moving with dreamlike slowness along a horizontal limb high overhead. Their footfalls are soft, deliberate, and echo like someone knocking on the door of an empty cathedral. None of the animals seem startled by your presence. They have never learned fear; nothing has changed here long enough for evolution to need it.

There are no artifacts, no ruins, no signs that anything intelligent ever tried to claim this place. The Basilica tolerates visitors but does not acknowledge them. It simply continues its slow, loud, damp labor of being exactly what it has always been.

If you stand still long enough in one of the great open naves, you begin to feel the deep equilibrium as a physical pressure. The temperature does not waver. The light does not shift. The dripping continues at its own unhurried tempo. The pumps inside the walls keep their eternal industrial rhythm.

This is not a ruin.  
This is the finished world — small, hollow, loud, cool, and alive beyond measure — resting in the perfect, plant-made silence that comes after the end of all change.
