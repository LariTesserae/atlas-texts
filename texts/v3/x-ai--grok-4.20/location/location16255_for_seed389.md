The place has no name that any tongue still moving could speak. It calls itself only by the low, metallic groan it makes when the heat shifts.

You stand on the upper lip of a caldera that has never known rain. The elevation is punishing: the air is so thin that every breath feels like sucking molten glass through a straw, yet the temperature presses down like a furnace lid slammed shut at 2.7. The sky is a pale, almost bone-colored dome, normal light but somehow wrong—flattened, as though the sun itself has been stretched thin across anvils of altitude.

Below you, the city-terrain unfolds in dense, larval folds. This is not a city built upon the land; it is a city that is the land still learning how to become a city. Raw black basalt and industrial cinder have been half-fused into streets, towers, and half-recognizable civic forms. Everything is emerging. Nothing is finished. Colossal half-machines—part engine block, part geological intrusion—rise in layered ziggurats of rust-red iron and obsidian glass. Their surfaces sweat beads of condensation that have no source; the moisture simply appears, trembles, and evaporates again in slow, patient cycles.

There is almost no vegetation. What little exists is not plant. It is a gray, velvet-like fungal lattice that grows only in the permanent shade beneath overhangs of cooling piping, breathing so faintly you can watch the color drain from it over decades. Mostly the world is mineral, metal, and the slow sweat of rock learning to rust.

The density is oppressive. Structures lean against one another like sleeping beasts, near-touching, riveted, welded, fused. Pipes thick as subway tunnels braid and re-braid in three dimensions, carrying superheated fluids that gurgle with the rhythm of glaciers. Every surface is covered in a fine, ever-shifting scale of industrial ash and microscopic iron flowers that bloom and die in geologic time. Walkways, ladders, catwalks, and spiraling exterior staircases have been bolted, drilled, and grown into the rock itself; many of them end in mid-air where the next phase of construction has not yet arrived. The entire city is a scaffold awaiting its final form.

Fauna is rare. Once in a long while something like a graphite-colored lizard made of overlapping machine plates will scuttle across a heat-veined ledge, its joints clicking with the sound of distant rail switches. They do not eat. They do not seem to die. They simply wait, like everything else.

Sound is mostly quiet, yet never silent. The dominant noise is a low, planetary hum—half tectonic, half industrial—that vibrates in your teeth. Every few hours a distant pressure valve opens somewhere in the labyrinth and a single, drawn-out exhalation of steam escapes, lasting exactly as long as it takes for a mountain to remember it was once a volcano. Then the quiet returns, thicker than before.

The strangeness is in the geometry. Angles do not quite meet. Horizons inside the caldera curve in ways that suggest the bowl is larger on the inside than its rim allows. Certain districts appear to be sinking very slowly into a lake of mirror-black liquid that reflects not the bone sky but another city, older, colder, standing upside-down beneath this one. If you stare too long at the reflection you begin to hear a second, even quieter hum beneath the first.

This is a world still being forged. The forges themselves are the world. Conduits the thickness of redwoods carry lava and coolant in braided loops; foundries the size of cathedrals lie half-buried in fresh basalt, their roofs not yet roofs, their chimneys still deciding which direction is up. Everything is patient. Everything is hot. Everything is close enough to touch the next thing, yet nothing is quite complete.

You feel watched by the city’s own future. It is not hostile. It is simply unfinished, and therefore immense with possibility and threat in equal, unhurried measure.

Walk carefully. The ground is still choosing what it wants to become.
