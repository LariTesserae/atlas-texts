The place is called the **Under-Roof**.

It is a single, self-contained pocket-world no larger than a modest cathedral, yet it feels vaster than any valley because its ceiling is the underside of a mountain. You stand inside the hollowed keel of a peak that has never seen the sky. The mountain above is still young—its bones barely cooled from whatever cataclysm or engineering event lifted it—so the stone overhead is raw, black, glassy in places, fractured in others. Every surface still carries the memory of recent violence: clean shear-planes, fresh crystal fractures, and long weeping seams where supercooled water condenses out of the thin, high-altitude air and runs in silver threads down the walls.

The temperature never rises above a biting cold that makes breath plume and metal sing. Yet the air is wet. Moisture beads on every surface, gathers into trembling lenses, drips, trickles, finds hairline channels in the rock. The water is not from rain; it is exhaled by the mountain itself as it continues its slow, millennia-long settling. It gathers in shallow black mirrors between the structures, reflects the city that should not be here, then vanishes again through microscopic fissures that lead… elsewhere.

The city is almost entirely mineral.

There is no wood, no plastic, no living tissue. The buildings rise in sparse, deliberate clusters—never crowded, never truly empty. Between them lie wide lanes of polished obsidian and sintered basalt where nothing grows and almost nothing walks. The architecture is beyond modern: geometries that feel topological rather than architectural. Towers that taper upward only to bloom into inverted domes against the roof-stone, as though the mountain and the city are kissing. Bridges that are not bridges but negative-space sculptures—voids shaped so perfectly that the rock left behind becomes load-bearing lace. Every surface is etched with nanoscale lattices that shimmer faintly when the light shifts, lattices that are simultaneously circuitry, text, and immune system for the stone itself.

Light arrives from no visible source. It is simply present—cool, even, shadowless in the way only carefully engineered light can be. It feels like the memory of daylight filtered through ten kilometers of ice. Colors are therefore mineral: gunmetal, hematite red, the blue-white of glacier milk, occasional veins of electric turquoise where exotic salts have precipitated along stress fractures.

Fauna is rare and almost abstract. A few silicate-based pseudo-organisms drift in the upper reaches like slow jellyfish made of frost and static. Once every few hours one will descend, graze along a wall, sipping the condensing water and the faint charge that leaks from the city’s lattices, then rise again on convection currents no warmer than −12 °C. They make no sound.

The city itself is active, but quietly so. You can hear the mountain breathing: a low, almost sub-auditory creak as immense crystalline structures deep in the rock slowly reconfigure themselves. Every few minutes a thin chime rings out—not from bells but from thermal contraction in the latticework. Pipes of glassy rock carry cold water from one tower to another with the soft rushing sound of distant rivers heard through stone. Occasionally a structure will emit a single bright note, like a wineglass struck, as it vents pressure or completes a computational cycle whose purpose is unknowable.

There are no streets in the human sense—only processional voids between buildings that were never meant for feet. The inhabitants (if any still remain in the conventional sense) move through the negative spaces, or perhaps they have become the negative spaces. Every so often a ripple of light will travel along a wall like a school of fish, and the stone will momentarily remember how to be liquid before freezing again into new, sharper angles. The city is editing itself in real time, carving fresh details into its own skeleton while the mountain above continues its slow, millennial shrug.

This is not ruin. It is not even maintenance. It is early formation frozen at the moment of becoming—urban geology still in the act of deciding what kind of city it wants to be, ten thousand years after the decision was supposedly made.

Stand still long enough and you can feel the water condensing on your own eyelashes. The mountain is crying on you. The city is listening to the sound. Both of them are young, cold, impossibly busy, and perfectly content to remain hidden forever inside the hollow keel of a peak that has never seen the sky.
