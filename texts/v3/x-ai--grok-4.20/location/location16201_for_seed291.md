The place does not yet have a name that anyone agrees upon. The locals, when they speak at all, simply call it the Wetworks.

You step out of the last functioning funicular and the air immediately feels like cold sweat against your face. The temperature hovers just above freezing, yet the moisture never quite becomes snow; instead it condenses on every surface in heavy, trembling beads that slide, merge, and fall again in slow silver chains. The ground itself exhales. Every footfall releases a soft exhalation of mist that smells of iron, wet concrete, and something faintly sweet, like crushed lilies left in a basement.

This is a low, wide basin that has only recently decided it wants to be land. The geology is still arguing with itself. Raw black basalt shoulders push upward through layers of half-cured ferrobeton and antique brick. Sinkholes open and close like slow lungs. New streets are born each week where groundwater forces itself between old factory foundations, carving miniature canyons that fill with opaque turquoise water. The water is never still. Even in the quietest hours it trembles with subterranean pressure, as though something beneath is perpetually clearing its throat.

Yet the basin is packed to bursting.

Factories, half-built and half-sunk, lean against one another like drunk giants. Their brick and rust exteriors are furred with thick carpets of dripping moss and pale, rubbery lichen that glows faintly bioluminescent in the perpetual damp twilight. Conveyor bridges crisscross thirty meters above the streets, dripping condensation in steady metronome beats. Every vertical surface—walls, silos, the flanks of dormant blast furnaces—is colonized by tier upon tier of hanging gardens gone feral: thick ropes of ivy, fern, and something that looks like giant horsetail but bleeds dark sap when touched. The vegetation has learned to love rust. It drinks the oxidation like nectar.

The city is being knitted together and torn apart at the same time. Nothing is finished. Scaffolding grows its own scaffolding. Entire districts are simultaneously under demolition and under construction by machines that no longer have clear instructions. Towering automated cranes stand motionless for months, then suddenly lurch into life at 3 a.m., swinging steel beams through the fog with the lazy violence of dreaming animals. Their movements are strangely graceful, almost balletic, as if the machines have begun to develop aesthetic opinions.

Fauna is everywhere, and none of it is quite right.

Clouds of small black moths with metallic wings rise from the moss like smoke whenever anything moves. Salamanders the color of wet copper scuttle across power lines. In the deeper pockets of water—those sudden mirrored courtyards that appear after every heavy rain—live things that are partly fish, partly hand tools. They surface at dusk making soft clinking sounds, as though they are still trying to remember what they were before the runoff changed them. Birds nest in the lungs of broken smokestacks; their calls sound like distant factory whistles.

And always, beneath the hush, the sound of water working.

It is mostly quiet here, yes. But the quiet has weight. You hear the soft ticking of condensation falling from a thousand eaves. The low electric hum of cables that have never been disconnected. The occasional deep, geological gulp as the land swallows another section of abandoned street. Sometimes, for no reason anyone can explain, every machine in a district will start at once—hiss of steam, clank of chains, groan of enormous gears—only to fall silent again thirty seconds later, as though the city itself just had a nightmare and woke up gasping.

The light is normal, almost aggressively so. A flat, even, sourceless daylight the color of old pewter filters down through perpetual low cloud and the canopy of hanging vegetation. There are no true shadows, only darker and lighter grades of damp gray. Everything looks equally revealed and equally hidden.

This is a place of embryonic industry. The urban tissue is still differentiating. Streets argue with sewers. Power substations bloom into cathedrals of moss and copper. Entire neighborhoods are being grown rather than built—self-assembling worker barracks whose concrete skin is veined with living root systems that pulse slowly, like arteries.

Nothing here is stable. The Wetworks is in permanent argument with itself, a turbulent negotiation between the insistence of water, the hunger of machines, and the raw rock that refuses to stay buried. New districts erupt overnight like fungal fruiting bodies. Old ones dissolve back into marsh and memory. The citizens—those few who remain—move through it all with the calm fatalism of people who live inside a half-finished thought.

They say if you stay long enough, the city will begin to finish its thought using parts of you.

Most people only stay a season.

The Wetworks does not seem to mind. It has all the time in the world, and it is still deciding what it wants to become.
