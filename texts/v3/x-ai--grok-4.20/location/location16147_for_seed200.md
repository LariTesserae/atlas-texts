The land is called the Wet Incubation.

It does not rise and it does not rest. Everything here is still being born.

The ground is not yet quite ground. It is a warm, dark, fleshy matrix of half-made stone and living peat that has never decided which it wants to be. In places the surface is glossy black mud veined with slow crimson seepage; in others it is a lattice of pale porous rock that sweats clear water like a fevered brow. Both states feel temporary, as though the world is still choosing its own skin.

Heat presses down at full tropical intensity, yet the light is only the colour of old brass filtered through perpetual low cloud. The air itself is wet enough to drink. Condensation beads on every leaf and stem, then slides, then beads again in an endless slow circuit. The humidity is so complete that breath and atmosphere are barely distinguishable; every inhale tastes of rot, iron, and green sugar.

Vegetation has arrived early and in overwhelming force. There are no clear vertical strata, only a single saturated tangle from ankle to canopy. Palms with trunks like wet rope lean against one another as if exhausted by their own growth. Orchids the size of dinner plates bloom directly from the mud, their petals thick and waxy, exhaling perfume that is almost narcotic. Vines thick as a man’s thigh loop through the understory in slow spirals, their leaves so densely layered that the eye cannot find a single unobstructed line farther than four paces. Wherever a trunk or stone breaks the green, epiphytes and mosses colonise it instantly, turning every surface into a miniature dripping jungle of its own.

The fauna is not merely abundant; it is the dominant texture of the place. Insects move in constant sheets—iridescent beetles the colour of fresh blood, dragonflies with wings like stained glass, clouds of midges that glitter like metallic dust. Tree frogs the size of fists pulse with soft bioluminescent light along their throats. Somewhere above, hidden by the canopy, large unseen things shift and click and moan in overlapping choruses so loud the air itself seems to tremble. Their calls never resolve into rhythm or melody; they simply layer, a continuous wet roar of becoming. Nothing here is fully predator or fully prey yet. The food web is still inventing itself.

The land itself is embryonic. Great rounded bosses of new rock—neither fully igneous nor fully sedimentary—push upward through the peat like knuckles under skin. Between them lie shallow, tea-coloured basins that are not quite lakes and not quite bogs. Their surfaces are perfectly still, reflecting the bronze sky with such fidelity that it is difficult to tell where water ends and air begins. From time to time one of these basins will suddenly bulge upward as though something vast is trying to be born beneath it, then settle again with a sigh of released gas that smells of sulphur and crushed flowers.

There is no wind. There never has been. The atmosphere is too heavy, too saturated, too much in equilibrium. The only movement is biological or geological: the slow creep of root systems, the patient dripping of condensation, the almost imperceptible heaving of the land as it decides what shape it will eventually keep.

Everything feels recent and permanent at once. You sense that if you stood in one place for a thousand years you would witness the precise instant when the world chooses whether this place becomes forest, swamp, or something that has never existed before. But you also understand that the choosing has already been going on for longer than time can measure, and will continue long after any witness has dissolved back into the warm wet dark.

The Wet Incubation does not want you.  
It is too busy inventing lungs, and leaves, and stone, and names for all of them.  
It simply tolerates your presence the way a fever dream tolerates the sleeper—necessary for the moment, already half-forgotten.
