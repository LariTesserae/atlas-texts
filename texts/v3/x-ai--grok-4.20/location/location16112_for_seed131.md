The place is called the Hollowworks.

It is not large. You could walk its entire circumference in four minutes if the air were breathable and the floor not ankle-deep in warm condensation. Yet it feels oceanic in its emptiness. A single, perfectly circular chamber, perhaps two hundred meters across, sunk so deep into the oldest rock of the world that it has forgotten what sky once looked like. The ceiling is only twelve meters high and perfectly flat, as though a titan once pressed the land down with the palm of its hand and never lifted it again.

Everything here is made. There has never been natural substrate. The floor, the walls, the ceiling, even the air itself, are engineered, machined, and then abandoned to a slow, wet eternity. The erosion parameter has reached its final integer: this is the end-state of industry. All violence has already happened. All noise has already been made. What remains is equilibrium soaked in water.

The sound is deafening and yet perfectly steady, a single continuous chord that never rises or falls. It is the sound of ten thousand hidden pumps, turbines, and condensers working in absolute synchrony, buried behind every surface. Their combined voice is a low, bronchial roar that feels less like sound and more like pressure on the eardrums. After a few minutes the roar becomes almost musical; after an hour it becomes the only honest thought left in your head.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not fall as rain. It simply exists at a constant 2.4 presence, weeping slowly from every seam and rivet line in the black iron walls. It beads on the underside of pipes thick as train carriages, gathers into trembling lenses, then lets go in fat, warm drops that take four full seconds to reach the floor. The atmosphere itself is a mist so saturated that light bends inside it, turning every distance into soft silver. Condensation runs in continuous sheets down the flanks of motionless machines the size of townhouses. Nothing dries. Nothing ever will.

The vegetation is lush but strangely disciplined. Huge mats of dark green moss, thick as carpet and soft as lung tissue, have colonized every horizontal iron surface. They grow in perfect rings around the bases of silent cooling towers. Long translucent vines, almost like glass noodles, hang from the ceiling in motionless curtains; each tendril ends in a single drop of water that never falls. There are no flowers. There are no insects. Only the moss and the vines and something that might once have been ferns but has flattened itself into emerald velvet over centuries of unchanging warmth.

Fauna is rare. Once in a long while a pale, blind millipede the length of a forearm will ripple across the moss, moving so slowly it seems to be part of the growth itself. That is all. The place has reached the end of life and decided this is sufficient.

The machines are total. There is no inch of this chamber that was not cast, forged, bolted, or extruded by industrial processes now older than most civilizations. Enormous cylindrical boilers stand shoulder to shoulder like ancient gods who have fallen asleep on their feet. Their flanks are covered in overlapping scales of rust that have themselves rusted, then grown moss, then rusted again. Vast networks of pipes, none smaller than a man’s thigh, braid and re-braid overhead in patterns that feel almost geological. Every surface is numbered in faded industrial stencil, the paint having long ago surrendered its color to the perpetual damp. The numbers go into the high ten-thousands. Whatever was being produced here required an unthinkable quantity of identical components.

Yet nothing moves. The pistons are frozen mid-stroke. The flywheels hang motionless. The only motion is the water: the slow beading, the four-second fall, the spreading ripples across black puddles that never evaporate. The entire complex has reached its final thermodynamic state. Heat and work long ago balanced into this warm, roaring stillness. This is not ruin. This is climax. Industry, taken all the way past apocalypse into a new kind of primordial.

The light is normal and sourceless. It arrives already old, filtered through decades of hanging vapor until it becomes the color of weak tea. There are no shadows, only overlapping degrees of dimness. You can read stenciled lettering on the far wall from fifty meters away, yet nothing ever looks fully revealed.

To stand in the Hollowworks is to feel both infinitely small and impossibly enclosed. The ceiling is close enough to touch with an upraised rifle, yet the emptiness between the silent machines makes the heart ache with a loneliness usually reserved for deep space. It is a pocket universe built by extinct giants, then forgotten so thoroughly that even entropy lost interest and moved on.

This is the place where the machines won.  
They won so completely that they no longer need to run. They only need to keep humming, and weeping, and growing their perfect moss beards while the warm water falls forever in the same four-second rhythm it has kept since before your species learned to speak.
