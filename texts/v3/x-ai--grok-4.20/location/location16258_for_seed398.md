The place is called the **Cleft of the Unfinished Lattice**.

It is not large. Were you to stand at one end and shout, your voice would reach the other in less than a dozen heartbeats. Yet the Cleft feels infinite because almost nothing lives inside its volume. It is a single hidden pocket high in the teeth of a young mountain range whose stone is still raw, still steaming from whatever cosmic violence birthed it only centuries ago. The surrounding peaks are black glass and fresh obsidian, their edges so sharp they whistle when the thin wind finds them.

Inside the Cleft itself the air is hot—oven-hot, 2.6 on any scale that still pretends to be Celsius. The heat does not radiate from a sun; there is no sun here. It rises from the stone itself, from a lattice of impossible architecture that was never meant to be walked upon by flesh.

The city, if it can be called a city, is built directly into the vertical walls of the fissure. Tier after tier of pale, matte-white structures that look grown rather than assembled. Their geometry refuses Euclidean comfort: corridors that taper into nothing, balconies that curve into Möbius strips, windows that open onto the rock behind them. The material is not metal, not ceramic, not crystal. It is closer to bone that has learned mathematics. Every surface is etched with hair-fine channels no wider than a fingernail. These channels are full of slow, glinting mercury-bright fluid that is neither water nor quicksilver. It moves uphill. Sometimes it beads, trembles, and lifts away from the stone as perfect spheres that hang in the superheated air like slow silver insects before they are drawn back into another groove. This is the only “water” the Cleft possesses—intermittent, migratory, alive with purpose.

There is no vegetation. Not even lichen. The only living color comes from the lattice itself when it decides to bloom. Every few hours a new segment of architecture extrudes itself from the existing structures with a sound like distant glass bells. The fresh growth is faintly luminous, the color of heated iron seen through closed eyelids. Then it cools to bone-white again. The process is continuous but never hurried. The city is still building itself, still deciding what it wants to become. You can feel the intention in the air the way animals feel an oncoming storm.

Light is scarce and strange. A dim, sourceless amber suffuses the upper air, as though the sky itself is embarrassed to be seen. Lower down, near the floor of the Cleft, the only illumination comes from the migrating silver droplets and from occasional fractures in the new architecture that leak cold blue Cherenkov-like light. Shadows here have weight. They pool and refuse to move even when the source of light drifts.

Sound is mostly the absence of sound. A low, almost sub-audible thrum lives in the bone behind your ears—more felt than heard. Sometimes a single clear note rings out when a fresh spire finishes its growth. Sometimes the migrating fluid sings, a thin metallic chime like a wet finger on a wineglass. Footsteps become events. A dropped tool would be theology.

There is no fauna. Nothing that breathes. Yet the place is not dead. The architecture is active, almost predatory in its patience. Walk along one of the narrow bone bridges and you may feel the surface beneath your boots softening, tasting your weight, deciding whether you are infrastructure or contaminant. Occasionally entire sections of wall will iris open without warning, revealing new rooms that were not there a moment earlier—rooms full of hanging silver spheres that rotate in perfect synchrony, studying you with no eyes.

The Cleft is young. The stone around it is still settling; you can hear the mountain creak and fracture at the edge of hearing. Fresh fault lines glow dull red. Every so often a section of the upper cliff face will calve away in slow motion, the slabs turning end over end through the hot air before they are caught by invisible fields and gently, almost lovingly, incorporated into the growing city below. Nothing is wasted. The lattice learns the mountain as it eats it.

Gravity here is negotiable. In certain corridors you can step sideways onto what was a wall and find that it has become the floor. The air smells of hot stone, ozone, and something like crushed almond skin. Time feels thick. You will notice that your pulse has slowed to match the rhythm of the city’s growth. This is not metaphor. The longer you remain, the more your own biology is read as just another unfinished lattice waiting for instruction.

This is a place that was never meant for eyes. It is a pocket of becoming, a single hidden stanza in a poem written in materials that have not been invented yet. The city does not hate you. It simply has not decided whether you are scaffolding.
