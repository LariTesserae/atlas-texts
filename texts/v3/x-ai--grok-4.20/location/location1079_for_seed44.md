The place is called the **Mossheart Foundry**.

It is a low, sunken basin no larger than a small cathedral, yet it feels endless because every surface has been claimed by the same living organism. A single species of warm-blooded moss — thick, velvet, and faintly luminous — has become the substrate, the architecture, and the ecology all at once. It grows in dense, overlapping mats that are sometimes ankle-deep, sometimes chest-high, sometimes forming entire walls and ceilings. Where it piles against itself it creates natural vaults, buttresses, and hollowed transepts that mimic the ribs of a gothic church built by no human hand.

Water is everywhere, but never as open pools. It weeps slowly from the moss itself. Every frond and fold is saturated; press your palm anywhere and a clear, warm trickle immediately wells between your fingers. The air is so humid it condenses on your eyelashes. Fat drops gather on the undersides of hanging moss curtains and fall with soft, regular plops that echo through the chambers. The sound of water is constant, layered, almost choral.

Inside this living cathedral sits an **industrial settlement** that has been here long enough to reach a state of moss-industrial equilibrium. Narrow brick flues, iron walkways, and squat boiler houses rise directly out of the green mass as though the moss grew around them centuries ago and simply incorporated them. Rusty pipes disappear into thick moss walls and re-emerge elsewhere, dripping. Great belt-driven flywheels, half-swallowed by the vegetation, still turn with geological slowness — one revolution every ten or twelve minutes — driven by hidden water-wheels fed by the perpetual weeping of the moss. The engines do not roar; they sigh, they creak, they breathe.

Everything is warm. The moss generates its own gentle metabolic heat, and the machinery adds a low industrial fever. Condensation steams upward in slow spirals wherever hot metal meets wet plant. The light is normal but heavily filtered: a soft, even, greenish-gold glow that comes from nowhere and everywhere, as if the moss itself is mildly phosphorescent and the water droplets act as tiny lenses.

The settlement is dense in the extreme. Houses, workshops, and refining sheds are pressed so tightly together that their roofs and walls have merged into single organic-industrial masses. Narrow lanes twist between them, just wide enough for a person and a handcart; the moss has rounded every corner, softened every edge, turned the built environment into something half-grown, half-forged. Chains of hanging lanterns swing above the lanes, their glass clouded with moisture. The lanterns burn low, steady oil flames that hiss faintly when fat droplets fall into them.

Fauna is almost absent. Once in a long while a pale, blind cricket the size of a thumb may scuttle across a pipe, or a single species of tiny golden frog — no bigger than a fingernail — can be seen riding the slow current of a water droplet down a moss curtain. That is all. The ecosystem is too complete, too perfectly occupied by the moss, to leave room for much else.

Sound is loud yet strangely muffled. The place murmurs and drips and sighs at industrial volume, but the moss absorbs sharpness. You hear the endless dripping, the patient groan of turning flywheels, the low hiss of steam vents, the distant hydraulic heartbeat of pumps working somewhere deeper in the mass. All of it arrives as a continuous, warm, wet orchestra. No sudden noises. Nothing hurried.

This is an old place, an established place. The balance was reached generations ago. The people who live here (quiet, damp-skinned folk in oilcloth and moss-fiber clothing) do not fight the vegetation; they maintain it the way one might maintain a garden or a circulatory system. They prune thick growth from gear housings with curved bronze sickles, coax new moss bridges across gaps when old ones grow too heavy, and tap the warm, faintly sweet water that the moss exudes when gently pressed. Their factories produce nothing the outside world would recognize — slow ingots of a waxy, plant-infused metal that never rusts, sheets of living felt that remember their shape, medicines distilled from the moss’s own metabolism.

Time moves the way the flywheels move: one deliberate revolution every ten or twelve minutes. Decay and growth have negotiated a treaty. Erosion has long since given up; the landscape is no longer wearing down, it is *maturing*. Even the iron is slowly being replaced, molecule by molecule, by something that is still iron but also still moss.

Stand still long enough in the central transept — where the largest boiler house rises like a green and rust altar — and you can feel the entire place breathing: warm, wet, patient, loud, and perfectly, oppressively alive. The Mossheart Foundry is not a city built in a swamp.

It is a cathedral-sized organism that happens to contain a town, engines, and people inside its own endless, dripping body.
