**The Sighing Flat**

You stand in a low, primordial basin that has long forgotten what it means to rise. The land has been planed down by three billion years of patient water and wind until it is almost perfectly level, yet it never feels flat. Everywhere the surface is broken by shallow, overlapping bowls and ripple-scars, like the footprint of an ocean that left before memory began. The rock itself is dark, glassy, and sweating. Every surface weeps.

Water is not a visitor here; it is a permanent resident that has simply grown tired of moving. It pools in long, skin-thin sheets no deeper than your ankle, then vanishes into invisible fractures only to bead again on the next slope of stone. Condensation drips from nothing, from the air itself, forming slow silver threads that slide across mirror-black obsidian and basalt. The ground is never dry and never flooded. It simply exhales.

There is no vegetation. Not a blade of moss, not a lichen, not even the expected crust of extremophiles. The mineral world has refused every offer of green. What grows instead are vast, translucent fungal lattices—ghostly, vein-like networks of palest violet and bone-white that spread beneath the thin water films. They are not alive in any way you recognise; they are more like slow mineral thought, crystalline mycelia that sometimes pulse with inner bioluminescence when the deafened fauna pass overhead.

The air is cool, almost cold, yet heavy with moisture. It presses against the skin like wet silk. Sound, however, is the true tyrant of the place.

The roar never stops. It is not one sound but a thousand braided together into a single deafening cataract of noise: the constant hollow boom of water falling into deep hidden cisterns beneath the crust, the high metallic ringing of stone plates grinding against each other far below, the wet slap of enormous translucent wings, and—most terrible of all—the endless, overlapping cries of the fauna. The sky (if it can be called a sky) is low and pearl-grey, and across it move drifting colonies of membranous creatures the size of small houses. They look like vast jellyfish crossed with manta rays, bodies rippling with rows of breathing pores that scream in overlapping chords. Their voices are so loud they vibrate the water on the ground into delicate standing waves. When hundreds pass at once the noise becomes physical, a pressure that pushes against the ribs and makes the teeth ache.

Yet for all the noise the land itself is sparse. The great beasts keep their distance from one another, gliding in slow, deliberate circuits above the weeping stone, rarely descending. Between their patrol routes lie wide, echoing silences of open rock where nothing moves except the constant drip and the slow creep of the violet fungal lattices.

Scattered across this ancient, flattened world are the remnants of something that once tried to live here in a more familiar way. Primitive structures—low domes of fitted basalt blocks, half-sunk into the stone as though the ground itself had exhaled and swallowed them. Their doorways are sealed with the same glassy black material, and faint chisel marks suggest they were made by hands that understood levers, rope, and simple bronze. No one knows who built them. The current fauna show no interest in the domes, treating them as just another kind of rock. Occasionally a membranous wing will brush against one and the structure will answer with a deep, bell-like tone that cuts through even the constant roar for three full heartbeats before being swallowed again.

Light is perpetual dusk. There is no sun, only a diffuse, sourceless glow filtered through perpetual low cloud and the bodies of the screaming sky-beasts themselves. Their translucent membranes act as living lamps, staining the wet stone beneath them in shifting hues of cold aquamarine and bruised lilac. When a large colony passes directly overhead the entire basin lights up like the floor of an aquarium, every bead of condensation flashing like scattered diamonds.

The ecology is ancient, active, and mercilessly balanced. The sky-beasts feed on the violet fungal lattices by lowering long, whip-like tendrils that drink the mineral thought directly from the stone. In turn, something lives inside the beasts—smaller, faster, bat-like things that scream at an even higher register and burst from breathing pores in glittering black clouds when the larger creatures are threatened. Everything eats sound as much as matter. The louder the landscape becomes, the more vigorously the fungal lattices bloom beneath the surface.

Nothing here is young. The rock has settled into its final form. The water has found every crevice it will ever need. The great membranous herds follow circuits so old they have worn faint luminous grooves into the low clouds above. Even the primitive domes feel less like ruins and more like fossils—structures that have been allowed to remain only because the world has grown around them and no longer considers them important.

This is not a dead place. It is a place that has outlived every definition of life except its own, and it is still, after all this time, loudly, wetly, and defiantly thinking.
