The place is called the **Veins of the Hollow Saint**.

It is not large—no farther than a single cathedral might stretch if you walked its length and breadth—but every cubic inch of it is crowded, seething, and ancient. You stand inside a wound that the mountain has worn into itself over thirty thousand years. The elevation is punishing; the air is thin enough that your own pulse feels loud in your ears, yet the temperature clings to a stubborn, fevered warmth, as though the stone itself is slowly digesting something.

The ceiling is lost in darkness only a few body-lengths above your head. Light is almost nonexistent: a faint, sourceless phosphorescence leaks from certain wet patches of rock like pale mold, enough only to turn absolute black into a deep, bruised violet. You move through layers of near-touching things.

Water is not a river here, nor a lake. It is a chronic condition. Every surface sweats. Beads of condensation the size of grapes tremble on the undersides of overhangs before letting go with soft, deliberate *plips* that echo too long. Tiny rivulets no wider than a finger crawl down walls, find crevices, disappear, then reappear elsewhere hours later. The stone drinks, holds, and exhales. The air is always damp; your clothes never dry.

Vegetation is almost an insult to the word. What grows does so in microscopic patience: black, leathery lichens that look like scabs, hair-fine fungal threads that knit cracked stone together, and, rarely, a single stunted spear of something resembling iron-colored bamboo no taller than your forearm. These are not plants so much as slow geological thoughts.

Yet the place is overwhelmingly, almost unbearably alive with **fauna**.

They are small.

They are numberless.

Beetles the color of wet hematite pour in slow, gleaming sheets over every surface, their wing-cases clicking like distant rain on a tin roof. Millipedes as thick as a thumb and long as your arm move with the solemnity of processionals, each segment ridged like ancient armor. translucent cave-salamanders no larger than a coin cling to the ceiling in constellations, their throats pulsing rose-gold as they breathe. Spiders with legs like jointed sewing needles knit and re-knit hammocks between the slower bodies. Things with no names—soft, eyeless, pulsing—slide through the film of moisture that coats everything. Every creature here has evolved to live inside the same few degrees of warmth and the same perpetual twilight. They do not flee from you. There is nowhere to flee. This pocket of mountain is the only world they have ever known.

The sound is constant and loud in the way a forest at night is loud, yet slower, more mineral. A low, wet scraping. The clicking of a billion feet. The soft percussion of dripping water. The faint, papery rustle of wings and carapaces. Sometimes, from deeper chambers, comes a sound like stone clearing its throat—an immense, patient grinding as the mountain continues to settle by millimeters per century. The noise never stops, yet it never hurries. It has the rhythm of something that has already won and is now simply digesting its victory.

The erosion is so complete it feels architectural. Every surface has been licked smooth by time, water, and the patient bodies of invertebrates. Grooves and runnels twist into natural spiral staircases that lead nowhere useful. Alcoves have been carved into perfect knee-high niches, as though the mountain once expected worshippers. Columns of stone have been worn until they resemble bundled nerves or the pleated robes of forgotten saints. Nothing here looks accidental anymore. The wilderness has had so many millennia that it has achieved the composure of deliberate design.

There is no metal, no woven fiber, no carved mark of anything that walks on two legs. The only technology belongs to the creatures: the precise geometry of spider silk, the hinged mandibles of beetles, the chemical lanterns inside the bellies of certain worms. Primitive. Perfect. Older than language.

And the place is **weird** in the way only something that has never been witnessed can be weird.

If you stay long enough (and you will not want to), you begin to notice that certain clusters of insects and salamanders keep the same formations for days. That the dripping water sometimes falls in deliberate rhythms—three drops, pause, seven drops—like a code no one is meant to read. That in certain corners the stone is warm in a way that has nothing to do with the ambient temperature, as though something inside the rock is still faintly, faintly breathing.

The Veins of the Hollow Saint do not want you here.

They also do not particularly mind.

They have been digesting themselves, very slowly and very loudly, for longer than your species has had fire. They will still be doing so long after the last beetle has clicked its last click. You are simply another temporary moisture moving through their cathedral-sized gut—warm, loud, and ultimately unimportant.

Stay still long enough and the beetles will walk across your boots. The salamanders will descend on silken threads to study the heat of your face. The mountain will continue its slow, wet grinding, shaping itself into one more perfect, impossible chamber no human eye was ever meant to see.

This is not a place.

It is a process wearing the mask of a place.

And it is in absolutely no hurry at all.
