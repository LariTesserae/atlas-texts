The place is called the **Hollowing Cradle**.

It is a high, cool basin no larger than a modest cathedral or a stand of ancient oaks—perhaps four hundred paces across—yet the sky feels impossibly distant above it, as though the very air has been stretched thin. The ground is still being born. Black basaltic stone, glossy and wet as newborn skin, rises in raw plates and ribs that have not yet decided what shape they will keep. Between these plates, water moves constantly but never quickly. It seeps, beads, trickles, and condenses. Every surface weeps. Mist rises from the rock itself in slow, deliberate exhalations, gathering under the vault of the basin and then falling again as lazy, fat droplets that strike with audible, deliberate plops.

Vegetation has arrived early, almost indecently eager. Mosses the color of oxidized copper and wet emerald cling to every ledge and seam. Ferns taller than a man uncoil from crevices with painful slowness, their fronds dripping. Long strands of translucent lichen hang like wet hair from overhangs, swaying even when the air seems still. Everything is lush, almost violently alive, yet spaced so far apart that each plant feels like a solitary witness. Between them lies emptiness—great volumes of cool, echoing air that tastes of iron and green sap.

The settlement is small, primitive, and strangely formal. Thirty-seven low stone-and-timber structures crouch on the few level shelves the rock has offered so far. Their roofs are living: thick mats of the same moss and ferns that cover the stone, kept trimmed by hand into neat, dripping rectangles. Narrow gutters of hollowed log carry water from the weeping cliffs directly onto these roofs so the houses remain permanently wet, glistening, and faintly steaming in the cool air. There are no streets—only ropes of braided lichen and braided hemp strung between buildings so people can walk without sinking into the saturated moss. Footsteps make soft, sucking sounds that echo upward for a long time.

The sound of the place is constant and loud in its own patient way: the overlapping drip and plink and trickle of a thousand separate seepages, the low gurgle where several streams finally meet in a central pool no wider than a village well, the creak of wet wood expanding, the distant hollow boom when a slab of new rock finally splits under the pressure of trapped water and frost. These noises never stop and never hurry. They simply persist, like a slow heartbeat measured in hours.

Fauna is rare. Occasionally a pale, long-legged heron-like bird stalks between the ferns on feet that make no sound. Sometimes a clutch of tiny iridescent frogs detonates upward from the moss like living jewels when a person approaches, only to vanish again. Nothing larger seems to live here yet. The ecology feels embryonic—plants and water have arrived, animals are still hesitating at the threshold.

Light is normal, even generous. The basin sits at such an elevation that clouds often drift below its rim, yet direct sunlight still reaches down in thick, slanted pillars that turn every falling droplet into a brief spark of white fire. The mist catches the light and holds it, so the entire hollow glows without glare.

There is one strangeness at the center.

In the middle of the pool stands a single column of raw black stone that was never cut by human hands. It rises twenty feet, perfectly circular, its surface covered in slow-moving beads of water that trace the same paths every day as though following invisible grooves. Around its base the water is deeper and perfectly still, reflecting the column, the hanging lichen, the dripping roofs, and the bright sky all at once. The locals call it the Unfinished Rib. They believe the world is still growing, and this high wet pocket is one of the places where the planet’s bones are still pushing upward through the skin of moss and mist. They build their little town not in spite of that knowledge, but because of it—half temple, half nursery, half construction scaffold for a geology that has not yet decided what it wants to become.

Everything here is loud with quiet water, lush with careful green, empty with vast wet air, and unfinished in the most deliberate way. The settlement feels less like a town and more like gentle scaffolding placed around something immense that is still, after centuries, only beginning to wake up.
