The place is called the Underhalls.

You descend through a fissure no wider than a cart, and the world opens sideways. Not downward into some vast cavern, but sideways—into a low, horizonless belly of stone that feels both embryonic and ancient. The ceiling is rarely more than thirty feet high, often less. In places it brushes the tops of your shoulders; in others it vaults away into dim vaults the color of wet bone. Everywhere the rock looks half-finished, as though the world itself is still being knitted together and you have arrived during an intermission.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not roar or flood. Instead it persists as a permanent dampness: beads of condensation sliding down pillars that have not yet decided whether they are stalactites or simply clots of raw limestone. Shallow, ankle-deep channels meander between the built structures like hesitant veins, appearing and vanishing again into cracks. Occasional rain finds its way down through unseen vents far above; you hear it before you see it—a soft, irregular patter like hesitant fingers drumming on the stone roof, then silence again for hours.

The city—if it can be called a city—has grown in the gaps where the geology allowed it. Because the density parameter is so high and the ceiling so low, the settlement has been forced into extreme compaction. Stone buildings, all hand-cut with primitive chisels and wooden wedges, press shoulder-to-shoulder in organic, almost cellular clusters. Alleys are rarely wider than a man’s hips. Roofs are shared; one structure’s upper floor becomes the next structure’s courtyard. Stairways switch back and forth like ladder fungi growing between trunks. Everything is built of the same damp, pale limestone that is still forming around it; walls often show fossils mid-emergence, as if the rock is giving birth to the city in slow motion.

Vegetation is sparse but stubborn. Matte-black liverworts and grey mosses coat every northern face. In the occasional pockets where seepage brings richer minerals, small colonies of blind white ferns unfurl like sea anemones. These are tended by children with bone hooks. No trees. No grass. Just these low, patient organisms that seem to breathe in the same rhythm as the dripping stone.

The light is normal, but its sources are strange. Long horizontal shafts have been cut through certain ridges so that daylight, thin and cool, slides in from distant surface clefts many miles away. The beams are dusty, almost blue. They fall in narrow, slanted lattices across the packed rooftops and are sliced again by a thousand hanging cords of braided sinew and dried gut from which the inhabitants hang cured meats, clay tablets, and small copper bells. The overall effect is of living inside an endless, half-lit scriptorium written in stone and water.

Sound is hushed. Footsteps are muffled by the constant thin film of moisture. Voices do not echo so much as they are absorbed. The loudest noises are the slow, irregular drip of water into stone basins and the soft wooden clack of looms worked by hand in the deeper chambers. Once every few hours a low, tectonic groan travels through the rock as some unseen layer settles a fraction of an inch—birth pang of the world still forming. The people pause when they hear it, the way sailors pause at a change in wind.

Fauna is rare. A few pale, almost translucent lizards move like slow thoughts across the walls. Bats the color of wet clay hang in dense, breathing curtains from selected vaults; the citizens regard them as weather—when they all fly at once it means rain is coming to the surface world above. Occasionally a blind white cave fish will surface in one of the sluggish canals, glittering like a dropped coin, and children will shout in quiet voices.

The settlement is active in a slow, relentless way. Fires are small and smoky, fed by dried fungal cakes and driftwood carried down by the intermittent rains. Smiths work copper and very early bronze with stone anvils. Potters turn their wheels by foot in alcoves no larger than cupboards. Everything smells of wet limestone, woodsmoke, sour milk, and the faint metallic breath of green copper.

This is not a ruin. It is not even fully born. The Underhalls are still choosing what they will become. New chambers are being widened by hand even now; you can hear the distant, patient chink of hammer-stone on chisel-stone, like the heartbeat of something vast and larval. The city and the cave are learning one another’s shape at the same time.

And always, beneath every conversation, beneath every sleeping child curled against damp stone, you can feel the slow, geological pulse of a world that has not yet decided where its surface ends and its depths begin.
