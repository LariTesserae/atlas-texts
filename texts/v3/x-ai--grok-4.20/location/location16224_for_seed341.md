The place is called the Hollowing.

It is a low, sunken district no larger than a modest cathedral quarter, yet it feels both intimate and endless because every surface exhales water. The air itself is hot and wet, thick enough to drink. Condensation gathers on every pipe, every girder, every leaf, then slides downward in perpetual slow tears. The ground never dries; it is a living sponge of black peat, rust-orange clay, and fractured concrete that has long ago surrendered to the moisture. Nothing here is older than two centuries, yet everything already looks ancient, as if the district were born already weathered.

You stand at the edge of a drowned industrial grove.

The buildings are not tall. They huddle low and close, shoulder to shoulder like wet beasts, their brick and cast-iron skins furred with moss and dripping filaments of algae. Between them run narrow lanes of open water—no wider than a cart—black and perfectly still except where heavy droplets fall from overhead pipes and strike the surface with rhythmic, metallic plops. These lanes connect into a single looping canal that circles the entire Hollowing, so the whole place is an island inside an island.

Vegetation is lush but strangely disciplined. Great banks of ferns and elephant-eared leaves press against the foundations of the factories, but they never quite invade the structures. Instead they form green vaults and living colonnades between the buildings, creating dim, dripping arcades where the only light is the sour yellow glow of sodium lamps strung on sagging cables. The light is never bright. Even at what should be midday the atmosphere is a perpetual amber twilight, the sun reduced to a rumor behind layers of steam and low cloud that never quite rains but never quite stops condensing.

The sound is constant and industrial. Enormous rotary pumps, housed in half-flooded brick sheds, labor day and night to keep the water from rising any further. Their rhythm is a deep, wet heartbeat—thump… hiss… thump… hiss—echoing off wet stone and metal. Steam vents shriek every few minutes as pressure is released from the old geothermal exchangers that once powered the district. Pipes sweat and clang. Hidden turbines buried beneath the peat growl like distant, drowning lions. The combined noise is loud enough that after ten minutes your own voice feels unreal.

There is no animal life. Not a bird, not an insect, not even the expected midges that should swarm in such heat and moisture. The only movement that is not mechanical belongs to the water and the vegetation. Massive leaves flex slowly, opening and closing in time with the heat cycles. Vines thick as a man’s wrist uncoil by the centimeter, seeking new purchase on rusted gantries. In the corners where two walls meet, pale fungal curtains bloom and collapse in the space of an hour, only to regrow fatter the next.

The strangeness reveals itself slowly.

Every structure is slightly tilted, not from damage but by design, as though the entire Hollowing was built on a very shallow, imperceptible spiral. If you walk the circuit of the canal long enough you eventually notice that you have descended perhaps half a meter without ever seeing a slope. Water moves in both directions at once along the same channels; you can watch two separate currents, one warm, one almost cool, sliding past each other like oil and ink.

The buildings themselves are layered in time. Newer iron walkways have been bolted over older brick galleries; those in turn rest on even older concrete foundations that are now half-dissolved into the peat. In places the walls have been eaten through by moisture and replaced with living lattices of root and vine that have been trained—deliberately—into load-bearing walls. You can see the remains of old company sigils still riveted to the brick, but the metal letters have oxidized into lace and the names have become unreadable.

At the center of the grove stands the single largest structure: the Pumping Cathedral. It is not a church. It is an engine house the size of a small basilica, its roof long collapsed and replaced by a jungle of hanging pipes and dripping ferns. Inside, a forest of cast-iron columns rises from black water. Between the columns hang enormous piston rods, each thicker than a tree trunk, moving up and down with stately, sweating dignity. The sound here is almost holy—low, resonant, and ceaseless. The water that pools between the columns is perfectly clear; you can see the old mosaic floor three meters down, its tiles still bright, depicting gears and saints of industry now forever underwater.

Rules of the Hollowing:

- Nothing ever quite dries. Even fire, if you could light one, would only produce more steam.
- All machines eventually become architecture. The longer a mechanism runs, the more the vegetation and corrosion claim it until it is impossible to tell where the device ends and the district begins.
- Sound travels strangely. A whisper spoken beside the central pumps can sometimes be heard clearly on the far side of the canal an hour later, distorted and slowed, as though time itself is damp.
- There are no shadows, only deeper and shallower kinds of dimness.

This is not ruin. It is balance. An equilibrium reached after a hundred and fifty years of hot water, relentless industry, and patient green pressure. The Hollowing has become exactly what it was always going to be: a low, loud, sweating machine-grove where water, iron, and living green have negotiated an uneasy, dripping truce that will likely outlast every soul who ever tried to own it.
