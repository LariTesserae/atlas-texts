**The Hollow of Weeping Stone**

You stand inside a high, folded pocket of the world, a single cupped basin no larger than a modest cathedral, yet so perfectly contained it feels like a room carved by time itself. The air is warm, heavy, and wet; every breath arrives already half-condensed, tasting of minerals and green. Mist beads on your skin and on the leaves without ever quite becoming rain.

The basin sits at a middling elevation where the mountains have grown tired. Around its rim, sheer walls of dark basalt have weathered for so long they no longer look hewn; they look grown. Water does not fall from above so much as it is *secreted*. It wells from inside the stone in slow, glistening tears that trace every crease and hollow, gathering in thousands of hairline rivulets before dripping, plinking, or simply sliding into moss. The sound is almost nothing: a scattered, patient dripping, like the world thinking aloud in a language of single syllables.

Vegetation has taken the invitation. It is lush without being crowded. Between the scattered columns and shelves of stone grow dense but widely spaced thickets of ferns whose fronds are the color of oxidized copper. Pale, wax-coated bromeliads clutch the rock faces, their urns brimming with captured water. Here and there rise single, ancient conifers no taller than two men, their trunks wrapped in velvet moss so thick it looks like the trees are wearing robes. Between these sentinels stretch long, clear stretches of bare stone worn into gentle undulations by ten thousand years of the same slow tears.

There is no animal life. Not a bird, not an insect, not even the suggestion of one. The silence is therefore absolute except for the dripping. The quiet has weight; it presses softly against the ears the way deep water does. You become aware of your own pulse.

The strangeness reveals itself gradually.

The water that weeps from the rock is not merely water. Where it pools in shallow basins it catches the normal daylight and bends it into soft, impossible hues—rose, viridian, a violet so faint it feels like memory. These colored pools never overflow. Instead the stone drinks them again over decades, leaving behind faint rings of metallic sheen that form glyphs no hand ever drew. The rings migrate. If you return after many years you will find the patterns have moved, as though the stone were slowly spelling a sentence too long for any single age to read.

The trees, too, are wrong in a patient way. Their needles do not fall. When one finally dies it simply turns to stone in perfect replica, becoming another weeping pillar that continues the slow work of weeping. You can find them—perfect stone trees, roots and all—standing among the living ones like ghosts who refused to leave.

Everything here is in balance, but the balance is geological, not biological. Change happens, yet it happens with the patience of continents. A new fern may unfurl in a single season, but the shape of the basin itself will not alter in ten thousand years. The air condenses, the stone cries, the moss thickens by a hair’s breadth each century, and the colored rings migrate a finger’s width every human lifetime.

There are no paths. There is only the choice of where to place your feet between the dripping pillars and the copper ferns. The boundary of the place is abrupt: beyond the rim the land rises steeply into dry, ordinary mountain, as though this wet, warm, silent hollow had been set into the world like a single green-and-silver jewel pressed into rougher stone.

It feels less like a landscape and more like a question the mountain asked itself long ago and has been quietly answering ever since.
