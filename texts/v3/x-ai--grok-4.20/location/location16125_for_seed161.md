The place is called the Hollow of the Last Lanterns.

It is not large — no more than what a modest cathedral might cover — yet it feels like the final room of the world. You stand inside an ancient, flattened caldera no wider than a hamlet, its rim worn down by three billion years of wind and nothing else. The walls rise only thirty or forty meters before they curve inward and seal into a domed ceiling of naked, glass-smooth basalt. There is no sky. There has never been a sky here.

The air is warm, thick, and dry as bone-dust. No water has touched this stone since the planet was still molten. What little moisture exists is locked inside the flesh of the vegetation itself.

And the vegetation is everywhere.

It grows in dense, overlapping layers so tightly packed that the eye cannot find a single clear path between stems. Tall, fibrous stalks the color of rust and dried blood rise like bundled spears. Between them hang curtains of pale, leathery fronds that sway with almost imperceptible slowness. Every surface — floor, walls, even the underside of the dome — is furred with a living carpet of tiny, many-jointed succulents that click softly when they expand. Their leaves are thick as tongues and the same deep maroon as congealed blood. In the dimness they look wet though they have never known water.

Light comes only from the Lanterns.

These are not flames. They are colonies of luminous bacteria housed inside fist-sized, bladder-like fruits that grow in dense clusters along every vertical surface. Each fruit glows with a sullen, amber-orange light, like dying embers. The oldest Lanterns have begun to sag and darken; some have gone black and hang like withered organs. The newest ones give off a faint heat that makes the air above them shimmer. Their collective glow is enough to see by, but only just — a cathedral soaked in perpetual dusk.

The sound is constant and loud in its own patient way. Millions of small, armored insects — chitinous, many-legged, almost beetle-like — move through the vegetation in slow, tidal surges. Their bodies click and scrape against the stalks in a continuous dry rustling that rises and falls like breathing. Every few hours the entire chamber answers with a deeper note: the ancient stone itself contracting or expanding by fractions of a millimeter as the planet’s core heat ebbs and flows. The sound is low enough to feel in the teeth.

Fauna is abundant but never hasty. Fat, soft-bodied lizards the color of pale clay drift among the stalks on six delicate legs, pausing for decades at a time to drink the faint condensate that forms on the Lantern fruits. Clouds of tiny iridescent midges drift in slow spirals through the warmer columns of air, their wings making a sound like distant rain on tin. Everything moves as if time has grown tired here and decided to rest.

The settlement is woven into the vegetation so thoroughly that it is difficult to tell where the city ends and the living carpet begins. Primitive dwellings of woven fiber, dried frond, and shaped basalt crowd against one another in layered terraces no taller than a man. Narrow lanes twist between them, barely wide enough for two people to pass. Rope bridges of braided rootfiber sag overhead. There are no sharp angles; every structure has been rounded by centuries of careful hand-scraping and the patient pressing of living fronds against wood and stone.

The people are few — perhaps two hundred — and they move with the same slow dignity as the lizards. Their tools are simple: blades of volcanic glass, hammers of dense root-wood, needles made from the hollow leg-bones of long-dead megafauna. They tend the Lantern colonies with ritual patience, harvesting the oldest darkened fruits and pressing them into oil that burns with a clearer, whiter light for a few precious hours before guttering out. They speak rarely. When they do, their voices are low and seem to come from inside the walls.

This is the end-state of an ecosystem that has had three billion years to settle into final, dense equilibrium. Nothing is wasted. Nothing hurries. The chamber has reached its oldest-stable-form: a warm, dim, loud, impossibly crowded little world sealed beneath a dome of primordial stone, where life has learned to burn its own light and live by it until the core itself cools.

Even the erosion has finished its work. The floor is perfectly level, polished by the ceaseless brushing of a billion tiny feet and the slow drag of living fiber. There are no ruins here, because nothing has ever been allowed to fall completely into ruin. Everything is maintained, repaired, woven back into the living mass with the same slow, devotional labor that has continued without interruption since the first Lantern was lit.

If you stand still long enough, you can feel the entire chamber breathing with you: the soft click of the carpet-plants, the creak of ancient fiber bridges, the low stone note that vibrates in your ribs, and the endless, patient rustle of the small things that have never known a horizon. 

This is not a desert.  
This is what remains when a world has grown so old that it has folded in on itself, pulled its edges together, and decided to live forever in one small, warm, loud, and densely luminous room.
