The place has no sky and no ground.  
It is called the Thalassoverdent by the few machines that still speak.

You are suspended inside an endless, cool volume of water. Not an ocean in the old sense — there is no surface, no floor, no shore. The liquid is dense enough to feel like breathable mercury, yet clear as window-glass. It stretches away for thousands of kilometers in every direction, and the pressure remains mysteriously, impossibly constant, as though the entire world is one single, balanced lung. Currents exist but they are slow, almost geological, drifting in wide, continent-sized gyres that have not altered their paths in ten thousand years.

Every solid thing here is alive.

The vegetation is not merely dominant; it is substrate, architecture, and atmosphere at once. Titanic pale-green fronds, each thicker than a train, rise and fall in the slow currents like the ribs of an impossible cathedral. Their surfaces are not smooth — they are shaggy with secondary forests of smaller kelp, mosses that bloom only in ultraviolet, and colonies of translucent anemones that function as living solar panels. These plants do not photosynthesize with light alone. They drink the faint industrial heat that still leaks from buried, half-forgotten machines.

The machines themselves are older than the plants. Vast, rust-colored ruins of what were once orbital refineries and arcologies now lie half-swallowed, their iron bones overgrown and gentled by layer upon layer of living tissue. No one built them here; they fell, long ago, from a sky that no longer exists, and the Thalassoverdent simply grew around the wreckage until the wreckage became reefs. Their reactors still glow, faint orange hearts deep inside living green cathedrals, powering nothing anymore except the slow chemistry of endless growth. The sound of the place is therefore permanent: a low, cathedral-scale thrum of ancient turbines turning in water, the creak of colossal stems under pressure, and the constant roar of ten billion bubbles rising from trillions of tiny breathing pores. It is loud the way a glacier is loud — not sudden, but total.

Fauna is abundant yet strangely spaced. Great herds of translucent whale-things the size of freight ships glide between the plant-columns, their bodies patterned with living bioluminescence that never repeats. Schools of metallic fish, descendants of forgotten maintenance drones, still twitch with broken programming and flash like schools of knives. Predators exist, but they are slow, almost ceremonial. Everything has had time to reach equilibrium. Nothing here is in a hurry; nothing here has been in a hurry for geological ages.

The light is normal, yet sourceless. It filters from everywhere and nowhere — a cool, even, blue-white radiance that feels like perpetual early morning on some northern sea that forgot to end. There are no shadows, only gradients of green.

Elevation is meaningless in the usual sense, yet the place is high — you can feel it in the bones. The pressure, the thinness of the water at the farthest reaches, the way the largest stems thin out into delicate spires that vanish into distances too vast to parse. The entire Thalassoverdent floats, or once floated, in the upper strata of what used to be an atmosphere before the Flood-that-Was-Also-a-Garden. Now it is its own planet-sized biome, drifting in the high, cold silence between two layers of former sky.

It is never changing.

The same current has turned in the same direction for longer than mammals have existed on any other world. The same stems have grown, died, and been re-absorbed into new stems without variation. The machines have rusted to the exact shade of umber they will always possess. The sound is a single sustained chord. The light does not flicker. Even the strange things — the occasional kilometer-wide bubble of vacuum that drifts like a ghostly cathedral bell, the sudden lattices of ice that form for no reason and melt again, the pockets where gravity forgets itself and everything spins in lazy zero-g gardens — even these recur in perfect, timed cycles so long they feel like laws rather than events.

There is no weather here except the weather the living machines and living plants have agreed upon. The world has become its own equilibrium, vast, loud, cool, green, metallic, and gently, permanently strange.

You can swim in any direction for weeks and the scene will barely alter.  
Only the scale itself changes — the feeling that you are a speck of plankton inside the ribcage of something that has already become both forest and factory, both reef and ruin, and has simply decided, long ago, to stay exactly like this forever.
