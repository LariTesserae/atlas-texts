The place is called the Under-Grove of the Veiled Cistern.

You descend through a narrow throat of limestone no wider than a coffin, and suddenly the world opens sideways. Not upward—sideways. The chamber is low-ceilinged, never more than twelve feet high, yet it stretches in every direction like a forest that has been pressed between two dark slabs of stone. Everything here is breathing, slowly, wetly.

The air is thick and hot, 34 °C at rest, rising to 38 °C when the hidden waters stir. Condensation beads on every surface and falls in irregular, warm drops that strike leaves with soft, deliberate plops. There is no single river. Instead the ceiling weeps in a thousand places; each tear finds a groove in the rock, gathers companions, and becomes a filament of water that slides along roots, drips from fungal shelves, or simply vanishes into black pockets of earth only to reappear elsewhere. The floor is never fully dry and never fully flooded. It is a mosaic of shallow basins, spongy moss hummocks, and glistening ribs of stone that look like the knuckles of something buried long ago.

Vegetation has gone half-feral in the heat and perpetual damp. Palms no taller than a man grow so close their fronds interlock like green chainmail. Between them rise forests of fern taller than a child, their crosiers still uncurling after centuries of uninterrupted growth. Vines thicker than wrists drape from ceiling cracks, flowering with tiny white stars that open only when touched by falling water. Every vertical surface is upholstered in moss so dense it has begun to form its own miniature hills and valleys. Orchids the color of old bone cling to the undersides of stone ledges, drinking the humid air. Nothing here grows toward a sun that has never existed; everything grows toward moisture, toward the next drip, the next breath of mist.

The ecology is loud in its silence. There is almost no wind, yet the air moves—pushed by the slow exhalation of millions of stomata and the faint convection of warmer water rising through colder stone. Sound itself feels submerged. Footsteps are swallowed by wet moss. The only noises are biological and precise: the soft click of mandibles, the wet rustle of something large turning over in a pool, the distant, patient ticking of water measuring time on stone. Occasionally a single drop strikes a cupped leaf with a bell-like note that seems far louder than it should.

Fauna is everywhere, layered in three dimensions. Clouds of tiny white moths drift between the trunks like animated mist. Millipedes the length of your forearm move across the ceiling in slow, armored caravans. In every pool, blind fish the color of milk circle endlessly, guided by pressure waves and the taste of dissolved minerals. Tree frogs no larger than thumbnails sing in frequencies so low they are felt in the teeth rather than heard. Higher up, in the root canopy where the vines knot into living platforms, pale geckos with translucent skin stalk orb-weaver spiders whose webs are spun from silk that glows faintly blue when disturbed. Everything is predator and prey in the same breath; nothing is wasted.

Scattered through the grove are the only marks of anything that might once have thought in straight lines. A broken clay jar, its rim overgrown with miniature ferns. A single obsidian blade wedged upright in a moss bank, now a perch for a tiny orange salamander. The weathered stubs of three stone columns that once held up nothing; their carvings have been reduced by ten thousand years of condensation to gentle, ambiguous curves. These artifacts feel less like ruins and more like seeds that never quite managed to grow into buildings. The grove has simply absorbed them.

Light is almost an afterthought. A faint phosphorescence rises from certain fungal veins in the ceiling, the color of moonlight seen through seawater. In other places, thin shafts of surface light find their way down through unseen fissures and arrive as soft, dusty pillars that evaporate before they reach the floor. Most of the grove lives in a perpetual cathedral dimness—clear enough to see the next branch, too dark to ever be certain what is watching you from behind it.

The strangest quality is the sense that the entire chamber is a single organism wearing the disguise of a forest. Roots from trees that do not exist up above snake through the stone, connecting distant groves in ways that defy mapping. When one pool is disturbed, distant leaves tremble in perfect sympathy. At rare intervals the whole chamber seems to inhale; the temperature rises slightly, every leaf angles itself a fraction differently, and for a minute the dripping stops—as though the Under-Grove is listening for something only it can hear.

Then it exhales, the warm rain begins again, and the slow, dense, patient life of the place continues exactly as it has for ten thousand years, cathedral-sized, intimate, and utterly indifferent to whether anything human ever finds its way down again.
