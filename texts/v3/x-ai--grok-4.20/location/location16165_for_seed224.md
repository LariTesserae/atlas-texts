Here is the place.

You are inside a single, self-contained fracture no larger than a modest cathedral, yet it feels vaster than a continent because distance itself has been wounded. The fracture floats, or perhaps is embedded, within something that was once called a mountain range but has long since forgotten the word “range.” Elevation is almost meaningless; the pocket simply sits at a height where the sky has thinned to a violet bruise and the air itself has grown brittle.

There is no water. Not even the memory of water. The chemistry here is anhydrous and noble; everything is silicate, carbide, and frozen noble gases laid down in lattices that hum at frequencies just below hearing. What little atmosphere exists is a thin, razor-cold soup of neon, argon, and trace fluorocarbons that tastes of metal on the tongue and never moves fast enough to be called wind.

The terrain is all fracture and facet. Titanic plates of black volcanic glass (some older than the concept of planets) lean against one another at impossible angles, their edges so sharp they cut the concept of sharpness. Between them lie slow, geologic meadows of frost made from crystallized xenon and microscopic diamonds that have grown over epochs into delicate, branching reefs. These reefs are the closest thing to vegetation the pocket possesses: immobile, mineral “antler-coral” that accretes one atom at a time across millennia. Their color is the color of starlight frozen at absolute zero.

Light is dim, almost courteous. It arrives from no obvious source, leaking instead through microscopic fissures in the outer shell of the pocket like bioluminescence from a dying god. The glow is pearl-gray, cold, and seems to lose confidence the farther it travels. Shadows here are heavier than the objects that cast them.

Everything is sparse. Between the glass monoliths and the diamond reefs there are cathedral-sized volumes of nothing. Sound is nearly extinct. What little remains is patient and tectonic: the occasional sub-audible groan as two crystal plates ease against one another over the course of a human lifetime, or the microscopic chime when a single xenon snowflake lands on another after drifting for centuries.

Built things are present, but shy. Scattered across the emptiness are structures that clearly did not grow here. They are remnants of a technology so advanced it has begun to dream. A single obsidian needle, thirty meters tall, stands perfectly vertical in the middle of nothing; its surface is inscribed with equations that solve themselves and then rewrite the solution. Further on, half-buried in a dune of microscopic diamonds, lies a torus the color of old moonlight. It rotates once every nine hundred years. When it completes a turn it exhales a single, perfect sphere of negative pressure that drifts away like a soap bubble and eventually pops, removing a cube-meter of reality with it. These absences slowly heal over the next several thousand years.

The scale is intimate and wrong at the same time. You can walk from one wall of the pocket to the other in twenty minutes, yet every step feels like crossing a private universe. The ceiling (if that word even applies) is a single flawless slab of something that reflects what little light there is at delayed intervals, so that your own reflection arrives seconds after you do, slightly older, slightly less certain it is you.

Weirdness is not an event here. It is the substrate. Causality has settled into a gentle, recursive loop so slow it almost qualifies as geology. Time loosens. An hour spent inside the pocket may equal three heartbeats or three centuries once you leave; the fracture does not keep conventional calendars. Entropy itself seems embarrassed and tries to tiptoe.

There is no fauna. Nothing that moves of its own accord has lived here for longer than the concept of “moving of its own accord” has existed. Yet the place is not dead. It is pre-life in the way a seed crystal is pre-crystal: everything is waiting for the right dislocation, the right stray photon, the right impossible event that has not yet arrived and may never arrive.

The dominant impression is of immense, cathedral-quiet patience.

This pocket has been here since before the mountain that contains it was a mountain. It will be here after the mountain has worn down to a memory. The few artifacts of beyond-modern engineering left behind are not ruins. They are punctuation marks in a conversation between two states of matter that have not yet decided whether they are speaking or simply dreaming one another.

If you stand still long enough (and you will, because hurry feels obscene here), you can feel the slow, patient heartbeat of the place: a sub-bass throb that arrives once every few hours, like the turning of an invisible page in a book the size of a galaxy.

The page reads:

*Nothing is finished.  
Nothing is beginning.  
Everything is becoming, very, very carefully.*

And then the silence closes over the words again, gentle as diamond dust, cold as the memory of snow.
