The place is called the Hollow Basilica.

It is a single, vast, enclosed chamber no larger than a cathedral, yet it feels enormous because almost nothing occupies its volume. Every surface—floor, walls, pillars, vaulting—is entirely made. There is no bare earth, no living rock; every inch has been shaped by hand over uncountable generations with nothing more advanced than chisel, mallet, and lever. The stone is a soft grey limestone that drinks water the way bread drinks broth. It has been worked so thoroughly, for so long, that the architecture has settled into its own geology: buttresses have thickened like old tree trunks, ribs have sagged into gentle arches, and the floor has developed a faint, almost biological undulation, as though the entire building were an enormous fossil.

Water is everywhere, but never in open sheets. It sweats from the ceilings in slow, glassy beads that travel along hairline channels carved into the vaulting. It weeps from the eyes and mouths of eroded saint-like figures whose features have worn into abstract, dripping masks. It condenses on every cold surface and runs in thin, deliberate veins along grooves that were cut centuries ago to guide it. The air itself is humid and heavy; breath fogs. Dripping is constant. The sound of it—thousands of separate, irregular plinks and glissandos—fills the hollow space and becomes a loud, wet percussion that never stops. Sometimes the drips fall thirty metres before they strike stone or water. The echoes turn the falling water into a kind of endless, arrhythmic music.

Vegetation is almost absent. Here and there a resilient black lichen crusts the wettest stone like ink, or a thread of pale moss hangs from a ledge, but nothing green truly thrives. The only living colour comes from the fauna.

And the fauna is overwhelming.

Hundreds of thousands of pale creatures move across the made surfaces in slow, glistening tides. Most are blind. Finger-sized white isopods ripple in living carpets over the lower walls. translucent snails the colour of milk drift in long, spiralling processions along the carved channels, following the water’s path. Bats—no larger than moths—hang in dense velvet clusters from the highest vaults, their wings twitching in unison like a single slow heartbeat. In the shallow, artificial pools that have collected in worn basins, eyeless crimson newts drift among clouds of tiny white shrimp. Every surface that is not constantly streaming with water is stippled with life: jewel-like beetles with shells like frosted glass, long-legged spiders that skate across damp stone on invisible films of secreted oil, and something that might be a kind of flightless cricket whose chirrup is so deep it feels like a pulse in the sternum.

All of these creatures are loud. The chamber is never quiet. The dripping, the clicking, the soft wet scraping of innumerable feet and shells, the ultrasonic drone of the bat colonies, and the low, wet creaking of the stone itself as it slowly settles under its own weight and the weight of water—all of it merges into a constant roaring hush that presses against the ears.

The light is dim and sourceless. A faint phosphorescence bleeds from certain lichens and from the bodies of a particular species of centipede that glows soft blue when disturbed. Occasionally a shaft of weak daylight finds its way through a hairline crack high in the vault—perhaps a forgotten ventilation shaft from when the Basilica was still being built—but the beam dies long before it reaches the floor, scattered into a pale, ghostly haze by water droplets suspended in the air.

The Basilica is active. Water moves, creatures migrate in slow seasonal tides from one side of the chamber to the other, and the stone itself is still settling. Every few days a pillar gives off a deep, booming crack as it accepts another millimetre of load. When this happens the entire population of the chamber pauses for a single shared second, then continues.

There are rules here, though they were never written down.

Nothing may be removed.  
Everything that is dropped or left behind is slowly incorporated—first by the water, then by the patient mouths of the isopods and snails, finally by the stone that grows imperceptibly around whatever remains. Even bones disappear in decades.

You may pass through, but you may not stay still for long. The damp cold will settle into your marrow, and the creatures will begin to investigate you with the innocent curiosity of things that have never known dryness or heat. They are not hostile. They are simply abundant, and they have all the time in the world.

The Hollow Basilica does not want you.  
It has already become itself—perfectly balanced, utterly made, endlessly dripping, and louder than any ruin has a right to be. It simply continues, century after century, a small world that outgrew the need for sky, sun, or soil, and now exists only as an enormous, wet, singing machine of stone and blind life.

If you listen long enough, it starts to sound like breathing.
