The place has no name. It never needed one.

It exists inside a single titanic geode the size of a modest cathedral, a sealed cyst of black glass and manganese that formed in the cooling heart of some ancient impact event. The geode’s inner walls are lined with crystals the colour of dried blood—razor-sharp, still growing, still wrong. They have not finished forming. Nothing here has finished forming.

There is almost no water. What little moisture exists drifts as microscopic beads of supercritical fluid suspended in the dense, metallic-tasting air, refusing to condense or fall. The chemistry is non-aqueous, alien, running on halogen cycles and exotic polymers that behave like living oil.

Temperature clings to a perpetual cool that feels colder than it is—perhaps 4 °C—because the air is so thick with suspended particulates and charged aerosols that it conducts heat away from your skin like invisible teeth. Every surface is slick with a fine, electrically active dust that never quite settles.

The entire pocket is no larger than a theatre, yet its verticality is insane. The floor and ceiling are only thirty metres apart, but the architecture that has been bolted, welded, grown, and accreted between them makes the space feel like a mile-high city folded into a shoebox. Walkways, gantries, lattices of black iron and pale bone-coloured ceramic stack in impossible density, layer upon layer, many of them upside-down or sideways, fastened to the geode walls like coral. Some structures pierce others. Some phases in and out of solidity. All of them are new.

This is a young place. The crystals still crack and ping as they lengthen by microns. The machines are still learning what they are for. The city is building itself faster than it can be understood.

Lights are few and dim—sickly amber floods from behind clouded glass, stuttering arcs of violet lightning that crawl along rails like slow serpents, and the cold bioluminescence of things that are neither machine nor animal. Most of the illumination comes from the crystals themselves: a dull, arterial glow that pulses once every seventeen seconds, as though the geode is a heart.

The fauna is everywhere.

They are not animals in any useful sense. They are abundant, dense, near-touching. Some resemble clusters of chrome millipedes the length of a forearm; others are clouds of glittering black dust that resolve into shapes only when they wish to bite. Many look like half-finished tools that have learned to scuttle. They crawl in layered carpets across every surface, rewiring, polishing, dismantling, and reassembling the city in a ceaseless, turbulent frenzy. When two swarms collide they merge, fight, or birth something new in seconds. The sound of their movement is a constant low susurration—like dry leaves, like distant rain on metal, like a library full of turning pages—never loud, yet impossible to escape.

The city itself is industrial in the way a fever is industrial. Vast piston-engines the size of tramcars labour in vertical shafts, driving nothing visible. Cables thick as thighs pulse with slow, peristaltic waves. Refineries no larger than wardrobes belch microscopic storms of metallic snow that swirl in perfect clockwise vortices before being inhaled by other machines. Everything is electrified. Everything is arguing with everything else.

And the place is turbulent.

Nothing remains where it was put. A staircase you climbed three minutes ago is already being devoured and rebuilt two metres to the left, now leading nowhere. A chamber that was an engine hall becomes a nursery for newborn crystal clusters, then a waste sump, then a cathedral of stacked servo-skulls, all within the time it takes to draw three careful breaths. The geode itself seems to breathe; its inner walls flex by fractions of a millimetre, causing harmonic stresses that ripple through every girder and cable. The entire pocket-scale world is in a state of continuous, violent becoming.

There are rules, though they are felt more than known.

- Nothing may remain still for long.  
- Anything that dies is immediately dismantled and fed to the swarm.  
- The crystals remember every shape that has ever pressed against them; sometimes they bloom into perfect negative sculptures of vanished machines or creatures. These are revered.  
- Sound is mostly quiet because the air is too dense and the swarms too efficient at damping vibration. What little noise exists feels intimate, close to the ear, as though the place is whispering directly into your skull.

If you stand very still in one of the few stable niches—perhaps a corroded iron balcony that has so far resisted rewriting—you can feel the whole pocket pulsing around you. A single, dense, turbulent, half-living engine the size of a theatre, crawling with metallic life, lit by its own blood-coloured veins, forever building a city that will never be allowed to finish.

It is beautiful the way certain deep-sea horrors are beautiful.

It is also hungry.

And it is still waking up.
