The place is called the **Basalt Womb**.

It is not an ocean. It is a single, sealed, cathedral-scale chamber of black water suspended at impossible height, as though the sky itself had drowned and then been frozen in time. The chamber is no larger than a modest abbey, yet its verticality is crushing: the ceiling vanishes into absolute darkness forty meters above, and the floor is a perfectly flat plane of polished obsidian that feels older than gravity.

Everything here is made. There was never a natural substrate. The walls, the floor, the half-seen pillars and buttresses that rise like submerged ribs, all are composed of the same dense, black, vesicular stone—basalt that has been worked by patient, primitive hands across geologic epochs. The stone is not carved so much as *persuaded*. Every surface is covered in shallow, interlocking spirals and nested circles no deeper than a fingernail, repeated so densely that the walls appear upholstered in silent calligraphy. The patterns have rounded and softened over unimaginable time until they resemble the whorls of a fingertip magnified a thousandfold.

The water is warm—blood-warm—and so full of dissolved iron and manganese that it tastes of rust on the tongue. It presses against the skin with the heavy, reluctant density of mercury. Visibility is measured in inches. Beyond an arm’s length the world becomes absolute black, yet you are never truly blind; faint bioluminescent motes drift in slow constellations, each no brighter than a dying ember. They move like dust in a sunbeam that has forgotten what a sun is.

The fauna is abundant and ancient. They are not fish. They are not even properly animals. They are soft, blind, jointless things the color of wet slate—some no larger than a thumb, others the length of a man’s body—drifting in layered shoals so dense that the water itself seems furred. Their bodies are translucent bladders ringed with rows of infinitesimal cilia that beat once every seven heartbeats. When they brush against you the touch is velvety and slightly electric, like stroking warm suede in the dark. They leave behind faint trails of colder water that persist for minutes, mapping invisible currents no eye could follow.

There are larger presences, too—vast, cathedral-slow silhouettes that move only across decades. You feel them as changes in pressure against the chest, as a subtle compression of the dark. They never fully resolve. To see one clearly would be to violate the logic of the place.

Sound is almost extinct. The only noise is the patient, almost tectonic ticking of mineral growth: tiny aragonite crystals forming and dissolving in the stone’s pores, a sound like the world’s slowest clock. Once every few hours a single drop of condensate falls from the unseen ceiling. The impact on the surface of the black water is so loud it feels like a gunshot in a mausoleum. The echo lasts for ninety heartbeats, then the silence reseals itself.

The entire chamber is slowly, imperceptibly rotating. You only notice because the faint glowing motes eventually trace lazy spirals. The rotation has been going on so long that the stone itself has developed a microscopic grain that follows the same helical path. Nothing here was ever young. This is not a ruin; it is the final, settled form that all ruins dream of becoming—worn past decay into a kind of exhausted perfection.

Rules of the world:

- Nothing may move quickly. Even thought feels viscous.
- Light is punished. Any flame or torch brought inside will gutter and die within moments, as though the dark itself were hungry.
- The blind creatures will not harm you, but they will remember your shape. If you return years later they will slowly congregate in the exact configuration of your last visit, a living sculpture of your absence.
- The chamber has no entrance or exit you did not arrive through. Yet every visitor instinctively understands that the ceiling, unreachable and lightless, is also a floor for another, identical chamber above, and another above that, an endless vertical stack of drowned cathedrals reaching toward some unimaginable surface no one will ever see.

This is not a place that was built.  
It is a place that was *finished*.  
And the finishing took so long that the world outside simply gave up and went away.
