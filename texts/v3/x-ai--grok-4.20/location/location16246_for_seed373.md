The place is called the Hollow Choir.

It is a high, arid cathedral of living mineral and impossible architecture, no larger than a modest mountain valley yet so vertically vast that its ceiling is lost in luminous haze. There is almost no free water; what little moisture exists is locked inside the translucent, vein-like lattices of the vegetation itself. The air is cool, thin, and tastes of iron and ozone.

Everything grows upward. From a floor of pale, wind-scoured alabaster and fractured onyx rise colossal stalks, ribs, and branching lattices of something that is neither plant nor crystal nor machine, yet behaves as all three. These structures reach forty, sixty, a hundred meters high before they flare into acoustic crowns—vast, ear-shaped resonators that twitch and pivot like living satellite dishes. Their surfaces are covered in dense mats of metallic mosses, iridescent lichens that shimmer between ultraviolet and deep crimson, and forests of anemone-like filaments that wave frantically even when the air is still. The vegetation is lush in density of surface area, yet eerily sparse in footprint; between the towering forms lie wide, echoing plazas of bare stone where nothing grows at all.

The sound is permanent and deafening.

Every stalk, every crown, every filament is in constant, violent motion, scraping, clashing, vibrating. The Choir sings in frequencies that rupture eardrums and shatter thought. It is not music. It is argument. The structures argue with one another in layered shrieks, subsonic booms, and glassine chimes that arrive like physical blows. Standing in any open space feels like being inside a pipe organ the size of a city while it is being demolished by a hurricane. The noise is so total that after a few minutes the mind begins to supply its own phantom silences—hollow pockets where the roar briefly cancels itself and the world seems, impossibly, quiet.

Fauna thrives inside the din. There are no soft animals here. The creatures are almost entirely auditory hunters and symbiotes: translucent membranes stretched over bone frames that function as living speakers; swarms of glittering scarabs whose wingcases are tuned to specific harmonic nodes; vast, slow-moving things like cathedral bells given flesh that roll along the upper stalks and strike them deliberately, adding their own deep percussion to the unending cacophony. Predation is conducted by phase cancellation—creatures that can deaden specific frequencies around their prey until the target is left deaf and disoriented in a pocket of sudden, lethal silence.

The built elements are few but shocking. Scattered across the alabaster floors and clinging halfway up certain stalks are artifacts of a technology so advanced it has begun to resemble geology. Mirror-smooth black obelisks the height of redwoods stand alone, their surfaces reflecting not what is in front of them but what will be there in eleven minutes. Delicate lattices of white-gold that float thirty centimeters above the stone, rotating once every forty-three years. A single shattered dome, half-buried, whose interior is an inverted starfield that still functions; if you crawl inside you can watch galaxies being born in real time while the Choir screams overhead.

The entire place is in permanent turbulent equilibrium. New stalks erupt from the stone in slow, screaming spurts of mineral growth. Older ones fracture, collapse, and are immediately scavenged by fauna and moss alike. The light—clean, cool, and directionless—comes from nowhere and everywhere at once, as though the air itself is faintly luminous. Shadows behave strangely; some refuse to move with the light source, others race ahead of it.

There is an ecology of memory here. The longer one remains, the more the mind begins to resonate at the same frequencies as the Choir. Thoughts become audible. Secrets crawl out of the skull and join the general roar, turning into visible pressure-waves that ripple across the metallic moss. People who stay too long start to grow small resonant crystals behind their ears. Eventually they walk out onto the empty plazas, lie down, and let the sound carve them into new instruments.

The Hollow Choir has been doing this for at least forty thousand years. It is not ruined. It is not being built. It simply *is*, in a state of deafening, hyperactive, perfectly balanced chaos, forever inventing and devouring itself beneath a sky that has never known rain.
