The place is called the **Braided Rime**.

It is a high, cold plateau-valley system where every surface has been colonized so completely by vegetation that the original geology is now only a suggestion beneath a living, breathing crust of plant matter. The vegetation is not forest, nor moss, nor lichen in any familiar sense. It is a single, hyper-dense, self-sculpting super-organism—pale, fibrous, frost-tolerant—whose growth rate is frantic and whose death rate is equally frantic. The result is a landscape in permanent metabolic storm.

The air is thin and bitter (elevation and cold working together), yet always damp. Water does not arrive as rain so much as it condenses out of the sky in slow, silvery mists that drift between the plant-towers like exhaled breath. These mists bead on every surface, run in braided rivulets along cellulose gutters grown by the vegetation itself, and collect in cold black pools cupped inside enormous folded leaves the size of rowboats. The water is never still for long; the plants are constantly shifting, and the pools overflow, drain, re-form elsewhere in a restless hydrological nervous system.

The plant itself is architecture and substrate at once. Great pale trunks—some white as bone, some faintly bluish—rise in dense, near-touching columns, splitting and re-fusing, forming natural vaults and colonnades that stretch for kilometers. Between them hang curtains of finer growth: filamentous mats, dangling root-threads, and translucent fronds that function like sails. Every few minutes some part of the structure tears free under its own weight or from internal pressure, crashing downward in slow motion through the layered canopy. The sound is never loud—only a muffled, wet *crumpling* like heavy wet paper being crushed—because the entire valley is acoustically damped by the living mass. The place is almost perfectly quiet except for that occasional vegetal collapse and the faint, constant ticking of condensation falling from height to height.

Yet the silence is deceptive. The ecosystem is in a state of violent, turbulent equilibrium. What looks like a forest is actually a slow-motion riot. New shoots explode upward at visible speed, thicken, lignify, then senesce and rot within weeks. The dying sections do not fall away cleanly; they are immediately reabsorbed by neighboring growth, digested by fungal symbionts that live inside the same continuous tissue. The smell is sharp, mineral, and faintly sweet, like cold iron left in snow.

Here and there the plant-mass has formed hollows and vents where warmer, moist air from deeper metabolic layers rises. In these pockets the vegetation grows even faster, creating spiraling, chimney-like structures that breathe visibly. Condensation plumes drift from their tops like smoke from living smokestacks.

Scattered throughout this pale green-and-white tumult are the only non-plant objects: the rusted, half-digested remains of an industrial civilization that tried to settle here generations ago. Enormous pipes, collapsed cooling towers, and the skeletal frames of unfinished factories protrude from the living mass like broken bones. The vegetation has not destroyed them so much as incorporated them—growing through turbines, wrapping girders in fibrous sleeves, turning abandoned railways into raised root-roads. Metal and cellulose have reached a weathered truce. Nothing is maintained. Nothing is built anew. The machines have become reefs.

Fauna is almost absent. A few species of small, silent, white-furred gliders move between the upper layers like pale moths. Occasionally one sees the slow, deliberate movement of something larger—perhaps a kind of root-burrowing pseudomammal—but they are vanishingly rare. The ecosystem has little caloric room for animals; almost all energy is locked in the endless vegetative civil war.

The light is normal, even bright on clear days, yet it feels filtered. The pale vegetation reflects so much of it that the entire valley is bathed in a cold, shadowless glow, as if the air itself were frosted glass. When the slow mists roll through, the light turns milky and religious.

The strangest feature is the way the plant sometimes dreams.

In certain regions—always near the half-buried industrial remains—large sections of the growth fall into temporary synchronized senescence. Thousands of trunks and fronds turn deep indigo at once, then begin to secrete a translucent resin that hardens into mirror-smooth plates. For a few days these living mirrors face one another at precise angles, forming strange optical arrays that reflect and concentrate the thin sunlight into focused beams. No one knows why. The beams wander across the valley floor, burning tiny precise holes through other vegetation before the mirrors dissolve back into normal growth. The locals (if any still existed) once called these events “the eyes opening.”

There are no trails here that last. Any path you try to force through the living mass is healed or redirected within days. The only permanent features are the cold black pools, the breathing chimneys, the rusting iron bones, and the slow, wet, continuous sound of a vegetable civilization eating and rebuilding itself without pause.

This is not a forest. It is a single cold-blooded, turbulent, self-devouring body that happens to be the size of a mountain range. You do not walk through it. You are, for the brief time you are allowed, a very small and temporary inflammation inside it.
