The place is called the Underforge.

You descend through a dripping fissure no wider than a rail car and suddenly the world opens outward in every direction at once. There is no sky. Instead, a black vault of engineered stone curves away above you like the inside of a titanic cooling tower, its ceiling lost in condensing haze and distance. The air itself is cold enough to bite (0.3) yet perpetually wet (1.6), thick with the metallic taste of mist that beads on every surface and never quite falls as rain. Water weeps constantly from seams, rivulets, and deliberate weep-holes cut into the architecture, tracing bright veins down every flank before vanishing into grated channels.

Everything here is made.

The ground is not ground. It is a lattice of vast iron plating, black and rust-orange, bolted and welded in overlapping scales that stretch for kilometers in every direction. Between the plates, sharp new fractures still gleam where the structure is still settling; the Underforge is young (1.3 erosion), only a few centuries old, and the raw violence of its birth can still be read in the geometry. Jagged ridges of fresh weld-metal rise like frozen waves. Immense buttresses, each thicker than a cathedral nave, march away in diminishing perspective until they become a forest of pillars holding up the invisible roof.

The light is dim (1.1), industrial and sour. Sodium arcs and mercury lamps hang in staggered constellations from overhead gantries, many of them half-dead or flickering, casting everything in sickly amber and bruise-purple. Their glow reflects wetly off every surface, turning the endless dripping into slow cascades of liquid gold and violet.

It is loud (1.8).

The air vibrates with overlapping mechanical voices: the bass heartbeat of distant geothermal exchangers, the metallic scream of active drop-forges somewhere beyond the next ridge, the constant wet clatter of water hitting hot metal and instantly boiling away. Pneumatic valves hiss in arrhythmic choirs. Every few minutes a freight lift the size of a city block grinds past on rack-and-pinion rails cut into the floor, its wheels shrieking, carrying unknown cargo under tarps that drip steadily. The sounds never resolve into a single rhythm; they layer, fight, and interfere until the entire volume feels alive and argumentative.

Vegetation is sparse (1.3) and strange. What grows here does so in defiance of every natural law. Black ironwood creepers—no, not wood, but some hybridized ferro-organic lattice—snake across the plates in slow motion, their “leaves” actually small heat exchangers that click open and shut like metal gills. Ghost-pale lichen colonies, genetically tuned for low light, spread in perfect fractal rings around leaking steam vents. There is no soil. Roots, where they exist at all, are diamond-tipped augers that drink directly from mineral-rich condensate.

Fauna is rare (1.0), but what exists is unmistakably engineered. You glimpse them only at the edge of vision: pale, elongated things that might once have been rats or bats, now hairless and luminous, moving with the precise economy of machines that have been given metabolism. A maintenance drone the size of a horse clatters past on six articulated legs, its carapace encrusted with generations of dripping minerals that have formed delicate stalactites beneath its belly. It pays you no attention. Nothing here has evolved. Everything has been authored.

The scale is open and regional (1.6). You stand in something the size of a buried river valley, yet every horizon is artificial. In one direction the plated plain rises toward what must be the foot of an internal mountain—an accumulation of discarded furnace slag and slag-glass the color of dried blood. In another, the floor simply ends at a sheer drop where the Underforge is still being extended; you can see the raw cutting face two kilometers away, where titanic boring machines the size of office towers gnaw forward through living bedrock, spraying arcs of steam and pulverized stone into the dark.

The entire environment is active (1.9), almost frantic. Convection currents driven by waste heat create their own micro-weather: sudden gusts of colder, wetter air sweep down from the unseen ceiling, condense on every surface, and trigger small artificial flash-floods that race along carefully engineered channels before disappearing into reclamation sumps. Pipes the diameter of subway tunnels thump and groan as they move superheated or supercooled fluids between distant districts. Every hour or so the whole structure gives off a low metallic moan as it thermally expands or contracts, a sound so deep it is felt more in the teeth than the ears.

There are no natural materials left. Even the air is processed—filtered, humidified, ionized, and periodically scrubbed by roaming atmospheric engines that drift overhead on blurred rotors, their running lights dim red stars in the perpetual dusk.

This is not a ruin. It is not abandoned. It is a work in progress, a continent-sized machine that is still building itself, still finding its final form while already operating at industrial scale. The dripping, the screaming, the cold, the wet iron smell, the slow creep of half-organic machinery across half-organic architecture—all of it is deliberate.

The Underforge is not a place that contains industry.

The Underforge is an industry that has been given the shape of a world.
