The place is called the **Underbay**.

It is not an ocean. It is a city that drowned so long ago that the water itself has grown thick with memory. Every drop feels saturated, almost syrupy, warm as blood (1.7), and so full of suspended silt and microscopic life that light (0.6) dies only a few body-lengths from its source. You do not swim here; you drift, pushed by imperceptibly slow currents that behave like the breathing of something vast and asleep.

The Underbay is small (1.2) yet feels infinite because it is completely packed (2.8). Imagine a single drowned district of some forgotten industrial capital, folded in on itself, every street, factory, tenement, and rail yard pressed together like pages in a waterlogged book. Cathedral-sized engine halls lean against one another at strange angles. Brick smokestacks rise only to pierce the iron undersides of other factories that have settled on top of them over centuries. There is no “between.” Every cubic meter is claimed by rusting metal, black-stained concrete, or the living skin of organisms that have learned to wear industry as their reef.

The vegetation (1.7) is not gentle. It is lush in the way strangling can be lush. Pale anemone-forests bloom from shattered windows. Long ribbons of kelp-like weed drape over gantry cranes in slow, deliberate undulations. Tubular stalks the color of old bone grow through the spokes of locomotive wheels, their interiors glowing with faint internal chemistries. Everything grows in layers: first the machines, then the rust, then the pale, rubbery mats of biofilm, then the vegetation that roots in the biofilm, then the creatures that live inside the vegetation. A slow, patient archaeology of rot.

Fauna is rare (1.3), but what exists is strange (1.7). Most of the time the only movement is the water itself, thick and warm, moving in cycles so long a human lifetime would not witness one full turn. Occasionally a shadow the size of a tram drifts past—some translucent, many-jointed thing that may once have been a maintenance submersible and is now something else. Schools of thumbnail-sized silver fish move like spilled mercury through the ruins, vanishing the instant you focus on them. Once every few days a low, resonant thump travels through the water, as though a distant heart the size of a city block has remembered it still has work to do.

Sound barely exists (0.5). What little there is feels stolen. The soft creak of iron shifting under millennial pressure. The whisper of sediment falling like snow through the water column. A muted metallic moan when two structures settle another millimeter closer. These noises arrive months apart. Between them is a silence so perfect you can hear your own blood moving behind your ears.

Everything is below surface (0.5), deep enough that the surface is only a theory. Yet the architecture remains stubbornly urban (1.6). Street grids are still legible if you squint through the murk; corroded signs still name avenues that no longer lead anywhere. The city has not collapsed. It has settled, in balance (1.6), weathered into a single continuous sculpture of rust, bone-colored polyps, and black iron. Factories have become cathedrals. Tenements have become reef systems. The industrial machinery (2.4) still stands in monumental ranks—enormous silent turbines, press-stamps the size of houses, conveyor belts that have fused into strange organic curves. None of it works. All of it continues. The distinction is meaningless here.

Light, when it appears, is dim, bluish, and treacherous. It comes from bioluminescent colonies that have colonized the highest spires, or from the occasional ancient emergency lamp still somehow receiving power from some impossible geothermal tap far below. These lamps burn the color of old brass and illuminate only a few meters before the water drinks them.

The Underbay does not change quickly (0.9). Its processes are geological in patience. A handrail may take three hundred years to acquire a new coat of coral-like growth. A collapsed roof may require a millennium before the next floor settles into its place with a sound like a continent sighing. The city is in its own state, neither dead nor alive, but established, saturated, warm, and dreaming.

If you remain here long enough, you begin to feel the water thickening around your thoughts as well. The pressure is gentle. The silence is courteous. The city does not want you, exactly, but it is in no hurry for you to leave. There is room, after all. Not between things—there is never room between things—but inside them. The Underbay has learned, over its long drowning, that everything can become something else if you give it enough quiet centuries.

And the water, heavy and warm and older than names, keeps its slow, patient watch.
