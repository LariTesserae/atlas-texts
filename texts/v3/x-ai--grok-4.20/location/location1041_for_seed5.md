The place is called **the Hollowed Lantern**.

It is a single, vast, self-contained chamber tucked inside the root-mass of an ancient petrified forest that no longer exists above ground. The ceiling is so densely braided with living root and stone that it feels like the inside of a ribcage. Everything here is low, intimate, and completely saturated; there is almost no open air. You could stretch your arms in most directions and touch both wall and hanging growth at once.

The temperature is a constant, bone-deep chill that never wavers. Moisture is everywhere without ever becoming flood: water beads on every surface in slow, fat droplets that hang like glass fruit before falling with a sound so soft it is almost imaginary. Condensation runs in thin silver threads down columns of living wood and down the faces of worked stone. Tiny rivulets disappear into pockets of black moss only to reappear elsewhere, as though the chamber itself is breathing the water in and out. There are no rivers, only this perpetual damp respiration.

The vegetation is outrageous in its density. Every vertical or horizontal surface that is not polished stone is furred, fringed, or armored with mosses, liverworts, miniature ferns, and pale orchids that have never seen true daylight. Their colors are unnaturally vivid—acid greens, bruise violets, luminous creams—because the light here is not sunlight. It is a cold, unwavering phosphorescence that leaks from the roots themselves and from fist-sized colonies of luminous lichen that have been cultivated in geometric patterns across the ceiling. The brightness is therefore total and shadowless, yet somehow arctic. You feel you could read small text anywhere, but the light itself seems to have weight, pressing gently downward.

The architecture is both grown and built, and it is impossible to tell where one ends and the other begins. Primitive hands—long accustomed to working without metal—have shaped the living wood into vaults, stairways, balconies, and narrow streets no wider than a pair of shoulders. The same hands have carved and stacked blocks of pale porous stone into buttresses that merge seamlessly with the root columns. Every surface is covered in low-relief spirals and knotwork worn almost to nothing by centuries of damp fingers and dripping water. The city is not large in footprint, perhaps the size of a single cathedral nave, yet it is layered seven or eight stories high in places, all of it packed so tightly that the distinction between house, temple, bridge, and garden has dissolved. Alleys are tunnels. Windows are apertures between living trunks. Doors are curtains of woven moss that never quite dry.

Nothing here changes.

The same droplets fall from the same roots in the same rhythm. The same pale moths—large as a man’s hand, wings dusted with silver—drift in slow, identical circuits through the same shafts of cold light. The same small amphibians, blind and pearl-white, click across the same stones. Even the people (there are perhaps two hundred of them) move with the deliberate, repetitive grace of ritual. Their speech is quiet, almost breathless, as though louder sounds might crack the equilibrium they have spent generations maintaining.

The fauna is abundant but hushed. Besides the moths there are tiny velvet bats that hang like black fruit in the root-vaults, clusters of translucent shrimp that drift in the shallow basins where water collects, and something that might be a very small, very slow sloth-like creature wrapped in living moss so that it is nearly invisible until it moves. All of them appear to have been here so long that they have become part of the ornamentation.

There is a strangeness at the center.

A single vertical shaft of black glass—no wider than a man’s thigh—rises from floor to ceiling. It is perfectly smooth and perfectly silent. Around its base the moss has been carefully kept back, forming a perfect black circle. The locals call it the Lantern’s Wick, though it gives no heat. If you press your ear to it you can sometimes hear, at the very edge of perception, a sound like distant, extremely slow bells. They never hurry. The people have arranged their entire city in concentric rings of increasing humility around this one object, as though it were both shrine and anchor.

The rules of the world here are simple and absolute:

- Nothing may be removed.  
- Nothing may be hurried.  
- Nothing may be made louder than a whisper.

Violate any of these and the droplets stop falling for exactly one heartbeat. Everyone—human, moth, moss, and stone—seems to notice at once. The silence that follows is heavier than any scream could ever be.

This is not ruin. This is not wilderness.  
This is a place that has achieved a state of saturated, luminous, ice-cold perfection and has simply refused to go any further. It has been like this for so long that the concept of “before” has atrophied. The people who live here do not appear unhappy. They appear, instead, like part of the moss: permanent, vivid, and perfectly still.

You could stay here forever and the chamber would never once disappoint you by becoming something else.
