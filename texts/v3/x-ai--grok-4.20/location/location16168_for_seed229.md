**The Hanging Grove of Veyr-Keth**

You stand inside a place that should not be able to exist.

It is a single, self-contained pocket of high-altitude world no larger than a modest cathedral, yet it feels infinite because every direction ends in soft, breathing walls of living green. The ground is not ground. It is an immense, ancient lattice of petrified root and fungal shelf, warped into high arches and balconies that float without visible support. The entire structure drifts, very slowly, inside the upper belly of a vast, dry mineral sky. There is no water here—has never been water. Moisture is stolen from the air by capillary lattices of velvet-black lichen that drink lightning and exhaled breath instead.

The temperature is warm in the way a fever is warm: close, constant, intimate. The air smells of burnt cinnamon, ozone, and the sweet-rot perfume of overripe spores.

Light is dim, sourceless. A bruised amber glow leaks from within the living walls themselves, as though the vegetation has swallowed lanterns and forgotten how to digest them. Everything is back-lit, everything casts three shadows that disagree with one another.

The vegetation is almost obscenely lush. Vines thick as thighs, ferns tall as houses, orchids the color of fresh bruises, and trees whose trunks are braided from a dozen impossible species. Yet the density is sparse. Each colossal plant stands far from the next, separated by wide balconies of naked root-bone, so the eye can travel great distances between green explosions. The emptiness itself feels alive, like the pause between drumbeats in a song that never resolves.

Fauna is everywhere and loud. Thousands of creatures move through the lattice at once. Glass-winged cicadas the size of ravens scream in overlapping choirs so constant the sound becomes texture. Six-legged velvet sloths with human-like faces drift along the undersides of branches, humming in low chords that make your teeth ache. Swarms of thumb-sized scarlet beetles rise and fall in sudden tidal waves, their wings producing a metallic rattling roar. Every few minutes a flock of pale, eyeless birds bursts from a hollow and wheels through the open space between canopies like living confetti, shrieking a single sustained note that sounds like tearing silk. When they collide with the living walls they simply melt into the leaves and are reborn elsewhere, screaming again.

The built environment is almost nothing. Here and there you see the fossilized remains of earlier cultures: a primitive stone altar half-swallowed by a root the width of a city gate; a broken obsidian knife still clutched in a mummified hand protruding from a moss cushion; tally marks carved in repeating spirals that follow no language you know. One structure still stands whole—a small, windowless ziggurat of black fired clay no larger than a woodshed, perched on a root-balcony. Its door is open. Inside, something is slowly turning in the dark, clicking.

Everything is in motion. The entire grove is turbulent, restless, hyperactive. The root-lattice creaks and shifts like a ship in a storm. Entire balconies rise or descend by several meters without warning. Vines lash and retract. New flowers detonate into being and die within heartbeats. The air itself seems to boil with floating seeds that ignite spontaneously, burn white for three seconds, and become new insects before they hit the floor. Nothing here is finished. The place is locked in permanent, ecstatic becoming and un-becoming.

It is old beyond memory (erosion parameter 1.7), yet newborn every moment. The primitive tech of the long-vanished people who once sheltered here has been absorbed, metabolized, and spat back out as new biology. Their bone flutes now grow as hollow reeds that whistle when the wind moves through them. Their clay pots have become seed pods. Their children, perhaps, became the humming velvet sloths that watch you with wet, ancient eyes.

There are rules here, though they are felt rather than spoken:

- Nothing may remain still for long.
- Every scream must eventually become song.
- If you speak your own name aloud, the grove will try to grow you a new mouth with which to unsay it.
- The ziggurat is watching. It is always watching, even when you turn your back.

Welcome to Veyr-Keth, the smallest, loudest, strangest hanging world that has never known rain. It has been screaming beautifully for twelve thousand years and has no intention of stopping.
