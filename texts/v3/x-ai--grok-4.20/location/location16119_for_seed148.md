The place is called the Hollow Basilica.

It is not large, yet it feels endless. A single, low-ceilinged chamber no wider than a modest cathedral, but the ceiling is so high in places that it vanishes into absolute dark. Everything here is still being born. The geology itself is soft, wet clay and black peat that has not yet decided whether it wants to become stone. Fingers of raw mineral push upward like half-formed thoughts, only to be immediately swallowed by vegetation.

And the vegetation is total.

There is almost no ground. What looks like floor is a living mat of interwoven roots, mosses, liverworts, and pale fungal sheets so thick they have become architecture. Vines thicker than a man’s torso hang in slow, deliberate arcs, not from any visible ceiling but from the bodies of other vines. Every surface—every column, every wall, every fractured machine—is furred, bearded, or armored in green. Leaves the size of dinner plates overlap in perfect tessellation, forming vaults and transepts of pure chlorophyll. In the few places where bare earth or metal still shows, it is only because the plants have not yet finished their conquest; even now you can watch hair-fine rootlets creep forward like time-lapse ivy, tasting the air, tasting the iron, tasting the dark.

The air is cool and damp. Condensation gathers on every leaf and drips with the slow, patient rhythm of a cathedral clock. Sometimes it falls as a single heavy drop that rings against an abandoned gear far below. There is almost never true rain, only this perpetual beading and falling, as though the entire space is exhaling.

Light is a memory. A few dying industrial lamps still burn far apart—sodium-yellow, weak, half-choked by vines that have grown through their housings. Their glow travels only a few meters before the vegetation drinks it. Most of the Basilica is therefore black, yet not empty black. The dark itself seems pregnant, humid with unseen life.

And the life is abundant.

Things move everywhere, but never quickly. Vast pale moths with wingspans wider than your outstretched arms drift between the hanging root-columns like slow ghosts. Their wings make no sound. Ladder-backed millipedes the length of coffins glide along the undersides of leaves, their legs clicking faintly, a sound like distant rosary beads. Something that might once have been a deer moves through the undergrowth on too many joints, its antlers now branching into living candelabras of lichen. Every creature here has been gently, lovingly repurposed by the plants. Many no longer have mouths; they breathe and feed through symbiotic gills and suckers that merge with the leaves they rest upon. Predator and prey have mostly forgotten the difference. They simply circulate, like blood moving through a single enormous body.

The machines are older than the current shape of the place. Great rusting engines, half-buried boilers, and looping pipeworks protrude from the living walls like ribs from a devoured giant. Some still run. You can hear the occasional wet thump of a piston somewhere far off, laboring under tons of moss and root pressure. Electricity sometimes arcs between exposed cables, but the spark is immediately caught by bioluminescent fungi that drink the current and glow soft teal for minutes afterward. The city—if it can still be called that—has been digested and is still being digested. Yet the digestion is tender, almost reverent. The plants do not wish to kill the machines; they wish to wear them like jewelry.

Reality here is tired.

Perspective occasionally forgets itself. Walk ten paces in what you are certain is a straight line and you will find yourself back at the same broken floodgate you passed an hour ago, except now the water trickling from it is flowing upward. Shadows have weight. If you stand still long enough, your own shadow will begin to grow rootlets. Time is not broken so much as irrelevant; the place is still deciding what it will be when it grows up. Sometimes entire sections of the Basilica simply bloom overnight—new vaults of translucent leaves that glow with captured starlight stolen from no visible sky. Then they wither again, making room for something stranger.

The sound is mostly quiet, but the quiet has texture.

There is the dripping. The soft creak of living wood under tension. The breathing. Yes—breathing. The entire chamber inhales and exhales once every few minutes, a slow tide of air that moves the hanging gardens like seaweed underwater. When the Basilica breathes out, it smells of iron, chlorophyll, and wet stone. When it breathes in, it smells of distant rain that has not yet arrived and perhaps never will.

This is not ruin. This is not overgrowth.

This is a place that has not yet chosen between city and forest, between machine and flesh, between now and later. It is embryonic, enormous in its smallness, and it is still thinking.

If you stay long enough, it will begin to think with you.
