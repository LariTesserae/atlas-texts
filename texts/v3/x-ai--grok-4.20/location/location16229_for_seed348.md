The place is called the Hollowing.

It is a low, bowl-shaped depression no larger than a cathedral, yet it feels far older than any structure that size has a right to be. The ground is not soil but a single, continuous slab of pale, bluish-grey mineral that has been weathered for so long it has taken on the texture of elephant skin—every pore and crease permanent, every shallow runnel fixed in place for ten thousand years. Water does not flow here so much as appear and vanish. It beads on the stone in fat, trembling drops that never quite fall, condenses along hairline fractures, and gathers in perfectly circular pits the width of dinner plates. These pools never overflow and never evaporate. Their surfaces are mirror-still, reflecting a sky that is not quite the sky outside.

There is no vegetation. Not even lichen. Nothing green has ever been permitted. What grows instead is mechanical.

Hundreds of iron-and-brass mechanisms rise from the stone like half-finished sculptures left by an absent titan. Some are no taller than a man—stubby, riveted pillars topped with slowly turning governor wheels. Others stand three or four storeys high, blackened boiler chests and piston limbs frozen mid-stroke, their gears locked in perfect, eternal engagement. Pipes of every diameter snake between them, jointed with bulbous valves that have not moved in centuries yet still gleam with oil that never dries. The entire assembly forms a single, motionless city-machine no bigger than a hamlet, yet dense with the suggestion of unimaginable industry. Walkways of perforated iron cross above your head at odd angles, leading nowhere. Chimneys rise and stop, as though the sky itself refused them exit.

The air is cool, damp, and tastes faintly of ozone and wet iron. Condensation drips from every horizontal surface with a slow, metronomic *plick… plick…* that never accelerates and never stops. This is the only water that truly moves.

The sound, however, is overwhelming.

From deep inside the machine-city comes a constant, layered roar: the bass throb of boilers that contain no fire, the metallic whine of dynamos spinning in vacuum, the hiss of pressure that has nowhere to go. Every gear, though immobile, still sings at its own resonant frequency. The combined chorus is so loud it becomes architectural; you feel it in your teeth and in the small bones of your ears. It never rises, never falls. It simply *is*, a frozen avalanche of noise that has reached equilibrium and will remain exactly this loud until the universe forgets the concept of time.

The fauna is the part that breaks the mind.

They are not animals. They are better described as *occupants*. Vast numbers of them—thousands upon thousands—move in slow, choreographed circuits through the gaps between the machines. Most are the size of large dogs: segmented things of dull chrome and black glass, many-legged, many-eyed, carrying no obvious purpose. They click and whirr as they walk. Some trail thin copper cables that spark lazily against the stone. Others have translucent bladders on their backs filled with the same motionless water from the pits. They never touch one another. They never pause. Their paths are worn into the stone over what must have been geological time—shallow, perfectly parallel grooves that all lead back to the same few openings in the largest mechanisms, where the creatures vanish inside and eventually re-emerge, unchanged.

A few are larger. One colossal mantis-like construct of rust and porcelain stands motionless atop the highest boiler, wings spread, compound eyes full of tiny red lights that never blink. It has been in that exact posture longer than any human language has existed. Smaller things orbit it in endless, silent procession, as though it were a saint.

Everything here is in perfect balance. Nothing decays further. Nothing improves. The condensation drips at the same rate it has always dripped. The gears sing at the same pitch. The chrome creatures trace the same grooves they traced when the first fish crawled out of the sea far above.

The sky above the Hollowing is the ordinary grey of a cool, overcast day, but the light that reaches the basin is somehow older—thick, mineral, almost underwater in quality. It makes every rivet and every droplet stand out with painful clarity. There are no shadows that last longer than a few minutes; the angle of light is fixed, as though the sun outside has also reached equilibrium and simply stopped its arc.

If you stand still long enough (and the roaring makes it hard to want to stay), you begin to understand the central strangeness: the entire place is a single machine that has achieved absolute homeostasis. It no longer needs fuel, purpose, or movement. It has become a perfect, self-sustaining fossil of industry—an industrial climax community.

And the chrome creatures are not its maintenance staff.

They are its immune system.

They keep it exactly, eternally, as it is.

Welcome to the Hollowing.  
Stay as long as the sound will let you.  
Nothing here has ever left.
