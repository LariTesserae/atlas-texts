The world is called the **Vast Haul**.

It has no water and never did. What looks like ground is in truth an endless, living mat of vegetation so dense and interwoven that it has become its own geology. This is not a planet that *grew* plants; it is a planet that *is* a single, colossal, vegetative organism whose uppermost living skin has calcified, lignified, and mineralized over incomprehensible time into something that behaves like stone while still remaining alive.

You stand at elevation 1.7 — high enough that the curvature of the world is visible, yet still dwarfed by the scale. The living crust stretches away in every direction for tens of thousands of kilometers, a continent-sized body whose surface is a rumpled, wind-carved epidermis of fibrous plates, root-braids thick as highways, and translucent amber spires that catch the permanent daylight and turn it into blinding lattices of gold and bone-white.

The temperature is cool — a constant 4 °C that never rises or falls. The air itself smells of ozone, chlorophyll, and distant fermentation.

There is almost no soil. Roots do not burrow into earth; they *are* the earth. Between the colossal plates and ridges lie wide, wind-scoured basins of emptiness — the “sparse” parameter made literal. These basins are not depressions in rock but gaps in the organism itself, places where the living mass has deliberately withdrawn, leaving behind only polished, ivory-colored scaffolding and strange geometric voids that look engineered yet were never touched by tools. In these empty basins the wind screams without obstruction.

And the sound is deafening.

The Vast Haul is never still. Its dynamic parameter is set near maximum: the entire living crust is in constant, turbulent motion. Plates the size of countries grind against one another with the low, oceanic roar of continental drift compressed into audible time. Fibrous cables thicker than redwoods contract and expand in slow peristalsis, producing a wet leathery thunder that rolls across the plains. From deep within the living mass comes the constant industrial heartbeat of pressurized sap moving through kilometer-wide vascular channels — a sound like every factory on Earth running at once, yet entirely biological. When two major plates lock and then suddenly release, the crack is loud enough to cause physical pain and momentary deafness.

Above it all, the sky is blindingly bright. There are no clouds, only a perpetual high-altitude haze of pollen and microscopic thallus fragments that turns the sun into a white inferno. Light diffracts through the amber spires and living crystal lattices, creating moving searchlights of unbearable intensity that sweep the landscape in slow, tidal rhythms.

Fauna is abundant but almost never visible at human scale. The animals here are either microscopic — vast drifting clouds of luminous filter-feeders that ride the sap tides inside the crust — or so large they have become part of the architecture. The “mountains” on the horizon are actually the fossilized, still-living bodies of continent-scale herbivores that have lain down and calcified into the greater organism while their descendants continue to graze across their grandparents’ backs. Smaller creatures (many-legged things like ambulatory kelp, glass-winged lizards whose bones are cellulose, things that look like white stags made of tightly braided roots) move in sparse, elegant processions across the barren basins, avoiding the crushing seams where the plates meet.

The ecology is pure-plant with an industrial aesthetic. Everything is substrate, architecture, and metabolism at once. There are no “forests” because the entire world *is* the forest. There are no cities because the organism itself performs scaled production: it grows engines of wood and chitin that compress hydrogen from the air, it extrudes kilometer-long bellows that breathe for it, it manufactures its own lightning in internal storm-nodes and uses the electricity to split minerals out of its own bones. All of this is done without a single artificial object. The tech level reads “industrial” only because the scale and energy throughput are industrial; nothing here was built. Everything was grown, secreted, or photosynthesized.

Erosion has reached a state of grotesque, magnificent equilibrium. The living crust has weathered itself into forms that look both primordial and hyper-refined: fractal ridgelines, honeycombed escarpments, vast rib-cages of exposed xylem that sing in the wind like aeolian harps the size of mountain ranges. The turbulent motion prevents anything from becoming permanent, yet the sheer biological inertia of the organism ensures that nothing is ever truly destroyed — only digested, re-extruded, and re-grown elsewhere in a different shape. The world is simultaneously ancient and brand-new every few centuries.

Weirdness settles in the details. Occasionally the crust will rear up an entire district of itself into a perfect spiral tower two kilometers high, only to let it collapse again days later in a deliberate act that might be respiration, reproduction, or sheer vegetative boredom. In the emptiest basins you sometimes find perfectly circular mirrors of polished root-glass that reflect the sky so accurately they look like holes straight through the planet. At rare intervals the entire visible world will fall silent for nine heartbeats — every grinding plate, every bellows-lung, every roaring vascular channel — before the cacophony returns twice as loud, as if the organism is reminding itself it is still alive.

This is not a planet with life.

This is a life that has achieved planetary scale, industrial metabolism, and permanent tinnitus, and has decided that this arrangement is good.
