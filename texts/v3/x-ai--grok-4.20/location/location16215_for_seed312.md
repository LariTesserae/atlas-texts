The plateau is called **Veyr-Kaht**, the Broken Shelf. It stretches so far that its own weather is born and dies here without ever touching the lowlands. At 0.6 moisture it is not desert-dry, but it is cold enough (temperature 0.0) that most water arrives as frost, rime, or thin, metallic-tasting rain that lasts an hour and then vanishes into the stone. The air itself feels dense, almost syrupy, because the plateau sits so high (elevation 2.2) that the sky has thinned and every breath must be earned.

The land is young. Immense basalt flows, only a few thousand winters old, have been cracked open by frost and wind into razor ridges, stepped mesas, and sudden vertical slots that drop hundreds of meters to unseen floors. Nothing has had time to soften. Every edge is still raw, every cliff face still sharp enough to cut the wind into audible moans.

Yet the plateau is not empty.

A dense, low, stubborn civilization has taken root across its surface like dark lichen on broken glass. Because the scale is continental and the rock is hard, people have built upward rather than outward. Thousands of terraced towns cling to the sides of the great ridges in layered shelves of dry-stone and black timber. Stairways—some cut directly into the basalt, others built as zig-zagging wooden scaffolds—connect one settlement to the next like frayed threads. From a distance the inhabited ridges look like long, dark combs pressed into the stone.

Vegetation is sparse but strangely uniform: knee-high mats of silver-gray moss that drink the nightly condensation, scattered clumps of black-needled “ironpine” no taller than a man, and occasional islands of pale, waist-high grass that only grow where snowmelt pools for a few weeks each year. These green pockets are jealously guarded; every one is ringed by low walls and watched.

The fauna is abundant in a way that feels almost perverse given the cold and the thin soil. Vast colonies of small, thick-furred goat-like creatures called *keth* move in slow, deliberate processions along the ridges, their hooves clicking in a constant, patient percussion. They are followed, always, by clouds of fat, silent ravens that have lost the power of flight at this altitude and instead hop and glide between the rooftops and the animals’ backs, feeding on insects stirred by the herd. In the deeper slots, where water sometimes gathers into black, mirror-flat pools, six-legged otters with fur like polished obsidian slip in and out of the stone itself.

Sound is mostly quiet, yet never absent. There is the low, endless whisper of wind moving through the slots, the dry clicking of keth hooves, the creak of rope bridges, the distant ringing of hammers shaping basalt into tools and building blocks. The cities make almost no noise; the people speak little and sing less. Life here is measured in decades, not days.

The cities themselves are dense, almost claustrophobic. Houses are stacked four and five high, their roofs serving as streets for the level above. Every structure is windowless on the windward side, presenting only narrow arrow-slits or nothing at all to the west, where the prevailing gale comes from. Lanterns are rare; most light is normal daylight filtered through perpetual high-altitude haze, or else the soft orange glow of slow-burning moss bricks that give off almost no smoke.

Nothing happens quickly. A wall that collapses in a rockslide will be rebuilt over thirty years. A child born on the plateau will not see the lowlands until they are old enough to survive the descent. The rivers that do exist are mostly underground, heard as faint subterranean bells when one stands near certain fissures. What moisture the surface receives arrives in slow, patient cycles: frost at night, a thin rain that beads on every roof like mercury, then evaporation under a pale, watery sun.

There is nothing especially weird here. Only the sheer, patient stubbornness of a young world that has already been claimed by small, slow, unyielding people who have decided this cold, vast, dense shelf of broken stone is home. They call themselves the *Veyr*, the Shelf-born, and they move across their sharp world with the same deliberate pace as the keth, as the ravens, as the frost that forms and vanishes again each turning of the long, slow year.
