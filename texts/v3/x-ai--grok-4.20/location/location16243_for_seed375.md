The place is called the Hollow Choir.

It is a single, self-contained chamber no larger than a modest cathedral, yet it feels far vaster because almost nothing occupies its volume. The ceiling is lost in darkness forty meters above; the floor is a single slab of freshly fractured mineral the color of bone left too long in starlight. Every surface—floor, walls, the jagged spurs that rise like broken ribs—looks as though it was born yesterday. Edges are painfully sharp. Fractures still sparkle with microscopic cleavage planes that have not yet had time to blunt. The rock itself is anhydrous, without history of water; it is a dry silicate laced with strange metallic salts that gleam faint mother-of-pearl even in the gloom.

There is no water here. None has ever been. Chemistry proceeds through slow catalysis of exotic ices that sublimate directly from solid to trace vapor, leaving behind delicate efflorescences that crumble at a breath. The air itself is thin, cold (just above freezing), and smells of nothing at all.

The city—if it can be called that—was not built. It grew.

Hundreds of pale, bony spires and vaults rise from the floor in ordered rows, each one a living mineral organism. They are not architecture; they are organisms that happen to arrange themselves into streets, arches, and hollow towers. Their surfaces are covered in sparse, black, wire-like growths no thicker than eyelashes—half vegetation, half nervous system—that twitch and realign themselves in imperceptible waves. These growths are the only “plants” the Hollow Choir possesses. They move with the slow, tidal rhythm of something that thinks, folding and unfolding according to patterns that have no season, only pulse.

Everything is in motion, yet nothing seems to hurry.

The Choir sings in perfect silence. Every few minutes a new fracture opens somewhere with a sound too low to be heard by human ears; the mineral beings respond by shifting their internal lattices. Streets realign by a finger’s width. A tower that stood for three hundred years folds in on itself with a sigh of released tension and becomes a bridge. Another bridge dissolves into dust that is immediately reabsorbed by neighboring spires. The city is editing itself, constantly, according to rules no outsider has ever deciphered.

Light is dim and sourceless. It arrives as a faint, bluish-gray phosphorescence that leaks from the joints between the living structures, as though the city were lit from its own wounds. Shadows are absolute. In the rare places where two beams cross, the air itself seems to thicken into drifting veils of slower time.

Fauna is rare but uncanny. The only visible creatures are the Memory Eels—long, transparent ribbons of something between crystal and flesh that drift horizontally through the empty air, propelled by faint thermal gradients. They have no eyes. They feed on the memories that crystallize as microscopic inclusions inside the mineral architecture. When an eel passes, portions of the city’s own history are gently erased and re-written. No one knows what they replace those memories with.

Sound barely exists. Once every hour or so a single clear note rings out—high, sweet, and short—like a glass bell struck once and then instantly smothered. The note always comes from a different direction. No one has ever found its source. After it fades, the silence feels deeper than before, as though the Hollow Choir had inhaled.

The strangest law of the place is this: the farther you walk toward the center of any “street,” the smaller the surrounding structures appear to become, while the distance to the far wall never seems to shrink. Perspective is broken here. The city is simultaneously cramped and measureless. You can stand between two living towers that rise twenty meters high, turn slowly, and feel you are at the bottom of an abyss whose walls are only an arm’s length away.

It is a young city. A new city. A city that is still deciding what a city is.

And it is watching you with the patient, alien curiosity of something that has never needed eyes.
