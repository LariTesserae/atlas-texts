The place is called the Hollow Choir.

It is not a single valley but a continent-scale basin that has been eroding for so long that the word “mountain” no longer applies. What remains are the stumps of primordial ranges, worn down until they resemble the knuckles of a hand long drowned beneath time. The elevation is still extreme: the basin floor lies more than three vertical kilometres below the shattered lip of the surrounding escarpment, yet the escarpment itself has been bevelled by a billion years of wind and water into a soft, almost gentle horizon. You can stand on its edge and feel that the planet has exhaled here until its ribs show.

Everything is damp, but never flooded. Moisture arrives in sighs: slow, warm mists that rise from hidden aquifers, sudden glass-sharp rains that last three minutes and vanish, heavy condensation that beads on every surface and drips with the rhythm of a failing heart. The air itself feels spongy. Lichen and moss drink it directly; ferns the height of three-storey houses unroll their fronds in minutes after each shower.

Vegetation is lush in the way a fever is lush—excessive, almost obscene. In the few places where soil has managed to cling to the ancient rock it explodes into layered green: mats of clubmoss, aerial gardens of bromeliads, hanging curtains of lianas thick as a man’s thigh. Yet these riotous pockets are separated by vast stretches of naked stone, so that the overall impression is of a green archipelago floating in a stony sea. Between the pockets the land is nearly empty. You can walk for half a day across warm, echoing basalt and hear nothing but your own breath and the distant, unending roar.

The sound is deafening because the wind never stops. It pours over the escarpment lip in a continuous waterfall of air, funnels down the old glacial valleys, and collides with thermals rising from the sun-baked stone. The result is a constant, many-voiced thunder that has worn grooves into the rock itself. The noise is not random; after days you begin to hear architecture inside it—deep organ drones, high shrieking overtones, rhythmic booms like distant kettledrums. The locals (there are a few) call it the Choir. They say the planet is still singing the memory of the oceans that once covered everything.

Nothing stays still for long. The dynamic here is almost violent. Whole hillsides of scree rearrange themselves overnight. Rivers appear where yesterday there was only a damp seam in the rock; they carve a channel, run red with eroded iron, then vanish again into a new sinkhole that simply opens beneath them. Trees topple not from age but because the ground beneath them has quietly migrated. In certain basins the air itself seems to congeal into fleeting, half-organic shapes—mist sculptures that twist, reach, and dissolve within minutes. The locals leave small offerings of carved bone and knotted grass at places where these apparitions are most common, the way other cultures leave flowers on graves.

Fauna is rare. What exists is strange and mostly subterranean or nocturnal. Pale, long-limbed creatures like hairless sloths move with dreaming slowness across the vertical faces of the escarpment, licking condensation from the stone. In the deep, echoing caves live colonies of blind, white cicadas whose synchronized calls add a metallic, bell-like layer to the Choir. Once every few years something enormous surfaces in the largest sinkhole lakes—something that breaks the water like a rolling whale, breathes once with a sound like mountains cracking, and submerges again for another half-decade.

Technology is primitive. The few thousand humans who still live here are descendants of a forgotten migration. They build nothing larger than a single-room shelter of stacked slate and living rope. Their tools are stone, wood, and the sharpened shoulder-blades of the cliff-sloths. They have no metal; the constant moisture and the abrasive wind eat it in weeks. Instead they weave, carve, and remember. Their highest craft is the shaping of wind-traps—delicate lattices of bone and hollow reed that sing individual notes into the Choir, allowing them to “speak back” to the land. Some of these traps have been tended for fourteen generations and are considered living ancestors.

Built things are scattered and ancient. You will come across a single standing stone incised with spirals so eroded they look like fingerprints. A collapsed watchtower of dry-laid masonry on a needle of rock. A perfectly circular pool, thirty metres across, lined with turquoise tiles whose glaze still shines after what must be ten thousand years. No one knows who made these things. The current inhabitants treat them with the same wary reverence they give the mist-shapes.

Light is normal, yet never feels ordinary. Because the basin is so deep and the air so full of moisture and pollen, the sun arrives softened, golden, almost underwater. At certain hours the mist ignites and the entire landscape becomes a cathedral of drifting light-beams and roaring shadow. At night the stars are impossibly bright, and the wind carries their reflection in the wet stone so that the ground itself seems to be starlit.

This is a place at the end of its life and simultaneously in the middle of some ferocious, ongoing act of self-creation. It is ancient, turbulent, nearly empty, deafeningly loud, and impossibly, feverishly green in its few permitted places. It feels like standing inside the lungs of a god who has been dying and dreaming for longer than time has names, and who still, every dawn, cannot decide whether to finish dying or start all over again.
