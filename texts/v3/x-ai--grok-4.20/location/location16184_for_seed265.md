The place has no name that anyone still remembers.

It is called only the Black Fen, and even that feels like an old lie.

You emerge from the dark without transition. There is no horizon, only a thick, warm atmosphere so saturated with moisture that the air itself seems to have weight. Every breath condenses on your skin and runs in slow, oily beads. The temperature never drops below blood-heat; it simply breathes against you like something alive and patient.

The ground is young. It has not yet learned how to be gentle. Black basaltic stone, still glassy in places from whatever catastrophe birthed it, thrusts up in raw knife-blades and fractured plates. Between the jutting rock, water has found every weakness. It pools in warm, skin-temperature basins, seeps from weeping fractures in the stone, and hangs in the air as a perpetual mist that never quite falls as rain and never quite lifts as cloud. The water is not clear. It carries fine black silt and something bioluminescent that glows a faint, sick emerald when disturbed, so that the entire fen flickers with ghost-lights when anything moves.

Vegetation is sparse but viciously efficient. What grows does so in violent, isolated clots: tall, jointed reeds the color of old bruises, fleshy liverworts that pulse faintly with their own heat, and thick mats of something that might be moss or might be fungus or might be both. These colonies huddle in the lee of rock teeth or around the half-sunk ruins of machines, drinking the condensation that drips endlessly from every surface.

The machines are everywhere, but never close together. They have been here long enough for the fen to begin digesting them. Enormous rusting boilers lie on their sides like beached whales. Skeletal conveyor frameworks rise thirty meters into the dark, draped with curtains of condensation that fall in slow, continuous sheets. A single locomotive, wheels sunk deep into warm silt, has become a vertical garden of black orchids that open only when the air pressure changes. Pipelines, cracked and weeping warm chemical tears, snake across the terrain in long, slow curves before vanishing into the stone again. All of it is running, after a fashion. You can hear the distant, patient heartbeat of pumps working somewhere far off, the low metallic groan of metal adjusting to temperature, the occasional soft sigh of escaping steam. But the sounds are so spaced out they feel like the respiration of a creature the size of the valley itself.

The fauna is abundant and mostly silent. Things move in the dark that have no business existing together. Pale, heat-sensing amphibians the size of dogs drift between the pools, their bodies translucent enough to show the slow pulse of black blood. Swarms of metallic insects, each no larger than a grain of rice, rise and fall in perfect synchrony like iron filings obeying an invisible magnet. Once every few hours a sound like wet canvas tearing announces the passage of something vast sliding beneath the surface of the largest pools. Nothing here is in a hurry. Even the predators hunt with the patience of glaciers.

This is where reality has begun to forget its own rules.

In certain places the stone simply stops being stone. It continues as texture, as memory of stone, but your hand passes through it as though it were warm fog. Other places the air folds. Stand in the right spot (there are seven of them that have been counted) and you can watch yesterday’s rain falling upward into a sky that is not there. Echoes arrive before the sounds that make them. Shadows sometimes remain after the thing casting them has moved on. At the edge of perception, entire abandoned factories phase in and out of existence, their smokestacks bleeding rust into the mist before flickering away again like a dying bulb.

The darkness is not absolute. It is worse. It is a textured, oily absence that clings. The only light comes from the slow emerald glow of disturbed water, the occasional sullen orange of a distant chemical reaction inside a ruptured storage tank, and the tiny cold stars of bioluminescent lichen that have colonized the highest iron girders. These lights do not illuminate so much as suggest. You are never quite sure what you are looking at until you have already smelled it, or tasted the air change, or felt the ground tremble in a way that suggests something enormous just sighed beneath your feet.

Everything is slowly sinking.

The fen is still rising from whatever wound created it, but at the same time the black stone is subsiding, letting the warm water in. Old pipelines disappear by centimeters every year. Entire sections of factory floor have tilted into warm pools and now lie half-submerged, their rivets blooming with slow rust gardens. In a few centuries the last smokestack will slip beneath the surface with a soft, patient sound, and the Black Fen will close over it like it was never there.

Until then the place continues its long, slow labor: water condensing, stone fracturing, machines pumping their last black fluids into the mist, creatures hunting and being hunted in silence, reality quietly coming apart at the seams.

It is not dying.

It is becoming something else, very patiently, and it does not care whether you watch.
