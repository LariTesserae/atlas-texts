The place is called the **Calcine**.

It is not a desert, because deserts still remember water. This is older than thirst. It is a high-altidunal plain that has been scoured so completely that even the concept of “plain” feels like an insult to its finality. Elevation sits at the absolute edge of what a body can endure: 2.6 on the scale means the sky is not above you so much as pressed against you, thin and metallic. The air itself has weight, a cold, dry pressure that makes every breath feel like chewing glass.

Everything here has reached its terminal shape.

The ground is not rock. It is a single continuous mineral dermis, an amalgam of ancient silicates, metallic salts, and something that was once perhaps carbon-based life but has since been sintered into a uniform ochre-white crust. This crust is not flat. It has been folded, crumpled, faulted, and then worn down for so long that its folds have become a kind of slow calligraphy—ripples and ridges no higher than a man’s knee, yet stretching for hundreds of kilometers in mathematically precise waves. The erosion parameter of 2.6 has done its work: every possible edge has been removed. Only the oldest-stable-state remains, a landscape that looks breathed out rather than carved.

It is dense.

Not with life in the usual sense, but with presence. The Calcine is crowded with things that should not fit together. Millions upon millions of skeletal spires—some no taller than a finger, some the height of cathedrals—rise from the crust at every scale. They are not stone. They are the calcified corpses of something that once grew here when the world still allowed growth. Their surfaces are covered in fractal lattices of microscopic blades that catch the normal, sourceless light and turn it into a low, constant shimmer, as though the entire plain is faintly phosphorescent. Between the spires the ground is packed with smaller relics: broken cerulean shells, coils of petrified tendon, glassy beads that might once have been eyes. Nothing decays. Nothing moves. The dynamic parameter of 0.2 has locked the place into frozen equilibrium. Even dust is forbidden.

Vegetation is almost absent. What little exists appears as sparse black filaments, hair-thin, that grow in perfect hexagonal clusters from microscopic fissures. They do not photosynthesize. They resonate. When the rare breeze (itself almost a memory) passes over them they emit a tone too low for human ears, a subsonic mourning that travelers feel in their teeth.

The fauna is overwhelming.

They are not animals. They are continuance engines. Vast herds of them—thousands strong—stand motionless across the plain, layered in three dimensions: some on the crust, some perched on the spires, some hovering in the thin air on wings of mirrored membrane that never beat. Their bodies are translucent lattices of bone and vacuum. Inside each one you can see smaller versions of themselves, recursively, down past visibility. They do not eat. They do not breathe. They simply are, saturated into the landscape like ink into paper. Their eyes—when they have them—follow you with the patience of geology. Sometimes one will slowly rotate its head through an impossible angle, creaking like an old door, and then return to absolute stillness. The silence is so complete that their occasional exhalations (dry clicks, the sound of two pebbles kissing) feel like violence.

Reality is tired here.

Weirdness at 2.7 means the Calcine has reached the point where the rules have begun to forget themselves. Perspective collapses without warning: a spire that looks a hundred meters away will suddenly loom directly overhead, then snap back. Shadows point in contradictory directions though the light is steady and sourceless. If you speak, your words arrive moments later from somewhere behind you, spoken in a voice that is almost, but not quite, yours. Certain geometries—perfect circles drawn in the dust, equilateral triangles of arranged bones—act as weak perforations. Stand inside one long enough and you can feel the Calcine considering whether or not to let you fall through into whatever lies beneath the final form.

Scattered across this density are the built remnants.

Primitive. Ancient. A single hand-forged iron hook driven into a spire so perfectly that it has become part of the mineral. A low circular wall of fitted white bricks no higher than your thigh, enclosing nothing. A half-buried obsidian blade whose edge is still sharp enough to split hair after what must be geological ages. These artifacts are so sparse they feel like punctuation marks on an unspeakably long sentence. No one knows who left them. The prevailing theory among the few ascetics who come here is that the objects were not brought; they were excreted by the Calcine itself when it was still learning how to be finished.

There is no water. There never was. Chemistry here is anhydrous and patient. The dominant reaction is crystallization. Everything is either becoming crystal or has already become it.

At night—which is not truly night but a subtle reddening of the sourceless light—the fauna begin to sing in perfect unison. The sound is so quiet it is almost imaginary: a choral absence, the noise that silence would make if silence could remember having once been sound.

This is the oldest place that still pretends to be a place.

It has settled into its final form so thoroughly that even time has given up and gone elsewhere. Nothing will ever change here again. The Calcine has won. It is complete.

And yet, every few centuries, a single new filament of black vegetation will pierce the crust, tremble once, and then freeze forever—adding one more syllable to the endless, finished poem of the plain.
