The place has no name because nothing here has ever needed one.

It is a single, impossibly vast underground city that has been dying for so long that dying itself has grown ancient, patient, and almost gentle. The ceiling is lost in a haze of mineral dust and faint bioluminescence; the floor is a rolling plain of worn marble, basalt, and travertine that has been trodden, crumbled, and re-settled for so many millions of years that the original streets and plazas have melted into one another like wax left too close to a candle. Elevation is low, depth is absolute. There is no water anywhere. Not a drop has existed in this basin for geological epochs. What little moisture once flowed through these halls evaporated before the first trilobites learned to crawl.

Yet the place is lush.

Vegetation has taken the dry city the way ivy once claimed abandoned temples, except this vegetation is not green. It is the color of bone, pearl, and old ivory. Vast colonies of lithotrophic fungi and symbiotic crystal-plants grow in slow, deliberate carpets across every surface. They do not need light or water; they eat stone itself, exhaling microscopic clouds of metallic oxygen and nitrogen in return. Their fruiting bodies rise in pale, antlered thickets taller than a man, spaced far apart so that between each cluster lies an acre of empty, echoing pavement. The overall effect is a park designed by a civilization that died before it finished the plans.

The temperature is cool enough that breath would fog, if anything here still breathed with lungs. It is the steady ten degrees of deep rock that has forgotten the sun.

Everything is deafening.

The sound is not one roar but ten thousand overlapping drones. Colonies of blind, soft-bodied megafauna (descendants of something that might once have been bats or insects or neither) have evolved to sing instead of see. Their calls are subsonic, tectonic, felt in the teeth and the sternum. Millions of them cling to the distant ceiling in living constellations, each one producing a slow, patient note that lasts for minutes. The combined chorus is a continuous, bone-rattling chord that has never changed in recorded memory. It is the sound of the planet digesting itself. After a few hours a visitor no longer hears it as noise; it becomes architecture.

The city is urban in the most literal sense: tiered galleries, fallen aqueducts, vast sunken forums, leaning towers that have fused into natural buttes of brick and concrete. But the builders are gone so completely that even their bones have turned to dust. What remains is only infrastructure, worn down until it looks like geology. Bridges have sagged into elegant natural arches. Colonnades have become forests of stone trees draped in pale fungal garlands. Every surface is etched with the slow, millimeter-per-millennium graffiti of erosion and mineral accretion. The final form is neither city nor wilderness; it is something older than both.

Fauna is abundant yet strangely decorous. The megafauna on the ceiling. Vast herds of translucent, six-legged browsers the size of cattle that drift between the fungal groves, scraping nutrients from the stone with rasping mouthparts. Clouds of smaller creatures that glitter like mica when they catch the dim light. All of them move with the same unhurried rhythm as the vegetation. Nothing here is in a hurry. Predation exists, but it is glacial. A kill might take decades.

Light is dim and entirely biological. The fungi and crystal-plants emit a cold, bluish-white radiance that pools in the hollows and barely reaches the soaring ceiling. It is less like daylight and more like starlight filtered through milk. Shadows are soft, endless, and kindly.

Nothing is weird here. Everything is exactly what it has become after four hundred million years of uninterrupted peace. This is not a ruin. This is the final, stable state. The end of succession. The place where entropy itself grew tired and sat down to rest.

If you stand in the middle of one of the wider boulevards, the fungal spires spaced like obelisks around you, the living constellations singing overhead, the weight of the dead city folded into the living stone beneath your feet, you understand that time has not stopped. It has simply achieved its mature form: slow enough to be eternal, loud enough to be silence, empty enough to be full. 

This is what a world looks like when it has outlived every reason to change.
