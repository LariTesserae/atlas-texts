The Plateau of Hanging Veils is not yet a finished place. It feels as though the world has only half-awoken here, still deciding what it wants to become.

You stand at roughly 2,800 meters, high enough that the air is thin and always cool, yet the light is mercilessly bright. A white-gold sun hammers down through an atmosphere so saturated with water that every surface glistens. Mist does not drift here; it condenses directly out of the sky in slow, fat drops that fall straight, as if the clouds have grown tired of holding themselves together. The rocks themselves weep. Every cliff face, every boulder, every newborn ridge is streaked with constant rivulets that never quite dry. The water table is so high that the ground itself seems to sweat.

Yet the dominant substance is not stone. It is plant.

A single, incomprehensible species (or perhaps a symbiotic nation of species) has taken the plateau as its substrate. It does not grow on the rock; the rock grows inside it. Fibrous mats of living green and oxblood red, thick as ship’s cable, pour over every surface like slow lava. These mats are the ground. Where they pull apart you can see raw, embryonic geology beneath: black volcanic glass still cooling, pale granite only just beginning to crystallize, red clays that have not yet decided whether to become soil or remain mineral. The vegetation is building the land as much as it is colonizing it.

The plant architecture is both cathedral and factory. In the near distance rise colossal living buttresses—twisted trunks the width of grain silos—supporting horizontal lattices of vine and leaf so dense they form ceilings dozens of meters above the spongy floor. Sunlight falls through these ceilings in solid shafts, illuminating drifting clouds of spores that glitter like metal filings. Between the buttresses the land is almost empty. Vast lawns of moss, ankle-deep and brilliant emerald, stretch for kilometers with almost nothing breaking their surface except the occasional black iron pipe or rusted valve stem rising like a metallic weed.

This is where the industrial appears.

Scattered across the plateau are the half-forgotten works of a previous civilization that clearly mistook the place for ordinary mountain tundra. Enormous steam engines the size of locomotives sit half-swallowed by the moss, their boilers long cold, funnels plugged with living fiber. Copper pipes, thick as a man’s thigh, run in straight lines for hundreds of meters before disappearing into the root mass, as though the plants have decided these artifacts are now part of their vascular system. Here and there a skeletal iron watchtower leans at a drunken angle, its rivets weeping rust that the moss immediately drinks. A single, monumental factory hall—its roof long collapsed—stands in the middle distance like a ruined ribcage. Inside, the vegetation has grown into the shape of missing machinery, green phantoms of presses and looms.

The sound is deafening because it is total and low. It is not the sound of animals. It is the sound of water moving through ten thousand kilometers of living pipes. A constant, bone-deep thrum and gurgle, as if the entire plateau is one enormous cardiovascular system. Every few minutes a new section of plant-matter sighs open with a wet, tearing noise and releases a cataract that did not exist a moment earlier. The sound never stops, yet it never rises to panic. It is slow, patient, geological.

Fauna is almost nonexistent. Once in a great while a small grey bird, almost translucent, will dart between shafts of light and vanish. Nothing larger seems to live here. The plants do not appear to welcome company.

The scale is open in the way only half-formed places can be. You can see for twenty kilometers in every direction, yet the horizon is always veiled by hanging curtains of water and spore. New ridges are still rising, pushed upward by root pressure so gradual it would take a human lifetime to notice the change. Entire valleys are still only suggestions—shallow depressions where the moss is a slightly different shade, waiting for the water to decide where it wants to pool next.

There are rules here, though they are still being written.

The plants appear to be terraforming the plateau according to some logic older than any machine. The iron and copper left by humans are being slowly, lovingly incorporated—oxides and all—into new conductive veins that pulse with faint bio-electric charge. If you press your ear to the largest living buttress you can sometimes hear, beneath the deafening wet thunder, the distant clack and groan of metal parts being moved inside the wood itself, as though the vegetation is learning how to be a factory on its own terms.

This is not a ruin.

It is a birth still in progress, loud, wet, blindingly bright, and taking its time. The land is not yet decided whether it will be jungle, machine, or something that has never needed a name. For now it is all three at once, and the moss keeps growing, the rocks keep weeping, and the iron keeps rusting into something that might, one distant century, learn how to flower.
