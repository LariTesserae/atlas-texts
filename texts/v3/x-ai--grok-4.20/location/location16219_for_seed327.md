The place is called the **Hanging Vale of Veyr**, a high-altitudinal basin no larger than a modest cathedral yet so vertically exaggerated that the mind refuses the scale at first.

You stand on a shelf of black basalt no wider than a cart, perhaps four hundred feet above the basin floor and still a thousand feet below the jagged lip of the caldera that contains everything. The air is cool, almost cold, yet perpetually damp; every breath feels like it has already been exhaled by the mountain itself. Mist condenses on every surface, beads, and runs in silver threads. The stone weeps. Even the oldest cliffs sweat.

The basin itself is strangely small—scarcely three hundred paces across—yet the vertical drama makes it feel enormous. Sheer walls rise on all sides, fluted and folded like vast organ pipes. Water is everywhere without ever becoming a lake: it oozes from stratified layers of porous rock, drips in long glassy curtains from overhanging ledges, trickles down moss-slicked chutes, and collects in thousands of bowl-sized hollows before spilling to the next. The sound is constant and deafening: a layered roar of falling water, trickling water, dripping water, and the low, thrumming vibration of larger streams hidden inside the mountain. The noise never stops. It is the Vale’s heartbeat.

Vegetation has taken the wet stone as its empire. Because the basin is so small and the walls so high, every plant grows outward or downward rather than upward. Ancient dwarf hemlocks cling sideways to the cliffs, their trunks twisted into question marks, their roots groping like blind hands into crevices. Mats of emerald moss thick as saddle blankets drape every ledge. Ferns the color of oxidized copper hang in trembling curtains. Tiny orchids no larger than a thumbnail bloom in the perpetual mist, their scent sharp and mineral. Everything is lush, but nothing is tall. The tallest living thing here is a single lightning-blasted fir that somehow rooted in a crack halfway up the eastern wall; it grows horizontally for thirty feet before turning sharply skyward, as though it changed its mind about gravity.

The fauna is loud and strangely visible. Because the space is so bounded and the vegetation so low, animals cannot hide. A colony of white rock-marmots the size of small dogs screams territorial challenges from every ledge; their calls bounce and multiply until the walls themselves seem to scold you. Cloud-swallows with metallic blue throats dart through the mist in shrieking squadrons, snatching midges that rise in glittering columns from the wet stone. Pale, almost translucent deer no bigger than goats pick their way along invisible paths across the cliff faces, their hooves clicking like thrown pebbles. At night, bioluminescent millipedes the length of your forearm crawl out of crevices and trace slow, glowing constellations across the dripping rock.

Scattered among all this are the marks of something older than the moss. Primitive hand-tools—obsidian blades, antler picks, lengths of braided sinew—lie where they were dropped centuries ago, preserved by the constant cold humidity. Here and there, small leaning structures of dry-stacked stone no higher than your waist suggest hermit cells or shrines. One contains a seated skeleton still wearing a cloak of woven moss. Another holds only a single perfect circle of quartz pebbles. No metal. No writing. Only the patient geometry of people who once tried to live inside the roar.

The light is normal, yet strangely theatrical. Because the basin is so deep and narrow, direct sunlight only reaches the floor for perhaps two hours near true noon. The rest of the day is drenched in a cool, shadowless brightness reflected and refracted by uncountable running sheets of water. Every droplet becomes a tiny lens. Rainbows appear and vanish in the mist without warning. At certain hours the entire eastern wall ignites with prismatic fire while the western wall remains the color of wet slate. The effect is disorienting, almost hallucinatory.

Everything here is in frantic, noisy balance. Water carves new channels even as moss and roots seal old ones. Stone falls and is immediately colonized. Marmots gnaw roots and are in turn taken by shadow-colored lynx that flow along the cliffs like smoke. The air itself feels busy—always condensing, always falling, always being exhaled again by the mountain’s cold lungs.

There is one truly strange law in the Hanging Vale: nothing large is allowed to remain still. Anything that stops moving for too long begins to gather water until it grows too heavy and is pulled downward by the mountain’s endless dripping. Even the dead are slowly, politely escorted toward the bottom by moisture and gravity. The floor of the basin is therefore a soft, treacherous mosaic of bone, antler, broken stone, and living green—everything slowly becoming everything else. The only permanent inhabitants are the small, the quick, and the stubborn.

You understand, suddenly, why the old hermits left only stone circles and moss cloaks behind.

They had learned the first and only rule written in water on these weeping cliffs:

In Veyr, stillness is a kind of surrender.  
And the mountain is always listening.
