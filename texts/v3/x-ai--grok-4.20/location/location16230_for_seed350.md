The place has no surface.

It is called the **Vast Submersion**, though no one who enters it ever speaks its name again. What was once a low, young chain of volcanic islands has been swallowed so completely and so recently that the world has not yet decided whether this is ocean, atmosphere, or something that has slipped between the two. The water here is not water in the old sense. It is warm, almost blood-warm, thick as syrup, and it rains upward as often as downward. Every direction is submerged. Every breath must be stolen from pockets of compressible air that form and collapse like momentary lungs.

The light is the color of a bruise seen from inside the body: dim violet-brown, sourceless, as though the entire volume is backlit by a single dying filament somewhere far below. Bioluminescent plankton the size of dinner plates drift in slow galaxies, but their glow is constantly shredded by turbulence. Nothing holds still.

Vegetation has arrived faster than physics should allow. From the newborn black basalt spires that still steam and crack, colossal mats of kelp-like fronds erupt in dense, overlapping layers so thick they form cliff-walls of living rope. These mats are not merely growing; they are *surging*. Their growth rate is audible—wet, tearing, creaking, a sound like a forest trying to scream underwater. Tendrils thicken visibly while you watch, knotting around one another, around anything solid, around the metal bones that protrude from the dark.

And there are bones here. Industrial bones.

Half-drowned megastructures rise at strange angles from the fresh seafloor: skeletal oil platforms that were never finished, the fused remains of entire automated shipyards torn from some other continent and deposited here when reality flinched. Their rust is bright orange, almost festive against the bruised water. Turbines the size of apartment blocks spin uselessly in the chaotic currents, generating deep harmonic moans that boom through the liquid like whale song played on broken subwoofers. Power lines thick as thighs dangle in looping catenaries, spitting arcs of electricity that travel in perfect spheres instead of bolts, floating like captive miniature suns before popping with deafening cracks.

The sound is unrelenting. It is not one sound but a thousand sounds braided into a single deafening roar: the wet thunder of upward-raining water colliding with downward-raining water, the shriek of expanding basalt meeting cold flood, the metallic howl of stressed girders, the tearing of vegetation, the constant electric discharge. After only minutes the noise becomes a physical pressure against the skull. Most things that try to think here simply stop.

There is no fauna. Not truly. What moves through the frond-forests are not animals but *echoes* of animals—half-formed silhouettes that the place keeps trying to finish. A whale-shaped absence swims past, trailing bubbles that spell meaningless equations before dissolving. Schools of flickering human hands paddle through the gloom in perfect formation, finger-tendrils brushing the kelp with proprietary longing before they forget what they are and unravel back into silt.

Reality here is young and therefore arrogant. It has not yet learned the rules. Gravity forgets itself in pockets; you can suddenly find yourself falling *sideways* for thirty meters before the water remembers to be heavy again. Time dilates near the largest industrial ruins—stand too close to the half-built refinery tower and ten minutes outside become three hours inside its shadow. The vegetation grows into these temporal wounds and blooms into impossible colors that have no names because no eye has ever been meant to register them.

Scattered among the chaos are the only signs that people were ever here on purpose: a single perfectly preserved 1970s-era dive bell hanging upside-down from a lattice of kelp, its interior lights still burning. A flooded tram carriage embedded vertically in a basalt pillar, doors open, seats occupied by mannequins of hardened silt. A porcelain bathtub sitting peacefully on a mat of glowing weed, faucets running perfectly clear boiling water that never mixes with the surrounding syrup.

Everything is in the process of violent, ecstatic becoming and un-becoming at once. New volcanic vents open with deafening booms, spewing superheated mineral rain that instantly crystallizes into razor-sharp black trees. The vegetation lunges to claim them, only for new currents to tear both trees and kelp apart minutes later. The industrial ruins tilt, shear, and are swallowed, only to be spat up elsewhere, changed. The entire submerged valley (if the word valley still applies to a space with no surface and no true bottom) vibrates with hyperactive, almost anxious energy, as though the world is trying to dream an ecosystem, a civilization, and a catastrophe all at the same time, and cannot decide which one it wants more.

If you stay long enough, the noise begins to sound like language.

If you stay longer, you begin to answer it in the same wet, tearing voice.

And the warm, bruised water keeps rising. It has only just begun.
