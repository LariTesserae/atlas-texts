The place has no name, and it never will.

It is a low, newborn basin still wet from the world’s own birth-throes, cupped between knife-sharp ridges that look as though they were yanked upward yesterday. The ridges are black glass and raw basalt, their edges so fresh they glitter like obsidian blades even in the near-total dark. Everything here is young, violently young. The land itself seems embarrassed by its own newness and tries to hide behind a riot of green so dense it feels like drowning on dry ground.

A warm, heavy moisture hangs in the air like breath trapped inside a closed mouth. Rain does not fall in proper storms; instead the sky sweats. Fat droplets condense on the undersides of colossal leaves and plunge suddenly, noisily, twenty meters down. Pockets of standing water tremble in every hollow—inky, tea-colored pools fringed with pale fungal ruffs that glow faintly, the only visible light for kilometres. These pools breathe. Every few minutes a bubble the size of a human torso rises, pops, and releases a damp, earthy sigh that smells of iron and crushed mint.

The vegetation has not yet learned restraint. Palms with trunks thick as grain silos lean against one another like drunkards. Vines thick as thighs lash between them in living nets. Every surface—leaf, bark, stone—is furred, bearded, or lacquered with mosses that grow so fast you can hear the microscopic tearing of new cells if you press your ear close. Orchids the colour of raw meat burst open and rot within the same hour. Nothing here finishes growing; it simply changes its mind and becomes something else.

And the noise.

The noise is a physical pressure. It is not one sound but a thousand species screaming at once in a register just below pain. Cicadas the length of your forearm ratchet so loudly the air vibrates in your teeth. Tree frogs with luminous throats produce wet, metallic clangs that overlap into something like breaking glass. Hidden deeper, vast bellowing things—half whale, half elephant—send subsonic pulses through the root mat so powerful they make puddles dance. Every creature seems to believe that if it is not the loudest, it will die. The result is a deafening, polyphonic war that never pauses, only shifts key when one singer is devoured and another takes its place.

The fauna is not merely abundant; it is claustrophobic. Insects move in glittering curtains. A single step anywhere crushes six things and sends another sixty airborne. Mammals the size of cats scurry along branches in living braids. Serpents with bioluminescent fangs drift between the leaves like slow comets. High above, in the few gaps where the canopy tears open, leather-winged things wheel in vast, screaming gyres, their cries sharp enough to cut the humidity itself.

Nothing is still. The basin is in permanent metabolic overdrive. Whole sections of forest visibly surge upward while you watch, only to collapse minutes later under their own weight, rotting instantly into steaming compost that feeds the next frantic surge. Great curtains of lianas tear free and whip through the air like living bullwhips before slapping wetly against new hosts. Fungi bloom, sporulate, and melt in the space of a breath. The ground itself ripples—root systems migrating under the soil like schools of frantic fish. The entire valley is a single turbulent organism that has not yet decided what shape it wants to be.

There are no paths. Any trail you might force through the press of green is erased within minutes by eager growth or by the passage of some immense, unseen body that leaves behind crushed vegetation and a scent like hot copper.

Light is a rumour. The canopy is so complete and so high that the sky is only a memory. What little illumination exists comes from the creatures themselves: the cold blue fire of millipede flanks, the sickly chartreuse of certain orchids that only open after dark, the sudden magnesium-white flare of some predator’s lure. These flashes reveal, for split seconds, geometries too strange to parse—petals arranged like teeth, beetles with human-like hands, trees whose bark is covered in moving sigils that may be language or may be simple rot wearing calligraphy’s clothes.

This is a world still arguing with itself.  
It is lush and dark and deafening and warm and newborn and already ancient in its excess. Every creature, every leaf, every droplet of condensation seems convinced the universe is about to end, and so they are all trying to finish their lives at once, right now, in the loudest, greenest, wettest way possible.

If you stand still long enough the vegetation will begin to climb you.  
If you scream, no one will notice; your voice will simply become another thread in the deafening tapestry.

The basin does not want visitors.  
It has not yet learned how to be gentle, and it is in no hurry to learn.
