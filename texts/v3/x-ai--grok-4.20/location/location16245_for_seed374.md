The place is called the **Weeping Lattice**.

It is a low, wide basin that feels both embryonic and ancient, as though the world has not yet decided whether it is finished being born. Warm, heavy air presses down like a damp blanket. Every surface sweats. Water does not fall from the sky here; it condenses directly out of the atmosphere in slow, fat beads that cling to stone, bead along edges, and finally release themselves in continuous, musical drips. The sound of ten thousand separate trickles merges into a roaring, layered curtain of noise so constant it becomes a physical pressure against the ears. After a few minutes the roar itself starts to feel like silence, because nothing else is ever allowed to interrupt it.

There is almost no vegetation. What little exists is not plant life at all, but a pale, waxy fungus that grows in hair-fine films across damp rock, so thin it looks like breath on glass. Mostly the world is mineral: raw, unfinished stone still wearing the smooth, bulbous shapes of its first cooling. Great shelves of black basalt rise in irregular plates, their edges rounded and soft like cooling wax. Between them lie dense forests of mineral pillars — not yet true columns, not yet stalagmites — that have erupted from the floor in a single frozen surge. They stand so close together that a person must turn sideways to pass between many of them. Their surfaces are glossy, wet, and warm to the touch, as though the stone itself is still metabolizing.

The city — if it can be called a city — is not built *on* this geology. It is built *of* it. The inhabitants, a quiet people of small stature and enormous hands, have simply shaped the soft, emerging rock while it was still pliable. Every dwelling is a hollow scooped from a single bulging mass of basalt. Doorways are irregular ovals that never quite meet at the top. Windows are natural fissures widened by patient chiseling. Staircases spiral around the outside of the same outcrops that form the walls. The entire settlement is one continuous architectural organism, half geology and half craftsmanship, still growing. New rooms are not constructed; they are *encouraged* — the people pour warm water across chosen surfaces in careful patterns and wait for the stone to soften and slump into the shapes they desire. Scaffolding is almost unknown. Instead they use living ropes of braided fungal mat that harden when dried.

The air itself is thick with moisture and with life. Clouds of tiny white insects, no larger than grains of rice, drift in slow glittering storms between the pillars. They are not mosquitoes; they do not bite. They exist only to drink the constant condensation, and when they have drunk their fill they glow faintly with a cold, greenish light. Their collective luminescence is the only real illumination. Because of them the whole basin is bathed in a dim, aqueous twilight that never changes. There is no day here, no night. The temperature remains locked at the edge of uncomfortable warmth. The dripping never slows. The stone never quite finishes forming.

Fauna is everywhere, but never far away. Glossy black amphibians the size of small dogs move across the wet surfaces with suctioned feet, their throats pulsing in exact time with the dripping. Long, pale millipedes the thickness of a wrist weave patiently between the mineral pillars, their hundreds of legs clicking in a soft percussion that is instantly swallowed by the greater roar. In deeper crevices where water pools into slow, warm mirrors, eyeless fish the color of bone drift in perpetual equilibrium, neither hungry nor sated, simply *present*.

Nothing decays. Nothing really grows. The place exists in a single, saturated, deafening equilibrium. Even the people seem to have stepped out of time. They speak rarely; the sound of the water makes conversation nearly pointless. Instead they communicate by pressing their broad palms against the stone and feeling the minute vibrations that travel through the lattice. Their tools are simple — stone chisels, wooden wedges, ropes of hardened fungus — yet with these they perform miracles of patient geometry, coaxing the still-plastic rock into aqueducts, balconies, and spiraling ramps that fold over and under one another in three dense, dripping dimensions.

From any high shelf of rock the view is the same in every direction: an endless, dripping, warm, half-finished city of glossy black pillars and bulbous dwellings, all of it weeping, all of it singing with water, all of it locked forever in the moment just after creation and just before completion. The roar never lessens. The condensation never stops. The stone never quite hardens.

This is not a ruin.  
It is a world that has chosen to remain embryonic forever, and has built a civilization inside its own womb.
