The place is called the Hollow Choir.

It is a high, cold basin no larger than a modest cathedral town, cupped inside the flattened crown of a primordial mountain whose slopes have long since surrendered to time. The mountain itself feels older than mountains should be allowed to be—its ridges worn to nubs, its stone polished by epochs of wind and water until the entire formation resembles a single vast, cracked skull left open to the sky.

Everything here is damp but never truly wet. Moisture gathers in slow, deliberate ways: beads of condensation that slide down black basalt pillars like tears, thin silver rivulets that appear after the frequent, short-lived mists, and pockets of standing water caught in stone bowls older than any living thing. Rain comes as sudden, vertical lances that last only minutes, then vanishes again, leaving the air heavy with the smell of wet lichen and cold iron.

The vegetation is lush yet eerily sparse. What grows does so in extravagant, almost defiant clusters—thick emerald mosses that drape entire walls of stone like living velvet, groves of stunted white-needled conifers that twist sideways instead of upward, and colonies of pale, fleshy fungi that bloom overnight in perfect geometric rings. Between these pockets the ground is almost bare, exposing the ancient, glassy rock that catches what light there is and throws it back in cold, metallic glints. The contrast is violent: explosive islands of green life separated by wide, echoing voids of stone.

The scale is intimate and wrong at the same time. You can walk from one side of the basin to the other in twenty minutes, yet the emptiness between things makes it feel cathedral-huge. Every sound travels too far. And the sounds are loud.

This is the strangest part.

The Hollow Choir is never quiet. A constant, deep polyphony fills the air—part wind, part water, part living throat. Where two particular stone spires stand seventy meters apart, the wind is forced through natural apertures and fissures worn over inconceivable time, producing a low, continuous organ-note that changes pitch with the slightest shift in air pressure. Water trickling into hidden cavities creates rhythmic gongs and drips that never fall in the same pattern twice. The moss itself seems to hum when the temperature drops. Together they form something that feels dangerously close to song—melodies that almost resolve, chords that almost comfort, before the whole chorus fractures again into alien discord.

There are no tools here. No metal, no worked stone beyond what erosion itself has carved. Yet the basin contains the unmistakable geometry of a dead city. Streets of smooth bedrock curve between outcrops that look too much like temples. Tiered shelves of stone suggest amphitheaters. A perfectly circular pool at the exact center—fed by a single dangling thread of waterfall—feels like a font or an altar. Whatever lived here built nothing with hands; the place was grown or eroded into usefulness by processes so slow they might as well have been deliberate.

Fauna is rare. You might see a single pair of slate-colored birds with translucent beaks wheeling high above, or a translucent salamander the color of ice sliding across wet stone. Mostly the basin feels abandoned by animals the way old churches feel abandoned by worshippers. The few creatures that remain seem to understand the music. They move only during certain phrases of the endless choir, as though obeying conducting that no human ear can fully parse.

The light is normal, almost cruelly so. Harsh alpine sunlight slants in during the few hours it clears the surrounding teeth of the range, illuminating every droplet, every fungal frill, every grain of mica in the stone. Then the mists return and the light turns soft, pearl-gray, and the Choir sings louder to compensate.

Everything here is in motion, yet nothing ever seems to change. The water cycles in quick, theatrical bursts. The moss grows visibly by the hour and then dries, shrinks, and waits. The wind rises and falls like breathing. The stone itself—ancient beyond memory—continues its infinitesimal settling, producing new notes for the Choir every few centuries.

This is a place that has reached its final form and yet refuses to die. A small, loud, empty city of moss and music, high above the world, where the only inhabitants are the slow thoughts of stone and the patient, dripping dark between its ribs.

If you stand in the center, beside the circular pool, and close your eyes, the Choir eventually stops sounding like wind and water. It begins to sound like something enormous, ancient, and terribly lonely trying—over and over again—to remember how to speak.
