The place has no name anymore. It has outlived every name ever given to it.

You stand on what feels like the roof of the world, yet the world has forgotten how to be solid. The elevation is high enough that the air itself is thin and metallic, but the water parameter of 2.4 keeps everything weeping, dripping, condensing. Every surface exhales slow cold fog. The temperature hovers at the edge of freezing, never quite committing; it is the cold of something that has been dying for so long it has learned to enjoy the process.

Vegetation is total. Not merely dominant—total. The land has been swallowed and remade by a single, impossible organism that behaves like a forest, a coral reef, a nervous system, and a cancer all at once. It does not grow on the ground. It is the ground. Pale, fibrous mats of it stretch for kilometers in every direction, thick as ancient thatch, riddled with tunnels, pockets, and vents. Where it rises it forms living buttresses, arches, and dripping colonnades that look like the ruins of cathedrals built by something that never understood what a cathedral was. Every surface is covered in slow-moving beads of water that never quite fall; they crawl, they hesitate, they rejoin the mass.

The organism is ancient beyond comprehension. Erosion has reached 3.0—maximum. Whatever sharper geology once existed has been planed down to a final, almost tender smoothness by four hundred million years of patient vegetable digestion. The hills are low, breast-like, exhausted. They sag under their own living weight. In places the plant-skin has split and you can see older layers beneath, darker, drier, fossilized into something that is still technically alive but has not moved in geological epochs. Time has flattened the world into a single weary sigh.

Yet nothing here is still.

The sound is constant and industrial. A low, wet thunder of peristalsis. The entire vegetative mat is breathing, pumping, digesting, expelling. Somewhere far beneath the surface, vast sacs the size of cathedrals contract and relax with the slow rhythm of a dying heart. The noise is loud enough to vibrate your teeth: a wet, industrial churning mixed with the high ringing of water droplets striking metallic leaves that grow in sparse, deliberate clusters. The vegetation, though total, is paradoxically sparse in its vertical expression; most of the biomass is horizontal, a living crust. The upright structures—towering fungal stalks, dripping lianas thick as train carriages, translucent bladder-trees—stand far apart, like lonely monuments. Between them stretch wide, open basins of shivering moss-lawn where the wind moves in visible waves.

The fauna is abundant and wrong.

Things that look like car-sized moths made of wet paper beat slowly through the fog, shedding spores that glow faint industrial green. Herds of translucent, many-legged creatures the size of horses move across the living substrate in perfect synchrony, their bodies half plant, half animal; where their feet touch the mat, the vegetation opens small wet mouths to drink the oils they secrete. In the distance, something that might once have been a forest elephant has been completely overgrown—only its silhouette remains, still walking in a slow circle while the plant uses its skeleton as scaffolding. Every creature here is in some stage of negotiation with the mat. Some have won temporary independence. Most have not.

Reality breakdown is advanced.

The sky is dim and low, the color of old nickel. It does not stay still. Sections of it occasionally forget they are sky and collapse into vertical sheets of liquid that pour upward before remembering the rules and snapping back into place. Gravity here is polite but inconsistent. In certain basins you can watch water flow sideways along the ground in perfect defiance, forming slow rivers that climb gentle slopes before tumbling off invisible edges into the air. Time itself has become spongy. If you stand in one place long enough you can watch the same droplet of condensation take three different routes down the same stalk, each route existing in a slightly different decade.

There are no built structures. Not one. The industrial signature does not come from machines made by hands. It comes from the ecosystem itself, which long ago achieved something worse than intelligence: it achieved scale and repetition. The great breathing sacs far below are effectively biological factories. The mat produces, consumes, refines, and expels in endless turbulent cycles. Every few hours entire sections of the landscape undergo catastrophic, ecstatic collapse—towering plant-forms liquefy into nutrient sludge that is immediately devoured and reborn somewhere else. The sound of these events is tremendous: a roaring, wet avalanche of living tissue rearranging itself according to rules no outsider will ever understand.

This is not decay.

This is the final stable state after decay has won so completely that it has begun to build cathedrals out of its own corpse. A post-post-apocalyptic ecology where the apocalypse itself has been digested, metabolized, and turned into architecture. The world has settled into its oldest possible form and that form is still frantically, industriously, turbulently alive.

If you stay long enough, the mat will begin to taste you. Not with hunger. With curiosity.

It has been lonely for a very long time.
