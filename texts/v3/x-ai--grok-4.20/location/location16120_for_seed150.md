The place is called the Hollow Choir.

It is a single, self-contained basin no larger than a modest cathedral, cupped inside the knife-edge ridges of a young mountain range that still remembers the violence of its birth. The rock is black, glassy, and bitterly cold; every surface sweats. Water does not flow so much as bead, weep, and clot. It condenses from the air in slow silver pearls, runs in hair-thin veins down every vertical face, and gathers in black mirrors no deeper than a finger joint. The atmosphere itself feels soaked; breath fogs and the fog stays, drifting between leaves like spilled milk.

There is almost no bare stone left. The entire basin has been devoured and remade by a single continuous organism: a cold-tolerant, woody moss that grows in dense, felted plates the color of oxidized iron and wet slate. It is not ground cover. It is the ground, the walls, the ceiling of the hollow. It layers itself in overlapping shelves, buttresses, and vaults until the original geology disappears beneath meters of living fabric. Every ledge, every crevice, every fold is knitted tight with it. The plant-as-architecture is so complete that the distinction between tree, moss, lichen, fungus, and root has dissolved; it is all one body. Pillars of it rise like organ pipes, each one hollow and dripping. Arches form naturally where the moss has bridged gaps and then thickened for centuries, dripping stalactites of its own frozen sap that never quite harden in the wet cold.

Everything is saturated. There is no path; you move by pushing your body between spongy walls that give and then slowly push back, exhaling cold mist into your face. The air smells of iron, wet stone, and something faintly sweet like crushed wintergreen. The temperature hovers just above freezing; any lower and the water would lock solid, any higher and the moss would collapse under its own weight. Instead it exists in perfect, trembling equilibrium.

Light arrives thin and blue-grey, filtered through perpetual low cloud that never quite rains and never quite lifts. It leaks down through a single high breach in the ridges above, a slit no wider than a doorway. What little reaches the basin is immediately soaked up by the red-black moss, giving the entire hollow the dim, ecclesiastical glow of a church lit only by votive candles.

Sound is muffled to the edge of hearing. The dominant noise is the endless, patient dripping—thousands of separate drops falling at different rhythms from the undersides of mossy vaults. Sometimes a larger glob lets go and strikes a pool with a low, resonant note, and for a moment the whole basin seems to answer with sympathetic vibrations that move through the moss itself like a slow bell. Otherwise the place is quiet. Fauna is rare: a few translucent spiders the color of ice, a species of blind, velvet-furred millipede that inches along the wet felt, and, very occasionally, a small grey bird with no song that flits between the arches like a thought half-remembered.

Yet the place is not still. The moss is in constant, almost imperceptible motion. It grows visibly if you sit long enough—plate edges lifting and overlapping in slow-motion waves. Water moves through its capillary layers the way blood moves through tissue. Every few hours a section will suddenly release stored moisture; a soft landslide of water and moss fragments will slide down a wall, reshaping the architecture in a wet whisper before the plant knits the wound closed again within a day. The entire basin is a slow, cold digestion: rock gradually becoming moss, moss becoming more moss, water forever cycling through every living cell.

There are no tools here, no metal, no worked stone. The only “urban” quality is the impossible density and organization of the moss itself. It has built a cathedral without intention—naves, transepts, side-chapels, even something like a crossing where four massive pillars meet overhead and leave a perfect circular opening that frames the grey sky like an oculus. In that central space the dripping is loudest and most rhythmic, as though the Hollow Choir really is singing one endless, dripping note that has lasted for centuries and has centuries still to go.

If you stand motionless in the crossing long enough, you can feel the slow pulse of the whole organism beneath your boots: a faint, cold throb, like a heart the size of a mountain trying to beat once every hour.

It is not a forest.  
It is not a ruin.  
It is a single creature wearing the shape of sacred architecture, quietly eating the mountain from the inside out, weeping, singing, and growing in the cold blue dim.
