The place is called the Hollow Choir.

It is a single, self-contained basin no larger than a modest cathedral, sunk like a stone cup into the flank of a high, fog-bound ridge. Everything here has been settling for so long that the land itself seems to have forgotten any other shape. The rim of the basin is only thirty paces across, yet once you step over the lip the world outside vanishes as though it never existed. There is only this.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not fall as rain; it sweats. The porous black rock of the basin walls is perpetually damp, beaded with slow, cold tears that slide downward in perfect parallel lines, feeding a skin of water so thin it barely covers the stone. Where the floor levels out, this skin pools into black mirrors no deeper than a finger-joint. Step on them and they do not splash; they only sigh, releasing a breath of colder air that smells of iron and wet moss. From the ceiling — if one can call the overhanging rock a ceiling — condensation gathers into fat, trembling drops that never quite fall at the same rhythm. The sound they make when they strike the pools is the loudest thing here: a soft, round *tok* spaced irregularly, like a heart that cannot decide on its pace. Nothing else moves.

The vegetation has taken the moisture as permission to smother. Every inch of wall and floor not covered by the mirror-water is upholstered in moss so thick and wet it feels like breathing flesh. From the moss rise miniature forests of fern and clubmoss no taller than a child’s knee, yet so densely interwoven that to push a hand between them is to feel them push back, reluctant. Tiny pale orchids hang in curtains from the underside of overhangs, their roots drinking directly from the weeping stone. Everything is the same shade of deep emerald-black, as though the color has been steeped for ten thousand years. There is no space between things. Even the air feels knitted.

The temperature never rises above the point where breath becomes visible. It is not freezing, only relentlessly cool, the kind of cold that lives in cathedrals and deep cellars. Your skin tightens. Your pulse becomes audible inside your own skull.

The fauna is the true occupant.

They are small, mostly, and mostly silent. The basin is a single, closed ecology that has reached perfect, terrible balance. Pale, translucent newts the length of a finger drift in the mirror-pools like living glass. On the moss, colonies of velvet-black millipedes move in slow, synchronized waves, their legs making a sound like distant rain on leaves. Above them, in the hanging orchids, hang colonies of moth-like creatures with wings of wet parchment; they never fly, only pulse their wings open and shut in perfect unison, revealing and hiding luminous blue spots that glow coldly in the near-dark. Every few minutes the entire basin seems to inhale: every creature, every frond, every bead of water pauses for the space of three heartbeats. Then the exhalation comes — a collective sigh so low it is more felt than heard — and everything resumes.

There is one larger presence. In the exact center of the basin stands a single tree older than the concept of trees. Its trunk is no thicker than a man’s thigh, yet its roots have spread through the entire floor like veins under skin. The bark is black and glossy, perpetually wet. Its leaves are tiny, almost circular, and so dark they drink what little light exists. From its lowest branches hang hundreds of pale, sack-like cocoons, each the size of a human heart. Inside them something slowly turns. They never hatch. They never rot. The tree and its cargo have simply always been here, in equilibrium.

Light is almost absent. A faint, sourceless phosphorescence rises from the moss and the wet stone, the color of moonlight drowned in milk. It is enough to see shapes, never details. Shadows are thicker than the things that cast them.

Nothing has changed here in measurable time. The same drops fall in the same places. The same newts drift in the same slow circles. The same collective inhalation and exhalation has occurred every hour for longer than anything that could remember it. The basin is not dead; it is perfectly, oppressively *alive*, but its life has long ago reached the point where motion itself became unnecessary.

If you stand still long enough, the place begins to forget you are an intruder. The newts drift closer. The hanging sacks turn toward your warmth. The moss creeps a fraction of a millimeter nearer to the soles of your boots. Not with hunger. With recognition.

This is not a place that wants you to leave.

It simply no longer understands the concept of departure.
