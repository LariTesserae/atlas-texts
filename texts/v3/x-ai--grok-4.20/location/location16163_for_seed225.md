The place is called the Hollow Basilica, though nothing built it.

It lies beneath the skin of the world, a single enormous cavity no larger than a modest cathedral, yet so empty that the mind keeps inventing distance where none exists. The ceiling is a low, sweating dome of pale limestone, veined with slow orange rust. Every surface glistens. Water does not fall here so much as exhale: a perpetual, almost invisible drizzle that condenses on the stone and slides in long silver threads down the walls, collecting in shallow, mirror-bright basins before seeping away through invisible fissures. The air is warm, thick, and smells of wet iron and something faintly sweet, like overripe melon left too long in the sun.

There are no pillars. There is only negative space—immense, patient, and ancient. The floor is a labyrinth of smooth, thigh-high stone ribs that have grown over millennia into looping, intersecting ridges, as though the earth itself once tried to knit a ribcage and then gave up. Between these pale ribs lie pockets of darker soil, damp and almost black, where the only vegetation dares to appear. Sparse, yes, but not lifeless. Here and there rise slender stalks of ghost-fern, their fronds the color of old bone, trembling though there is no wind. Tiny colonies of luminous lichen cling to the undersides of the stone ribs, glowing a cold, steady aquamarine that somehow makes the light in the chamber feel even brighter, as if the space is feeding on its own reflection.

The light itself is the strangest thing. It has no visible source. It is simply *present*, dense and golden-white, as though the sun has been dismembered and its pieces scattered into the stone. Every droplet of water hanging from the ceiling becomes a tiny lantern. Every wet surface throws the glow back and forth until the entire hollow feels over-exposed, almost holy in its clarity. Shadows are thin and hesitant, reluctant to commit.

Fauna is abundant in the way of deep caves: not loud, not crowded, but everywhere once you learn to look. translucent crickets the length of a finger move with unbearable slowness across the ribs, their legs leaving faint wake-trails in the condensation. Blind white moths with wingspans wider than a hand drift in the bright air like ash; they have no fear of light because they have never known darkness. Small, six-legged lizards the color of wet clay bask on the warm stone, throats pulsing, occasionally flicking out ribbon-thin tongues to catch the drifting moths. Every few hours a soft, almost sub-audible chime moves through the chamber—not a sound so much as a pressure—caused by some distant shifting of water tables far above. When it passes, every creature pauses for three full heartbeats before resuming its patient life.

Nothing here is hurried. Erosion has long since reached equilibrium; the stone ribs are polished to an almost obscene smoothness by ten thousand years of the same slow tears. The ferns grow perhaps one new leaf per decade. The lizards may live thirty years without ever traveling more than twenty paces from their birth ridge. The chamber is a closed system that has forgotten the concept of urgency.

And yet it is not dead. It is *settled*. A place that has become perfectly itself, like a word worn smooth in an ancient mouth.

If you stand in the exact center—where the ribs form a shallow bowl filled with a single wide, perfectly still pool—you can hear the softest sound in the world: the continuous, almost loving drip of water into water. Each drop lands with the patience of geology. Each creates a ripple that travels outward, touches the stone edges, and returns, until the pool becomes a breathing mirror that reflects the impossible brightness back into the bright air.

There are no paths. There are no ruins. There is only this single, bright, hollow ribcage beneath the world, slowly drinking its own condensation, nursing its pale ferns and transparent beasts, waiting with the calm certainty of stone that has already won.
