The place has no name that any tongue could hold. It calls itself, in the long slow language of its own making, something like *The Throat That Remembers Snow*.

It is a high continental shelf newly born from the buckling of an alien crust — so young that the stone itself still feels tender. Sharp black ridges, hexagonal and glassy, rise like the teeth of a continent-sized comb. Between them, every fracture and fissure is packed so tightly with life that the rock itself seems to be sweating green. The vegetation is not gentle. It is a frantic, cold-blooded lushness: mats of sapphire moss thick as quilted armor, translucent succulents the color of arterial blood, forests of jointed stalks that grow in perfect spirals because the wind here only ever blows in one direction and has done so for ten thousand years.

The air is bright and vicious. Two suns — one white, one a searing cobalt — hang in a sky the color of oxidized copper. Their light is so intense it feels like sound. It rings against the crystalline ridges and shatters into rainbows that never quite fade. Even at “night,” when both suns slip behind the curvature of the shelf, the atmosphere itself fluoresces, a perpetual sourceless daylight that makes every shadow look guilty.

Temperature clings to the edge of survivable cold. The moisture is never enough to be called rain for long; instead the world exhales slow, freezing mists that bead on every surface, run in mercury-bright rivulets down black stone, and collect in perfectly round mirror-pools that never overflow. These pools are the only soft things here. Everything else is edge.

And yet the density is murderous.

There is no bare rock visible for more than a few strides. Every vertical face is colonized by tiered hanging gardens that sway like living drapery. Every horizontal surface is a battlefield of root and rhizome so tightly interwoven that if you tried to push a knife between two plants they would scream — not metaphorically. The flora here is loud. When the slow winds shift, entire hillsides of tubular reeds open their spiracles and howl in frequencies that make your teeth itch. The sound never stops; it only changes register over decades.

The fauna is worse.

They do not move quickly. Nothing here does. The entire ecology has settled into a geological pace, as though it knows it has millions of years to finish its argument. Vast herds of membranous creatures the size of small whales drift between the ridges on sails of living skin, filtering the bright air for planktonic spores. Their shadows slide across the landscape like slow eclipses. Below them, in the packed gullies, things that look like six-legged tortoises made of moss and obsidian graze with the patience of glaciers. When two of them meet they press together for weeks, exchanging colonies of symbiotic fungi in a transaction so gradual it appears statuesque.

But the real rulers are the swarms.

Microfauna — glittering, loud, industrious — fill every cubic meter of air and every capillary of water. They are not insects. They are something older and more mechanical: translucent lattices of silica and muscle that click and chime in choruses so dense the sound becomes weather. Their wings catch the brutal light and turn the valleys into strobing cathedrals. At certain hours the entire shelf rings with a single note so low it registers in the bones of your pelvis.

And then there is the tech.

It does not belong to anything living. It belongs to the world itself.

Great arteries of black iron and glass run through the stone like fossilized lightning, exhaling slow gouts of steam that freeze instantly into filigree sculptures and then sublime again over centuries. No one built them. They precipitated. The planet, still young and furious with tectonic indigestion, is running vast chemical engines in its own crust — bellows the size of cities, turbines of living crystal that turn for decades between each rotation. Pipes as thick as redwoods surface, breathe, and sink again. The vegetation has already grown into them, through them, using their waste heat and occasional electrical discharge the way other worlds use sunlight. The industrial and the biological are not merely coexisting; they have mistaken one another for organs.

Everything is changing, but so slowly you could die of old age between one state and the next.

A ridge that was bare obsidian a thousand years ago is now a hanging forest. A lake that was mirror-still last century is now a thick soup of singing worms. The two suns crawl across the copper sky in cycles that last weeks. The cold never quite kills anything and the moisture never quite feeds anything and the noise never quite stops.

This is not an ecosystem.

It is a single, vast, loud, bright, freezing argument the planet is having with itself, and every living and half-living thing here is a syllable it has not yet finished pronouncing.

If you stayed long enough — if the thin, metal-tasting air and the endless crystalline ringing did not drive you mad — you would eventually realize the land is not young.

It is only remembering how to be old.
