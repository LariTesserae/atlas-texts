The place is called the **Riven Choir**.

It is a single, self-contained chamber high in a newborn mountain range—cathedral-sized in volume but viciously vertical, no more than eighty meters across at its widest. The walls are raw, black, glassy basalt still cooling from whatever cataclysm thrust them upward only decades or centuries ago. Every surface is sharp enough to cut skin; edges have not yet learned how to be kind.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It does not flow as a river. Instead the mountain itself sweats. Great beads of condensation the size of fists slide down the sheer cliffs in slow, deliberate tracks, gathering into trembling sheets that suddenly release and fall eighty meters as cold, vertical rain. The air is permanently damp, thick with the smell of wet stone and ozone. Pockets of water collect in every ledge and fracture, black mirrors that tremble constantly.

The vegetation is obscene in its insistence. Out of every crack, every razor ledge, every vertical face, there bursts a violent green. Mosses so thick they look like emerald fur. Ferns the size of sails. Vines thick as ship’s rope that have webbed the entire chamber into a single tangled organism. The plants do not climb—they *colonize*, layer upon layer, crowding one another until the stone itself is only a suggestion beneath writhing greenery. Their leaves are glossy, almost lacquered, drinking the constant drizzle.

Everything is loud.

A perpetual roar fills the chamber: the combined sound of ten thousand separate waterfalls crashing onto stone, of leaves slapping one another in the up-drafts, of wind screaming through narrow stone flutes that the mountain formed by accident. The noise is industrial in scale—machinelike, metallic, relentless—yet nothing mechanical exists here. The mountain itself supplies the engines: pressure, gravity, condensation, and the ceaseless churning of air between cold stone and warm vegetation create a micro-storm that never rests.

Light is almost absent. What little arrives does so as a sickly, filtered grey that has to fight through hundreds of meters of overhanging foliage and swirling mist. Most of the chamber lives in deep shadow. Only occasional shafts of cold daylight stab down like searchlights when the canopy far above tears open for a moment, illuminating small circles of violent green and wet black rock before the mist swallows them again.

There is no animal life. No birds, no insects, no hidden mammals. The turbulence is too violent, the verticality too unforgiving. The only motion belongs to the plants and the water and the air itself. Leaves whip and thrash even when there seems to be no wind. Tendrils slowly but visibly tighten around one another in slow-motion wrestling matches. Whole curtains of moss tear free under their own saturated weight and slap downward like wet leather, exploding into spray on the jagged floor far below.

The floor—if it can be called that—is not flat. It is a violently uneven wreck of newly broken stone, tilted slabs, and deep crevices where black water gathers and boils with trapped air. Nothing has had time to settle. The entire chamber feels freshly cracked open, still bleeding, still arguing with itself.

This is a young wound in the world’s skin. A place that has not yet decided whether it wants to be mountain, greenhouse, or storm. It has chosen all three at once and is still fighting to reconcile them. The roar never stops. The dripping never stops. The green never stops pushing.

Stand in the center, if you can find purchase among the thrashing leaves and knife-edged rocks, and you will feel the mountain’s pulse: cold, wet, deafening, and utterly, electrically alive.
