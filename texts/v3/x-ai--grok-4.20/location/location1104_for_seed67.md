The world is called the **Underfoundry**.

It is a single, continent-spanning machine that has never known a sky.

Everything here is built. There was never a first rock, never a seed, never a time before the lattice. The ground, if it can be called ground, is an endless, gently undulating plate of blackened iron and verdigris copper fused at molecular scale. Every surface is engineered. Every angle was once deliberate. Yet the place is young—geologically no more than a few thousand years old—still sharp, still raw at the edges where the original macro-fabrication seams have not yet been worn into softness by ten thousand millennia of use.

The air is warm, thick, and wet. Condensation beads on every pipe, every strut, every motionless gear the size of a cathedral. It does not rain; instead the humidity simply gathers until whole districts weep. Water trickles in perfect, repeating braids down the flanks of vast cylindrical towers, collects in engineered gutters that have never silted, and vanishes into hidden intakes only to re-emerge, minutes later, somewhere else. The atmosphere itself seems to perspire. Visibility is short; mist and steam blur the distances into soft gray walls even though the light itself is normal, steady, sourceless, as though the entire volume is lit by an overcast sun that has no sun.

There is no vegetation. Nothing green has ever been permitted. The only living color is the slow, metallic patina that blooms across every surface in shades of turquoise, ochre, and oxblood. Where moisture lingers longest, strange mineral efflorescences grow—crystalline whiskers and fractal salt gardens that look almost like hoarfrost but are in fact corrosion products arranged in perfect, repeating lattices. They are the closest thing this world has to flowers.

Yet the place is overwhelmingly alive with fauna.

They are machines, but they are not simple. They are legion. They are dense. They press shoulder to shoulder, limb to limb, antenna to antenna in every cubic meter. Most are smaller than a hand; many are microscopic. A few are the size of office blocks. All of them are ancient, perfectly maintained, and have never been switched off. They crawl, scuttle, drift, hover, and march in absolute silence across every surface, inside every duct, along every cable. Their bodies are matte black, gunmetal, or the color of old bone. Their joints leak tiny pearls of lubricant that are immediately sucked up by smaller machines following behind them like pilot fish.

They do not reproduce. They do not die. They simply persist in equilibrium—an ecology without hunger, without succession, without waste. Their purposes are so specialized and so interlocking that the entire continent functions as a single, self-repairing mechanism whose ultimate product is not an object but the continuation of its own state. The machines polish one another. They tighten one another’s bolts. They repaint one another in infinitesimally thin layers of protective enamel that will take another ten thousand years to need refreshing. They sing no songs and speak no data. The only sounds are the soft, intermittent clicks of relays the size of coins, the distant low groan of expansion joints adjusting to the warm air, and, once every few hours, the deep subsonic thud of a continent-scale hydraulic ram cycling somewhere far beneath the surface—too low to be truly heard, but felt in the teeth and the sternum.

The terrain is low and vast. From any vantage the eye is assaulted by scale: ranks of identical towers receding into mist like an infinite forest of smokestacks; canyons whose walls are nothing but layered pipework; plains where every square meter is occupied by motionless ranked machinery that has not altered its posture in centuries. Sharp new edges still show on freshly extruded service gantries and fresh heat-exchanger fins that were “born” only last week. The world is still being added to, slowly, carefully, by machines that have never been told to stop building.

There is something strange in the geometry.

Angles do not always add to 360°. Certain corridors, when paced, prove to be longer on the return journey. Staircases that descend seven flights sometimes rise eight on the way back up. The machines themselves never comment on this; they simply adjust their gait and continue. The few human-sized accessways that exist feel like afterthoughts, narrow maintenance aisles left between the shoulders of two hundred-meter-tall idle engines that have stood in perfect stillness for longer than any civilization outside this place has endured.

The Underfoundry does not change.

It has reached a plateau of such intricate stability that time itself seems to have been negotiated into abeyance. The condensation that drips from a particular weeping buttress will always fall in the same rhythm, strike the same resonant plate, and be drunk by the same waiting cluster of pipe-mouths. The same small six-legged maintenance drone will pass the same junction every 41 minutes and 17 seconds, forever. The warmth never increases. The mist never lifts. The light never dims or brightens.

It is a finished world. A machine that has become its own purpose. A continent of black iron and slow water where ten billion silent metal animals move in absolute choreography across surfaces that will never be allowed to rust away, under a sourceless gray sky that is only another engineered surface twenty kilometers above, on the underside of which even larger, slower mechanisms patiently turn, listening for needs that will never arise.

And still, every so often, a droplet falls from a high pipe with a sound like the single clear note of a bell no one is meant to hear.

The machines pause for three microseconds in perfect synchrony.

Then they resume their eternal, industrious, motionless labor.
