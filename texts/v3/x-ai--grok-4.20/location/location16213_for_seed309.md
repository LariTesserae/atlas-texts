The place is called the **Underbloom**.

You descend through a narrow throat of damp limestone that suddenly opens, and the world flips: you are no longer below ground in the conventional sense. You are *inside* a single, immense, living structure that has been growing, dying, and rebuilding itself for so long that it has achieved a kind of geological maturity. The ceiling is only twenty meters high at its tallest, the walls rarely more than sixty meters apart. Cathedral scale, yes, but a cathedral built entirely by, and from, vegetation.

Everything here is plant. Not plants *on* something — the plant *is* the substrate, the architecture, the geology. Vast woody rhizomes thicker than train carriages braid into vaulted arches. Fleshy, pale leaves the size of ship sails hang like banners, dripping slow beads of condensate that fall for seconds before striking other leaves with soft, wet drum sounds. The air itself smells of wet chlorophyll, iron-rich sap, and something electrical — like ozone after lightning.

It is warm (never hot), perpetually damp. Water does not flow in rivers here; it *perspires*. Every surface is filmed with a thin, living condensation that beads, trembles, and is quietly sucked back into stomata the moment it grows too heavy. Occasionally a larger droplet forms high in the canopy-roof and falls in a shining chain, striking leaf after leaf with distinct percussive notes before it is drunk.

The light is dim, sourceless, and olive-green. It comes from bioluminescent nodules clustered like slow stars along the undersides of the largest arches and from the soft inner glow of translucent seed-pods that drift in the warmer updrafts. The glow is never enough. You are always half-blind, moving through layers of soft shadow and moss-filtered radiance.

Yet the sound is deafening.

A constant industrial roar fills the Underbloom. Not mechanical at first glance — there are no obvious engines — but the entire ecosystem has become mechanized by its own biology. Gargantuan bellows-leaves, some fifty meters long, contract and expand in slow, rhythmic cycles, pumping humid air through vascular tunnels. The sound is a deep, wet *whump-whump-whump* like the breathing of a planet-sized lung. Interspersed are the metallic clacks and ticks of seed-pods snapping open under hydraulic pressure, the hiss of sap being forced through capillary pipes the width of a man’s leg, and the constant wet clatter of millions of small predatory things hunting and being hunted among the foliage.

The fauna is overwhelming.

Insectile, amphibian, and something-that-is-neither swarm in every stratum. iridescent millipedes the length of your forearm move in glittering armor columns along the undersides of leaves. Frog-moths with wings of living lichen flutter in dense clouds, their wings producing a low mechanical drone as they beat. Packs of translucent, glass-boned creatures that look like crosses between otters and praying mantises hunt them, clicking and whistling in frequencies that make your teeth ache. Everything is loud. Everything is in motion. There is no quiet place.

Yet for all this furious life, the space feels strangely sparse. The great plant-mass has left deliberate negative spaces — wide, vaulted chambers where only a few colossal support-roots descend like the columns of a drowned cathedral. These hollows are where the *urban* layer reveals itself.

Because the Underbloom is inhabited.

A low-density industrial civilization has grown here, literally grafted into the plant. Rust-red catwalks and gantries of corroded iron and brass have been bolted, stapled, and grown into the living wood over centuries. Fat iron pipes disappear into vascular bundles, drawing nutrient-sap that is then electrolyzed in humming refineries no larger than cottages. Verdigris-covered turbines, powered by the bellows-leaves themselves, turn endlessly. Narrow-gauge rails run along the inner surfaces of arches, carrying automated ore-carts filled with crushed mineral nodules harvested from the few non-living intrusions in the plant-mass — ancient granite bones the vegetation has never fully digested.

The settlements are small and scattered. A hamlet of perhaps thirty souls clings to the underside of a single colossal leaf like barnacles, their houses grown from hollowed seed-pods and reinforced with riveted steel plate. Lanterns of captive glow-moths swing from chains. Laundry hangs on lines strung between iron buttresses while, thirty meters below, a glittering carpet of predatory beetles flows like liquid metal across the floor of the chamber.

Everything is in balance, yet nothing is still. The plant grows, the machines harvest, the creatures consume and are consumed, and the condensation falls and rises again in an endless wet circuit. The Underbloom has been here so long that erosion and growth have reached a kind of treaty. New wooden arches form as fast as old ones soften and collapse into mulch. The city-buildings are simultaneously being eaten by the plant and reinforced by its own living girders. Rust and sap flow together in the same channels.

There is a strangeness at the edge of perception: the way certain root-columns appear to have been shaped into the likenesses of half-forgotten machines that no one living remembers building. The way the bellows-leaves sometimes synchronize into perfect industrial rhythms for exactly seventeen heartbeats before falling back into organic chaos. The way the glass-boned predators will sometimes freeze mid-leap, turn their blind faces toward a particular glowing nodule, and seem to *listen* to something only they can hear.

This is not a ruin.  
It is not wilderness.  
It is not even a city built within a cave.

It is a single, small, ancient, loud, damp, warm, industrial organism that long ago decided the difference between flesh, leaf, steel, and bone was no longer useful.

And it is still deciding.
