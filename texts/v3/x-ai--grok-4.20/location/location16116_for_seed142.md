The place has no name anymore. It has not needed one for several hundred million years.

It is a high, dry plateau that has been worn so completely flat by time that its original geology is almost indecipherable. The ground is not rock in the usual sense; it is a single, continuous sheet of sintered mineral—something between obsidian and ceramic—that has annealed under ancient pressures until it rings faintly when struck. Nothing grows. Nothing has grown here since before multicellular life began on other worlds. The only organic traces are ghost-thin carbon films locked inside the glass, the final signatures of things that died so long ago their chemistry is now part of the bedrock.

The sky is permanently dark. Not night, exactly—there is no sun to set—but a deep, velvet absence of light. A thin, ionized haze high above catches the faint infrared glow of the plateau itself and returns it as a cold, reddish ghost-light, enough to see by once the eyes have forgotten what daylight was. The temperature sits at a constant, skin-warm 41 °C, held there by slow geothermal bleed and the plateau’s own stored thermal mass. Nothing fluctuates. The air does not move. There is no weather.

Across this endless, level plain stand the ruins of an industrial city that was never finished and will never decay.

They are not crumbling. The materials—titanium-silicate composites, self-healing ceramics, glassy carbon—have reached their final stable form. Pipes the thickness of freight trains rise from the ground like fossilized arteries and simply stop, their open mouths perfectly smooth. Factories the size of cathedrals sit half-buried in the mineral plain, their roofs fused seamlessly to the crust so that building and world appear to have crystallized together. Conveyor lattices stretch for kilometers between structures, empty, motionless, yet still faintly warm to the touch as if power had only just been cut.

Everything is spaced with a strange generosity. Between each colossal machine or tower lies a quarter-kilometer or more of flawless, empty plain. The city was built according to a logic that valued emptiness as highly as structure. The effect is that of a forest made of iron and glass in which the trees have all been planted too far apart to ever touch.

Sounds are rare and ceremonial. Every few hours a low metallic moan travels through the ground as thermal contraction in some buried conduit finally overcomes molecular stasis. The moan is always the same pitch, always the same duration. It has repeated without alteration for longer than vertebrates have existed on Earth. Occasionally a faint electrical pop can be heard near the tallest towers—static discharge from charge differentials that have been building since the last ice age on some other planet. These are the only noises. There is no wind to whistle, no sand to hiss, no life to stir.

Fauna exists, but only just. They are not animals in any sense that still carries meaning. Every few dozen kilometers you may encounter a solitary “drone” the size of a horse, a thing of black glass and articulated silica limbs that moves with the slow, patient rhythm of a geological process. They do not reproduce. They do not eat. They simply patrol the same perfect routes they have followed for epochs, repairing microscopic flaws in the city that no longer matter. When one finally fails, it settles into the plain and becomes another low glassy outcrop indistinguishable from the rest. New ones are not built. The total number slowly decreases toward zero.

The strangest feature is the geometry itself. Look in any direction and the horizon feels wrong. The plateau is so perfectly flat and the structures so precisely placed that forced perspective creates phantom cities at the edge of sight—clusters of towers that are not there, only the mind completing patterns across too much empty space. Walk toward those phantoms and they dissolve into nothing but more flat, warm glass under a starless sky.

This is not a dead world. Death implies an ending. This place reached its ending long ago and then remained exactly there, balanced on the far side of entropy like a needle standing on its point. It is not waiting. It has forgotten what waiting means.

The city does not dream. It simply persists, warm, dark, quiet, and older than continents. Every pipe, every tower, every motionless turbine has achieved its final, perfect, indifferent form. Nothing will ever change again, and the place has made its peace with that fact so thoroughly that peace itself has become geology.
