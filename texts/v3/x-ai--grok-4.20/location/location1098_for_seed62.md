The place is called the **High Condensate**, though no one map has ever agreed on its name for long.

It is a young plateau still shrugging off the violence of its own birth. The rock beneath everything is only a few hundred thousand years old—black basaltic glass and pale rhyolite fractured into knife-edged fins and fluted columns that have not yet had time to blunt. Elevation presses the air thin and cold; even at noon the temperature rarely climbs above what a temperate valley would call early spring. The light, however, is merciless. The sky is a polished enamel of glacial blue, almost white at the zenith, and it throws hard, shadowless illumination into every crease.

Water is not abundant here, yet it is everywhere. It does not flow so much as linger. Condensation beads on every cold surface the moment the sun drops behind the western ridges; thick, pearlescent droplets slide down obsidian pillars and pool in shallow cups of stone no larger than a palm. Occasional low-pressure fronts drift across the plateau and release a fine, silent rain that lasts three or four hours, then vanishes. The moisture never quite leaves. It simply waits inside the air, inside the moss, inside the lungs of anything that breathes.

And the moss—God, the vegetation. It is almost obscene in its vigor. Every fracture, every ledge, every wind-scoured table of rock has been colonized by a dense, dripping mat of bryophytes, clubmosses, and pale ferns whose fronds are the color of oxidized copper. Their roots and rhizoids have found purchase in the slightest roughness, and where they cannot dig they simply pile atop one another until the living carpet is knee-deep, sometimes waist-deep. The green is so saturated it looks wet even when it is not; when the condensation comes it becomes a single trembling mirror. In certain hollows the moss grows into soft, breast-shaped cushions so perfectly dense that a person could lie down and leave no footprint.

Scattered among this emerald excess are the ruins of an industrial effort that arrived, tried to tame the plateau, and then apparently changed its mind. Rusting iron towers rise like broken insect legs from the vegetation. Narrow-gauge rails disappear under curtains of hanging moss only to reappear twenty meters later, buckled by frost heave. A single brick power station still stands near the center of the largest basin, its roof long collapsed, red bricks furred with liverworts. Inside, massive turbines sit half-submerged in black, tea-colored water that has seeped up through the floor. The turbines have not turned in decades, yet their copper windings still gleam dully when stray shafts of light pierce the roof-hole.

The strangeness is subtle at first. Then it settles in the teeth.

Sound is almost extinct. The wind is too thin and too high to generate more than a constant, barely audible hiss across the moss. Footsteps are muffled instantly. Even the occasional drip of water from an overhang sounds strangely far away, as though the air itself has thickened into a medium that slows vibration. Birds are almost never seen. What little fauna exists tends to be small, silent, and slow: translucent snails the color of frozen milk that graze the moss at a pace measurable in centimeters per hour; pale, blind millipedes that move like living jewelry across the iron rails. Once in a great while something larger stirs in the distance—a shape too tall and too thin to be a deer—then it is gone before the eye can focus.

The most unsettling feature is the way the plateau is still growing. Not metaphorically. In certain basins the ground itself rises, millimeter by millimeter, year by year, pushed upward by some deep magmatic pressure that has not yet decided what shape it wants to become. Fresh obsidian dikes shoulder their way through the moss like black bones breaking the skin. Where they break the surface, the moss dies in perfect circles and is replaced, after a few seasons, by a different, stranger growth: translucent, succulent leaves that contain their own internal lamps. On bright days these leaves phosphoresce faintly even in direct sunlight, a cold violet that makes the surrounding green look almost diseased by comparison.

The entire region feels like a place that has not yet finished deciding what it is. It is simultaneously ancient and newborn, lush and austere, abandoned and watchful. The industrial relics look less like the remains of human intention and more like objects the land itself once grew, then rejected. The quiet is not peaceful; it is the quiet of something holding its breath.

If you stay long enough—and many who come here do, drawn by reasons they cannot name—you begin to feel the slow, geological pulse beneath your boots. A heartbeat measured in centuries. The moss drinks the condensation. The condensation drinks the sky. The sky drinks the light. And somewhere far below, the unfinished stone keeps rising, patient as cancer, pushing the whole bright, dripping tableau another fraction of a millimeter closer to whatever shape it is ultimately meant to take.
