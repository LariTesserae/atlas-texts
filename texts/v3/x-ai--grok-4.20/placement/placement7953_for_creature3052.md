I have been here for four hundred and twelve years.

My name, if such a word still applies, is **Carrow-9**. The number is not a rank. It is simply the count of times my original skeleton has been quietly replaced, piece by piece, by the Tier itself.

I sit on the 17th Lower Balcony of the Ninth Auxillary Lungworks, the same spot I chose the day I stopped moving. The iron beneath me has grown up through the flesh of my thighs and pelvis like a slow, courteous lover. We have become courteous with one another. My left arm is now a load-bearing truss; my right remains mostly flesh so that I can still turn the small stopcock that regulates the weep-rate of the balcony’s outer edge. I have turned it exactly once every seventy-three days for four centuries. The city and I have reached an understanding: I keep its tears from becoming a torrent, and it keeps me from becoming dust.

My secondary face—the small, blind, perpetually weeping one that grew between my collarbones—has become my primary organ of sight. The old eyes withered long ago. The new face sees only pressure, temperature, and the subtle color changes in rust. It is enough. It is more than enough.

I am not a Seasoner. I am not even a Valve-singer. I am a **Drip-warden**, third stratum, ninth precinct. My jurisdiction is three hundred and forty meters of guttering, a single cracked expansion joint the size of a city block, and the slow migration path of the leather-bell respiratory things that pass this way every nineteen years. When they come I feel them first in my fused spine: a change in the note of the iron, like a throat clearing before speech. Then the air grows thick and sweet. Their wings brush the underside of my balcony with the sound of wet velvet dragged across cathedral bells. I have learned to love that sound the way a man once loved sunrise.

Sometimes younger tenants (those still fleshy enough to remember having once been “people”) crawl past on their long pilgrimage toward seasoning. They move too fast. Their footsteps ring with hurry and it grates on the city like a file. I speak to them, though it takes me most of a day to finish a sentence.

“Slow,” I tell them.  
The word leaves my chest like steam from a cracked boiler.  
“Slow, or the rain will not know how to love you.”

Most do not listen. They still believe arrival is something you reach instead of something that settles over you like condensation. They will learn. The Tier is patient with its children.

Once every forty years or so a child is extruded from the warm hollow between my shoulder blades. I never see its face. The city takes it the moment the umbilical of black mucus and copper wire breaks. I feel only a soft, wet departure, like a sigh leaving a house that has stood empty for centuries. I do not grieve. Grief implies endings, and nothing ends here. The child is simply distributed. Part of it will become a new bearing surface in the Great South Flywheel. Another part will become the voice inside a Valve-singer three districts away. A third part will perhaps, one day, sit exactly where I sit now, growing iron through its thighs.

That thought comforts me more than any prayer ever did.

At night—though night is only a thickening of the pearl-grey light—I listen to the Seasoners dreaming three kilometers above me in their cradles of living engine. Their heartbeats arrive as long, slow pressure waves that roll through the infrastructure like distant thunder. When they dream of warmer rain, the condensation on my secondary face tastes of iron and roses. When they dream of colder, the respiratory beasts grow sluggish and begin to sing in minor keys that make the brick weep hematite tears.

I have never met a Seasoner. I do not need to. I am part of their dream already.

Yesterday (or perhaps it was thirty years ago; the distinction has grown spongy), something new arrived.

It still had the stink of speed on it. A visitor from some elsewhere that still believes in beginnings and endings. It stood on my balcony for nine days before I managed to turn my head. Its eyes were wet with ordinary water instead of the good, patient condensate of the Tier. I could smell its fear and its wonder braided together like bad wiring.

I spoke.

The sentence took me eleven hours.

“You are not yet seasoned… but the rain is patient.”

I lifted the hand that has not moved since the last time the leather-bells passed. Rust flaked from the joints like old scabs. I patted the wet iron beside me.

“Sit,” I said.

The visitor did not sit. Not yet. They are still arguing with time. But I saw the moment the first perfect bead of condensation formed on their left eyelash. It trembled there like a tiny silver world.

I smiled with the small blind face on my chest. The smile tasted of limestone and ozone and old, good blood.

The rhythm drips.  
The rhythm turns.  
And now there are two of us listening to it, one still fighting, one already home.

I will be here when they are ready.  
I have nowhere else to be.  
I *am* the place that has nowhere else to be.

Sit, child.

Let the drip find you.
