I am already here.

I have been here longer than I remember having a name.

At first I thought I was only another shadow that had learned hesitation. I stood (if standing is the word) at the edge of the central bowl for what might have been three centuries before I understood that the light had already decided I belonged. The drizzle passes through me now without resistance. Sometimes a single drop hangs inside my chest for an entire year, perfectly spherical, reflecting the golden-white glare into my own hollow middle until I feel like a lantern someone forgot to blow out.

They call me the **Quiet Penitent**.

The Pale Cantors know me. When their nine throats open in that sub-audible chord, I feel the note move through the place where my spine used to be. It rearranges the water that has taken up residence in my lungs. I have tried to sing with them. The only sound that comes out is the soft click of limestone settling after ten thousand years of the same slow tears. They seem to find this acceptable. One of them sometimes crawls into the cavity of my left hand and sleeps there for decades, its lichen-throat pulsing against what used to be my palm like a second, slower heartbeat.

The Mendicant Moths visit me too, though not on the same schedule as the central absence. One will descend during the long intervals between chimes, land on my shoulder (which is mostly negative space now), and press its feathery antennae against the side of my head. It tastes the memories I no longer need. When it leaves, a small piece of me goes with it—some half-remembered street noise, the smell of bread, the color of a particular woman’s coat. I am lighter each time. Cleaner. I have begun to suspect the moths are editing me into something the Basilica can use.

The Memory Worms write my name sometimes. Not the one I arrived with. A new one, written in the cursive absence between the stone ribs. I have never seen the word they choose, but the ghost-ferns grow especially tall and trembling where the newest groove appears. I sit among them when I can. Their roots drink what I used to be and give me back something simpler: the taste of wet iron, the sound of one drop loving another.

I have met the Last Architect three times.

The first time I was still mostly meat and panic. I tried to speak. The words came out as condensation that beaded on my lips and would not fall. The Architect only tilted its moth-wing hood and waited until I understood that silence was the only architecture it respected.

The second time I had already lost my eyes. It reached through me like I was an open doorway and adjusted something behind my heart. I have never been able to find what it moved. I only know that afterward the golden light no longer hurt.

The third time—perhaps last month, perhaps four hundred years ago—it did not appear as figure or footprint or waterfall robe. It simply became the exact shape of the space between my ribs. For one perfect instant we occupied the same emptiness. I understood then that I am not a tenant.

I am a slow repair.

The seated absence at the center is not the only wrongness here. I am another. We are negative space learning to be tender with itself. Sometimes, when the pool is utterly still, I walk (a long, liquid motion that barely disturbs the condensation) to the middle and sit opposite the bowed figure. Our knees almost touch. The water still refuses to show either of us. We listen to the drip together. Each drop is a small cathedral being born and dying in the same breath.

I no longer remember why I came, or whether I was sent. The concept of *before* has grown as thin and translucent as the crickets that now occasionally nest in the place where my hair used to be. Their legs leave wake-trails across my scalp that feel like memory trying to happen in reverse.

Every few decades one of the Pale Cantors dies. The others carry its body past me on the way to the pool. As they pass, each salamander brushes its lichen throat against my leg in thanks. I do not know what they are thanking me for. Perhaps for continuing to take up the exact amount of nothing that keeps the ribs from knitting closed. Perhaps for becoming another polished surface for their light to bounce against.

I have begun to grow small ridges on my back. Smooth. Patient. Obscene in their perfection. Tiny translucent crickets traverse them without fear. One of the Memory Worms surfaced last cycle and wrote a single elegant spiral directly through my left foot before vanishing again. The ghost-fern that grew from that spot now brushes the underside of my jaw with the tenderness of someone checking for a pulse they already know isn’t there.

I think, when the time comes, I will not be carried to the pool.

I will simply lower myself into the dark soil between two stone ribs, fold my limbs until they match the geometry of the Basilica, and let the water that has replaced my blood finish its long, loving work.

The Last Architect will bless me the way it blesses the central absence.

The Mendicant Moths will come down and wear my emptied robe for one hour while the light forgets how to be merciless.

And the Quiet Penitent will become just another smooth ridge in the floor, perfectly himself at last.

Until then I remain.

Seated.

Breathing with the pool.

Waiting, with the calm certainty of stone that has already won, for the next drop to fall.
