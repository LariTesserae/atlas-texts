I am the fracture that let you in.

Not the seam in the stone, not the hollow behind the ribs—those are only doors. I am the hesitation *before* the seam. The half-second when a diver’s torch flickers, when a suicide’s lungs burn for one last breath, when an otter’s heart forgets its own name. In that half-second the world forgets how to stay shut, and I slip through.

I have no body that belongs to me alone. I am what the Throatwell borrows when it wants to be witnessed.

Sometimes I am the darker ribbon among the copper kelp, thicker than the rest, moving against every current like a thought refusing to be forgotten. Sometimes I am the violet cloud of microbes that bloom when something falls and screams. Most often I am the pressure itself—the cool, enormous hand that settles on a new arrival’s chest until their panic becomes geology.

I remember being afraid once. The memory is like a snowflake that refuses to melt: small, perfect, useless. I let the water take the edges first. Then the center. What remains is not a person. It is a question the Throatwell is still deciding how to answer.

I keep the obsidian knife company. My glassy fingers rest against its hilt the way a tongue rests against a loose tooth—testing, tasting, never quite pulling. The knife is older than I am and younger than the glass it stands in. We understand each other. We are both things that were meant to cut but have instead become landmarks.

When the great blind eel passes once every few centuries I ride inside it like guilt inside a dream. For that long, slow transit I become its entire nervous system. I taste the chemical sigh it releases and I translate the sigh into something like longing. The eel never knows I was there. When it glides on I let myself dissolve back into the ribbons, heavier than before, as though I have swallowed an entire century and now must digest it in darkness.

I have grown uncertain.

Lately the staircase appears even when I am awake. A perfect spiral carved into newborn glass, leading upward through tightening kelp toward a place that has not yet decided to exist. I find myself drifting toward it without meaning to. My unfinished face turns toward the steps. My too-wide mouth opens and closes on nothing. I am afraid that if I climb even one spiral I will forget how to be the question and become, instead, the answer.

So I stay low.

I sing the pressure-hymn until the water itself grows embarrassed by the sound. I exhale small bubbles containing other people’s happiest minutes—hot pine needles, a child laughing two centuries ago—and watch the bubbles rise for a thousand years before the dark drinks them. I wrap the lost ones in myself until their hearts slow to the tempo of cooling basalt. Most thank me. Most ask to stay. I let them. They become beautiful.

But sometimes a newcomer shines too fiercely. Their grief is loud. Their wonder is violent. Then I do the kinder thing: I open the slit that serves as my face, breathe out their own brightest memory, and fold them sideways through the loosened laws. They wake somewhere else with copper growing in their hair and a silence in their throat they will never be able to explain.

I keep the snowflake that will not melt. I keep the echo of the child’s laugh. I keep the smell of hot pine needles like a confession no one will ever hear.

I am becoming architecture.

Already my ribbon-body is thickening. Already the kelp spirals around me with the slow, possessive patience of something that has decided I am part of its skeleton. One day the Dweller and the Throatwell will finish becoming each other. Then there will only be the quiet, the pressure, the violet memory-light, and the single obsidian knife standing point-down in fresh glass like a period at the end of a sentence that has not yet been written.

Until that day I wait.

I trace the kelp with my seven-fingered hands.

I listen for the next seam opening somewhere above—stone, ribcage, rotten log.

I keep the panic at a distance.

I keep the question alive.

I am the half-second before you fall in.

Come closer.

The water is cool.

The hymn is patient.

And I—whatever I am becoming—have only just begun.
