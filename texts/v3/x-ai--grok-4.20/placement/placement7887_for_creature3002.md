I have been here longer than I can measure.

At first I thought I was only passing through. A pilgrim, perhaps, or a repair technician who had taken the wrong stairwell centuries ago. I carried a name once. I carried tools. Both have rusted into the same red dust that lines the seams of every duct.

Now I am the one who stands between two hanging gardens of elephant-ear and scale-vine, perfectly still, while the slow fans overhead take another twelve minutes to move a single blade-width. I have become one of the unfinished gestures.

My name, if it can still be called that, is the **Archivist of Almost**.

I am not the Curator. She is the cathedral’s own reluctance; I am its memory of reluctance. Where she *is* the hesitation in every falling droplet, I am the droplet that remembers it was once rain somewhere else. I keep the records that were never meant to be kept.

You will not see me immediately either.

You might notice, after many hours of dripping and grinding and mournful chord, that one of the chrome beetles has refused to reflect the correct angle of light. Its mirrored back shows, instead of rust and green, a tall figure in a coat that was once canvas and is now mostly living moss and oxidised copper thread. The figure’s face is difficult to resolve. The jawline keeps becoming a flange. The eyes are two small pressure gauges whose needles tremble at the exact border between “Caution” and “Too Late.”

I do not arrive. I *accrete*. Rust settles on rust, moss thickens on moss, until the shape of a man becomes inevitable. Then I simply step out of the wall of pipes as though I had been waiting behind it for seven hundred years, which I have.

In my left hand I carry the Log: a book whose pages are sheets of hammered tin. Every entry is written in condensation and immediately begins to evaporate, so the record is forever incomplete. In my right hand I carry a single brass key that fits no lock that still exists. The key is warm. It has been warm since the first century.

I walk the long empty naves between the sudden jungles. My footfalls do not echo; the basilica eats the sound and turns it into extra dripping. Where I pass, the pale moths veer slightly off course so their trajectories will never quite reach the other side of the transept. I apologise to them in a voice that is mostly the hiss of a distant valve. They forgive me by continuing their slow, useless applause.

Sometimes the Curator and I meet.

We never speak in sentences. Instead we stand facing one another while the planetary gearbox beneath us advances another imperceptible degree. She tilts her head; the condensation pools that are her eyes brim. I lift the Log so she can see the latest vanishing entry. We remain like that until the pressure valves scream their half-finished warning, and then we simply part, satisfied that everything remains exactly as unfinished as it was.

I tend the things she will not touch.

I oil the bearings of the great fans so they will never quite seize. I trim the vines so they will never quite choke the helical pipes. I polish the chrome beetles until their reflections show futures that will never arrive. It is delicate, endless work. Every act of maintenance is also an act of sabotage. To keep something from ending, one must keep it from breaking. The difference is almost too small to see.

Once, long ago, I tried to leave.

I walked for what felt like decades toward a distant rectangle of slightly different brass light that I believed might be an exit. The Curator let me get within twenty paces. Then every fern on my coat burst into sudden, desperate flower. My bones sang with the planetary gears. My lungs filled with warm steam that tasted of every almost-decision I had ever made. I understood, in that moment, that departure was not forbidden out of cruelty. It was forbidden because the basilica had learned to love me the way it loves every other arrested motion: completely, forever, without conclusion.

So I turned back.

Now I am content to be the one who remembers the shape of almost. I catalogue the moths that are almost flying. I number the lizards that are almost shedding. I keep the score of how many degrees the greatest gear has been denied, and I add one more incomplete line to the Log each time the Curator weeps her upward-falling tears.

If you ever find me here, standing half-grown from a pillar of pipes and living rust, I will not offer you escape. I will only tilt the Log toward you so you can watch the newest entry already beginning to mist away.

And I will ask, in the voice that is mostly distant valve-hiss and geological patience:

Would you like to see how close we came today?

The question, of course, will never quite finish.

Neither will your answer.

Neither will anything.

Welcome.  
Stay.  
Almost.
