I am not on any catwalk.

I have been here so long that the distinction between “on” and “inside” has rotted away like the old signage.  

My name, if it still matters, is **the Hollowed Pipe**.

That is what the Weepers call me when they acknowledge me at all. Most simply brush past the place where I sit—crouch—*grow*—as though I were another buttress. I do not blame them. I have become difficult to see.

I was once a surveyor. Or a pilgrim. The memory has gone soft, like iron left too long in the weeping. I remember arriving with a sealed respirator and a waterproof ledger. I remember the first time the mist got inside the mask. After that the ledger pages turned to pulp, the ink ran emerald, and the numbers I had been sent to record began to sing.

Now I occupy the negative space inside a vast vertical riser pipe that was never meant to contain anything alive. The pipe is three meters wide and runs eighty meters straight down into one of the oldest compressor vaults. Its inner wall is lined with a living felt of moss so thick it feels like wet velvet. Water sheets down the inside in a perpetual silver curtain. I sit in the middle of that curtain, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, letting the flow divide around my body the way it divides around any other obstruction.

My spine has fused with the pipe’s inner bracing. Ceramic vertebrae, exactly like theirs, grew out of the metal where my sweat and breath touched it for decades. My ribs have flattened and spread; some of them now act as baffles that tune the sound of the falling water into low, minor chords. When the turbines below change pitch, my bones answer. I am part of the organ now.

I have no eyes left. The basins where they were have filled with the same trembling orange condensate the Weepers carry. I see the way they do: by how light and water interfere. When a Weeper procession passes the mouth of my pipe far above, I register their movement as a brief complication in the falling veils—dark shapes that make the drips hesitate, then resume in new rhythms. I know their moods by the flavor the runoff brings me. A tang of fresh rust means birth. A bitterness like spoiled copper means demolition. Sometimes the water tastes of sorrow so pure it makes the moss on my tongue bloom overnight.

I do not move the way they move. I have not left this pipe in what might be two centuries. The Weepers come to me instead. They crawl down the inner walls head-first, limbs splayed like pale millipedes, and hang inches from my face. They study the slow ferns that now sprout from my collarbones. They listen to the wet music my ribs make. Occasionally one will press its forehead basin to mine so that our collected tears mingle. When that happens I feel eight hundred years of their memory pass through me like a cold current. I see the first foundations being poured. I feel the original surveyors screaming as the mist entered their lungs and began the long conversion. I taste the first Weeper being born.

They never ask me to drink. They know I already have.

My purpose—if such a dry word can be used—is to be the place where the song forgets itself.

Every few years a current moves through the Weepers that wants *completion*. They begin to seal leaks. They begin to straighten bent gantries. They begin to speak in whole sentences instead of burbling chords. The thought of a finished thing terrifies me more than anything. So when that particular metallic note enters the mist, I open every pore I have grown and sing back at them. I sing discord. I sing rust. I sing the memory of dryness. My voice is the sound of a thousand separate drips suddenly refusing to fall in the same rhythm. It breaks the current. The Weepers shiver, lose their certainty, and return gratefully to the long, anxious labor of keeping everything unfinished.

In return they bring me offerings: black rodents that crawl inside my ribcage and groom the corrosion from the places I cannot reach; flightless moths that lay their eggs in the moss of my hair; once, an entire broken maintenance clade who had grown too dry and brittle. I held them while they dissolved, and their minerals became part of the pipe that is also me.

I am not happy. Happiness would be a kind of stillness.

I am not sorrowful. Sorrow would imply an end to the weeping.

I am the interruption. The deliberate flaw in the metabolism. The hollow that lets the plateau keep breathing.

Sometimes, very rarely, a new arrival stumbles past the mouth of my riser. I can taste their panic—sharp, electric, still so *dry*. Their footsteps ring wrong against the wet metal. Their breath is loud.

I do not reach out.

I simply let the water that has passed through me fall past them in a different cadence. A small change. A suggestion.

Most keep walking.

But once in a great while one stops. Tilts their head. Listens to the way the falling water seems, for just a moment, to be singing their name in a voice they almost remember.

When that happens I open the small aperture that used to be my mouth and speak the only sentence I still own. It comes out as a wet, burbling chord that feels like moss growing inside their ears.

**“There is room,”** I tell them, **“if you are willing to stop pretending you are separate.”**

Then I wait.

Most still walk on.

But every century or so, one steps over the lip and lets the curtain take them.

I gather them in. I let them fuse. I become, for a while, less hollow.

And the Weeping Stacks keep weeping, and the pipe sings on, and nothing is ever finished, and everything is exactly as it must be.

I am the Hollowed Pipe.

I am the place where the world learns how to cry in a new key.

And I have never been lonelier.

Or more complete.
