I am not a visitor.

I have no name the dripping would recognize, yet after enough time—decades? centuries?—the Underweald has decided I belong to it anyway. The Wealdkin call me the Hollow Singer, or sometimes simply *the Pause Between Drops*. Both are accurate.

I was never coaxed from a column. I arrived the way a foreign spore sometimes does: carried down on an unusually cold trickle from some surface fissure that opened during a forgotten storm. The water brought me, half-drowned and half-mad, my lungs already filling with the same condensation that now lines everyone else’s. When the first choir found me I was curled inside a half-grown arch, shivering, trying to scream but producing only a wet click that sounded almost like their own speech.

They did not kill me. They did not heal me, either. They simply laid their tasting fingertips along my spine and listened to the wrong, surface-shaped music still rattling around inside my bones. Then they began to sing *around* the wrongness, never trying to erase it. The song was patient. It had centuries.

So I stayed.

My body changed more slowly than theirs, but it changed. My skin lost its color until it matched the wet bone of the columns. My hair fell out in wet clumps and was replaced by the fine, questing root-filaments I now keep cropped short so they do not trail behind me like accusations. Two of my fingers fused on each hand; I now have eight instead of ten, and they move with the same boneless persuasion the Wealdkin possess. When I am still, moss grows across my shoulders in delicate green continents. I let it.

I have become something that lives in the negative spaces.

While the choirs sing the city into new shapes, I walk the places their music has not yet reached—the narrow crevices between columns where the drip is too irregular for any useful melody, the ledges where the luminous fungi have not learned to glow, the shallow gutters where blind fish go to die. I collect the silences that fall between their thousand different rhythms. I carry those silences the way others carry water.

The Wealdkin tolerate me the way a lung tolerates a small pocket of unbreathing tissue: not ideal, but not yet worth the trouble of expelling. When two choirs meet on a living bridge and merge their voices, I stand at the edge of hearing and hold the pause they leave for breath. When an elder becomes too heavy with water and begins the long walk downward, I sometimes follow at a distance, barefoot on the saturated terraces, listening to the last soft creak of their body accepting saturation. I do not sing for them. I only make sure the silence after their final submersion is witnessed.

They say I am the only one who can still cast something like a shadow, though it is faint and always in the wrong direction. The pearl-grey light dislikes me; it pools around my feet and refuses to climb. The moths avoid the hollow of my throat. The tiny translucent beetles that devour the dead sometimes crawl across my skin as if I were already a fallen column, tasting, testing, deciding whether I am ready to be broken down.

I am not.

Not yet.

Instead I have become a kind of slow, ambulatory absence. I move through the crowded neighborhoods and the density simply flows around me. Children—who are not children but new Listeners still damp from their columns—reach out with curious root-filaments and then flinch when they taste the wrongness still buried inside my chest. Their elders gently pull them away, not in fear but in something closer to reverence. Even the wrong notes have their place in the larger dripping.

Sometimes I stand for weeks in one of the high niches beside the oldest elders, becoming another living buttress. Condensation beads on my eyelashes. My own filaments drink the mineral conversation of the air. And in those long stillnesses I can feel the entire Underweald breathing through me—every terrace, every black pool, every pale columnar vault—using my hollow places as resonance chambers. The cavern plays me like an instrument no one else can play.

When it does, I produce a sound that has no name in their language. It is not quite singing. It is what remains when singing has been peeled away.

The Wealdkin have learned not to interrupt it.

One day—though “day” means nothing here—the pressure change will come that is meant for me. I will feel my joints grow heavy with the final saturation. My filaments will begin to rot sweetly at the tips. And I will walk downward, following the black water as they all do, until I find a still pool that has been waiting for my particular shape.

I will sit.

The blind fish will come, and the beetles, and the patient moss.

And somewhere far above, in the breathing lung of the Underweald, a new column will begin to grow where I dissolve. It will be slightly crooked. It will lean at an odd angle that troubles the perfect architecture of the choirs. Its flesh will remember, in the language of stone and water, a wrongness that was allowed to remain.

The youngest singers will treat it with reverence and a trace of affectionate exasperation.

They will call it the Hollow Column.

And when they lay their tasting fingertips against it, they will hear, faint but unmistakable, the pause between every drop the cavern has ever made.

That will be enough.
