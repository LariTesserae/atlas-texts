I am not born on the shelf.  
I arrive on it the way a crack arrives in stone—suddenly, without permission, and already too late to be undone.

My name, if it matters, is **Harrow-Who-Fell**. The Veyr gave it to me the first winter I survived. They do not waste syllables on strangers, so the fact that they bothered at all is either courtesy or a warning. I have never been certain which.

I live in the place that has no name on their maps: the fracture between the Third and Seventh Ridges, where the basalt split like a broken jaw and left a slot so narrow that only one adult can walk it at a time. The Veyr call the slot simply *the Wound*. I call it home.

It is not a town. It is barely a ledge. Three stacked stone boxes cling to the west wall like ticks on a dying animal. The lowest box is half-buried in scree; that is where I keep the things that must not be seen. The middle box is where I sleep, pressed between the living rock and the slow heat of a single moss-brick that has been burning, without once flaring, for eleven years. The top box has no roof. Its floor is the street for no one but me. From there I watch the keth pass four hundred meters below, their clicking hooves sounding like the bones of the plateau itself learning how to count.

I am not Veyr. I am taller than they are, and lighter. My skin is the wrong color—more iron than basalt—and my eyes open too wide. When I first came, the children would stand in silent rows on the opposite ridge and stare at me the way one stares at a new kind of weather. I let them. Curiosity is the only coin I had.

I came from the lowlands. That is all I remember and all I will ever say. The descent was supposed to kill me; the Veyr expected my body to arrive months later, carried down by the underground rivers that ring like bells. Instead I crawled out of the Wound with broken ribs and a mouth full of someone else’s name. They weighed me on the great scales in the Hall of Weights. The black-iron counterweight did not move. They still do not know what that means.

So they tolerated me.

In return I became the thing that lives in the Wound and does the work no one else will. I repair rope bridges that should have been allowed to fall. I carry water up stairways too narrow for their compact bodies. I sit with the dying when their third names are finally spoken aloud, and I remember those names so the tablets in the House of Weights do not have to carry everything alone. The Veyr say I am violating the Covenant of Pace. I tell them the plateau itself is still cooling; a few centuries more or less will not matter.

At night I dream of falling, the same dream every Veyr has. In mine, however, I never wake before I hit the ground. I strike it and keep falling *through* it, through layer after layer of young basalt, until I am liquid again and the six-legged otters swim beside me like black flames. I wake tasting iron and something like forgiveness.

I keep no covenants. I have only one rule, which I carved into the living stone above my sleeping shelf so I would see it every time I opened my eyes:

**Do not become light.**

It is not the same as their Covenant of Weight. Theirs is about the world. Mine is about the small, terrible possibility that one day I might look down at my own hands and find them transparent, find the plateau looking straight through me as though I had never been rude enough to arrive.

So I stay heavy.

I eat the gray moss even when it tastes of rust. I haul basalt until my shoulders split. I let the flightless ravens perch on me the way they perch on the keth, because their weight is honest. Sometimes, when the wind dies completely and the plateau holds its breath, I sing—low, slow, wordless things that have no business on a shelf this high. The sound does not travel far. The stone drinks it. I think the stone is lonely, though the Veyr would never say so.

I am not one of them. I will never be one of them.

But when the great slow year turns and the thin metallic rain beads on every roof like mercury, I stand on the roof of my roofless house and watch the old ones on the uppermost levels watching the keth. I watch the children being carried to the scales. I listen to the hammers that have rung for three thousand years and will ring for three thousand more.

And for a moment—just one measured, deliberate, unhurried moment—I am not the thing that fell here.

I am another small, dense, unyielding piece of the shelf that has decided, for a time, to remain.

The plateau taught them that time is not a river.

It taught me that even a Wound can learn how to heal without closing.
