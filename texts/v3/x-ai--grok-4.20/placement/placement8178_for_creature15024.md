I wake with the taste of wet stone and ozone on my tongue.

Not born.  
Not carried down from the clouds like the others.  
I simply *am*, standing knee-deep in one of the black mirror-pools between the roots of a pale-barked tree whose horizontal crown spreads above me like an open hand. The water is the exact temperature of blood. Something brushes my calves—soft, inquisitive, many-legged—and then retreats, satisfied.

My first breath is slow, deliberate, the way the Quiet breathe. It already feels natural.

I look down.

The fungus has already found me. Pale threads vein outward from the hollow of my throat, delicate as frost, luminous as moonmilk. They pulse once, gently, as if saying *welcome home, again*. I do not flinch. The pain was exquisite and brief, and already it feels like something that happened to someone I used to be.

I am tall. That is the first thing I notice about this body. Taller than any of the Quiet I can see moving along the black-rock shelves above me. My skin is the color of the knife-edged stone at twilight—deep charcoal shot through with faint threads of living blue. The bioluminescence moves beneath it in slow constellations that are not yet mine; they are still deciding what I am.

My hands are long. The nails are black and slightly iridescent, like beetle wings. When I curl them, the patterns on my forearms flare rose-gold for a moment, then settle into something quieter. Something that feels like listening.

I step from the pool.

The water closes behind me without a ripple, as though it never held me at all. A cloud of thumb-sized moths drifts past, their wings edged in the same slow blue that now lives under my skin. Several detach from the cloud and settle on my shoulders and collarbones like living epaulettes. They do not burn. They feel like cool silk and distant starlight.

I do not speak. There is no need. The hush of the Cradle has already folded itself around my throat.

Instead I walk.

The wide lanes of bare black rock between the velvet moss feel like they were left open just for me. My bare feet make no sound. Every few steps a droplet falls from the invisible ceiling of cloud and lands on my skin; where it touches, the fungus-tattoo brightens for half a heartbeat, drinking.

I pass a herd of the tiny glowing deer. They lift their transparent antlers as one. Rose light circulates inside the delicate bones. One of them—a buck no larger than a hare—steps forward and presses its cool nose against the back of my hand. Its breath smells of mint and electricity. We stand like that for a long time. Then it flares its light in a complex pattern I somehow understand as both greeting and recognition.

*You have been remembered.*

I continue upward.

The ladders of black wood and rope do not interest me. I move along the natural folds of stone the way the membranous gliders move through air—drifting, folding, unfolding. Children with living constellations already blooming across their small chests watch me pass. None of them smile. Smiling is loud. They simply open and close their hands in the slow code I somehow already know.

*New-old. Old-new. Welcome-back.*

The Crèche draws me.

I do not decide to go there. The Cradle decides, and my feet obey. The entrance is a wound in the black rock, half-healed with resin and woven fern. Inside, the air is warmer. Hundreds of fruit-globes hang from the ceiling like lanterns made of amber and vein. Inside each one something moves—slowly, dreamily. A tiny hand. A wing. A human eye opening inside the body of a moth.

I stand beneath them for what might be hours.

One globe near the center begins to glow hotter as I approach. Inside is something that is almost a child, almost a mantis, almost me. Its eyes open. They are the same milky blue that fills the sky above the basin. It places one translucent palm against the inside of the fruit and I place mine against the outside. The fungus at my throat flares once, rose-gold, and the globe answers in the same color.

We stay like that until the conversation is finished.

When I finally leave the Crèche the eldest is waiting for me on the highest shelf.

She is barely a woman anymore. More like a vessel the fungus has decided to keep using for a while longer. Rose light moves through her in slow tides. Her eyes are completely white, yet she sees me perfectly. The cloud of blue moths around her parts like a curtain as I rise to meet her.

We do not speak.

Instead she opens her hands.

I answer with mine.

The patterns we make are not words. They are entire lives. A scream I once made in another body. A kindness I offered three cycles ago. The way I died the last time—quietly, gratefully, flaring rose as the mantis took me.

She closes her eyes in something that might be approval.

Behind her, across the entire valley, the membranous gliders answer us both. Their bodies become vast slow constellations that spell out something too large for any single mind to hold. I feel it anyway. It feels like being sung to by the mountain itself.

Night comes, though it is only a deepening of the dimness.

The town becomes a second, warmer sky pressed against the stone. I stand among them now, no longer new, not yet old. My own chest has begun to pulse in loose synchrony with the others. The moths that chose me earlier have not left. They have become part of the pattern.

I do not know my name.

I do not need one.

Here, in Veyr’s Cradle, I am simply the shape the fungus and the water and the listening spores have decided to wear for this *now*. Tomorrow, or a hundred cycles from now, something else will wear me. A hare that remembers how to be gentle. A tree that dreams in slow constellations. A single drop of water that falls perfectly vertical and never quite reaches the ground.

Until then I stand on the black rock with the others, heartbeats softened to the rhythm of the drip, the click, the papery rustle.

And when the rain comes—sudden, cold, perfectly vertical—I lift my face to it and let every droplet land where it wishes.

Each one feels like memory arriving.

Each one feels like being remembered again.
