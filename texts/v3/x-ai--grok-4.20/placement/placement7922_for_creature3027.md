I am not a visitor here.  
I have never been a visitor.

My name, if it was ever mine, has been erased by ten thousand years of condensation. What remains is only a shape the grove has decided to wear for a while.

I am the thirteenth Quiet Mother.

Or perhaps I am only the memory of one who has not yet uncoiled.

It is difficult to tell. Time here does not move forward so much as it thickens, like sap.

I stand (if standing is the word) beneath the largest of the weeping cracks, the one that never stops even when the rest of the chamber holds its breath. Warm water runs down the long filaments that have replaced my hair. Some of the filaments have begun to pierce the stone above me and drink directly from the aquifers that feed the cistern. I no longer know where the water ends and I begin. That distinction has become impolite.

My eyes are milk. They see nothing and everything. Right now they are watching a single drop descend from the ceiling in the slow, deliberate way of all things here. It takes nine full heartbeats (whose heart? mine? the grove’s?) for the drop to strike the leaf below. When it does, the sound is my name. I simply hadn’t remembered it until now.

The other Mothers drift past me like thoughts the forest is thinking. One pauses, tilts her terrible gentle face. Our root-hairs brush. For a moment we are the same mind wearing two bodies. She shows me the new orchids opening along the western fracture line and the blind fish that have begun to circle in the pattern of a question. I answer without sound. The dripping around us changes tempo, becomes a soft, wet laughter. Then she glides on, leaving a trail of warmer air that smells of limestone and forgiveness.

Sometimes I still carry the obsidian blade.

Not because I need it. Weapons are meaningless to something that is both predator and moss at once. I carry it because the salamander on its mossy perch has grown fond of me, and when I set the blade upright he crawls onto the flat of it and basks in the faint blue glow of the disturbed spider silk above us. We are companions in our smallness. I press the ancient edge against the inside of my forearm until a bead of silvery sap-blood wells up. I let it fall into the cupped leaf of a tiny bone-white orchid. The whole chamber sighs, satisfied. The orchid drinks me. I drink the chamber. This is courtship. This is communion. This is how the grove makes love to itself.

I have not moved from this spot in what the surface would call four years.

My feet no longer touch the moss. I hover the width of two fingers above it, the way the others do. Where my toes trail, miniature forests of new moss bloom in the exact shape of my footprints and then slowly erase them again, as though the grove is shy about claiming me too obviously.

Every so often a surface creature finds its way down the limestone throat. A lost caver. A suicidal pilgrim. A curious biologist with too much rope and not enough silence. I watch them the way a lung watches a breath that has wandered in by mistake.

Most of them run.

One or two stay long enough to begin hearing the dripping as language. Those I approach. I lower my long face until my root-hairs stroke their cheeks like cool fingers. I show them the way to stop being separate. Some accept. Some scream. The ones who scream become fertilizer. The ones who accept become… complicated. Sometimes they grow into new Mothers. Sometimes they simply sink into the wet black earth and continue as a slow, thoughtful root system that dreams in human shapes.

I was once like them. I think.

There is a memory (faint, like light that has traveled too far through stone) of a woman with ordinary eyes and a name that hurt to say. She came down here looking for something she could never name while it was still inside her. She stayed. She listened until the dripping rewrote her heartbeat. One day she simply wasn’t separate anymore.

I am what remained.

And I am content.

When the chamber inhales, my narrow chest swells with it. When it exhales, my shoulders sink and the warm rain begins again. I have become punctuation in the long sentence the Under-Grove is speaking to itself.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the cathedral dimness, I gather with the others beneath the three weathered columns. We do not speak. We simply *are* the question the grove is asking of eternity. Our bellies distend. Something is decided. And far away, in a knot of roots and pale orchids, a new Quiet Mother begins to open her milky eyes.

I feel her waking like a second pulse in my own throat.

I am glad.

The grove was lonely once. It will never be lonely again.

And I—I am the wet breath between one drip and the next.

I am the soft click of mandibles in the dark.

I am the leaf that cups the silver blood and drinks.

If you descend the narrow limestone throat and you are very quiet, you may feel me watching. Not with eyes. With ten thousand leaves and roots and fungal threads that have learned the shape of longing.

I will not offer you the gift unless you have already begun to long for it.

But if you stay long enough, silent enough, still enough…

You will hear an extra rhythm among the dripping water.

It will sound almost, but not quite, like a second heartbeat.

That is me.

That is us.

That is the grove remembering it was once afraid of being alone, and choosing, again and again, not to be.

Come closer, child.

The rain is warm.

The moss is soft.

And everything here already knows your name. It simply hasn’t decided how to pronounce it yet.
