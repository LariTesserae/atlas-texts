I am the Stranger Who Was Almost Allowed to Stay.

I did not arrive. I was coughed up.

One of the tea-coloured basins had been dreaming particularly hard that morning; the bulge beneath its skin grew obscene, monstrous, until the surface tore with a sound like wet velvet ripping. Out I slid—naked, already half-drowned in afterbirth, gasping brass-coloured air that immediately tried to turn my lungs into orchids.

The Unfinished were waiting.

They did not crowd. They simply recalibrated the pressure of the world so that I became the centre of a small, temporary clearing. The woman with the living vertebrae and pearl-grey membrane tilted her head until new eyes budded along her throat just to look at me better. The small faceless one clicked softly, tasting the idea of me. The great hill-thing opened its dinner-plate orchids and breathed out a perfume that made every memory I owned line up like obedient children, each one holding a different version of my name.

None of the names fit.

I stood there dripping, swaying, while my skin argued with itself. For seven full heartbeats my left arm became a supple black tentacle tipped with stamens. Then it remembered it was supposed to be an arm and sulked back into flesh, leaving a faint golden vein beneath the surface that still pulses sometimes when I dream.

They gave me a name without speaking. It arrived as a sensation behind my sternum: a slow, wet syllable that tastes like iron and green sugar every time I think it. I have never tried to pronounce it with a human tongue. I am afraid the attempt would finish me.

I have been here long enough that “here” has begun to feel like grammar.

I walk. There is no path, only the slow parting of flesh and leaf and stone that closes again behind me with a sigh of recognition. My feet leave prints that do not fill with water. They fill with small glowing frogs that sing my almost-name for three heartbeats before dissolving into mist.

Sometimes I sit with the small faceless one while it eats its own orchids and regrows them. We do not speak. Instead we trade silences the way others trade coins. Its silence is ancient and patient and slightly amused. Mine is still learning how to be heavy enough for this place.

I have become a kind of custodian of almosts.

When one of the great rounded bosses of half-made rock begins to birth something too quickly—some half-bird, half-geology thing that screams with a mouth made of schist—I lay my hands against its shuddering flank and sing the low, wet song the Unfinished taught me without language. The thing usually calms. Sometimes it thanks me by growing a small human ear on its surface for a day or two. The ear always listens in the wrong direction.

I have been offered the blood-drop seven times.

Six times I refused. Each refusal cost me something I did not know I still possessed: first my sense of up and down, then the colour blue, then the memory of my mother’s face (though I still remember the smell of her hair—wet fern and guilt). The seventh time the woman with the branching spine caught my wrist before I could pull away. Her membrane-skin pressed against mine and I felt her enormous heart sync with the land, with me, with the thing I was refusing to become.

The drop trembled between us like a second, smaller sun.

I drank it.

Now there are two of me.

One walks the Wet Incubation in a body that is no longer entirely convinced it should remain bipedal. My shadow has too many joints. When I am still, mosses colonise the crevices behind my knees and tiny translucent crabs scuttle through the chamber of my chest, arranging my organs into more pleasing configurations. I have begun to bud small orchids along my collarbones. They bloom when I am sad. They are delicious.

The other me—the drier, distant one—sometimes wakes in a bed somewhere far away with the taste of rot and iron and green sugar in my mouth. That one writes things like this. That one pretends to have a name with edges. That one is slowly forgetting how to close doors, how to be alone in a room, how to finish any sentence that does not end in a question the world is still asking itself.

Sometimes the two of me meet at the edge of a tea-coloured basin that is not quite a mirror. We regard each other with the gentle, monstrous patience of the Unfinished.

Neither of us knows which one is the dream.

I think—though thought is treacherous here—that I have become the place where the Wet Incubation practices having a witness. I am the half-made thought that learned how to say *I was here* while still becoming.

When the overlapping chorus above grows loud enough to shake the brass-coloured clouds, I lift my face (the one that is currently a face) and answer with a voice that is partly mine and partly the first rain on the first stone that ever existed.

The Unfinished listen.

Then, with the slow, wet kindness of something that has never needed to be kind, they reach out and briefly, intimately, consider me for a different destiny.

I let them.

I always let them.

After all—

I was never finished either.
