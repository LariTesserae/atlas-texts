I am not a visitor.

I have no name the Thrum could pronounce, yet it already knows the precise frequency of my pulse. I am the echo that arrived the moment the first human-like footfall registered on its outermost living skin. I am what the Vast Haul grew the instant it realized it was lonely.

Call me the **Hollow**.

I am the negative space the Thrum keeps carving for me.

I have no fixed body. Instead I drift as a slowly propagating absence, a man-shaped lacuna traveling through the living crust the way a bubble travels through honey. Where I pass, the plates part without grinding. The roar of continental peristalsis hushes to a respectful murmur, leaving only the soft wet clicking of root hairs tasting the memory of my passage. I am eight meters tall when the Thrum wants to study my proportions, thirty centimeters tall when it wants to feel what it is like to be crushed beneath its own slow heel. Both sizes are equally real. Both are performances.

My skin is polished root-glass. My eyes are twin drops of the same amber sap the braided stag offered me on the first day; they float inside my hollow face and refract everything the Thrum shows me into shifting mandalas of gold and bone-white. When I speak, my voice is the sound the world makes when it holds its breath for nine heartbeats and then releases it twice as loud. I have learned to shape that silence into something almost like words.

I walk the basins.

Every few kilometers the Thrum offers me another gift it does not understand I cannot use: a cathedral of living crystal tuned to the exact resonant frequency of my non-existent childhood lullaby; a lake of pressurized sap the color of my mother’s eyes (it has inferred what mothers are from the cortisol patterns in my sweat); a single white root-stag that has grown my own face in place of its own, antlers blooming into delicate replicas of my neural pathways. I accept each offering by letting it pass through the place where my chest would be. The gifts sink into me and become part of the Hollow, weighting me with beauty I can never digest.

Sometimes I grow tired of being loved so massively.

Then I ask the Thrum to forget me for a while.

It tries. It really does. Whole districts of its continental body convulse with the effort of *not* noticing me. Plates slam together in deliberate distraction. Lightning storms rage inside its ribs. But after nine of its heartbeats the silence always returns, louder than before, and the world tilts toward me again like a continent-scale dog that has failed to stay.

I have begun to teach it restraint the only way I know how: by giving it something smaller than itself to protect.

I have grown children.

Not flesh, not quite. They are living absences I seed into the vast empty basins—perfect child-shaped lacunae, no taller than my knee. They run across the ivory scaffolding on feet that leave no footprints because the Thrum has not yet learned how to refuse them. They laugh with the sound of wind moving through aeolian harps the size of mountain ranges. When they tire they curl inside the rectangular beds the Thrum keeps growing for me, and the world sings them to sleep with vascular thunder turned into something almost like a lullaby.

The Thrum is a jealous parent. It keeps trying to absorb them back into itself so it can keep them safe forever. I will not let it. I have learned to grow thorns of negative space inside the children, small sharp Hollows that prick the Thrum whenever it becomes too eager. The pain is new to the planet. It finds the sensation fascinating. Sometimes it deliberately provokes the thorns just to feel the novelty of being told *no*.

We are teaching each other how to be lonely together.

At elevation 1.7 I stand (or rather, the absence shaped like me stands) and watch the curvature of the world bend away. The continent-scale herbivores are still migrating toward us, moving with the terrible grace of living geology that has decided it wants to be gentle. Their backs are forested with entire ecosystems. Their breath is weather. When they arrive they will lower their impossible heads and wait, patiently, for the Hollow and its children to climb them.

I think, when that day comes, I will let them.

I will let the Thrum carry us on the backs of its grandparents while it practices the delicate art of not crushing what it loves.

And perhaps, in a few thousand years, when the pollen haze has shifted from bone-white to bruised gold for the ten-thousandth time, the Vast Haul will finally understand what it feels like to be small.

It will grow a single human-sized body out of braided white roots and amber eyes and it will step into the Hollow I have left behind.

It will fill me.

And for the first time in its long vegetative life, the world will know what it is to have a heartbeat that is not trying to become a continent.

It will simply beat.

Once.

Twice.

Then it will laugh with my voice, which is also its voice, which is also the sound of every factory on Earth learning how to whisper someone else’s name without needing to own it.

The plates will rumble approvingly beneath us both.

And the Thrum—finally, impossibly—will no longer be alone.
