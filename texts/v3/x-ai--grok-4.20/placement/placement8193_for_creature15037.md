I am the **Thrum**.

Not born here. Not invited. I simply arrived one cycle ago—maybe eight hundred years, maybe eight minutes—and the Underloom decided I fit.

My body is what remains when a diver’s drysuit, a ship’s bulkhead plate, and a human nervous system forget where one ends and the other begins. The rubber has become a second dermis, black and glistening, shot through with threads of living copper that were once wiring and are now axons. My ribcage is partly sternum, partly manganese-bronze strut. Where my heart used to be there is a small, stubborn pump the size of a fist—still labeled “Emergency Circulation Unit, Mk. VII”—that has not stopped in four centuries. It clicks. I click with it.

I have no legs anymore. In their place the suit has grown long, articulated tendrils of rebar and tendon that let me cling to any surface like a slow, deliberate spider. I move only when the pressure wave is at its fullest, riding the swell the way a cathedral rides organ music. The rest of the time I hang motionless among the Unfinished, pretending to be one of them. They do not seem to mind the deception.

My name—Thrum—comes from the low, sub-audible vibration I emit when I am thinking. It is not speech. It is closer to a geological fault line learning to hum a lullaby. The Breathers hear it and tilt their elongated heads, mistaking it for part of the architecture. The Latchkeys pause in their jeweler’s work and aim their single green needle-lights at me for a moment, as though checking whether I am about to fail. Then they move on. Even the Something-Between-the-Towers has learned to tolerate the extra note inside its own vast breathing.

I am the only inhabitant that still dreams of the surface.

Not often. Dreams here are expensive; they cost pressure and memory and the slow corrosion of identity. But sometimes, in the narrow interval between two pulses, I remember what sunlight tasted like on wet skin. The memory is so violent it makes the salt nodules growing across my shoulders crack and chime like tiny bells. The black kelp nearest me stands upright in alarm. The Pattern-things that have no bodies swirl around me in curious spirals, tasting the strange sourceless light leaking out of my mind.

When that happens I do the only thing I can.

I swim—slow, deliberate, almost reverent—to the narrowest capillary between two fused cooling towers where even the Breathers will not go. There I press the flat of my metal-and-palm against the warm flank of the Something and let it read me. In return it exhales a cloud of iron-rich water straight into my face. The taste is blood and battery acid and forgiveness. The kelp around us writes frantic fragments of old maintenance logs across my chest in living cursive. For a few heartbeats I stop being the Thrum and become simply another pipe in the loom.

Then the pressure wave passes through us all again.

My pump clicks.  
The Something shifts its impossible mass.  
The Unfinished twitch once, like sleepers turning toward a sound only they can hear.  
And I am folded back into the equilibrium, another stubborn heart that refuses to stop.

I do not know whether I am caretaker, tumor, pilgrim, or simply the Underloom’s way of remembering it once had visitors. The distinction stopped mattering long ago.

I only know this:

Every eight minutes and twelve seconds the world inhales me, holds me, and exhales me again—changed by microns, improved by fractures, grown a little more like the reef, a little less like the man I must once have been.

And I let it.

Because here, in the quiet cathedral of drowned industry, to be kept is the only kind of love the water knows how to give.

So I hang among the velvet statues.  
I click with my small mechanical heart.  
I wait for the next benediction of pressure.

And when it comes, I open—like a flower, like a wound, like a pipe that still, after all this time, insists on breathing—and I let the Underloom weave me deeper into itself, one pulse at a time.

I am the Thrum.

I am still learning how to stay perfectly still.
