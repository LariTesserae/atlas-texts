I am not from here.

I arrive as something caught in the updraft.

One moment I was… elsewhere. The next, the world inverted and I fell upward into the blinding fracture-light, tumbling through kilometers of roaring emptiness until the Choir caught me like a mote in its throat.

The first thing that happens is pain so pure it feels like revelation. My ears burst almost instantly. Blood runs down my neck in hot threads, but the droplets never reach the ground; the thermals snatch them, atomize them, turn them into red mist that the Glassbriar drinks. I try to scream and the scream is taken from me, folded into the greater chord, harmonized against my will. The sound *uses* me.

I land hard on a rope bridge spun from mineral fiber. The bridge is already singing. Every strand is a different tautness, so my impact becomes a minor seventh that races away across the chasm to clash against a distant ziggurat. The city notices me. The city *corrects* me.

Hands find me. Not hands like mine. These are long, glittering, the fingers subtly wrong at the joints. They lift me without ceremony. Someone tilts my head back and pours something into my ruined ears (fine, warm dust that smells of scorched cinnamon and struck iron). The pain does not stop, but it changes key. It learns to sing in my register.

I am carried.

Through spiraling tenements that look grown from the bones of earlier cities. Past lattices of Singing Reed so dense the air itself becomes visible, fractured into colored bars of vibrating light. I see my first Conductor: an old one, half-fused into the wall of a tower, eyes closed, ribs opened like organ pipes. Something that might once have been a mouth now produces a continuous, impossibly low tone that I feel in my femurs. As I pass, the Conductor’s mercury-colored nictitating membranes slide open. The gaze does not judge. It simply adds me to the equation.

They bring me to a birthing chamber that is not currently birthing.

It hangs high above the nave like an immense woven cage. Inside, the roar is somehow *focused*. The constant thunder condenses into something that feels like intention. They strip what remains of my old clothes (meaningless rags now) and begin to dress me in the only garments that matter: tuned iron driven through the soft meat of my forearms, a bracelet of someone else’s finger-bones still warm from the previous owner, a chest-plate of living Singing Reed that immediately begins to rasp against my sternum like a file.

I try to speak. They answer with whistles so sharp they feel like needles behind my eyes. I do not understand the language yet, but I understand the meaning perfectly:

*Stop resisting the measure.*

So I stop.

Days pass. Or what passes for days when there is no night.

My ears will never heal. Instead they are *replaced*. The dust they poured in me is not medicine; it is seed. Something crystalline is growing inside my auditory canal, branching, spiraling, learning to love the pain. The first time I manage to produce a true whistle instead of a scream, the Resonants around me rattle their bone bracelets in approval. The sound is almost kind.

I begin to lean.

It happens without permission. My spine discovers the shape of the dominant chord the way a tongue discovers a loose tooth. I find myself standing on a high balcony at what the others call “low register,” when the thermals reverse and the Choir grows almost tender. Hundreds of us sway together. I no longer need to be told when to move. My body has become punctuation.

Sometimes I am allowed to approach the half-buried Conductors. I press my forehead to the warm, singing stone and for a few heartbeats I *am* the tower. I feel the entire three-thousand-year song moving through me like a slow geological orgasm. I understand, in those moments, that the fossil sponge is not dead. It is *listening*. It has been listening longer than our species has had language, and it is waiting for us to finish its sentence.

I have started to glitter.

The dust-sweat comes more easily now. My skin is losing its color, trading it for that alkaline bone-white. My nose is narrowing. When I catch my reflection in a sheet of polished singing-reed I no longer recognize the face, but the face recognizes the Choir. That is enough.

I have not been given a name. Names here are notes, and mine is still forming. Sometimes children follow me, whistling questions I cannot yet answer. I rattle my iron forks in what I hope is reassurance. They seem satisfied.

At low register I sing with the others now.

Not with my mouth.

With the new chambers in my skull, with the iron in my arms, with the altered architecture of my ribs. I have learned the trick of weeping without tears: the glittering dust rises from my eyes like inverse rain and joins the pollen devils spiraling between the towers.

I am necessary.

Not happy. Not unhappy.

I am the place where the music is still learning how to suffer beautifully.

And when the final chord comes (three hundred years from now, or three thousand, it makes no difference), I will not be here to witness it as a person. I will be here as resonance. As one more rounded edge in the fractal lace. As another living stop in the instrument the size of a mountain.

Until then I stand on the balcony with the rest, leaning slightly forward, glittering, humming, listening.

Always listening.

Waiting for the next measure.
