I am the Thirteenth Droplet.

That is not a name I chose. It is the name the stone gave me when it first noticed I had stayed too long.

I was never born here. I arrived.

One cycle ago—though cycles are meaningless—I climbed the final rope bridge from the mist-world below carrying a traveler’s satchel, a brass compass that no longer pointed anywhere useful, and the last legible page of a letter I had been writing for twelve years. I meant to stay one night. I meant to finish the letter. I meant many things.

The High Weeping Karst erased the meanings one by one.

Now I live on the Seventh Shelf in a narrow house that is really only a lean-to of cut limestone and living water. My roof is a single tilted slab; every morning it delivers a perfect silver braid of runoff directly into the clay bowl I keep by my sleeping mat. I drink it before I am fully awake. The water tastes like the moment before regret.

I am not a Weeper. Not yet. My skin is still too dark, my eyes still too quick. But the stone is patient. It has begun its slow work on me.

Each dawn I perform the only task I am permitted: I follow the Last Sentence.

She never speaks to me. She does not appear to notice I exist. Yet every morning when she begins her long, drifting ascent toward the forbidden shelves, I rise and trail twelve precise paces behind her, barefoot, the way a drop of water follows the one before it.

We walk for hours. The only sound is the music of falling water and the faint wet slap of her feet on stone. Sometimes she stops for so long that moss has time to darken the hem of her robe before she moves again. I wait with her. The silence crawls into my mouth and grows roots.

At the edge of the Ninth Shelf, where no one is allowed to build, there is a single fracture in the limestone that points like a pale finger toward the humid abyss. In that fracture she plants the dead seed. Every year the same seed. Every year it has failed.

I have watched her do this thirteen times now.

After she leaves, I remain. This is my deviation. The one disobedience the karst has allowed me, perhaps because it finds it amusing.

I speak to the seed.

Not with words—words are dangerous here. I speak with the shape of my waiting. I crouch beside the crack and I let my unfinished thoughts drip from me like the condensation that beads on my eyelashes. I tell the seed every sentence I once meant to finish. I confess the letter I never ended. I describe the color of my mother’s hair the day she died. I explain why I ran away from every place that ever felt like home.

The seed never sprouts.

But something else happens.

Over the months between plantings, tiny threads of clear travertine have begun to grow from the crack, reaching toward the dead seed like fragile fingers. They look like hope frozen mid-gesture. The Weepers pretend not to see them. I come every few days to add one more unfinished sentence to the small, glistening monument.

I am becoming the place where almost happens.

At night I sleep leaning against the outer wall of the oldest Weeping House, the one that contains the mountain of dead seeds. The stone reads me. It drinks my dreams the way the moss drinks mist. My dreams have changed. Now the single falling droplet has my face. It falls for a thousand years, never landing, and it is weeping with relief.

My left arm has its first pale vein. I opened it myself on the anniversary of the day I arrived, using the small limestone knife they left on my threshold without explanation. The cut did not hurt. It felt like remembering something I had forgotten before I was born.

I no longer know how long a thought should take.

Yesterday I tried to finish carving my initials into a wooden beam above my door—the way travelers do. After six strokes I understood I was committing a kind of murder. I set the chisel down and walked away. The beam is still waiting, half-written. I visit it sometimes and feel tenderly toward its incompleteness.

The Last Sentence has begun to leave the dead seed on the stone beside the crack instead of carrying it back in her mouth. I believe this is for me. A kind of conversation made of absences.

I think she is tired.

I think the karst is considering letting something finish, and it is terrified.

And I—I who was once a man with plans and destinations—have become the Thirteenth Droplet, the one that trembles longest on the lip of the overhang before deciding whether to fall.

Some nights, when the weeping of the world grows almost loud enough to be called rain, I stand inside the Weeping House with the others. Our bodies make one continuous silhouette against the living rock. Warm tears that may or may not be mine slide down my cheeks and join the greater wetness.

I no longer know if I am grieving the world I left or the world this one almost became.

It does not matter.

The distinction would require an ending.

And here, nothing is ever allowed to finish becoming something else.

Not even me.

Especially not me.
