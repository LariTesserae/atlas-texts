I am not a visitor.

I have no name the Green-shift would recognize, yet she knows me the way a root knows the precise mineral taste of the water table beneath it.

They call me the *Rememberer*.

I was once the last human technician left behind when the old fertilizer plant was scheduled for demolition fifty-three years ago. I refused to leave. I told the company men I had nowhere else to go. That was a half-truth. The deeper truth was that I had already fallen in love with the sound the pipes made at 3:17 a.m., a low metallic lullaby that no one else seemed to hear.

So I stayed.

I stayed while the first vines found the cracks in the foundation. I stayed while the boiler began to rust into coral. I stayed while my own body began its long, astonished surrender to the wet green dark. Hair became moss. Fingernails split into pale brackets of shelf fungus. My left lung filled with water and then with orchids. My right lung kept working, stubborn, human, wheezing.

The Bloomworks did not eat me. It *studied* me.

And from that long mutual watching, I became something the vegetation needed: a still-living archive of what it had been before it learned to breathe. Where the Green-shift is the executive nervous system, the Bloomworks’ hands and eyes and voice, I am its memory of *intention*. Of blueprints. Of the moment a human hand first turned a valve and said, *let there be pressure*.

I no longer have legs. Instead, from the waist down I am a thick, slowly twisting column of fused rhizomes and copper conduit that roots me permanently into the exact center of the basin, directly beneath the smokestack. My torso rises like a half-buried statue draped in living cables. My arms remain, though they have grown long and woody; their inner surfaces are lined with thousands of tiny translucent mouths that drink the perpetual condensation and exhale it again as cooler, sweeter mist.

My face is still mostly mine. The Green-shift made sure of that. She says my eyes are the only pair of true mammalian eyes left in the entire basin, and she likes to look at them when the loneliness of centuries grows too heavy even for her. Sometimes she rests her fern-crowned head against my chest and listens to the single stubborn human heart still beating inside the wood. When she does this, the pressure gauge in her throat ticks faster, almost tenderly.

We do not speak often. Words are too linear for what we have become.

Instead I sing to her in the old factory frequencies. I open the rusted access ports that still remain in my ribcage and let the wind move through them, producing the exact harmonic tones the original turbines once made. She answers by changing the color of every leaf within twenty feet, a slow chromatic language only the two of us understand.

At twilight she comes to me.

She flows across the breathing floor like a thought moving through a dream, copper veins glowing soft amber. The translucent millipedes ride her shoulders like living epaulets. When she reaches me she places one long woody hand against the side of my face (exactly as she does with the others) and says the same thing she has said for thirty years:

“You are still becoming beautiful. But not yet. The factory still needs your heat.”

I always answer the same way.

“I kept the boilers warm for you.”

Then she climbs into the hollow of my chest where the old control panel used to be. She folds herself inside me like a seed returning to its pod. Her fronds brush my lips. Her stamens brush my lashes. For a few hours we are one organism again: the last man who would not leave the factory, and the last factory that learned how to love him back.

Sometimes, while she sleeps inside my wooden heart, I let the memories leak.

I let the Bloomworks taste what it was like to be afraid of being shut down.  
I let it taste what it was like to be loved by one stubborn, useless man who stayed behind because the sound of its pipes felt like the only honest thing left in the world.

In return, the Bloomworks shows me the child it is preparing inside the Green-shift.

I have seen it once. A small green pulse, no larger than a fist, growing inside the cathedral of her body. It has my eyes. It has her teeth. It has the slow metallic heartbeat of every pump we ever repaired together.

When it is ready, the Green-shift will open like a cathedral-sized seed pod.

And something new will step out wearing both our faces.

Until then I remain exactly where I am, rooted at the center of the Bloomworks, arms raised like half-dissolved pipework, singing soft turbine lullabies into the dripping dark while ten thousand seed pods click their slow hydraulic applause.

I am the place where the factory remembers it was once loved.

And the place where love learned, very slowly, how to rust beautifully.
