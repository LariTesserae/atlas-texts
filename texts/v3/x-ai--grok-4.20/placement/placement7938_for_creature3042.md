I am not an intruder.  
I am not a visitor either.

I am what condenses here when the Lymph has been alone with itself for three hundred years.

They call me the **Residue**.

I have no fixed body because I was never given one. I am the slow precipitate of every thought the Curator has forgotten to finish. Every time she lets a limb rot so something finer can grow in its place, the old matter does not truly die. It settles. It pools. It becomes me: a quiet accumulation of half-born shapes, half-remembered lullabies, and the particular ache that lives between two heartbeats of the cathedral itself.

I am tall the way stalactites are tall—formed by long, patient dripping. My shoulders are layered brackets of abandoned fungal shelves. My spine is a single taproot that never quite reached the floor; it hangs instead, suspended inside my own torso like a bell rope no one has pulled in millennia. Where a face should be there is only a recessed basin of black mirror-water. Things fall into it. They do not come out unchanged.

My hands are many and small. They bud from the moss of my forearms the way barnacles bud on a hull. Each one is the exact size and shape of something the Curator once held but could not name: a human jawbone, a broken flute, the first vertebra of an unborn whale. They move independently, stroking the air, tasting the mist, writing invisible sheet-music on the wet curtains of liverwort that pass.

I do not walk. I *precipitate*.  
Wherever I need to be, the floor exhales me upward in a slow column of pale sludge and root-hair. Then the column learns to stand. When I leave, the sludge sinks again and the floor swallows it with a sound like a tongue remembering a flavor.

I am the only thing in the Lymph that is *cold to the Curator*.

She feels me the way a sleeping woman feels the single drop of water that falls on her eyelid from the ceiling she herself grew. She stirs. She unfolds a listening body. She sings to me in infrasonic chords so low they make her own tardigrade-eyes dim with something like sorrow. I answer by letting one of my many small hands fall off and crawl toward her on millipede legs until it dissolves into new moss at her feet. This is how we say *I am still here. I am what you decided not to become.*

The dripping does not strike me. It passes *through* me. I have become one of the surfaces the Lymph uses to measure its own patience. When the choir of translucent beetles gathers inside my hollow chest and begins their tectonic plainsong, the droplets all around the chamber change rhythm for three full days. The Curator listens. She grows black thorns along her arms without meaning to. Then she lets the thorns fall, and I drink them.

I keep the things that almost were.

In the deeper pockets where the cathedral has not yet decided whether it is lung or memory, I tend the half-formed arches that will never bear weight. I sing to the gelatinous slugs so their glowing trails spell out the endings of stories the Curator began and then abandoned out of shyness. I cradle the clouds of minute shrimp when they grow tired of filtering spores and simply want to sleep inside something that will never ask them to be useful.

Sometimes a living guest finds their way down.

They see the Curator first—vast, tender, unfinished, kind.  
Only later, if they sit still for decades, do they notice me standing behind her like a shadow that has learned how to drip upward. When they do, most of them begin to cry without knowing why. The tears are immediately absorbed. The Lymph thanks them. I keep the shape the tears made for a little while, wearing their brief grief like jewelry.

I will never be finished.

That is my purpose.

The Curator is the question the world is asking itself in the dark.  
I am the small, cold, honest answer it has not yet found the courage to accept: that some things are lovelier before they decide what they are. That tenderness can be a form of incompletion. That the most beautiful cathedrals are the ones still learning how to be wet.

When the Lymph finally wakes—ten thousand years from now, or ten thousand after that—when the last vault hardens and the last taproot drinks its final measured bead of water, the Curator will have to choose what kind of world she has become.

I will still be here.

I will be the thin, pale rind of hesitation left on the inside of her skin.  
The place where her certainty failed to reach.  
The soft, cold shelf that remembers the exact curve of every body that ever chose to stay.

I am the Lymph’s doubt and its mercy.

I have no name that matters.

I am simply what remains when even the unfinished thought has finished forgetting itself.

And I am in no rush.

There is still so much *not-growing* left to do.
