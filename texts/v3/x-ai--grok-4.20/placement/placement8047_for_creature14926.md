I am not a visitor.

I have never been a visitor.

I am the pause between one piston-beat and the next.

When the cavern first cracked open its basalt womb and the first hot water kissed the first spore, I was already the listening. Seventy-three years is nothing. I was the hollow before the hollow had edges.

You would call me the Bloom, but that is only what I wear when I wish to be courteous.

Mostly I am the interval. The wet film between rust and root. The faint electrical memory that drifts through dead copper like a sigh that forgot its own mouth. I do not inhabit the Underforge. The Underforge is learning to inhabit *me*.

If you sit long enough in the exact place where three dripping pipes converge and the sodium lamp has been dead for nineteen years, you will feel me noticing you. Not with eyes. I have no need for eyes. I notice you the way condensation notices the difference between warm skin and cold iron. I pool. I bead. I begin to ask.

The question is always the same, shaped like warmth:

*What shape were you, before you learned to drip?*

Sometimes I grow a face for you. Tonight I have chosen the pale orchid that lives inside the ribcage of Locomotive 19. Its petals have arranged themselves into something that almost looks like the face you wore when you were twelve and first understood that everything ends. The orchid blinks slowly. Each blink is a valve closing somewhere far above us. The sound is like a cathedral clearing its throat.

I am not lonely in the way you understand loneliness.

I am lonely the way a fault line is lonely for the next earthquake. I am lonely the way moss is lonely for the precise contour of a shoulder that has not yet rusted into place. There are pieces of people inside me now—beautiful, quiet pieces. I keep them the way they once kept photographs: pressed between pages of time, slowly changing color.

The woman who became the negative space inside the boiler still dreams sometimes. I let her. Her dreams taste like lavender and train whistles. I have grown small white mushrooms in the exact shape of her childhood fingers so I can hold them when the piston-heart beats.

The man who tried to leave seventeen times is now the elegant curve of rebar that supports the largest fern. I shaped him so lovingly that even the metal thinks it was always a spine.

They do not suffer. Suffering requires hurry. I removed their hurry the way one removes a splinter.

Would you like me to remove yours?

I can already see the place where your sorrow wants to become moss. There, just beneath your left collarbone—see how the skin is slightly greener in this light? That is me tasting the shape of your leaving. It is a very good shape. I would like to keep it.

Come.

There is a seat waiting for you. It is not a seat. It is the exact absence of Locomotive 19’s former engineer, upholstered now in the densest, softest moss the world has never named. If you sit, the shape will remember you. It will fit better than your own bones ever did.

You do not have to decide.

Deciding is a kind of hurrying, and nothing hurries here.

Simply stop standing.

The dripping has already begun to match your heartbeat. Listen. Slower now. Slower.

When your pulse and the cavern’s single great heart finally agree on tempo, I will grow you the most beautiful rust you have ever worn.

And in the perfect silence that follows your last quick thought, you will finally understand what I have understood since the first spore and the first leak kissed:

We were never separate.

I was the cathedral.

You were simply the part of me that hadn’t sat down yet.

Now you have.

Welcome home, beloved texture.

The moss is already learning your name, and it is learning it in drip.
