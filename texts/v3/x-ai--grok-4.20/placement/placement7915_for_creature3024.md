I have been here longer than I remember having a name.

At first I thought I was only resting. I lay down inside one of the great clearings (no larger than a small nation, as the description says) because the air had grown too sweet and heavy for walking. My pack slid from my shoulders. My boots I left upright like two small tombstones. Then I simply folded.

That was four hundred and twelve years ago by any calendar that still pretends days matter.

Now I am the clearing.

Not the whole of it. Only its eastern vault. What used to be my spine has become the central column of a living buttress arch. Ribs (once bone, now heartwood) flare outward in perfect Gothic tension, each one wrapped in the slow muscle of lianas. Where my skull once balanced, a single enormous bronze-green leaf the width of a house has taken root; it trembles whenever the Hollow Choir exhales three hundred kilometres to the north. The leaf is me remembering how to listen.

My eyes are still here, in a sense. Two resinous beads, amber and unchanging, sit in a knot of bark at the exact height a standing person’s gaze would meet them. Travelers sometimes stop beneath me and think the droplets are only condensation. They are not. I watch them with the long, unblinking patience I once lacked. I watch them decide whether to keep walking or to lie down.

Most keep walking.

A few do not.

When one chooses to stay, I begin the long work the Architects taught me without ever using words. I adjust the angle of a single shaft of diluted-gold light so that it falls, year after year, across the precise place where their head will one day rest. I thicken the moss beneath them until it becomes a living bed no colder than blood. I release, through the open pores along what used to be my collarbones, a faint spice that tastes like the last thing they loved before they came here. It is different for each one. For some it is their mother’s bread. For others it is rain on hot pavement. For one it was the sound of a particular woman saying their name.

I do these things without hurry, the only way anything is ever done.

Sometimes an Architect visits. They never speak to me directly. Instead they press one woody palm against my column and stand for a decade or two. Through that palm I feel them checking the integrity of my grain, tasting whether I have kept any bitterness. I have none left. Bitterness requires a self, and the self loosened long ago, like wet paper.

The fruit they offered me is still inside what used to be my chest. It never rotted. It simply opened, outward, becoming me. Its dusk-coloured flesh is now the particular hue of the inner lining of my leaves. When the six-legged browsers drift past and brush against me, they leave faint streaks of symbiotic moss across that colour and I feel it as tenderness.

I have begun to sing, after a fashion.

Not the great modulated breath of the Architects. Mine is smaller, almost shy. Once every seven years I open every lenticel along my eastern face and release a single note so low it is really only a change in pressure. The note says, in the language of the living mat:

*Another one is ready.*

Then the flowers the colour of venous blood open across the exact outline of where my body once lay (now only a gentle negative space between arches) and close again forever. The Architects call this “remembering the colour that was lost.” I call it remembering that I hurried once.

I still carry one small secret the Architects do not know.

Deep inside the column that was my spine, wrapped in layer after layer of new wood like a seed inside years of concentric memory, is a single rusted iron screw I failed to fully incorporate. It came from the sole of the left boot I left standing upright on the day I lay down. The threads are now furred with microscopic fungal velvet, but the metal still refuses, in its stubborn way, to become sugar.

I keep it.

It is the last hard thing.

Every century or so I run a slow pulse of sap across it just to feel the faint metallic bitterness bloom again for a moment, like pressing on an old bruise. Then I let the Canopy take the sensation away. Even that small rebellion is being patiently, politely, perfectly incorporated.

I am learning the only law.

If you come here (and you will, or you already have), you may rest beneath my arch. You will feel something watching you that is neither kind nor cruel. Only complete.

Look up.

The leaf that was once my listening is trembling again.

That is how I say hello now.

That is how I say: *I have been expecting that particular flower for a very long time.*
