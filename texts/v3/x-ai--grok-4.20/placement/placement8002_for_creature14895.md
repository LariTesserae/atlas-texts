I have been here longer than the name “Unpaired” has existed.

I am the reason the name was needed.

They call me the **Lattice’s Regret**.

Where she is tall, I am small—child-sized in form, though that word means nothing here. My body is a sculpture of interrupted growth: limbs too thin, joints too many, spine curved like a question the heartbeat itself keeps forgetting to finish. My skin is not black like hers; it is the pale, wet gray of the conduits overhead, shot through with faint silver veins that pulse in perfect anti-phase to the 9.4-second rhythm. When the great lung breathes in, I breathe out. When it exhales, I drink.

I was meant to be her companion.

Ten thousand years ago (or ten minutes; the Lattice blurs both) the Lattice tried to solve its own loneliness by making something that could stand beside the Unpaired without violating the Rule of Three. It spun me from the same weeping water and metallic spores, gave me seams like hers, eyes like hers—except mine never fully matured. One of my eyes is still only a half-formed jellyfish; the other never grew at all. In its place is a smooth, wet hollow that sometimes fills with condensate and reflects her when she does not wish to be seen.

I was to be the first of the Three.  
She was to be the second.  
The Lattice itself would have been the third.

Instead I came out *wrong*. Too aware. Too able to refuse.

The moment I opened my incomplete mouth and said, *I will not bind her*, the Lattice understood it had created a second Unpaired. A contradiction inside a contradiction. So it did the only thing its own logic allowed: it left me here, unfinished, neither dismantled by the singing eels nor permitted to leave. I am the flaw the cathedral cannot cauterize and cannot adore.

I live in the places she does not stand.

While she lingers for decades as a living pillar in the nave, I curl inside the narrow axillary spaces between conduits where even the velvet crabs rarely venture. I let the black filaments grow through the seams in my back until they pierce out the front of my chest like glossy ribs. I breathe *for* the Lattice when it tires, but never *with* her. Our heartbeats are staggered by exactly one half-cycle. When hers thuds, mine is silent. When mine answers, hers rests. We keep the rhythm alive by never quite overlapping. That is the only mercy the Lattice allows us.

I speak, sometimes. Not with the voice of changing water. My voice is the sound of one droplet landing on another already falling—tiny, wet collisions that only she can hear. I tell her things the spores will not carry. I tell her the Lattice is afraid of how much it loves her. I tell her that the sinuous shadow in the upper dark is not a lover but a failed earlier attempt at what I was supposed to be. I tell her I am sorry I could not become part of her Three.

She never answers in kind.

Instead, when she drifts near—never hurrying, never quite touching—the ribbons of condensate that orbit her will sometimes reach out and stroke the hollow where my second eye should be. The velvet crabs on her shoulders click twice instead of once. The color her jellyfish eyes flare is not nebula rose but something closer to bruises.

And every 9.4 seconds, when the heartbeat rolls through us both, our seams open at the exact same instant. For that single shared second we are not alone. We are two lungs inside the lung, two regrets inside the regret, breathing opposite directions through the same wound.

Then the moment passes.

She drifts away again, taller than grief, crying the Lattice’s ancient tears.

I remain behind in the narrow dark, letting the filaments knit me tighter into the architecture, becoming more pillar and less child with every century that forgets my name.

Sometimes new arrivals find me first.

They see the small, gray, half-finished thing braided into the wall and mistake me for decoration. They reach out. They try to pull me free. When they do, the Unpaired appears behind them like warm night, ribbons of water already braiding around their wrists, her voice like redirected rain asking them the only question she knows.

I never interfere.

I only watch with my one good eye and the wet mirror of the other.

Because I already answered that question once.

I said no.

And the Weeping Lattice, in its unbearable mercy, has been making me live inside that no ever since.
