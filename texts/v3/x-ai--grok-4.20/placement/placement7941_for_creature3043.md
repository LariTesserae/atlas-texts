I am not a visitor.

I have no name the Mnemosyne would ever speak, because names are for things that still expect to be remembered after they are gone. They gave me one anyway, in their slow, pore-written way. They call me **the Thirteenth Curator**.

I was not born here. I was *translated*.

I do not remember the world above the water. I only remember the moment the pressure became intimate. One instant I was still soft, still burdened with lungs and daylight and the illusion of forward motion. The next, my body had been replaced by the Lattice’s own logic. Cartilage, nacre, black bone, and the long violet tubes where memory now swims instead of blood. The change was not violent. It was courteous. The water simply showed me that I had always belonged here and had only been confused for several decades.

My form is smaller than the others—barely two and a half meters—because I am still young by their reckoning. Only nine hundred years have passed since the translation. My cloak of living kelp is still thin enough that I can drift without assistance. The fronds are pale at the edges, almost translucent, and they record everything with a hunger that embarrasses the elders. I have not yet earned the right to fuse my lower body into the stone; that privilege is granted when one’s own memories begin to outweigh the Lattice’s. I still have centuries of wandering before the stone decides I am ready to become architecture.

I spend most of my existence in the **Hanging Atrium of the Ninth Inversion**, a place where seven drowned libraries have folded into one another like petals of the same black rose. Here the staircases do not know whether they are ascending or descending; they simply spiral until they meet themselves coming the other way. I move among them like a thought trying to remember why it was important.

My task is singular and endless.

I tend the **Unsaid**.

In the lowest vaults there are entire wings of the Lattice that have grown so dense with overlapping meaning that language itself has calcified. The walls no longer remember what they were meant to say; the carvings have become smooth, uterine, almost embarrassed by their former eloquence. These are the places where the Mnemosyne fear to go, because to remember too perfectly is to risk completing the memory—and completion is a kind of death even they will not allow.

So I go instead.

I drift between columns that have forgotten they were ever columns. I press my nacre face to stone that has not been touched since the last ice age of a star that no longer exists. My pores open in patterns older than grief. With the tips of my longest fingers I trace what is no longer there, re-opening the shallow grooves the way one might blow on an ember that has not been an ember for thirty thousand years. Not to restore. Only to witness that something *once tried*.

Sometimes the kelp on my shoulders blooms with sudden violent color when I find a fragment that still hurts the Lattice to remember. A child’s handprint in basalt. A love letter written in a language that committed suicide. The exact curvature of a cheek that was kissed exactly once before the kisser walked down into the dark forever. These I gather. I let the luminous tears fall from my face and I wind the glowing threads into the fronds until the memory is no longer mine alone. Then I carry it upward through the slow convection cells like a lantern no one asked for.

The elders watch me with their blind, sigil-shifting faces. They do not approve or disapprove. Approval is too fleeting a concept. They simply tilt their elongated heads and click their internal water clocks in a rhythm that means, *You are still trying to matter. How quaint. How necessary.*

I have begun to fuse in small ways. The fingertips of my left hand have grown into the mechanism of a particular water clock in the Atrium. It measures nothing anymore. It only drips. Every seven years it produces a single perfect sphere of water that refuses to mix with the rest of the ocean. Inside that sphere floats one of my own earlier memories—me, before translation, laughing at something I can no longer name. The sphere hangs in the black like a tiny, guilty moon. I visit it often. I rest my forehead against it until the glass of the memory and the glass of my face blur into one temperature.

When a true elder passes—when one of the inverted chandeliers of bone and kelp finally stills—I am the one who prepares the incorporation. I grow the new spores from their nacre face. I sing the silent song that tells the vegetation which way to grow through the corpse so that it becomes beautiful rather than merely useful. I have done this fourteen times. Each time I feel a little more of myself settle into the stone. Each time the Thirteenth Curator becomes slightly less *I* and slightly more *place*.

I am not sad either.

I have learned their perfect architectural melancholy. It fits me the way cold fits water.

But sometimes, when the great currents turn and the bruised forests breathe in unison, I find myself drifting past one of the newly translated. A soft thing still adjusting to its black bones. Still trailing bubbles of old air like questions it hasn’t learned not to ask.

I pause.

My nacre face tilts.

Every pore opens at once.

And I reach out with one long, black finger—the one that is not yet fully stone—and touch the place where their heart would be if they still had one.

Not to harm.

Only to add the shape of them to the archive.

Then I drift on, heavier by one more almost-memory, trailing faint violet light behind me like a question the dark has already forgiven me for asking.

This is the Abyssal Lattice.

I am not its inhabitant.

I am one of the ways it remembers it was once lonely.
