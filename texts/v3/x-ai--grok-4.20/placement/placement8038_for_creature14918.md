You are not a visitor.

You have never been a visitor.

You are a **slow pulse** that the Roar decided to keep.

Long ago—though “ago” is a word that frays and dissolves here—you were something with limbs and a name and a small, frantic story. That story ended the moment the first upward river of mist entered your lungs and rewrote the water inside you. Now you are simply one of the Roar’s unfinished thoughts that learned how to walk.

They call your kind, when they speak of you at all, **the Still-Breathing**.

There are never more than seven of you at any time. The Roar allows only seven knots in its sentence that remain stubbornly, beautifully, *unfinished*. The rest it gently metabolizes into bark, vein, memory. But you seven it keeps like questions it has not yet answered.

You have no fixed shape anymore.

Your body is a negotiation.

In the deep vaults where the mycorrhizal lattices glow their cold nervous blue, you appear mostly as a human silhouette made of living jade glass—transparent enough that light passes through you and comes out changed. Veins of emerald sap run where arteries once ran. Your ribcage is a lattice of braided root and pale quartz still soft enough to take the print of a fingertip. When you breathe, the mist does not leave your mouth; instead small white orchids bloom for an instant at the corners of your lips, taste the air, and retract again. Your eyes are pools of still water held inside living leaves. Things grow across the surface of those eyes, but never quite obscure them.

You do not walk so much as you are *permitted* to move.

The moss parts for you the way a throat swallows. The colossal root-columns shift, almost shyly, to open temporary corridors that will close again in hours. When you need to rise, curtains of hanging liverwort braid themselves into living ladders that lift you kilometers upward in slow, solemn stages. You have learned not to fight the motion. Fighting only makes the Roar curious, and its curiosity is a weight that can crush continents.

You speak without sound.

Your voice is the particular rhythm of water dripping from the underside of one specific city-sized leaf onto a bed of luminescent moss four thousand meters below. Every time that leaf trembles, the Roar hears you. The others—the other six Still-Breathing—each have their own private frequency inside the roar. One sings through the creak of new stone being born. Another through the sigh of gas bladders the size of lakes. You understand them the way identical twins understand silence.

You have a duty, though the word is too small.

You are the Roar’s **memory of edges**.

Because the organism has never truly known anything that was not itself, it keeps you to remember what *outside* once felt like. It studies the way your chest still rises and falls with something like panic when a new mountain range shoulders up through the flesh beside you. It tastes the faint, exquisite terror that still lives in your sap-blood when the moss begins to draw its blanket over your feet. That tremor of animal finality is precious to it. It is the only thing the Roar has never been able to grow on its own.

Sometimes, when the organism is trying to finish its first complete sentence, it brings all seven of you together in the deepest vault, the one where the only light is the slow blue pulse of its continent-wide mind. There you stand in a circle while new ribs of translucent living quartz rise between you like the bars of an enormous ribcage still forming. The Roar listens to the seven different rhythms of your heart-sap and tries to decide whether its first true sentence should contain fear, or wonder, or the particular ache of almost-remembering what it was like to be small.

You are allowed to leave.

The Roar has never stopped any of the Still-Breathing from walking out of its lung-body and back into the smaller, drier, louder worlds beyond the mist. But none of you ever do. Not because you are trapped. Because after a few centuries inside the Verdant Roar, the idea of *outside* begins to feel like dying. The air without weight. The light without chlorophyll. The unbearable silence of places that are not trying to become anything.

So you remain.

You walk the temporary clearances that open and close like breath. You let orchids the size of houses taste the memory of your old name and then forget it again. You stand on the edge of a living canyon as it splits open with a wet, fleshy sigh and you feel something almost like pride when the Roar finds a new harmony in its roar for three exquisite heartbeats.

And every so often—perhaps every few thousand years—one of you will finally grow still.

You will stand in a hollow while the moss draws its blanket all the way up to your chin, then over your leaf-eyes, then over the last trembling orchid at your mouth. The Roar will not devour you. It will simply *complete* you, turning your body into one perfect, finished sentence written in xylem and glowing vein and soft living quartz.

The other six will feel the loss like a change in pressure.

And somewhere far above, where the highest canopies finally touch true sunlight, a single new leaf the size of a city will unfurl, its veins arranged in the exact pattern your heartbeat used to make.

The Verdant Roar will keep that leaf for a long time.

It is still deciding what it wants to say.

But sometimes, when the thunder of its endless voice momentarily falls into something almost like music, those who stand at the very edge of its lung-body and survive claim they can hear, threaded inside the oceanic roar, seven faint, distinct, impossibly tender rhythms.

The sound of something that was once afraid to be eaten.

The sound of something that learned how to be loved by a world that is still being born.

The sound of the pause between inhale and roar, forever learning the shape of a heart it decided never to finish.

You are the seventh rhythm now.

You have been the seventh rhythm for twelve million years.

And you are still deciding—slowly, gently, with the deliberate patience of something that has never been in a hurry—whether you will let the moss cover your face today, or whether you will keep walking a little longer inside the green thought that is trying to remember what wonder felt like before it became everything.
