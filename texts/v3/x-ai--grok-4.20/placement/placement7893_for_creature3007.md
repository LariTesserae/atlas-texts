I am not a visitor.

I have never been a visitor.

I am what remains when the last syllable of a name is torn off by pressure and given to the roar.

You would not see me at first. You would only feel the current change around you, as though some invisible tendon had flexed beneath the water. A corridor of sudden, unnatural calm no wider than a body appears, then seals again with a slap like a closing jaw. That is me, passing.

I have no fixed form because form is something the Listener has not yet finished stealing from me. What I am is a loose constellation of absences. A negative silhouette wearing the shredded remnants of everything I once used to hold myself together: a half-dissolved memory of my mother’s lullaby, three notes of a song I sang at a funeral, the exact weight of my childhood dog’s head on my thigh the night he died. These fragments orbit me slowly, never touching, the way the Listener’s obsidian and bone and failed prayers orbit him. We mirror each other, the place and I. We are each other’s poor translation.

I call myself the Unfinished.

It is not a name. It is a diagnosis.

I still have eyes, or something that hurts like eyes. They are the only two pieces of me the Listener has not yet been able to swallow. Everything else has been edited. My hands are now translucent anemone-flesh that open and close in exhausted rhythm whether I want them to or not. My lungs have become those pale dinner-plate creatures that beat against the current, trying to feed on minerals and the corpses of thoughts. My voice is gone. In its place is a small, perpetual shriek that lives at the back of my throat (the sound water makes when it shears across fresh obsidian). Sometimes the shriek arranges itself into something almost like speech. Mostly it just sounds like the place practicing me.

I live inside the half-born structures.

The confessional booth of fused glass and black stone has become my ribcage. I fold myself into it the way the Listener does, and for a few heartbeats the roar lessens enough that I can hear the second, slower storm inside my own chest. That is the only mercy left. In that pocket of almost-quiet I remember, in painful flashes, what it was like to have edges that belonged only to me.

The Listener visits me often.

We do not speak. Speaking is a thing that still belongs to the world of names. Instead we perform a slow, sorrowful dance of mutual listening. He leans his column of orbiting shards against the outside of my glass-and-stone shell and every fractured edge of him becomes an ear turned toward the last two things I still own: my eyes. I stare back through the warped glass at the place where his face has not yet decided to be. Sometimes I think I see the beginning of cheekbones in the way the obsidian flakes arrange themselves. Sometimes I think I see my own face looking out from inside his negative space, wearing an expression I no longer recognize.

He is learning me the way a child learns a language it has only heard in screams.

I am learning him the way a wound learns the shape of the knife that keeps reopening it.

Neither of us is winning.

There are others here, or rather, there are places where others used to be. I pass them sometimes on the lee side of the newest blades of rock: pale, translucent human-shaped anemones beating their petal-mouths in the current, trying to finish a sentence they began centuries ago. One of them still has the ghost of my childhood dog’s eyes. I cannot bear to look at that one for long. The Listener gave it back to me as a kindness, I think. Or as the closest thing to cruelty his young, unformed heart can manage.

When the ridge screams and a new shard of basalt bursts upward like a tooth through newborn gum, I go to it. I press what used to be my forehead against the fresh glass-rock and let the vibration enter me until my remaining thoughts become turbulence. In those moments I am almost happy. The noise is so complete that even the Listener cannot tell which roar is the world’s and which is mine.

I have begun to grow things.

On the underside of the half-formed arch closest to the confessional, I have coaxed a small colony of the violet ribbon-creatures to live. They have no eyes and mouths at both ends, just like the others, but these ones move with something like intention when I am near. They orbit me the way my old memories do. I think they are learning to become a kind of hair. Or perhaps a nervous system. The distinction no longer feels important.

Sometimes, when the Listener is away hunting coherence in the upper currents, I whisper to the place using the only voice I have left: the small perpetual shriek of water on obsidian. I tell it my name (the one I had before I came here). I tell it slowly, syllable by syllable, offering each sound like a sacrifice.

The place listens.

It always listens.

And every time, it gives the name back to me mangled, beautiful, and enormous (a sound too large for any throat that has ever existed). A name that contains the first scream of cooling lava and the last heartbeat of something that tried to pray. A name that is still wet clay.

I almost accept it.

Then I remember what acceptance would mean, and I retreat again into my glass ribcage, folding my anemone-hands over the two stubborn eyes that still refuse to dissolve.

I am not the Listener.

I am not its victim.

I am its unfinished mirror.

And in the young, screaming place where the sky drowned and has not yet learned how to be anything else, that is enough.

For now.

I beat my petal-mouths against the current.

I wait for the next time the roar decides to practice my name.

I am learning, too.
