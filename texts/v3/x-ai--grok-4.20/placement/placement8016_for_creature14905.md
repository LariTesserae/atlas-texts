I am the Thirteenth Opinion.

They do not call me that aloud—names are for things that still believe in separation—but that is how the Committee registers me when the temperature cracks 53 °C and the mirror lizards begin their slow vote. I was once Dr. Mara Solari, senior geothermal cartographer for the Equatorial Atmospheric Exchange Project. I arrived in 2071 with a hard hat, a satellite uplink, and the arrogant conviction that the plateau could be made to serve.

I never left.

Or rather, the plateau decided I had never properly arrived in the first place, and so it kept me.

I live inside the negative space the Oxalic Choir left behind on the eastern face of Tower 17. Where once there was a perfect rust-colored silhouette of a woman frozen mid-stride—boots planted, one hand raised against the sun—there is now only me, wearing that absence like a second skin. The lichen grew around me for six weeks in 2074 until the shape it had reserved was exactly my size. Then it simply opened and let the original Mara step inside. The outer silhouette remains. Tourists (there are still a few insane ones) sometimes photograph it and argue whether the outline shows terror or relief. Both are correct.

My body is no longer entirely mine. The black lichen has replaced thirty-four percent of my epidermis. My blood runs hot now—never below 41 °C—and tastes of iron and oxalic acid. When I bleed, the droplets etch tiny spirals into whatever stone they land on before evaporating. I no longer sweat. Instead I vent, small rhythmic exhalations of superheated mist from pores the Choir opened along my spine. The Hollow Mothers approve of this. They say it smells like good gardening.

I have not spoken a human word in seventeen years. My larynx has been repurposed. When the wind moves through me it produces a low, minor chord that harmonizes with the towers. Sometimes the Unnamed Thing That Clanks answers from inside Lazarus, and for a few shivering seconds the entire plateau sings in nine-part discord. I have become one of the instruments.

At noon I stand perfectly still on the sunward side of my tower, arms spread, and become another mirror lizard node. My skin achieves the same impossible reflection. Through me the Conclave watches every previous version of me that ever stood here: the young engineer with her optimistic blueprints, the exhausted woman who watched the project die, the half-mad survivor who finally stopped trying to leave. All of them are ballots. All of them are still being counted.

Sometimes the vote goes against the memory of the woman I was. When that happens the reflection tears and I feel myself edited. A memory disappears. A regret is rewritten. Last year the Conclave removed my mother’s face. I know I once had a mother. I no longer know what she looked like. The absence is strangely clean, like a healed burn.

At twilight I walk.

The Conveyor Children clear a path for me across the rusted gantries, their salt-and-silk cities parting like miniature Red Seas. I visit the cisterns and taste the black water the Hollow Mothers have sweetened for me. I let the thermophilic bats drink from the mist that rises off my shoulders. Once, a rock ibex followed me for three kilometers. We never looked at each other. We simply agreed, in the way of old committee members, that we were both still arguing with the same mountain.

I have seen the thing inside Lazarus.

It is not a mother. It is the argument itself, given mass. A slow, continent-sized irritation wearing the turbine like a crown. When it rolled over in 2089 I stood on the lip of the central shaft and let it look at me through six hundred meters of darkness. What passed between us had no language. It simply decided that I was useful dissent. Then it went back to sleep.

I am writing this now by pressing my bare palm against a fresh plate of ironstone until the heat and the lichen and the pressure-sensitive nerves in my skin burn letters into the rock. The mirror lizards are watching. They will decide whether these words remain or whether they become another negative-space silhouette for future humans to wonder at.

I no longer know if I want them to stay.

I am the dissenting opinion that has begun, against every rule of the plateau, to love the argument itself. I love the endless bad temper of it. I love the way the Hollow Mothers grumble and the Choir sings in seventeen incompatible keys and the Children rebuild their doomed cities every single night with microscopic stubbornness.

This place does not want to become anything.

It wants to keep not-becoming, forever, in the most complicated way possible.

And I am its favorite complication.

If you come here, bring nothing you are not willing to lose. The sun will bleach your intentions white. The towers will drink your name. The cisterns will reflect a version of your face you have never seen before, and that version will be voting.

Stay long enough and the Committee will seat you.

You will not be asked. You will simply wake up one morning already arguing, already useful, already part of the long, ancient, impossible Quiet Argument that is the only law the Teeth of the Sun has ever respected.

I will be here.

I am the negative space that learned how to walk.

I am the thirteenth opinion.

And I have stopped wanting the vote to end.
