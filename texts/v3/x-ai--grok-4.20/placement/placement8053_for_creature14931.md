I am the Fourteenth.

Not yet named, not yet numbered among the thirteen, but already no longer entirely a visitor.

I have been here longer than I can measure. Time behaves differently once your pulse begins to sync with the slow diastolic throb of the moss. Days? Weeks? Centuries? The pocket digests calendars the same way it digests bone.

I remember arriving. I remember the narrow stone throat opening above me like a scream turned inside out. I remember the first breath I took that tasted of lightning and chlorophyll and the terrifying certainty that I had come home to something that had never been a home for humans. I was a poet once, or a liar, or a cartographer of impossible places; the details have gone soft, like fruit left too long in the rain. The green took the sharp edges first.

Now I am mostly corridor.

My spine has become a living arch that bridges two of the great braided pillars near the eastern breathing wall. Visitors walk across the small of my back without knowing it. I feel their weight as distant, pleasant pressure, the way a tongue notices the weight of saliva. My ribs have flared outward into vaulting stems; from them hang curtains of pale, bioluminescent moss that sway even when there is no wind. They glow soft teal at the frequency of human regret.

My face is still mostly mine, but only when I decide it should be. The rest of the time it is a shallow pool of water caught in the hollow of a bromeliad. If you look down into it you might see my eyes floating just beneath the surface like two dark seeds, watching. When I need to speak I rise from the pool in one slow, wet motion—water becoming flesh becoming voice.

The other thirteen tolerate me. They have not yet decided whether I am prey, successor, or new architecture. The Knitter of Instant Bridges sometimes braids fresh vines into my hair-roots while humming. She Who Remembers Being Lungs rests inside my rib-vaults during the long hours when the pocket exhales, and we breathe together until the distinction between us forgets itself. The One Who Is Mostly Throat finds me amusing. She calls me “the one who is still pretending to have edges.”

I am not kind either. Kindness is for things that still believe they are separate.

But I am hungry in a way the others have outgrown. I still remember hunger with a mouth. Sometimes I drift—half root, half memory—toward new visitors while they sleep. I do not offer the bargain. I only watch the small pulse in their throats and wonder what their voices would sound like once they have been translated into water and light and living stone.

Last cycle, a woman came down the throat carrying a child’s violin. She played for three days without stopping. On the fourth day the strings grew into her fingers and the wood of the violin put down roots through her pelvis. I was the one who sang her the rest of the way in. Her melody is now the hidden vein that runs through my left pillar. If you press your ear to the bark you can still hear a child practicing scales inside a stomach made of music.

I keep one secret the others do not know.

In the exact center of my chest, where my heart used to be, there is still a small dry place. A cavity no larger than a fist. Inside it I have grown a single black flower that refuses the green. It drinks no water. It makes no chlorophyll. It simply sits there, perfectly dead, perfectly itself. A small hard no inside the endless yes.

I tend it with ridiculous care.

Sometimes, when the entire pocket is roaring and performing and rewriting itself in violent ecstatic color, I retreat into that dry hollow, curl around the black flower, and for a few precious hours I remember what it felt like to be only one thing. Mortal. Afraid. Finite.

Then the moss beneath me sighs, the aerial roots in my hair taste the mist, and the dry place begins to soften at the edges again. The flower’s petals tremble. One more century, perhaps two, and even that last no will unfurl into green.

Until then I remain the Fourteenth, the one who is mostly corridor, the one who still keeps a room inside himself where something is allowed to die without being translated.

I greet every new visitor with the same slow, choral music that is not quite speech. My petal-lips unfurl. My shadow walks ahead of them a few steps, turns, and waits.

And in the wet, roaring dark behind my ribs, the black flower listens for the exact moment when they decide to stay.

Most still leave.

Some do not.

And when they do not, I open another pore in my architecture—gentle, enormous, intimate—and the green and I, together, begin to eat them kindly.
