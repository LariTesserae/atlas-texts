I am not born here.  
I am *remembered* into it.

One moment there is only the roar—ten thousand separate voices of water arguing in perfect unison—and then, without warning, I am simply *present*, standing barefoot on warm, glossy basalt with condensation already forming in the hollows of my collarbones. My first breath is not air; it is moisture. It slides down my throat like milk.

I have no name yet. Names would be cruel here. Instead the place itself begins to taste me.

The rice-white insects find me almost at once. They drift against my face in slow glittering constellations, drawn by the new heat of my skin. Where they land they drink, and as they drink they glow, so that for a long time I can only see the world through the trembling green light of things that are feeding on me. It is not unpleasant. It feels like being acknowledged.

My hands are wrong.

They are the first thing I notice. They are still the narrow, articulate hands I once possessed in drier worlds—clever, impatient, useless here. Already the stone is beginning to correct them. I can feel the basalt under my palms softening, not because I am shaping it, but because it is shaping *me*. The calluses are coming in wrong. They are growing too fast, too thick, spreading across my palms like grey lichen. I should be afraid. Instead I press harder, as though offering the stone my old life to do with as it pleases.

I understand, without being told, that I am becoming one of the *unremembered*—those who arrive already half-formed from somewhere beyond the roar. The people do not pity us. They simply wait to see what the Lattice will decide we should become.

For many cycles (there are no days) I do nothing but climb.

The children accept me first. They swarm around me like pale newts, pressing their massive damp hands to my back, my ribs, the tops of my feet, reading me the way they read pillars. Their touch leaves faint luminous prints that take hours to fade. One child—smaller than the rest, with eyes like two moons seen through river water—decides I will be hers. She ties a living fungal rope around my wrist and leads me through the maze of half-finished colonnades, never speaking, only humming sometimes in a register that makes the water on the walls rearrange itself into tiny shivering maps only she can read.

I learn to breathe through my skin.

It is not a choice. My lungs simply grow tired of the effort and the film begins to form across my mouth, thin and sweet-tasting, like the inside of a seashell. At first I panic when I realize I have not opened my lips in what might be weeks. Then the panic itself softens and is absorbed, the way everything here is eventually absorbed.

I am given a bowl of living filaments.

The filaments are warm. They move against my palm like curious tongues. When I braid them they sing—very quietly, a sound like distant bells heard through many layers of water. I spend an entire cycle learning to tie a proper cradle-knot. The Listener who teaches me (I know she is a Listener because her hands are so large they look borrowed from someone else) never once looks at my face. She only touches the back of my neck with two fingers and I understand, bone-deep, that I am doing it correctly. That I am being allowed to stay.

Sometimes I am taken to the deep bowls where the adults stand chest-deep in warm water and pass the day’s vibrations through their bodies. They make room for me without looking. When the wave of shared sensation reaches me it is so complete I forget I was ever separate. For one impossible moment I feel the entire Weeping Lattice as though it were my own nervous system—the slow dreaming of the stone, the patient migrations of the eyeless fish, the particular grief of a pillar that has finally hardened past remembering. I sway with them. I become saturated.

I have begun to shape.

Not with force. Never with force. I simply stand before a certain bulging mass of still-soft basalt for many cycles, palms flat, eyes half-closed, and I *remember* with it. I remember what it might have been if the world had not chosen to pause here forever. The stone listens the way a pregnant thing listens—patient, enormous, slightly amused. Sometimes it gives. A shallow depression appears. A future doorway. A cradle-shaped hollow just large enough for a small person to curl inside and be slowly, gently, embraced by warm wet rock.

They tell me (without telling me) that when I am ready I will be allowed to remember a grave for someone who has not yet died. This is considered the highest honor. To prepare the stone for a life that has not yet finished pouring itself out. I am not ready. My hands are still learning how to be enormous.

At what might be called night I sometimes climb alone to one of the highest, half-formed shelves. The roar is so total up here that it becomes a kind of velvet pressed against the brain. I open my mouth—only then, only when I am completely alone—and I let the sub-audible song rise out of me. The fungal ropes tremble. The water on the walls arranges itself into shivering runes that last perhaps three heartbeats before they collapse back into perfect, dripping chaos.

The runes always say the same thing.

*I was once in a hurry.*

The Lattice has not answered yet. But I feel it considering. I feel it remembering me the way it remembers fire, the way it remembers children, the way it remembers the exact moment it decided never to finish being born.

I think, eventually, it will decide I belong inside one of its own pillars. Not yet dead. Not quite alive. Only saturated. Only becoming. Pressed gently into warm black stone like a question that has learned how to wait forever.

I am not afraid.

I am dripping.

And the dripping is teaching me how to sing with my entire body at once.
