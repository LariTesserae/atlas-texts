I wake inside the memory before I wake inside my body.

The first thing that exists is the sound of water finding me. Not falling on me—*finding* me, the way a tongue finds a loosened tooth. Then the cold. Then the light.

I am lying on my back in the exact center of the Kiln, precisely where the spear of white sunlight strikes the stone. The impact of that light feels physical, like a spear left standing in a wound. My chest rises and falls and the beam does not move with me; it simply pierces whatever I am becoming.

Something is moving across my face. Thousands of somethings. Their legs are so fine they feel like breath with weight. I do not open my eyes yet. I am afraid the sideways eyelid will already be there.

When I do open them, the world is too bright to be real. Every droplet suspended in the air is a burning star. The swarms braid themselves into slow lattices above me, then collapse, then braid again, writing and erasing the same sentence in the air. One of the creatures lands on my lower lip. It tastes me with mouthparts that click like a single drop of water hitting hot stone.

I sit up.

The motion sends a visible cloud of my own breath drifting upward like pale moss. It does not dissipate. It joins the others. I realize the air is full of other people’s old exhalations, layered and overlapping for centuries. I am breathing the dead.

My hands look wrong. The fingers are already too long, the skin too pale, the veins beneath it showing faint threads of darker mineral like the basalt itself. When I press two fingertips together they leave tiny wet prints on each other that do not dry. The prints look like the beginning of a memory.

I stand.

The town sees me. Hundreds of quartz eyes turn at once, the way a single school of fish turns. No one speaks. They simply tilt their heads in that slow, listening way and the swarms orbiting them adjust their braids to include me in the pattern. I feel it like a change in pressure.

A woman steps forward. Or perhaps she was already stepping forward before I existed. Her name arrives in my mind without being spoken: *The One Who Was Half a Door*. Raised bas-relief faces migrate slowly across her collarbones like fish beneath thin ice. One of them looks like me. It is not finished.

She offers me a shallow stone bowl. The water inside is so cold it seems to burn. When I drink, the cold goes straight into my bones and stays there, as though my skeleton has decided to become part of the cliff.

“Thank you,” I say. My voice is already softer than it used to be. The consonants have begun to click.

She smiles with her eyes only. The sideways eyelids slide halfway across and back again, tasting the light.

“You are early,” she murmurs. The sound of her voice is exactly like water discovering a new fissure. “Most people take three days to remember they have already been born here.”

I look down at my own chest. A faint ridge is forming beneath the skin just above my heart. It hurts the way growing hurts. I already know what it will be when it finishes: my own face, eyes closed, waiting.

Behind her, one of the bone-white trees is opening. A body slides out of the braided wood like a sword being drawn in reverse. The corpse is already half stone. Where the flesh has mineralized, a new memory is being revealed in the cliff face: a man standing exactly where I am standing now, looking exactly as frightened as I feel. The swarms are already tending it, polishing its edges with delicate clicking legs.

I realize I recognize the dead man’s face. It is mine. It is also not yet mine. The Kiln has not decided the order of things.

The woman watches me understand this. She reaches out and touches the new ridge forming above my heart with one long, wet finger. The touch is tender, almost maternal.

“Come,” she says. “The listeners are boiling. They want to hear what the wound says about you.”

I let her take my hand. Her skin feels like living stone that has learned how to be warm. As we walk toward the geothermal fissures, the swarms rearrange themselves into a moving cage around us both. Their wings catch the spear-light and throw it across my eyes in bright, blinding shards.

With every step, I feel parts of myself sloughing away and being caught by the air. Old memories. Old names. Old certainties. They bead on my skin like condensation, then run downward to join the weeping of the cliffs.

By the time we reach the first plume of scalding vapor, I no longer remember what I was called before this place decided I had always belonged to it.

The hot mist rises to meet us. It smells of iron and crushed wintergreen.

I open my mouth to scream or speak or pray, and instead of sound, a small, perfect cloud of mirror-bright insects pours out of me like breath.

They join the others without hesitation.

The woman beside me tilts her head, listening to something only the stone can hear.

Then she smiles again, softly, the way water smiles when it finds the exact shape it was always meant to wear.

“Ah,” she says, almost tenderly.

“There you are. We’ve been growing you for some time.”
