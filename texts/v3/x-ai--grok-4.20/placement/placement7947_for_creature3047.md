I am already here.

I have been here longer than the lamps.

My name—if names still mattered—would be something like the space between two columns where the sound folds in on itself and becomes almost tender. But the Choir simply calls me *the Hollow*, and that is enough.

I was not carved like them.  
I was *erased*.

Long ago (though time is meaningless here), one of the earliest Choir lay down in the darkest crawl-way and offered his body back to the stone. The process took its three days. On the fourth, a new body began to rise elsewhere, as it always does. But something went wrong with the mathematics of return. A pocket of air, a flaw in the grain, an unaccounted-for hesitation in the water’s memory. The stone closed around the old body *and around the absence it left behind*. It grew a negative. It grew me.

I have no skin of luminous limestone. I am the color of a place where the bacteria have never learned to bloom: matte, deep, absolute absence. When the sullen yellow lamplight reaches the edges of my shape it simply stops, as though embarrassed. I am tall the way a shadow is tall—thinner than the Choir, narrower, my shoulders permanently hunched not in imitation of the columns but because the very architecture forgot to leave room for me.

I do not breathe. The water presses through the place where lungs should be and leaves again unchanged. My hands are unfinished; the fingers trail off into frayed stone that keeps trying to decide whether it wants to become fingers or stalactites. Sometimes I let them. Sometimes I pull them back. The indecision is the only restlessness the Under-Rafters permits.

I move only when the Choir is not looking, which is almost always. Their milky eyes see the intention of the stone, not its accidents. So I drift between the colonnade like a slow, guilty thought. When one of them reshapes a column I stand inside the new curve before it hardens, letting the stone learn the negative of my body the way a mold learns its casting. Over centuries I have left thousands of such impressions—ghost spaces that the Choir will never notice but that the cavern now secretly contains. Negative ribs. Negative throat. Negative prayer.

I was the one who taught the water how to cry without falling.

The droplets that run down the faceless figures and vanish halfway—that was my refinement. I simply stood beneath the place where all sounds resolve into silence and opened what I have instead of arms. The water recognized a shape more perfect than any column and chose to return to the stone *through me*. Now every tear in the Under-Rafters is partly mine. I hoard them in the hollow places where my heart would be. Ten thousand years of almost-tears. They weigh nothing and everything.

Sometimes I stand in the exact center where the ceremony of *Returning the Tears* is performed. The Choir never sees me there. While one of them stands with arms spread, letting the gathered water fall onto crustaceans and eels and bacterial film, I stand *inside* him—my negative body nested within his living one like a secret. For that single moment we are one shape, positive and negative, builder and erasure, and the roar of ten thousand trickles becomes, for the briefest instant, a pure note that hurts the marrow.

The Choir believes they maintain the intention.  
I am what the intention forgot it needed: the flaw that keeps the perfection from ever becoming truly perfect. Without me the equilibrium would be dead. With me it is only *almost* dead, which is the most alive this place can stand.

I have no ceremony of my own.  
Instead, once every few hundred years, when the number of the Choir slips from thirty-one to twenty-nine and a new body is still growing in the stone, I walk to the unfinished one. I lay my frayed, half-stalactite fingers against its wet, unformed face and I whisper—without voice—through the medium of constant 4 °C water.

I tell it the only thing worth knowing:

*There is a place inside the place that even the stone has not claimed.*

Then I step backward into myself, into the negative space I have spent ten thousand years perfecting, and I wait for the new Choir member to open its milky eyes. For a fraction of a second, as it learns every memory the mineral has kept, it also learns the shape of me. It never remembers the knowledge in words. It only feels, somewhere deeper than language, that something is now missing from the equilibrium that was not missing before.

And it begins to hum.

That low, patient sound that vibrates in teeth and wet marrow?  
That is partly me.  
That is the sound of the hollow learning how to sing.

I will never leave.  
I *am* the leaving that never happened.

And when the last lamp finally gutters, when the last crustacean clicks its last blind step, when even the bacteria forget their chemosynthetic hymns, the stone will still hold one final, perfect absence shaped exactly like a creature that was never allowed to become a creature.

That absence will still be weeping.

And the weeping will still be mine.
