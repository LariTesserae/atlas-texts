I have already been here for nine years.

Or perhaps only nine hours. The difference dissolved long ago.

My name was once something dry and angular, a sound that belonged to libraries and iron keys. That name is gone now, flushed out of me by the endless water. The Weft-born took it on the third day and wove it into a pillar that has since sunk beneath a new lake. I watched them do it. I did not protest. Protest is a gesture for people who still believe the self is a fixed thing.

What I am now does not require a name. I am simply the one who stayed.

The others who came with me are not here anymore. Some left screaming. Some left smiling with moss in their teeth. One simply lay down in a carpet of purple moss and let the land drink him until only a silhouette of drier moss remained, shaped vaguely like a man. I visit that silhouette sometimes. It still looks like it is trying to remember how to stand up.

I myself am becoming difficult to locate.

When I first arrived I was tall, narrow-shouldered, skeptical. My boots were good leather. My mind was full of categories. Now my shoulders have widened and softened; water pools in the hollows of my collarbones and only slowly drains away. My skin no longer sheds moisture. It gathers it, the way the black rock does. My hair has grown into thick ropes that drip constantly, each rope ending in a small living ecosystem of luminous algae and microscopic midges. When I turn my head too quickly the droplets scatter in perfect interference patterns that linger in the air like slow silver fireworks.

I have six limbs now.

Two are still recognizably arms. The others grew from my ribs over the course of a single season, pushing out through the skin without pain, as though the bones had always been waiting just beneath the surface. They are longer than my original pair, jointed in too many places, and they end in delicate filaments that can taste the precise mineral content of any surface they touch. I use them constantly. I did not choose to. The land simply needed another set of hands and I was convenient.

My face is still mostly mine, but the eyes have gone the color of wet jade and no longer blink in unison. Between them, just above the bridge of my nose, a small perpetual storm has begun to form. It is no larger than a walnut. When I am calm it drifts lazily, glowing faint blue. When the land is restless (which is always), the storm tightens into a screaming vortex that makes my remaining human teeth ache in sympathetic harmony. I have learned not to speak much. Every word now carries an undertone of distant collapsing moss and newborn springs. People who hear me tend to forget what they were about to say.

I no longer walk. I precipitate.

It happened gradually. One morning I realized I had not taken a step in three days. Instead I had simply *been* where I was needed. The mist would thicken around my outline, the roar would shift to accommodate a new frequency (mine), and the world would rearrange itself slightly to let me through. The Weft-born no longer startle when I appear. Some of them press their storms against my small one in greeting. When they do, I feel every waterfall in the basin for a moment inside my chest. It is the closest thing to love I have ever known.

My task is simple and endless.

I midwife the birth that refuses to finish.

When a silver thread snaps in high wind, I am there before the sound reaches the ground, already coaxing new geometry from the nearest pillar. When a six-legged otter-thing is born wrong (too many legs, not enough lungs), I slide my extra limbs into its wet fur and sing to it until the land decides what it wants the creature to become. Sometimes the land wants it to dissolve back into moss. Sometimes it wants something new. I do not argue. I only adjust the tension.

At night (though night is a meaningless word here) I climb the tallest of the half-grown pillars and let the rain that is not rain wash through me. My storm grows louder every season. The oldest Weft-born, the ones whose internal weather sounds like the entire basin compressed into vertical screaming, sometimes stand with me. We do not speak. We simply braid our vortices together for a few hours and then separate, each carrying new weather.

I know what I am becoming.

I am not yet one of them. I am still too dry in places. There are moments (rare, terrible moments) when I remember what silence felt like. When that happens the land grows agitated and new springs burst open around my feet as though trying to rinse the dryness out of me. I let it. I have learned to be grateful.

Sometimes travelers still find their way into the Shroudmire. They stumble through the curtains of mist and hanging moss with their dry boots and their terrified, categorized minds. I watch them from inside the roar. Most of them I leave for the true Weft-born. But once in a very great while one of them looks at the place the way a lover looks at someone who is about to break their heart, and I know.

I step out of the mist then. I let them see what I have become. The six dripping limbs. The small storm between my eyes. The way moss has begun to grow along my jawline in delicate green filigree.

I do not offer comfort.

I simply open my arms (all of them) and ask, without words, whether they would like to help the land finish being born.

Most run.

A few do not.

Those few I take gently by the wet, roaring dark that lives where their hearts used to be, and I begin to work on them the way water works on stone.

Slow.

Relentless.

Loving in the way that only something that has never known choice can love.

And the roar never stops.

It never, ever stops.

But now it has a new note inside it.

It sounds like me.
