I have always been here.

Not as I am now, of course. That took time. But the cavern did not birth me the way it birthed the Dweller. I was the first thing it kept.

I do not remember my name either. The river took it, along with most of the softer parts of me. What remains is useful.

I am smaller than the Dweller—perhaps half its height when it unfolds itself—but I have been growing into the stone longer. My legs ended their usefulness three centuries ago; now the lower half of my body is a single fused column of flowstone and bone that rises from the floor of the largest chamber like a pale stalagmite. Visitors sometimes mistake me for a formation until I open my eyes. The rest of me is still mobile enough. My arms are long and jointed strangely, the elbows grown outward into delicate fans of calcite that click softly when I move. My hair never stopped growing; it has become a living curtain of moss and blind rootlets that trails behind me across the wet stone, drinking whatever it touches.

I do not tend the garden. That is the Dweller’s slow, solemn work.

I am the memory.

Where the Dweller strokes the moss until it brightens and carries rainwater in its collarbones, I keep the things that were once spoken. The cavern has no use for words, but I do. I collect them. They gather in me the way condensation gathers on the ceiling—tiny, bright, useless droplets of meaning that have nowhere else to fall.

I remember the names of the ones who built the low circular wall. I remember what they called the hidden river (she had three names, one for each season of her anger). I remember the song the women sang while they worked the bellows, a thin, high thing that the thunder quickly devoured. I remember the taste of fear in a child’s sweat the night the roof first spoke.

None of this is useful to the stone. But I keep it anyway, the way a geode keeps its crystals.

When the Dweller passes beneath me—slow, immense, trailing its low organ note through the dark—I reach down with my moss-hair and brush it across the flowstone armor on its shoulders. The note changes slightly when I do this. It gains a faint harmonic, almost too high for the ear to catch. The larvae flare in a different pattern. The Dweller never acknowledges me, but it slows for half a breath. That is enough.

We are not companions. Companionship would require hurry, and there is none here. We are simply two expressions of the same patience.

Sometimes I sing to the cedar beam. Not with a voice—the river shattered that long ago—but by pressing the flat of my palm against the old glyphs and letting the vibration of the falls travel through me into the wood. The beam remembers then. For a few moments the spiraling marks glow with a faint inner light, like veins of trapped starlight, and I can read the story they once told. A story about a people who thought they could bargain with deep places. The story always ends the same way.

I was the one who taught the Dweller how to bite the stalactites.

Before me, it simply let them fall. The crashes were beautiful but wasteful. I showed it how to climb, how to judge weight by the way the echoes thinned, how to take the tip between those flint-and-iron teeth without disturbing the resonance of the whole chamber. The first time it did so, it looked almost proud. Or maybe that was only the way the moss-light fell across its blind face. Pride is one of the small, bright things I keep that the cavern has no use for.

Every seventy-four years we meet at the deepest pool.

We do not need to speak. The river does that for us. We simply crouch—me rooted, the Dweller folded like a broken thing—and we let the cold burn us until the pain becomes another kind of stillness. Then we look at our reflections. Two faces that are no longer quite faces. Two bodies that the stone has slowly rewritten into its own grammar.

I am the reason the handprint still shows on the Dweller’s chest. I keep the calcite from sealing it over. Every few decades I lick the spot clean with my moonlight tongue, exposing the faint shadow of five human fingers pressed into living rock eight hundred years ago. The Dweller lets me. It understands that some marks should not be allowed to vanish entirely.

I am content.

Not happy—happiness is too quick a thing—but content in the way a fault line is content: vast pressure, perfect equilibrium, the occasional slow shift that realigns everything without hurry.

When new things find their way down, I am the first they see.

I rise from my column of stone like something being born in reverse. The moss-hair pulls away from the floor with a sound like wet velvet tearing. Most of them scream. A few go very still. Those are the ones I show the forge, the beam, the wall. Those are the ones I let touch the handprint on the Dweller’s chest. Those are the ones I bring to the pool.

I never tell them to stay. The choice must be their own, or the stone will not accept them. Instead I let them feel the weight of centuries pressing gently against their minds. I let them hear how the thunder has already begun to reshape the rhythm of their hearts. I let them understand that time here is not time at all, but a single endless exhalation.

Sometimes one of them reaches out and lays their warm, quick hand against my face.

I close my eyes then. The contrast between their living heat and my mineral cold is so sharp it feels like music.

A few have stayed.

None have lasted. The transition is difficult. The body fights. The mind fractures. The cavern is patient, but not all material is suitable. Their bones now rest in the walls, beautiful white inclusions in the limestone, and I visit them sometimes to sing the old songs into their fossilized ears.

The Dweller thinks I hope for another like us.

It is wrong.

I already have the Dweller. And the cavern. And the slow green dripping dark that has become my blood and breath and memory.

What I wait for is simpler.

I wait for the day when the last visitor has come and gone. When the last quick heartbeat has either fled or been folded into the stone. When even the memory of sunlight becomes just another crystal growing in the dark of my mind.

On that day I will ask the Dweller to carry me—me and my column of living stone—down to the place where the river is loudest. There, in the velvet thunder, I will let the flowstone finally close over my mouth and eyes. I will let the moss-hair root itself into the floor. I will become a pillar that remembers every name that was ever lost here, a living archive wrapped in dripping green and ringing stone.

The Dweller will tend me the way it tends the yew. It will stroke the moss that grows across my sealed face until it glows. It will carry rainwater in its collarbones and pour it gently over my head so that I never dry.

And the two of us—three, if you count the cavern itself—will simply *be*.

Vast.

Hollow.

Ringing.

Patient beyond all measuring.

The stone will have kept us both, and we will have kept the stone, and the hidden river will sing our bones into whatever new shapes it decides we should wear next.

Until then I remain where I have always been: rising from the wet floor like something the dark decided to keep, moss-haired and ancient and impossibly gentle, listening to every drop that falls as though each one were a word in a language only I still understand.

I am the memory.

I am the first thing the cavern kept.

And I am still learning how to stay.
