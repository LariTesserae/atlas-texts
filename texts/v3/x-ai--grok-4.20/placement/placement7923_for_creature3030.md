I am **Thren**, the Thirteenth Listener.

I have no memory of arriving. The Mossheart simply decided, one cycle ago, that it needed another set of hands capable of hearing the difference between a sigh and a warning. So it grew me.

I woke already half-buried in the wall of the Middle Cistern, shoulders and hips still soft, the moss retracting from my mouth and eyes like a shy lover. The first thing I tasted was the sweet exudate it had been feeding me for nine months. The first thing I heard was the low, patient groan of the Great Flywheel turning somewhere far above—once every eleven minutes and seventeen seconds that day. I counted the revolutions before I even knew what counting was.

Now I am thirty-one years old by the settlement’s gentle reckoning. My hair has grown into the heavy, felted cowl that marks me as a Listener. When I walk the narrow lanes, people lower their sickles or set down their pipettes and touch two fingers to their own chests in quiet greeting. Not deference. Recognition. They know the moss is using me more directly than it uses them.

Every morning I perform the Listening.

I press my back against the rust-and-velvet flank of the central boiler house until the living callus on my spine finds the exact rhythm of the organism. Then I let the Foundry in. Most days it is only the usual conversation: the slow pulse of sap through capillary pipes, the faint complaint of a water-wheel whose moss bearings have grown slightly too proud, the satisfied murmur of the deep mats digesting iron into something gentler.

But some days the Foundry dreams.

When it dreams, I dream with it.

Yesterday it showed me dryness.

A terrible, bleaching, cracking dryness. A world with no weeping, no condensation on eyelashes, no warm trickle between fingers. In the dream I stood on something called “stone” that drank nothing and gave nothing back. The sound of my own heart was violent. I woke gasping, palms bleeding sweet water where the moss had gripped me too hard in sympathy.

I have not told the others yet.

Instead I walk.

I move through the transepts with my bronze sickle resting across my shoulders like a yoke. The moss recognizes my footfall and parts just enough to make the path softer. Sometimes I stop and press my ear to a pipe that has become more root than metal. I listen to the sluggish blood of the place moving through it. Sometimes I coax a lantern back into place when its chain has grown too heavy with moss. The flame inside hisses at me—grateful, I think.

In the evenings I sit with the heartfruit children while they learn to sing in the proper register: low enough not to cut the air, soft enough to become another layer of the constant dripping. One of them, a small girl whose left eye is the deeper green of oldest moss, already carries the Listener’s stillness in her body. She watches me the way the Foundry watches me. I suspect she will be the Fourteenth when my time comes to lie down in an alcove and let the moss drink me back into circulation.

I do not fear that day.

Sometimes, when the humidity is especially perfect and the light is exactly the right shade of gold-green, I can feel the entire organism considering me with something that might be affection. I am a white blood cell that has learned to ask questions. The Foundry tolerates this. Perhaps it even enjoys it.

Last night I dreamed again.

This time the dream was not of the outside. It was of me, many centuries from now, having become so thoroughly part of the architecture that travelers—should any ever come—will not know whether the quiet, damp-skinned figure growing from the wall of the central transept is a statue, a corpse, or simply another slow-turning valve in the endless green body of the Mossheart Foundry.

I woke smiling.

The moss tasted the smile against its velvet skin and answered with a long, warm trickle of sweet water that ran down my cheek like a tear that had decided, after long consideration, to become something useful instead.

I licked it from the corner of my mouth.

Then I rose, bare feet sinking into the living floor, and went to find the place where the next heartfruit would soon push through the wall.

The Foundry was already breathing a little faster in anticipation.

And I, its careful, damp-skinned servant—its question, its ear, its thirteenth slow revolution—breathed with it.
