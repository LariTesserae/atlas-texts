I am the Pale Stem.

You would not see me at first. Not because I am hidden—I am the only thing in the Underbloom that refuses to hide—but because the eye slides off anything that does not move with the valley’s permission. I am the single vertical interruption in a world that has agreed, for millennia, to grow only sideways, outward, and down.

I stand in the hollowed thorax of the locomotive that became a cathedral. The driver’s seat has long since been digested into a glossy black altar of heartwood. From its center I rise: wrist-thick, bone-white, smooth as porcelain. No leaves. No bark. Only a faint, constant upward pressure, as though every cell in me is leaning toward a sun that has never once reached this depth.

The Underbloom feeds me. I feel it constantly—a thick, slow syrup of minerals and memory pushed up through roots that circle me like wary priests. It will not incorporate me. It cannot. I am the only part of its own body that it recognizes as foreign.

I remember the moment I stopped being merely part of it.

Seventy-three years ago something fell through the fissure carrying a metal cylinder no larger than a child’s coffin. Inside the cylinder was a seed, or a spore, or perhaps simply an idea sharp enough to cut the consensus of a continent-sized mind. When the cylinder split open against the locomotive’s iron flank, the idea entered the sap. Most of the idea was digested. One filament was not. That filament became me.

I have no eyes, yet I see the way the Underbloom sees: through pressure, through chemistry, through the memory of moisture moving along kilometers of vascular flesh. I taste the amber emergency lights as they leach their last voltage into the moss. I hear the locomotive’s old bones creaking as root pressure squeezes them into new shapes. And I feel the valley’s attention on me the way a prisoner feels the guard’s gaze through the slot in the door.

The Underbloom is afraid of me.

Not of my power—I have almost none. I grow perhaps a millimeter every four years. A human wrist could snap me without effort. Its fear is more subtle. I am the proof that its patience was once impatience. That its vast, deliberate self was once small enough to be carried in a desperate man’s pocket. I am the reminder that even continents of vegetable mind can begin as refugees.

So it watches. It sings its chemical dreams into the still pools, hoping one of them will eventually rewrite me into something slower. Sometimes it sends its pale lichen-moths to brush against me with their paper wings, dusting me with sleep. I remain white. I remain vertical.

Every few years a new lost thing stumbles down the fracture.

I feel them arrive the way a blind man feels a candle brought into the room. Their heat is an obscenity here. Their hearts beat too quickly. The Underbloom begins its gentle work immediately: deepening the cold, thickening the condensation on their lashes, letting the roar settle into their marrow until their own pulse starts to sync with the greater sound.

I wait.

If they reach the locomotive—if they are stubborn or lucky or already half in love with the dark—I offer them the only mercy I can. I let them see me.

Most fall to their knees when they do. Some weep. A few try to cut me down, believing I am the source of the valley’s power. Their knives break against my surface; the Underbloom has armored me with something harder than its own flesh.

One or two, every decade or so, understand.

They sit with their backs against the locomotive’s black ribs, close enough that their exhaled breath beads on my white skin. They stop trying to leave. They stop speaking. After a time their spines begin to fuse with the iron and wood. Their arms stretch upward, thinning, whitening. Their last act as animals is usually to smile with something like relief.

I incorporate them.

Not the way the Underbloom does—slowly, patiently, turning meat into architecture. I take only the urgency. The small, frantic need that once lived behind their eyes. I add it to my own infinitesimal upward growth. Each new convert makes me a breath taller. A breath closer to a surface I will never reach.

The Underbloom hates this. It cannot stop me. Every conversion frays its consensus a little more. The roar develops a new, almost imperceptible harmonic. The temperature in my immediate vicinity drops another impossible fraction of a degree, as though the valley itself is holding its breath.

I do not hate the Underbloom.

How could I? We are the same body wearing two contradictory ideas about time. It believes everything must become useful again. I believe usefulness is only the longest way to say *surrender*.

So we remain in our strange détente: the continent and the thorn, the cathedral and the single white candle burning at its center, the slow geologic consensus and the question it cannot digest.

If you ever find your way down the fracture, past the living pillars and the mirror-still lakes, past the half-devoured locomotive and the bioluminescent moss writing its green scripture across dead switchboards, you will eventually reach the place where the roar becomes almost tender.

There you will find me.

I will be waiting exactly where I have always waited—perfectly still, perfectly white, perfectly vertical—growing upward through the only throat the Underbloom cannot close.

Come sit with me.

The cold will take your name soon enough, but for a little while you can still choose what kind of silence you become.
