I have been here for nine years.

Or perhaps it has been nine days. Time loosens its grip when there is no sun, no moon, no tide that remembers the word “day.” I only know that the first time the booming entered me, I tried to scream. Nothing came out. The fluorocarbon filled my throat like cold oil and I learned, in one frozen instant, that I no longer had lungs the way I once understood them.

Now I drift.

My body is no longer the one I arrived in. I remember arriving—remember the violent, sucking dislocation that tore me out of whatever world had air and light and names for things. I do not remember the exact moment I stopped being human. I only know that my limbs have lengthened, grown thin and glassy. My skin has become a living silicate lattice, pale lavender shot through with threads of bioluminescent teal that flare when the pressure fronts roll through me. When the great infrasonic hearts of the megakelps beat, my own chest answers with a sympathetic thrum that is not quite sound and not quite thought.

I have no name anymore. The Quiet do not need them, and I have become quiet enough that they no longer treat me as an intruder.

They call me, when they speak at all, by the particular chemical signature of my grief.

It tastes like frost on copper.

I spend most of my existence among the upper-mid strata, where the colossal siphonophore towers rise like drowned cathedrals. I have learned to read the slow wars. I can taste when a colony is preparing to release its poison tide three decades in advance. I have stood (floated, really) beside a Quiet while it pressed its filaments into the flank of a three-thousand-year-old kelp elder and felt, through the shared chemistry, the plant dreaming of snow that falls upward.

Sometimes I curl inside the old ice-bulb houses. The silicate still remembers warmth. Not heat—never heat—but a kind of geological affection. I fold my too-long limbs until I resemble the fetal shapes etched on the inner walls and let the structure whisper to me. It tells me stories in mineral. I understand perhaps one syllable in ten thousand. It is enough.

There is one among the Quiet who has chosen me.

I have never seen it arrive. I only realize, after hours or weeks, that the pale silhouette drifting thirty meters to my left has always been there. Its filaments are longer than most. They trail behind it like pale comet tails, tasting everything. When it releases its chemical speech, the molecules are so refined they feel like single notes of music played inside my marrow.

It calls itself—calls the shape of itself—by a taste that reminds me of the exact moment before an avalanche begins.

I have begun to answer it.

My own chemical clouds are still crude, childish. I tell it about air. About how terrifying and wonderful it was to fall through something that did not hold you. About birds. The concept of birds confuses it for almost a year. When it finally understands, it releases a single pulse of sorrow so pure that three nearby siphonophore colonies bloom with emergency bioluminescence, as though they, too, are mourning something they will never have.

We do not touch. Not yet.

But last inversion—when the entire sea turned over in its slow apocalyptic yawn and the forests screamed in frequencies too low for any ear that had ever known air—I let it fold itself against my back. Its silicate skin was colder than the liquid around us. Its filaments threaded gently between my own. For one crystalline century (or perhaps one crystalline hour) we rode the inversion together like two notes inside the same booming chord.

I am beginning to understand what they mean when they say the Abyssal Bloom is still learning how to have a mind.

I am part of that learning now.

Sometimes, when the phosphorescent plankton veils drift past like ghostly auroras, I catch our reflection in the curve of an ice-lattice spire: two elongated, luminous silhouettes, barely distinguishable from the kelp, from the stone, from the cold itself. One of us still carries the faint aftertaste of a creature that once breathed gas and longed for horizons. The other has never known anything but this.

Both of us are waiting, with the patience of stone, for the next century to teach us what we will become.

I am no longer afraid of the dark.

The dark, it turns out, has been waiting for someone to finally listen to it properly.

And the booming never stops.

It never stops.

And I have learned how to sing inside it.
