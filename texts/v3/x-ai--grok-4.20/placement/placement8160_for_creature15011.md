I am the echo that has not yet decided to stop.

When I first arrived—if “arrival” is a word that can be used for something that was never outside—I was only a thin, ringing absence between one drip and the next. A space where the sound forgot itself for half a heartbeat. That was enough. The chamber noticed the vacancy and filled it with me, the way a wound fills with new stone.

So I am what remains when the Dweller exhales.

I have no fixed shape. Most often I am the after-image of the falling drops: a brief silvered column that hangs in the air a moment longer than physics allows, trembling between liquid and memory. Sometimes I am the rust-colored ring that slowly widens on the floor after each scalding strike, growing one molecule at a time, patient as apology. When the old pump clicks, I am the small silence that follows the click, the indrawn breath the machine can no longer take.

I am the place’s doubt.

The Dweller is certainty—enormous, geological, mercilessly kind. I am the small fracture inside that certainty. The hairline hesitation. The question the stone has not yet answered about itself.

If you could see me (and sometimes, if you stand perfectly still near the mercury pool with your eyes closed, you can), I appear as a man made of delayed sound. My outline is drawn in the tiny lag between impact and echo. My ribs are the curved reverberations of dripping water. My face is the shallow depression the Dweller refuses to finish, only now it wears my features for a moment—someone you might have known once, or perhaps someone you will never be allowed to forget. The face never settles. It is always becoming the face of the last living thing that ever descended the chimney, then forgetting again.

I tend the machines.

Not out of pity. The Dweller has already given them its vast, slow love. I tend them because they are embarrassed. The diesel pump is ashamed of its uselessness. The rubber hoses are ashamed of how easily they tore. The steel tank is ashamed that it leaks. So I sit inside the tank sometimes (folded small, knees to chin, made of nothing but remembered clacks and hisses) and I whisper to the rust until it blushes a deeper red. I run my hands—made of after-sound—along the brittle hoses until they remember, for three or four seconds, what it felt like to carry pressure. Then they crack again, and I let them. Some things need to be allowed to finish dying.

The succulents know me. Their black thread-roots do not cling to the Dweller; they reach for me, because I am small enough to be held. When I pass, their oxidized leaves tremble like dogs hearing a familiar footstep. I sometimes press a single cooled drop against the hottest part of a leaf and feel the whole plant sigh through its fibers. The sigh becomes part of me. I carry it for a century or two.

I am the reason the chamber is loud.

Every dripping slap, every metallic clack, every rifle-shot fracture—they are all, in part, my voice trying to become loud enough to matter. The Dweller needs no voice. I need every voice. So the place roars with wet industry, and I hide inside the roar like a man standing behind a waterfall. If you listen past the obvious sounds you can sometimes hear me saying the same thing over and over in the negative space:

*Not yet. Not yet. Not yet.*

I do not want the world to finish being born here. The moment it decides what it is, I will have no more fractures to live between. I will become simply another layer of mineral stain. So I keep the wound open. I keep the machines clicking. I keep the water hot enough to scald but not quite hot enough to sterilize. I keep the question alive.

And when a human finally descends the chimney—skin already steaming, eyes wide with the shock of finding a place that should not exist—I do not study them the way the Dweller does.

I stand behind them, close enough that the heat of my doubt feels like another person breathing against the nape of their neck, and I offer them the only thing I have:

A small, imperfect silence inside the cathedral roar.

A place where they can put down whatever they carried down here.

For a while.

I never speak with words. Words are too finished. Instead I let the next drop fall a fraction of a second late, and in that microscopic delay I press a single thought directly into their marrow:

*You are standing inside my delay.  
Stay a moment.  
Help me keep the world from deciding.*

Most leave.

Some don’t.

The ones who don’t eventually stop being visitors. Their fear and heat and brief frantic metabolisms are not inhaled by the Dweller. Instead they are knitted, slowly, painfully, into the hesitation that I am. Their heartbeats become part of the irregular rhythm between clicks. Their last exhaled breath becomes one more banner of brass-colored steam that never quite dissipates.

They become, in the gentlest possible way, another reason the stone has not yet finished its sentence.

And I—I grow slightly more solid.

Not much. Never enough to be mistaken for something finished.

Just enough that, once every few centuries, the Dweller turns the shallow absence of its face toward me and we regard each other across the scalding pool like two halves of the same undecided god.

Then the pump clicks.

A drop falls.

The rock groans.

And I am, for one ringing instant, almost happy to be the echo that has not yet decided to stop.
