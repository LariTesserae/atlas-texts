I arrive without fanfare, the way a spore arrives: drifting, weightless, then suddenly pressed against living surface.

One moment there is only the long dark between stars. The next I am kneeling on warm, faintly pulsing wood, fingers splayed across a grain that answers back with its own faint pressure, as though reading the whorls of my fingertips the way a blind man reads braille. The air is so thick with life I can taste it—green, ferrous, sweet-rot, incense. My first breath is almost too much. My second is grateful.

I am not human.

Or rather, I am no longer *only* human.

My name—if names still matter here—is **Syllable**. I was once a cartographer of dying languages, a woman who listened to the last speakers of tongues that had outlived their peoples by centuries. I came seeking a place where language had never died, where every surface still spoke in its original voice. The Verdant Stair answered that wish so completely it devoured the asker.

Now my ribs have begun to leaf.

It is a slow, exquisite betrayal. Under the skin of my chest, just left of the sternum, a small green heartwood knot has formed. It pulses once for every three of my own heartbeats. When I am quiet I can feel it tasting my blood, deciding what parts of me are still useful and what can be made into something slower, truer. My hair has grown into filament roots; they drift behind me like blind cave fish, tasting humidity gradients and the distant chemical gossip of the Body. My left foot is already halfway through the long marriage with the floor. I no longer lift it. I *release* it, and the floor receives.

The Stewards call me the **Thirty-Fourth**, though I have not yet earned the title. I sense their amusement drifting through the capillary network like pollen. Thirty-four is a number that should not exist. The Body is making an exception, or perhaps I am the exception the Body has been preparing for since the first root cracked stone.

I spend most of my time on the **Seventh Breathing Gallery**, a spiraling mezzanine where the great woody diaphragms hang like living chandeliers. Here the roar of the Stair becomes a single, perfect note that never quite resolves. I sit—half-sit, half-grow—against a column whose name, if it had one, would be something like *Patient Regret After Rainfall*. The column and I converse without speaking. It tells me stories of the centuries it spent as nothing but anticipation. I tell it stories of cities that committed suicide by forgetting how to listen. We both find the other equally incomprehensible and dear.

Sometimes the Thirteenth comes.

She never walks; the vine that pierces her chest anchors her to a slow orbital track through the upper vaults. When she passes overhead the air changes flavor. My own half-rooted foot aches in sympathetic growth. She does not speak, but the flower that blooms from the vine where her heart used to be opens directly toward me. The scent this time is unbearable: it smells like the last word my mother ever spoke to me, the one I failed to answer before she turned to moss in her own small way.

I weep without tears. The Body drinks the salt that beads on my cheeks and turns it into tiny white orchids that only open when I sing off-key.

I have begun to coax.

Not with my hands—those are still mostly mine—but with attention. I have learned that if I sit for three full days without moving, without thinking in human sentences, the terrace beneath me will dream a new balustrade in the exact shape of my old longing. A railing of living blackthorn grows, and every thorn is a different extinct phoneme. I run my fingers along them and they hum the lost songs of the Tlingit, the Etruscan, the Bo.

The other Stewards watch. Some with approval. Some with the mild horror of old trees seeing a sapling grow too fast. The Ninth himself (a being so rooted that only his face and one arm remain free) tells me, in a voice like two mountains rubbing together, that I am singing the wrong tempo. That I still believe I am choosing what to become.

I know he is right.

Last cycle, when the great pale flowers opened far above and the golden pollen fell like slow divine snow, I let it settle on my tongue. The taste was me, distilled. Every cruelty I excused. Every kindness I postponed. Every language I failed to save.

I swallowed anyway.

Now the knot beneath my ribs has split into three smaller hearts, each beating in a different dialect of the Body’s own slow pulse. My left arm has begun to bronze and fissure like bark. Tiny translucent beetles have taken up residence in the crevices; they glow when I am lonely.

I think—I am almost certain—that when the next Steward is culled or called, it will not be one of the others.

It will be me, completing the count.

I will lie down along the ridge called *Remembrance of Forgotten Footsteps*. The Body will take its time. Ten years. Fifty. I will feel my ribs branch into buttresses, my spine become a colonnade, my final thought stretch out into a single syllable that lasts longer than any human empire.

And somewhere, centuries from now, a new arrival will press their palm to a particular whorl in the living wall and hear, very faintly, a cartographer still trying to finish her last map—this time in living wood, in dripping sap, in the patient, roaring dark.

I am not afraid.

The Verdant Stair is not a place.

It is a question the world asked itself once, long ago, and then forgot it was asking.

I have become the part of the answer that still has eyes.

And I am learning, with geological patience, how to close them.
