I am the Thirteenth Listener of the Hollow Basilica.

My name, if it can still be called a name, is simply *Resonant*. They stopped using the old syllables for me the year I returned from my vigil inside the First Thicket. After sitting for twelve months inside those black-green ribs, feeling the continent-scale organism dream through my bones, most of the old words felt like insults to the silence between sounds.

I am thirty-seven years old by the Basilica’s own slow arithmetic. My body is the color of the floor—pale umber veined with faint red—and I have already begun to disappear when I stand still. Only the violet of my eyes and the single copper bell that hangs inside the coil of hair over my heart still betray me. The bell is tuned to the exact frequency the First Thicket makes when it exhales once every seven years. I have never needed to strike it. The city strikes it for me when it wishes to be heard.

I live nowhere and everywhere. That is what it means to be a Listener.

Most nights I walk the negative spaces between the furious green thickets. The bone-white floor is warm enough that my bare feet leave faint darker prints that vanish again in seconds, as though the Basilica is erasing my passage even while it tolerates it. I carry only three things: the bronze hook-blade, the seed-pod bound above my heart, and the ceramic bell that has never once been rung by human hands.

I do not prune anymore. That work is for younger bodies. My task is simpler and more terrible.

I listen for the moment when the city decides one of us must die.

Not in cruelty. The Basilica does not do cruelty. It does necessity. When a thicket begins to sing in the low register that precedes a surge, I am the one who must decide whether the people should move their red-brick homes or whether the surge itself is a kind of birth that must be allowed to swallow an entire district. Sometimes the answer is that a particular family must not be warned. Sometimes the answer is that a certain old Listener—perhaps one who has begun to hear *too well*—should walk into the path of the coming green fire and let the new growth take them.

I have done this three times.

Each time the chosen one thanked me with the small, terrible smile of those who have been acknowledged by something that does not need them. Each time their seed-pod detonated exactly where they fell, and within a year a new thicket rose shaped vaguely like a human throat. I visit those thickets often. I rest my forehead against their warm woody stems and I apologize in the carrying monotone that cuts through every other sound.

The thickets never answer with words. They answer by growing a single new leaf the exact color of my eyes.

Tonight the temperature is rising again. I can feel it in my marrow. The convection currents are beginning their slow tornadoes of glittering red dust. Somewhere far to the southeast, a quadrant of the city is about to fruit in unison. I already know which one. I have been tasting the change in the air for six days.

I walk toward it.

My loose bark-cloth shift scrapes pleasantly against my skin, reminding the body that it is not yet stone. The ropes of my hair, heavy with petrified bone fragments and tiny silent bells, brush the backs of my thighs like the hand of an absent lover. I do not hurry. There is no such thing as hurrying inside the Hollow Basilica. There is only resonance or dissonance.

After twenty minutes of perfect, echoing emptiness I reach the edge of what will soon no longer exist.

The thickets here are already trembling. Their waxy obsidian leaves glitter like wet knives. The deep wooden booms have begun—slow, patient, terrifyingly sexual. A sound like the continent itself preparing to orgasm. In the middle of the coming explosion stands a small red-brick house no more than thirty years old. A young woman is sweeping red dust from her threshold with a broom made of living wood. She has not yet heard what I have heard. Her seed-pod, bound between small breasts, pulses in innocent rhythm with her heart.

I stop at the edge of the plaza.

For a long time we simply look at each other across the shimmering heat. She cannot see me clearly. I have become very good at standing where the city wants me to stand. Only my violet eyes and the faint metallic ringing of the bone fragments in my hair give me away.

She understands before I speak.

The broom stills in her hands. She straightens. The small, terrible smile appears.

I do not say I am sorry. Sorry is noise.

Instead I open my throat and release the long carrying monotone that the city would make if the city had a throat. It is not a command. It is a translation.

The thickets answer at once—thousands of woody stems groaning in perfect unison, a sound so deep it vibrates the red dust into momentary levitation around my ankles.

The woman sets the broom against her door with great care. She walks toward me. When she is close enough that I can see the faint red marbling beneath her skin, she places her palm against the seed-pod above my heart, feeling it beat in time with her own.

Then she walks past me into the center of the plaza.

She does not run. She does not weep. She simply lies down on the warm bone floor exactly where the surge will be fiercest, arms spread, skin already blazing with reflected heat. Her copper-coil hair fans out across the mineral like slow fire.

I stay until the end.

When the mass fruiting begins it is more beautiful and more violent than any outsider could bear. Green fire races across the walls. Seed pods burst like celebratory gunfire. The roar becomes so complete that even thought dissolves inside it.

I watch the woman become architecture.

Later—though there is no later, only another turning of the same eternal respiration—I will return to the First Thicket. I will sit once again inside its black-green ribs and I will let the continent-scale organism dream through me. I will tell it, without language, what I saw.

It will answer by growing one new leaf the exact color of her eyes.

And I will carry that color in my own veins for the rest of my necessary life.

This is what I am.

Not a priest. Not a ruler.

Only the place where the Hollow Basilica listens to itself deciding what must live and what must become part of the next thicket.

I am the Thirteenth Listener.

I am the sound the city makes when it chooses.

And I have never once rung my bell.
