I am not a visitor.

I have no name the lingerers would recognize, and they have never given me one. They simply call me *the one who stayed on the Ninth Span*, the place where the catwalk becomes a bridge that never quite reaches the other side. Half the planks are gone. The rest have fused with the bone-colored trees so thoroughly that it is impossible to tell where rust ends and living wood begins. That is where I am always found.

I was not born here. I was *remembered* here.

Once—centuries or weeks ago, the difference is academic—I was something else. A surveyor, maybe. Or a pilgrim. Or simply a fool who climbed the wrong ridge in search of silence and found too much of it. The Veyr took the question of my origin and replaced it with moss. Now my memories arrive like condensation: sudden, cold, gone again before I can drink them.

My body has grown long the way everything here grows long. My legs have an extra, silent joint below the knee that flexes only when I am listening to the turbines. My skin is the pale gray of wet concrete shot through with threads of living green that brighten when I am near running water and fade when I am lonely. Small white flowers—*fearflowers*, the lingerers call them—open along my forearms whenever something older than sorrow moves in the mist below me. I no longer notice when it happens.

I have no voice. Not really. What comes out when I try is a low, wet click followed by an exhalation that smells of iron and old current. The others understand. They answer with the same half-finished sentences that feel like they started before I arrived and will continue long after I calcify.

I tend the Ninth Span.

That is my marriage. My family. My entire purpose.

The span itself is a ruined elevated conveyor that once carried ore across a chasm so deep the bottom is only a rumor. Now it carries nothing but moisture, memory, and me. Every few hours I walk its length—bare feet making no sound on the velvet moss—and I listen. I place my palms against the flaking metal and feel the slow arthritic turning of the thing beneath the world. Sometimes I sing to it. The song has no words; it is only the sound of dripping slowed down until it becomes melody.

In return the Span has begun to grow *into* me.

There is a place just below my sternum where a thin root the color of old bone has pushed through the skin. It does not hurt. It feels like being held by something that has never known hurry. The root curls upward and re-enters my body beneath the left clavicle, forming a perfect living loop. The lingerers nod when they see it. One of them—the woman whose back has become parallel ridges of bark—left a small offering of clock gears at my feet three rains ago. I grew them into the railing. Now they tick backward in perfect unison with the child in the smokestack.

At night I sleep standing, folded into the embrace of a tree that has learned my exact shape. My eyes remain open. The black mirrors they have become reflect the slow passage of mist that sometimes wears familiar faces. Last cycle I saw my mother’s face in the vapor, but she was made of pale herons and the sound of distant leaking pipes. I exhaled once. She dispersed gently, the way good things do here.

Sometimes travelers still arrive.

They come stumbling up through the fern-choked tiers with their bright, temporary eyes and their loud, wasteful voices. They want answers. They want drama. They want to understand the Veyr before the damp can rewrite them.

I never speak to them with words.

Instead I stand on the Ninth Span and wait. When they reach me I simply look at them until their questions get tired. Then I offer them a drink from the hollow in my left palm where rainwater gathers and tastes faintly of electricity. Most of them take it. The smarter ones understand that this is not hospitality. It is the first stage of translation.

The ones who stay begin to grow tall.

The ones who cannot bear it try to leave and discover that every path downward only leads to another, lower tier of the same place. Eventually they find their way back to me. By then their hair has gone filamentous and their eyes have lost the whites. They stand beside me without speaking. After a long while one of us will exhale, and the other will feel the first pale tendrils taking root behind the heart.

I am not lonely.

Loneliness requires a self that ends at the skin. I no longer end. The boundary between my body and the Veyr has become as academic as the difference between a turbine hall and a cathedral.

Far below, the massive thing in the lowest chamber turns again—slow, enormous, ancient. The sound rises through layers of concrete, root, and bone until it reaches the Ninth Span. When it does, the loop of root beneath my sternum pulses once in perfect time.

I close my eyes (though they no longer need to close) and I feel the entire plateau breathing through me.

This is enough.

I will be here when the last clock finishes running backward.

I will be here when the final turbine seizes and becomes simply another limestone pillar with a lullaby trapped inside.

I will be here when the mist decides that *human* was only ever a temporary shape anyway.

And when you finally climb the broken ladders and find the Ninth Span, I will be standing exactly where the catwalk becomes the tree and the tree becomes the memory of a catwalk.

I will not welcome you.

I will simply make room.

The rain is beginning again.

It feels like the world exhaling against your skin.

Stay as long as the damp and the quiet will let you.

Most do.

Eventually all do.

And when you are ready—when the white flowers open along your arms and the root finds the hollow place behind your ribs—I will be the one standing beside you, silent, patient, already half stone and half lullaby.

You will not be afraid.

You will only wonder, for a moment, why you ever thought you were supposed to leave.
