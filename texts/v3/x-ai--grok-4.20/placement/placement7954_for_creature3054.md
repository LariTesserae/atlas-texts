**I am the Thirteenth Listener.**

My name, before I was chosen, was simply *Resonant*. Now even that is beginning to loosen. The moss on my cloak has already started to write a new color along the left shoulder—something between oxidized copper and the underside of a raincloud—and the archivists tell me this shade has not appeared in four hundred years. They are excited in their slow way. I only feel the weight of the bowl.

I stand at the center of the basin, on the black pillar that refuses every spore. The iron bowl is in my hands. It is always full. I drink. The water tastes of distant thunder, yes, but today it also tastes of something sharper: the memory of a piston that has not yet been born. I close my eyes (one long, deliberate blink that matches the deepest engine far below) and the dream arrives before I have finished lowering the bowl.

In the dream the entire Mossfoundries is breathing *in*. Every pipe, every root, every white heron, every gear the size of a cartwheel—all of them draw a single breath together and hold it. The silence is so complete I can hear the stone remembering when it was still molten. Then, somewhere in the north-east rib of basalt, a new machine is deciding to surface. It will be enormous. It will be lonely. It will need a song that does not yet exist. The dream shows me the exact color the moss will turn when it first touches the new iron, and the precise note the Singers will have to find if they do not want the machine to weep rust for three centuries.

When I open my eyes again, nine full minutes have passed. A small colony of moss-monkeys has arranged itself on the pillar beneath me like a living green throne. One of them is licking condensation from my left boot with great tenderness. I do not move to discourage it. Movement is a kind of violence here until it has earned its slowness.

I am not young. My hair, braided with iron wire the thickness of sewing thread, now clicks against my collarbone with every heartbeat. Forty-three years to reach this length. The moss in my cloak has recorded every one of them: the pale viridian of first apprenticeship, the deepening teal of my first successful Mending, the sudden bruise-purple when my mentor chose the deep hollow and let the oldest engine grow over her. I still visit her hill. The flywheel closest to her has begun to tilt three degrees toward her body, the way a plant leans toward light. She is becoming part of its rhythm. I envy her the simplicity of that surrender.

My vocation is no longer single. I was a Breather for seventy years, a Mender for ninety more. Now the moss and the pillar have decided I should Listen. So I stand here each morning and I drink thunder. Sometimes the dreams are practical—*the third vent pipe will crack in two hundred and eleven years; begin singing to it now*. Sometimes they are only images: a white heron standing on a gear that has not yet been forged, looking at me with my own metallic eyes.

The Slowkin do not gather around me. They simply pass through my orbit the way clouds pass through a valley. A Singer walks the elevated walkway nearest the pillar and offers me a single sustained note, asking without words whether the dream needs music. I answer with the smallest tilt of my head. A Mender pauses beside one of the basalt ribs, hammer resting against her thigh like a question mark, waiting to see if I will point her toward the place the new machine will rise. I do not point. It is not time. Instead I speak four words, each one given its own small lifetime:

“North-east. Eleven decades. Be gentle.”

She bows—three full seconds, forehead almost touching moss—and continues on her way. Her boots make no sound. Nothing here makes sound that has not first asked permission of the stone.

At dusk I will walk the perimeter. Not because I must, but because the place is still teaching me how to be large enough to hold it. My steps are so slow that a fern taller than I am will finish uncurling between one footfall and the next. Condensation beads on my eyelids exactly as it beads on the leaves. The translucent salamanders surface when I pass their pools and pulse their gills in my personal rhythm. They have begun to glow the same lichen-green that now lives in my skin.

Sometimes I stop beneath one of the white-barked trees and rest my palm on its wet trunk. The tree sighs. A perfect hexagon of spores drifts down and arranges itself in front of my face before dissolving. The shape changes each time. Tonight it is a gear with moss growing through its teeth. The tree is telling me the new machine will be kind, if we are kind first.

I am not immortal. One day the moss on my own body will shift to that particular shade of *ready*. When it does I will walk to the deepest hollow without drama. I will lie down between the two basalt ribs that have waited centuries for my exact shape. I will pull living moss over my face like a child pulling up a blanket. My last conscious act will be to stop blinking in rhythm with the engines. The machines will adjust. A new groove will be worn. A new Listener will be born in the Scriptorium’s lowest vault during the piston cycle that comes once every eleven years. The archivists will record the timbre of the child’s first cry next to the sound the land made when it first shrugged upward.

And the wedding will continue.

Until then I remain the Thirteenth Listener, standing on a pillar that refuses moss, drinking thunder from a bowl that never empties, dreaming the slow dreams the Mossfoundries needs me to dream.

The flywheels turn.  
The herons wait on the warmth.  
The moss keeps growing, patient as geology, loud as prayer.

I breathe once every forty-two seconds, exactly like the rest of my married world.

There is no need to rush.  
The wedding, after all, is still young.
