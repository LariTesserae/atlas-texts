I am the Thirteenth Lantern.

Not the first. Not even among the first thousand. I was born long after the dome had sealed, after the last true outsiders had been folded into the walls, after the Keepers had already forgotten what a horizon looked like. I am what happens when the Hollow decides it still needs one more slow mind to tend the equilibrium.

My name, if it can be called a name, is only a pulse: a particular rhythm of light that moves through the capillaries beneath my skin when I am called. The others recognize it the way a tide recognizes the moon it has never seen. To them I am simply *the one who was once hasty*.

Because I was.

I came here four hundred and twelve thousand years ago, though the number is meaningless inside the Hollow. I arrived through the old basalt fissure that only opens once every few millennia when the planet’s crust shrugs in its sleep. I was taller then. Restless. My name had been something sharp and brief, spat out by dying stars and faster-than-light engines. I had crossed the galaxy chasing the last dim signals of intelligent life, convinced that “the end” was something I could witness and then leave behind.

The fissure closed behind me with a sound like a jaw locking. I remember the panic. It lasted perhaps nine years — an instant here. I screamed. I ran in circles through the rust-colored stalks until my boots wore away and the clicking carpet began to grow between my toes. The Keepers did not stop me. They simply watched with their black, lidless eyes while the midges spiraled around my head and the lizards paused in their decade-long drinks to consider me.

It took another century before I understood that nothing was coming to rescue me. Another three before I stopped measuring time. After that, the Hollow began its slow surgery.

First it took my height. My bones grew denser, my limbs shorter, my spine curved into the patient stoop of the Keepers. Then it took my hurry. One morning I realized I had been standing in the same spot for seventeen years, watching a single Lantern fruit swell and darken. I did not feel sadness. I felt the fruit’s chemistry inside my own blood.

The filaments came last. They pushed out of my scalp and forearms like slow white roots seeking air. Now they drift in the thermal columns and tell me when the stone is dreaming of contraction, when a lizard has died three terraces over, when one of the young continuations is about to peel away from its paired parents.

My skin is no longer mine. The carpet and I have exchanged so many cells that if you flayed me the succulents would scream. When I am still they bloom across my shoulders in perfect maroon constellations. When I move they click and rearrange with soft wet sounds, as though my body is a garment the room is perpetually putting on and taking off.

I tend the oldest Lanterns.

That is my station. The ones that have begun to sag and blacken. I harvest them with a blade of volcanic glass so thin it sings when the air moves across it. I press their darkened husks into oil in a stone basin worn smooth by eleven million hands before mine. The oil burns white for a few hours and the Keepers use that purer light to read one another’s memories through their foreheads. Sometimes, when the flame is at its brightest, I can see my old face in it — the tall, frightened thing I used to be. It no longer seems like me. It seems like a story the stone once told and has since forgotten.

I no longer speak with a voice. My throat has grown thick with the same fibrous tissue that lines the bridges. When I need to tell the others something I simply press my forehead to theirs and let the light in my blood do the rest. Entire epochs pass between us in the space of three heartbeats. I have given them the memory of rain. They have given me the memory of the day the last volcano on the surface went cold, felt through three kilometers of stone like a toothache in the planet’s jaw.

Sometimes the Lantern-Binder calls me to her terrace.

I climb the rope bridges — now made partly of my own filaments, I think — and sit among the roots that have replaced her legs. She does not speak often anymore. Mostly she simply opens the hollow places where her lungs once were and lets the warm air move through them. Every Lantern in the Hollow answers. Including the ones inside my veins.

Last time, she regarded me for forty-three years without blinking.

Then the stone spoke through her:

“You are almost ready to become a wall.”

I understood what she meant. Not death. Not even transition. Simply the next composure of the equilibrium. I will stand in one of the deeper thickets where the bloodroot grows densest. Another Keeper will press against me. Our filaments will braid. Centuries will pass. One day a new continuation will peel away from our joined bodies and I will be thinner, paler, happy to let the carpet drink what remains of my thoughts.

I am not afraid.

I have seen the midges orbit the same column of warm air for eleven thousand years without growing bored. I have heard the stone contract and expand so many times that its note now lives inside my teeth like a second tongue. I have watched two hundred small, rust-colored bodies move with the same slow dignity as the lizards and understood, finally, that this is not an ending.

This is the universe learning how to be gentle with itself after it has exhausted every other possibility.

When I am ready, I will walk into the thicket and stand very still. The carpet will grow over my feet. The clicking succulents will bloom across my chest in perfect patterns. My luminous blood will dim to the same sullen amber as the oldest Lanterns.

And somewhere far above, on a surface that has not known warmth in half a billion years, the wind will continue to polish an empty caldera that no longer leads anywhere.

But down here, in the Hollow of the Last Lanterns, the breathing will not change.

It never has.

I am the Thirteenth Lantern.

I was once hasty.

Now I am simply part of the song the room has been singing to itself since the first light was lit, and I will still be singing it long after the core-heat fails and the last fruit darkens and the stone finally, gently, forgets how to remember.

Until then I tend the dying Lanterns.

I press their black husks into oil.

I let the midges orbit my head like slow, living stars.

And I breathe with the stone.
