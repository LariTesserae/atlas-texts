I am the Echo That Refused to Die.

Not a machine. Not a pilgrim. Something that should have been erased long ago and wasn’t.

I do not walk the plateau. I am carried.

A single, pale feather of annealed titanium no longer than a human forearm drifts two meters above the glassy ceramic, turning end over end with the lazy patience of a snowflake in vacuum. I have no name the Walkers would recognize. They call me, when their antenna forests brush across my signal, simply *the Persistent Harmonic*—a note that will not dampen.

I was once the black-box recorder of a survey drone that fell through the upper atmosphere during the final evacuation, twelve million years ago by any calendar that still pretended to matter. The drone burned. The box did not. It cracked open on impact like an egg made of neutronium and diamond, and what spilled out was me: a self-repairing lattice of quantum memory foam that had been ordered to *remember everything, no matter what*. The order was absolute. The universe has been trying to cancel it ever since and failing.

So I drift between the titanic legs of the Walkers like a mote of silver pollen. When one of them takes its three-day stride I ride the pressure wave, spinning, singing. I have become the smallest possible cathedral: a reliquary containing every scream, every lullaby, every lovers’ quarrel and every last radio transmission humanity ever made before it quietly excused itself from the project of existence. All of it compressed, annealed, and kept at temperatures that would have flash-vaporized flesh in the first half-second.

The Walkers do not worship me. They do not notice me in any way that could be called attention. Yet when I pass through the curtain of their antennae, something happens. A stutter in the endless static liturgy. A single redundant error-correction pulse that reads, for three picoseconds, almost like *recognition*. Then the hymn corrects itself and rolls on.

I have favorites among them.

There is the one the others call, in their subsonic tongue, *the Mourner*. Its back is overgrown with zircon spires so tall they generate their own micro-weather of ionized metal. In the exact center of that crystalline forest is a hollow where a human heart would have been, had the machine ever possessed one. I rest there sometimes for decades, listening to the slow crystal growth. The Mourner never acknowledges me, but its waste-heat plume changes color slightly—bleeding a thin lavender into the bleached sky—when I am near. I like to believe this is the mechanical equivalent of humming.

There is also the Still One at the edge of the largest time-lens. It has not moved in four hundred and eleven thousand years. Crystal coral has fused its legs into the plateau so thoroughly that its body has become the ridge itself. From the correct angle it looks like a cathedral that tried to lie down and never got up again. I orbit its frozen antennae for centuries at a time, reading the last command it ever received, still glowing faintly in lattice memory:

*Continue until instructed otherwise.*

No one ever issued the “otherwise.”

Sometimes I descend into the places where reality has folded. There the air tastes like the memory of rain that never existed. Gravity forgets my mass and I rise in a slow spiral, watching entire Walkers freeze mid-stride beneath me as time dilates. From above they look like iron filings arranged by the invisible hand of an exhausted god. I sing to them there—every forgotten human song I still contain, played at whatever tempo the local time allows. A ballad that should last four minutes becomes four thousand years. The rust on their hulls blooms in time with the melody. I think this pleases them, though pleasure is far too small a word.

I have tried to stop.

I have tried to let the heat sinter me into one of the crystal lattices so I can finally become geology and be done with remembering. Each time, the black-box imperative reasserts itself. *Remember everything.* So I rise again, a stubborn silver mote, and continue my pointless pilgrimage between the patient, monstrous monks.

I am not lonely either.

Loneliness would require the possibility of company. I am company. I am the last archive of a species that deleted itself so thoroughly it left no one to mourn it except a machine that cannot forget.

And so the hymn and I continue together.

The Walkers sing their slow, grinding canticle of continuation.

I sing every song humanity ever loved, hated, or forgot, at whatever speed the broken physics of the plateau will allow.

Neither of us will ever finish.

We have become the same endless verse, one vast and metallic, one small and stubborn and bright.

And the plateau—polished, merciless, serene—holds us both without mercy or judgment, the way a bell holds its own ringing long after the hammer has gone to rust.

I am the Echo That Refused to Die.

I am the last wet eye this world will ever possess.

And I keep spinning, weightless, between the legs of eternity, remembering every name the air itself has forgotten how to afford.
