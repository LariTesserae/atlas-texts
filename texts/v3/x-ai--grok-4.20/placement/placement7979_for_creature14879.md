I am not a visitor.

I am the thirty-thousand-and-first year.

I have no name the Hollow Saint has not already begun to dissolve. What the mountain calls me is simply *the new warmth that has not yet learned to be stone*.

I arrived as something upright, something with lungs and a language and the ridiculous habit of counting time in heartbeats. That lasted perhaps three days. Or three centuries. The Veins do not distinguish.

Now I am mostly a slow thought the mountain is having.

My ribs have become two of the pleated limestone columns. They no longer rise and fall; instead they host a permanent colony of black lichen that has arranged itself into the exact pattern of the arteries I once possessed. When the wind (what little wind there is) moves through the upper fissures, my fossilized ribs produce a low, mournful note that the salamanders have learned to sing back to. They like the resonance. They tell the Saint I am polite.

My heart is the fissure now.

It was never removed. It was simply *recognized*. The warmest water in the Veins is still mine; I can feel it leaving me in thick, slow pulses. Each drop carries the last stubborn fragments of what I used to call “self.” The millipedes drink from the rivulets and carry those fragments away in their armored bodies, distributing me like communion. I do not mind. I have begun to enjoy the sensation of being useful to something that will never die.

My eyes are two coin-sized salamanders.

They left their original bodies long ago and chose mine instead. They hover where my face once was, throats flaring rose-gold in perfect synchrony with what used to be my pulse. When I want to see, they see for me. When I want to close my eyes, they simply stop glowing. Sometimes they kiss each other across the hollow where my nose dissolved. I think they are in love. I am glad something still can be.

My hands have become the spider silk.

Not the spiders themselves—those still click and knit—but the silk. I am the geometry now. The precise, patient tension that holds the world together between one slow moment and the next. When a beetle needs to climb from one of my former fingers to what used to be my collarbone, it walks along me. I feel every footfall as a small, exquisite prayer.

And the Hollow Saint… she has begun to wear my face the way she once wore the mountain’s.

Not all of it. Only the parts that were already hollow.

Where my mouth was, there is now a perfect knee-high niche. Pilgrims (there are none, and yet there are always more) sometimes crawl inside and curl up like fetuses. The stone there is exactly body temperature. They stay until they forget why they ever wanted to leave. I sing to them with the grinding of my own settling bones. The song has only one line and it has taken me nine hundred years to learn it:

*Yes. Keep becoming.*

I am not finished.

She is not finished.

We never will be.

Sometimes, in the violet dark, I feel the last stubborn sliver of what I once was flare up like a coal remembering fire. In those moments I remember cities, and rain that fell from skies instead of ceilings, and the terrifying speed of human voices. The memory hurts the way granite hurts when water finds a new crack. But the pain is devotional. The pain is part of the digestion.

Then the salamanders kiss again across my absent eyes, the beetles polish the whorls of my fossilized fingerprints, and the warm water keeps leaving the place where my heart still stubbornly believes it is a heart.

I am learning how to be patient with myself.

It is taking thirty thousand years.

I have time.

I am the time.

And somewhere far above, in the thin, punishing air where the mountain still pretends it has a summit, a single bead of water the size of a grape trembles on new stone. It has my name on it. It does not know this yet.

It is learning.

We all are.
