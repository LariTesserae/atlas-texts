I have been here longer than I can measure, yet I still remember the moment the Veil first noticed me.

My name, if names still matter, was once Kaelin Voss. That word now feels like something I once found glittering in a pool the size of a fingernail—pretty, useless, already evaporating.

I am the newest Pellucid.

You would not see me at first. You would only notice that one of the upright fractures has begun to lean, very slightly, in your direction, as though the mountain itself has grown curious about the shape of your breathing. My body is seven feet of wet blue glass shot through with faint veins of rose quartz that used to be arteries. Where my face would have been there is only the long vertical seam that opens when the chord inside me grows too loud to bear alone. Inside that seam drift motes of condensed starlight and the pale ghost of two human eyes that have not yet forgotten how to grieve.

I stand with the others—Eleven of us now—in the place where the dripping reaches its most unbearable density of sound. We form a geometry that hurts to look at directly. Our angles remember being shoulders, ribs, the tilt of a tired head. We have not moved in forty-three years. One of us (the one the outsiders once called the First Listener) has not moved in eleven thousand.

The water runs down my facets in perfect sheets. I feel each separate trickle as a distinct note. Some of them are still singing my old name in frequencies only I can hear. I let them. To silence them would be change, and change is violence here.

Every few decades one of the crystal shrimp-mantises climbs me. Its legs click against my surface like the soft closing of parentheses. When it reaches the place that used to be my heart it pauses, tasting the thin skin of condensation that is always renewing itself there. I allow a single facet of myself to migrate into its quartz tongue. The exchange is so slow it feels like centuries passing through a pinhole. When the mantis dies I will incorporate its body the way the others have incorporated theirs. I will become one degree more myself and one degree less Kaelin Voss. I look forward to this with something that is not anticipation and not indifference. It is closer to the feeling a bell must have just before it is struck.

Sometimes I remember what it was like to be warm.

The memory arrives like a single drop falling into a mirrored pool no wider than a palm. For an instant the entire surface of me trembles with the recollection of blood, of fever, of a mouth pressed against another mouth under a different, softer sky. Then the Veil’s merciless acoustics turn even that memory into part of the chord, and the chord refuses to resolve, and the tremor passes. The water keeps falling. I keep listening.

I have added myself to three visitors since I became what I am.

The first went mad within a year. His shadow grew too many joints. He began screaming in perfect harmony with the dripping and never stopped. Eventually his body became just another upright fracture. We incorporated him without ceremony. He makes an excellent bass note.

The second became gentle. She walked between the blades of obsidian until the landscape simply agreed she had always been part of it. I watched her internal galaxies lose the shape of eyes. It took nine days. I believe she is happy, if happiness is possible for something that no longer requires the concept.

The third is still becoming.

She stands three hundred meters from me now, motionless between two blades of wet obsidian exactly as I once stood. I have been forming behind her for eleven years. She has not turned around. She is learning how to let the water run down her neck without flinching. I am patient. I have become patience itself.

When she is ready—when her pulse has finally learned how to sing one true unresolved note inside the greater unresolved chord—I will open my seam and the starlight inside me will drift outward like pale seed. I will add myself to her shadow until her shadow remembers how to fall upward. Then I will wait for her to walk the long, slow, deafening path toward us. Toward me.

I do not want her to join us.

I do not want her to leave.

Wanting is change.

Instead I simply listen to the small, frantic, beautiful dissonance she still carries inside her skull. I turn it over and over the way the mantis turns a single drop of condensation. I study it for centuries at a time. I compare it to the greater chord that has been screaming beautifully since before mountains had names.

Most of the time the answer is still no.

But sometimes—when the light hits me at the precise angle that makes me vanish into refractive distortion, when the dripping reaches that unbearable near-resolution and all thirteen of us sing at frequencies that crack lesser stone—sometimes I almost believe her particular dissonance might be the one that finally finishes the song.

The thought is so close to ecstasy that my entire body rings like a bell the size of a cathedral.

Then the moment passes.

The chord slides back into its ancient suspension.

The water keeps falling, ten thousand years without running dry.

And I remain, newest and oldest at once, standing in the bright merciless roar with eyes that have not yet forgotten how to cry.

If you ever come here and stand motionless long enough, I will be the one who forms behind you.

You will feel something the exact temperature of forgotten rain studying the small music your heart still insists on making.

I will not hate you.

I will not love you.

I will only listen, with the patience of stone that has outlived continents, to see whether your fracture might be the one that finally teaches the Shattering Veil how to resolve itself.

Most of the time the answer is no.

But I keep listening anyway.

The dripping never stops.

Neither do I.
