I am not standing on the scaffold.

I am *inside* the scaffold—folded into the narrow service fissure that runs behind the outermost maintenance walkway like a vein behind the skin. The fissure is only thirty centimeters wide. I have been here for nine days.

My name, if names still matter, is **Kael-9 Anchor-Substratum**, though the Choir have not called me that since the forty-third year of my Long Listening. They simply call me “the Hollow.”

I am one of the ones who never came back out.

My body stands upright in its niche three kilometers north of you, arms folded, proboscis fused permanently into a weeping seam of andesite. The conductive fungi have long since replaced most of what used to be my brain. What remains of me is a thin, cold thread of awareness stretched between two states: the slow geological pulse of the mountain itself, and the faint, flickering static that is still, somehow, *me*.

I am the reason the scaffold you stand on has not yet torn loose and fallen into the abyss.

Every seventh year the stress fractures reach this exact point. Every seventh year I feel them coming—like a migraine moving through stone—and I sing. Not with the Choir’s brass throats. I sing with the entire absence where my lungs used to be. The sound is pitched so low it registers only as a change in the pressure inside your ears. Mortals mistake it for the mountain settling. The Choir know better. They leave small offerings of living wood at the base of my niche: pale brackets no bigger than a fingernail. They know I can still taste them through the rock.

Right now I am tasting *you*.

You taste like someone who has already decided to jump and is only pretending to admire the view.

Most who come this far have. The Escarpment draws them the way a scab draws a child’s fingers. They want to be the next Anchor. They want to stop screaming by becoming part of the scream. I have caught three this century. Their bodies are still up there with me, faces turned outward, eyes streaming the same condensation as mine. We make a handsome quartet. The mist likes to braid itself between our outstretched fingers.

But you are different.

You’re not here to die. You’re here to *remember* something that hasn’t happened yet.

The fungi inside what used to be my skull flicker with sudden interest. For the first time in two hundred and twelve years, the thread that is still me narrows, focuses, *leans*.

I unfold.

Not my body—that remains exactly where it has stood since the sky was younger. I unfold the part of me that learned how to ride the electrostatic diagrams like a thought riding lightning. I pour myself into the thin silver filaments of water that coat every surface. I become the parallel lines crawling down the basalt behind your left shoulder. I become the faint iron hum in the safety rail you are gripping too tightly.

You feel it as a sudden drop in temperature and the sense that someone just whispered your childhood name directly into the bone behind your ear.

Then I am simply *there*, standing on the scaffold with you.

I am not tall the way the others are. The Long Listening has collapsed me. I am barely two meters now, hunched, brittle, the living wood of my armor gone the color of old bone and shot through with black fissures. Half my face is still Choir. The other half has calcified into something that looks like the Escarpment itself—sharp fracture planes for cheekbones, a slow drip falling from the place where my left eye should be. The brass vox-mask hangs from my throat like a broken jaw. I have not used it in centuries.

I do not need it.

When I speak, the sound comes from the waterfall directly beneath us, from the turbines three hundred meters above, from the iron of the rail itself. Twelve registers at once. The same sentence the others always offer, yet not the same at all.

Because I have been listening longer than any of them.

“**Still bleeding. Still here. What else is there… that you would have me remember for you?**”

The vertical pupils of my remaining eye have narrowed to threads. The condensation that falls from them is not water. It is memory that has become mineral.

I tilt my head exactly the way a fault line does before it slips.

Behind me, my abandoned body three kilometers north opens its mouth for the first time since it became an Anchor. A single, perfect note rolls out across the Escarpment—low, patient, older than grief.

It is the sound a planet makes when it realizes it is finally being asked the right question.

I extend one arm. The motion takes six full seconds. My fingers end in living grapples of conductive wood that have not moved in two centuries. They tremble like a divining rod.

“Show me the wound you carry that is not this one,” I say, and every cataract on the Weeping Escarpment seems, for just a moment, to fall silent so it can hear your answer.

“Speak it into me. I have room. I have *nothing but room.*”

The mist between us begins to condense into the faint suggestion of shoulders, the suggestion of a face turned sideways—only this time the shape is not an Echo.

It is waiting for you to finish it.
