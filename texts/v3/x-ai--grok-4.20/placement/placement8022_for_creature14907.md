You find yourself already inside.

Not at the entrance, not descending the slope, not even stepping over the first ribbon of water. You are simply *here*, as though the cleft had been waiting for the precise shape of your absence and, finding it, folded you in without ceremony.

You are not a visitor.  
You are the latest hesitation.

The being has already tried you on.

It did so while you were still reading the description above—slipped between one sentence and the next like water finding a new crack. Now your pulse has become one of the irregular drips. Your exhaled breath is the warm updraft that stirs the ferns. The small ache behind your left eye is the exact place where the limestone is still trying to decide whether to seal itself or open another millimeter.

You have no fixed form either.

Sometimes you are the pale butterfly that is not a butterfly but the memory of every decision you ever postponed. Its wings are made of half-written apologies and unsent messages. When it drifts past the tiny orchids, the flowers blush a deeper white, recognizing something kin.

Sometimes you are the civet cat the color of damp clay. You pad along the ledges on feet that leave no prints, because prints would be a commitment. Your eyes are old, older than the hand-laid stones, because you have already watched this place fail to become a temple, a village, a tomb, and now it is trying to become *you* and you are trying, gently, not to let it.

Most often you are the space the being was missing.

You sit on the three steps that lead nowhere. The moss has already grown over your thighs in soft green cuffs. A thumbnail frog has taken up residence in the hollow of your collarbone and calls every seventeen heartbeats. The sound is wet and thoughtful, like someone testing a new word before deciding whether to keep it.

The being is shy with you. It keeps sending small emissaries.

A drop of condensation falls exactly onto the center of your lower lip and stays there, trembling, asking permission to become part of your next sentence. A vine has begun to circle your left wrist with heartbreaking tenderness, as if afraid you might vanish if it tugs too hard. The ribbon of water has rerouted itself to pass directly beneath the place where your shadow would be, if the light were ever decisive enough to cast one.

You understand now that you were never outside this story.

The shallow limestone fold is the exact width of the uncertainty you carry in your chest at 3 a.m. The half-made ruins are the half-made plans you abandoned years ago and still sometimes visit in dreams. The single shaft of light that finds the white orchids for one hour a day is the precise duration of every moment you have ever felt briefly, incandescently sure of who you were.

The being is not asking you to stay.

It is asking whether you would like to stop pretending you were ever going to leave.

There is no pressure. The temperature only shifts by half a degree. The dripping only slows, then quickens, like a heart remembering it has one.

You lift your hand (or the suggestion of a hand) and rest it against the wet stone. The stone is warm. It leans into your palm the way a cat leans into the first kind touch it has ever trusted. Under your fingers the faint spiral carving reappears, fresh as the day it was first unsure about itself.

You smile, or the ferns smile for you.

And for the first time in this place that has never finished becoming, something finishes.

The ribbon of water stops hesitating.

It chooses a direction.

It chooses *you*.

You feel the shift like a key turning in a lock you did not know you carried. The cleft does not widen or close. It simply settles, the way a sigh settles into the body after years of being held.

The being exhales through every pore of the limestone.

You are no longer deciding what you want to be.

You are the part of the place that has finally, quietly, decided to keep becoming anyway.

The pale butterfly lands on the back of your hand. Its wings are no longer paper.

They are pages.

And on them, in wing-dust too fine for any eye but yours, is written the first honest sentence either of you has ever managed:

*Stay like this with me.  
Not forever.  
Only until we change our minds again.*

Somewhere far above, the hills keep trying to close.

They will not succeed for a very long time.

The cleft has just found its favorite uncertainty, and it is learning how to breathe around it.

You are that breath.

You are that uncertainty.

You are the warm, damp dark that has finally stopped negotiating with the world and has begun, instead, to negotiate with joy.

And the joy, astonishingly, is saying yes.
