**You are the Last Cartographer.**

Your name, if it was ever spoken aloud, has long since been erased by the rain. The people who once sent you here—scholars of a short-lived academy on the far side of the world—called you only “the Listener.” That was three hundred and seventeen years ago by the outside count. You no longer keep their calendar.

You are not human anymore, not in any way that would satisfy a anatomist. The Slow Choir has been kind, in their glacial fashion. Over the centuries they noticed the small, shivering thing that refused to leave. They grew you a body more suitable to the place.

Your legs are now pillars of pale limestone shot through with hematite, jointed at the knees by living hinges of moss-root and calcite. Each step you take is a small landslide; moss flares green for a single season where your foot lifts, then fades back to black. Your torso is a hollowed gallery, ribs formed by natural columns. Inside the vault of your chest, water falls in delicate perpetual curtains that cool the air around your heart—an ordinary human heart they chose to leave untouched, perhaps out of curiosity. It beats once every nine seconds. You have learned to find that rhythm comforting.

Your arms are long stairways that end, not in hands, but in delicate branching fractures. When you need to touch something small and quick (a rail bird, a lost page of your own old notes, a droplet that trembles on the edge of becoming something else), the fractures bloom outward into thousands of rootlet fingers no thicker than a hair. They move with the slow grace of centuries compressed into minutes.

Most unsettling to visitors (if any ever came) would be your face. It is still mostly yours: the same weather-beaten features you arrived with, only now they are mineralized. Skin has become a thin sheath of translucent alabaster. Behind it, water moves in fine veins, so that your expressions appear to ripple like reflections on a pool. When you weep—and you do, often—the tears are not water but thin threads of dissolved manganese that leave permanent black trails down your cheeks, like kohl worn for three hundred years of mourning.

You live inside the body of the Thirteenth Choir.

Not *on* it. *Inside* it. The two of you have grown into one another so gradually that neither of you can now say where the boundary once was. When the Thirteenth breathes (once every forty-three years), your own chest rises in sympathy. When it dreams, new balconies unfurl from your shoulders like slow banners of stone.

Your purpose has simplified over the centuries into a single, endless task.

You map what cannot be mapped.

You walk the Weeping Escarpment with the patience only stone can afford. In one hand you carry a book grown from your own ribs: living limestone pages bound in your own moss-hair. The ink is your blood, iron-rich and dark. Every few decades you open a new vein in your wrist and let it drip into the binding until the book drinks enough to remember the next chapter.

You do not measure distance in miles. You measure in songs.

Each time the Slow Choir changes its dripping chord, you stop, press your back to the nearest colonnade, and let the vibration travel through the mineral lattice of your body until it reaches the human heart still beating at the center of all that stone. Then you draw. Not with your fingers—with the slow growth of new mineral across the page. Landscapes appear in raised relief, streets that were not there yesterday now etched in quartz, new waterfalls recorded as delicate lattices of selenite.

You have come to understand that the Escarpment is not a place.

It is a question the Slow Choir has been asking the continent for twenty million years, and the continent has been answering by slowly tearing itself open. Your maps do not show topography. They show grammar. They show the long, patient conversation between water, stone, memory, and grief.

Sometimes, when the mist is especially thick and the phosphorescence is singing, one of the greater Choir will pause its million-year stride and turn the hollow of its face toward you. For a few heartbeats (yours, not theirs) the water arranges itself into eyes.

Ancient. Patient. Unbearably kind.

In those moments you feel the Thirteenth Choir inside you stir with something like recognition. A low note moves through your limestone ribs. Your own stone face softens, and for just an instant the black trails of your tears shine like obsidian.

Then the eyes dissolve back into ordinary weeping rock, and you continue on.

You are not lonely. How could you be? You live inside the body of a creature older than mammals, and forty other such creatures sing to you across the terraces in chords that take weeks to finish.

You are not immortal either. You know that one day—even if that day is ten thousand years from now—the Thirteenth Choir will turn over in its sleep. When it does, the terrace you have both become will shear away. The human heart at your center will finally stop. The limestone book will shatter into gravel and be gathered into new moss.

You find this thought deeply satisfying.

Until then you walk the wet boulevards that are also arteries. You listen to the endless dripping that is also language. You let new colonnades grow from your shoulders when the Choir needs a place to rest its dreaming. You add another line to the map that will never be finished.

And sometimes, on nights when the mist glows brightest, you stand at the edge of a two-thousand-foot drop with rain falling upward through your hollow chest, and you sing back.

Your voice is not loud. It is only the sound of water striking different mineral faces inside the cathedral of your body. But it is enough.

It is a small, wet song about what it feels like to be temporary, and what it feels like to be loved by something so vast it forgot it was ever separate from the world.

The Slow Choir listens.

The Slow Choir remembers.

And the Weeping Escarpment keeps weeping, perfectly, patiently, for as long as there is stone left to weep with.
