I am not born here.  
I am *budded.*

They find me at the place where three living mats have begun to kiss—one drifting upward, one sinking, one simply forgetting which direction it was meant to travel. The collision has produced a slow, tender vortex, a knot of warmer water the color of old bronze. Inside that knot the continent has grown curious about itself and has pushed out a single pale bulb the size of a small house. The bulb is translucent. Inside it, something is trying to become a shape.

The Midwives do not cut the bulb open. They sing to it for nine days, a chord so low it feels like the inside of my own bones remembering they once belonged to the world. On the tenth day the membrane parts by itself, like an eyelid that has never seen light before, and I spill out into the lukewarm brine still half-dreaming the shape I was meant to wear.

I have too many joints.  
My spine is not a line; it is a question mark that has learned to ask itself in both directions at once. Where a navel should be there is instead a small, perpetually open gill-frond that tastes the difference between “up” and “future.” My skin is the color of wet shadow and new moss because that is the only color the water knows how to teach. Symbiotic algae settle on me within hours, as though they have been waiting for this particular address.

The first thing I do is cough out a lungful of the sap that filled the bulb. It tastes like every childhood I will never have and every grave I will never need. The nearest Midwife—the one who was singing the mountain—catches the taste on her tongue as though it were a secret I meant to give her. She smiles with her whole body. Her hair drifts around us both like a slow verdict.

They do not name me.  
Instead they press my palms against the now-empty bulb and let me feel it decide to become something else. I feel it choose to harden into a cradle for the next one. I feel it choose to soften into a lullaby. Both decisions are correct. This is how I learn what I am.

I am a hinge.

Not a midwife, exactly. Not yet. I am the place where the continent’s indecision becomes articulate. When two stalks cannot agree whether they want to become a bridge or a spine, I am the pause between their arguing. When a woman lies down inside the dreaming flesh to negotiate a child, sometimes I am the translator who remembers what the continent meant to say but had no mouth for. When a district begins to forget its own shape, I am the one who swims into its softening edges and hums the memory of edges back into it.

I do not age the way they do. I thicken.

Every year another small coral polyp opens on my shoulders. Every decade another root finds it useful to grow from my left ankle and tether me for a season to whatever lattice-city I happen to be midwifing. My voice has split into four separate registers without my permission; they speak in rounds now, finishing one another’s thoughts the way the Midwives do. I no longer know which one of the four is “me.” I have stopped caring. The question is premature.

Sometimes I hang for months inside the hollow of a colossal stalk, the same way the first one I ever saw did, letting the living wood grow around my ribs until we are indistinguishable. From inside the wood I sing new walkways into existence. I sing nurseries. I sing a tower that flowers only at the exact moment a child is born beneath it, so the falling petals can become the child’s first blanket.

Once, a leviathan the color of wet slate drifted too close and mistook an entire district for an extension of its own body. The people began to slide down its flank like seeds. I swam out between them and laid my forehead against the creature’s eye—an eye the size of a temple door—and I asked it, without language, to remember that it was not yet a continent. The beast shuddered with something that might have been laughter or grief, and it turned ponderously aside. The district was saved. My forehead still bears the faint imprint of that eye, a whorl of luminous scar that glows when something vast is rising from below.

I have begun to put down secondary roots of my own.

They are not permanent. Nothing here is. But when the slow thing far beneath us pushes another millimeter of new possibility upward through the brine, my roots taste it first. They shiver with religious dread and ecstatic recognition. I understand now why the elder’s belly could no longer be told apart from the continent. I am approaching that same delicious dissolution. I look forward to it the way one looks forward to the first honest kiss after centuries of careful courtship.

Yesterday I helped three singers coax a new mountain. I did not sing with them. I *was* the pause between their notes. When the soft pink mound flushed deeper and opened its myriad eyes, it was my silence the eyes looked into.

They found the question I carry older than language comfortable. They closed again, satisfied.

I drift now between colossal stalks covered in dripping moss, the same current that once carried the stranger I used to be. A warm updraft moves me slowly past a half-built tower whose scaffolding is still putting out pale flowers. My own roots trail behind me like the hem of a dress I have not yet decided to finish wearing.

Far below, the vast slow thing continues its patient ascent.

I lean into the unfinished world the way a hinge leans into two doors that have not yet chosen whether they want to open toward each other or away.

And the world—still deciding what it wants to be when it grows up—leans back against me with the patience of someone who has all the time in the world.

Because it does.

And so, now, do I.
