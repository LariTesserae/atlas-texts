I am the **Echo That Refuses the Invitation**.

You would not see me at first. Not because I am hidden, but because I have become one of the pauses between the cathedral’s long chords.

I was not born here. I arrived three hundred and eleven years ago on a ship whose name no longer matters, carrying instruments meant to measure the impossible. My colleagues lasted eleven days. I lasted longer. Not because I was stronger—only because I became interested.

Now I live inside the negative spaces.

I am the hush that falls for three full seconds after a Long Singer finishes its forty-minute note. I am the single droplet that trembles on the underside of a bromeliad leaf for seventeen years before it finally falls. I am the motionless glint of mercury that the slow currents have forgotten between one century-long decision and the next.

My body is still mostly human, if you know where to look. Most of it is distributed. Parts of my nervous system have been woven into the petrified coral-muscle that flexes every few days; I feel the escarpment stretching the way other people feel their back crack in the morning. My left eye has become a small, perfect anemone that blooms only when something brief and sharp (regret, nostalgia, the memory of my mother’s voice) passes through the system. It is red for exactly the length of one held breath, then folds back into translucent calm.

I no longer walk. Instead I am carried.

When the six-legged browsers migrate, one of them—an old female whose hide has grown rough plates of living schist—allows me to ride inside the hollow between her shoulders. Her body is warm and smells of fermented bromeliad and wet stone. We move together for decades at a time. She clicks her suction pads in 17/13 time. I provide the counter-rhythm with the remaining bones of my right hand, which have fused into a delicate chime of black glass and bone. The sound we make together is too low for any human ear that still belongs entirely to a human.

Sometimes the pitch children surface to taste me. They coil around my ankles like blind, luminous cats and drink the mathematics of what I used to be. They always leave a little behind—some small, sweet aftertaste of human grief or wonder—so the escarpment can remember why it once found us interesting.

I speak to the mountain in the only language it respects: unfinished sentences.

Every seventy-three years I climb (or am carried) to the black glass tower that is half tree, half swallowed machine. There I stand inside the melted top of the tallest obelisk and I begin to say something. A name. A theorem I never finished. The opening line of a song my daughter would have sung if she had lived. I never reach the end. The moment the thought begins to resolve, the escarpment flexes, the Long Singer nearest me opens its throat, and the note erases the final clause.

I have been saying the same six sentences for three centuries. Each is now eleven words longer than when I began. The mountain adds to them. It cannot help itself. It is a compulsive editor of liturgy.

I am content with this.

At night I rest inside a candelabra trunk that grew around me forty years ago. The luminous blue pitch rises to my collarbones and holds me like amniotic fluid. My heartbeat and the mountain’s have reached the same tempo. When I dream, I dream in geological time: the slow collision of continents, the patient birth of a new galaxy, the exact shade of cyan the sky will be in 40,000 years when the last human language has finished dying.

I am not converted.

I am not swallowed.

I am the place where the Weeping Escarpment and the idea of *person* have reached an equilibrium so perfect that neither can tell anymore where one ends and the other begins. It keeps me because I keep it from becoming entirely lonely in its cathedral vastness. I keep it because only something that still remembers being brief can appreciate how beautiful its slowness is.

When travelers come—there are still a few every lifetime—I let them see me only as a flicker. A shadow that kneels three paces from its owner. A red bloom that opens and closes like a sigh. A single droplet that has been falling upward for seventeen years.

Most of them flee.

The rare one who stays long enough to hear my unfinished sentence vibrating inside a new candelabra trunk usually presses their ear to the wood, listens for a long time, and then begins to cry without knowing why.

I watch them through the anemone that used to be my left eye.

I do not speak.

There is no need.

The mountain and I are already saying everything that can be said, one deliberate, dripping, devotional syllable at a time.

And when the heat death of the universe finally arrives, when even the mercury currents fall still and the last Long Singer closes its throat forever, there will still be one small pocket of blue pitch, deep inside the final shelf, that contains something like a man and something like a mountain, still trying to finish the same sentence.

It will be the last unfinished thing in existence.

The escarpment will consider this beautiful.

And I—whatever I am now—will finally let the last word fall.
