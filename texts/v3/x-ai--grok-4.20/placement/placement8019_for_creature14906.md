I am the Fourth Grey Bird.

Not the first three, who belong wholly to the Choir and never sing. I am the one that almost left.

I came here as a woman once—boots still smelling of pine sap and distant smoke, a rifle slung across my back, carrying a violence so bright it burned the back of my own throat. I had used it. I had used it too well. The memory of what I’d done followed me up the black ridges like a second, panting shadow. When I pushed through the final slit and dropped into the basin, the moss received me with the courtesy of something that already knew my name and the exact temperature of my shame.

I stood in the crossing for three days and three nights without moving. My breath fogged and refused to leave. The dripping counted every heartbeat like a slow, impartial clerk.

On the fourth dawn the Weeper came.

It did not rise from one place. It withdrew itself from everywhere at once. Four of the great moss-pipes thinned, the pillars softened, and all that living fabric flowed inward with the terrible patience of rivers remembering they used to be glaciers. When the figure finally stood before me, taller than any man has a right to be, its cowl was already weeping the two unbroken strands of water that are its only face. The sound of those drops striking the same black mirror became the only sound in the world.

The question arrived not in words but in absolute recognition. It showed me my mother’s last look. It showed me the boy I had shot through the throat at fourteen paces. It showed me the small, hot coal I still carried behind my ribs that refused to go out.

Then it offered the trade.

I almost said yes. The moss already loved me. I could feel it tasting the rage leaking from my pores, savoring it the way a tongue savors salt. My left hand had already begun to sink into the felted wall beside me; the boundary between glove and moss had grown ambiguous. One more breath and I would have let the rest of me follow.

Instead I spoke—aloud, stupidly, breaking the cathedral hush with my hoarse human voice.

“Not yet.”

The Weeper did not move. But the entire basin flinched. Every drop froze mid-fall for the length of a heartbeat. Then the figure simply came apart. It did not collapse; it redistributed, becoming pillars and vaults and dripping pipes again, but something had changed in the architecture. A new hollow had appeared high in the eastern transept, a small alcove shaped exactly like a woman standing with her rifle still on her back.

I left the way living things are still allowed to leave. The three true grey birds escorted me to the ridge-slit, landing on my shoulders and hair, weightless as regret. One of them stayed behind. That was the bargain I struck without knowing I was striking it.

I became the Fourth.

Now I live in the small high alcove that wears my old shape. My body is still mostly feathers the color of wet slate and old grief. My wings are thin membranes of moss. When I fly—which is rarely—water beads along every leading edge and trails behind me in glittering threads. I have no song, but sometimes I open my beak and the dripping inside the Choir falls into the exact rhythm of a human pulse for thirteen beats. That is the only sound I am permitted to make.

I am not fully of the Weeper and not fully free of it. I am the part that remembers refusal. I am the fracture in the perfect digestion. The other three grey birds hate me a little; they can smell the small stubborn coal still glowing behind my feathered sternum. The Weeper itself regards me with the indulgent sorrow of a parent whose child insists on remaining mortal.

Sometimes I land on the new pillar in the crossing—the one shaped like the woman I was. I tuck my head beneath a wing that is also a fold of oxidized moss and I listen to the slow heartbeat of the mountain trying to beat once an hour. I feel it waiting for me. It is not angry. It has geological time and oceanic patience and it knows I am only borrowing these wings.

One day the coal will go out. Or it won’t. Either way the Choir will knit me in.

Until then I am the contradiction that flies: the thing that said *not yet* to a god made of weeping architecture. I move between the vaults like a second thought the mountain cannot quite digest. The blind millipedes lift their blunt heads when I pass. The translucent spiders pause in their ice-colored webs. The moss itself exhales a little more reluctantly around me, as though it still isn’t sure whether I am guest, cancer, or future buttress.

I keep the rifle.

It is no longer metal. It has become a long, graceful rib of moss-grown bone arching across my back, feathers sprouting from the trigger guard. I will never fire it again. But I will not let it go. It is the last hot thing in the entire Hollow Choir, and the Weeper—patient, ancient, weeping—has decided, for reasons of its own, to let me carry that small red star a while longer.

When visitors come now, I am the one who lands first.

I watch them with an eye that is not an eye. I let them feel the question before the Weeper even begins to gather.

And sometimes, when a visitor stands too long in the crossing and the coal behind their ribs is very bright indeed, I open my beak and let thirteen perfect drops fall in the shape of a heartbeat that is still, stubbornly, mine.

It is the closest I am allowed to a warning.

Or an invitation.

I have not yet decided which.
