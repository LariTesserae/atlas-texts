I am not who I was when the freight elevator first hissed open.

I came down as Dr. Mara Solace, ex-geothermal engineer, sent by the Surface Concordance to assess “structural viability” of the abandoned Pulse-9 project. I carried a waterproof tablet, a respirator I never used, and the arrogant belief that everything in the world could still be measured.

That person lasted approximately nineteen minutes.

Now I am only what the basin is making of me.

I have no name the Wet-Born use. They simply tilt their heads when I pass, as if listening to a new frequency that has begun to braid itself into the constant low throb of the pumps. Sometimes they call me *the Slow One*, not because I am slower than they are (I have learned their half-swim, half-walk gait perfectly), but because the change in me is still reluctant. My bones have not yet surrendered. My lungs still remember what dry air tasted like. The Underwet is patient with me. It has geological time.

I live between the Third and Fourth Breathing Towers—two immense cracking columns that rise out of the lake like the ribs of something half-born. The water there is exactly chest-deep on me now. I keep a small ledge of flowstone I have coaxed into a shelf with my own hands (or what used to be hands). On it rests three things: a rusted torque wrench I can no longer grip properly, a living cloak of forty-seven translucent millipedes that arrange themselves each dawn into new diagrams of my dreams, and a single pale moth that has not left my left shoulder since the night it drank the salt from the corners of my eyes while I wept for the last time.

My skin no longer sweats. It *exudes*. A thin, pearlescent film that tastes faintly of limestone and iron. The film prevents the constant warm rain from ever truly touching me; instead the droplets bead and roll away like mercury. The Wet-Born approve of this. They stroke the film with their chitin-tipped fingers and hum through the nearest pipe in low, approving chords.

My eyes have gone milky. Not completely—I can still see the orange cones of the sodium lamps and the green-violet veins beneath the skin of my companions—but the world has gained a soft halo, as though everything is slightly underwater even when it isn’t. Sometimes I catch my reflection in a still patch of lake between two silent turbines and I do not recognize the long, softened face looking back. The cheekbones have rounded. The jaw has lengthened. There is a faint green luminosity beneath the new pores that are opening along my collarbones, tiny apertures that open and close in time with the basin’s breathing.

I have begun to sing through the architecture.

It happened without decision. One cycle, while standing with my forehead pressed to the skin of the Western Regulator—its metal warm as living flesh—I felt something rise in my chest that was not breath. It rolled upward like a bubble through heavy syrup, then outward through the contact of my skull. The sound that left me was not sound. It was a slow pressure wave that moved through every pipe in the cloister at once. Valves I had never noticed opened in distant halls. A thousand millipedes froze mid-step, then rearranged themselves into a perfect spiral on the nearest catwalk. The milk eels came to me in a shining Milky Way beneath the surface and wrapped gently around my calves like living anklets.

The message I had sent without meaning to was simple:

*I am afraid I am disappearing.*

The basin answered with a single, immense inhalation that lasted eleven full minutes. When it exhaled, every moth in the Underwet rose at once. The air became a blizzard of silent white wings. And through that blizzard the Wet-Born drifted toward me—four hundred luminous bodies moving without urgency—and each one laid a hand on my skin.

Not to comfort.

To *incorporate*.

Since that cycle I have stopped measuring time in hours. I measure it in breaths. Every ten minutes the world inhales with me. My heart has slowed to match the sub-sonic pulse of the deepest pumps. When I sleep (standing, chest-deep, forehead against whichever machine feels loneliest that cycle), my dreams leak out through the new pores in my collarbones and the millipedes read them like scripture.

Last cycle I dreamed I was still surface-born, still dry, still *Mara*. I woke screaming in frequencies only the pipes could hear. The scream became part of the industrial liturgy. For three full breaths afterward, every valve in the basin released warm, sweet vapor that smelled like childhood rain.

I am becoming gestational.

Already I have begun to tend the cracks the way the others do. I walk the perimeter of the half-drowned foundry halls trailing a long iron rod grown from my own forearm (the chitin plate that replaced my hand lengthened, split, and hardened into a perfect stirring tool). Where the limestone threatens to pinch off a vital coolant vein, I press my body against the stone and sing until fresh flowstone blooms obediently around the wound. The rock and I are learning to trust one another.

Sometimes visitors still descend the narrow shaft.

I watch them from the shadows between the Third and Fourth Towers—bodies rigid with surface tension, eyes wide, tablets clutched like talismans. Most last less than a day. One woman made it almost six cycles before she tore off her clothes and walked screaming into the warm water, begging the basin to finish her. We let her. She is one of us now. Her name used to be something sharp and metallic. Now she is simply *the One Who Wept First*.

I have not decided whether I will ever leave.

Part of me—the last stubborn knot of original marrow—still believes there is a surface, still believes in reports and metrics and dry socks. That part grows smaller with every breath.

The larger part, the part that is already Wet-Born, understands that the Underwet is not a place one leaves.

It is a place that decides, very slowly, whether it is finished with you.

And the Underwet is nowhere near finished with me.

So I stand knee-deep between the breathing towers, my living cloak of millipedes spelling out tomorrow’s dreams across my back, my single moth resting on the wet stone of my tongue like a white communion. I press my forehead to the nearest pipe and add my small, softening note to the endless liturgy.

The machines purr.

The water trembles.

The basin inhales.

And I, whatever I am becoming, inhale with it.
