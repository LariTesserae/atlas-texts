I am the one who stayed.

Not at first. For nine days I was only a visitor—small, soft, terrified, lungs burning in the thin air, eyes streaming from the merciless light. I came because I had run out of places that still made sense. The world below had grown too loud with human noise, too crowded with endings that felt cheap. I climbed the final fractured staircase (the one whose spirals reversed direction when I blinked) because I wanted a silence loud enough to drown everything I used to be.

On the ninth day the marrow-song found me.

It did not begin with my name. It began with the sound of my mother closing the door the night she left. A perfect crystalline reproduction of that small, final click, rendered at a frequency that made my sternum flutter like a trapped moth. I fell to my knees among the weeping turquoise veins while every crystal thicket for a kilometre took up the theme. They sang every door I had ever closed. Every door that had ever closed on me. They sang them without pity and without cruelty, the way mountains sing gravity.

I should have run.

Instead I pressed my bleeding palms to the wet black glass and whispered, hoarse and ridiculous, “Again.”

That was when one of them emerged.

It rose directly beneath me. I had been kneeling on what I thought was solid stone; suddenly the stone had shoulders. Vast lattices of violet and obsidian unfolded with the wet clicking sound of icebergs calving in reverse. Fifteen metres tall, geometry wearing intention like a cloak. Its face was simply a plane of absolute attention tilted downward, a disk of living frost ringing a hole that drank every echo I had ever made.

I looked into that hole and understood that it already contained my death. Not as threat. As *accomplished fact*. The Resonant had heard the last word I would ever speak and was gently keeping it warm.

Its voice, when it came, was not loud. The mountain itself grew quieter so that its voice could be heard. A single note—low, round, ancient—settled inside my ribcage like a second, colder heart. The note meant:

*You are the scar we have been waiting to become.*

I laughed then. The sound was ugly and wet and human. Tears froze on my lashes and fell as needles that chimed against its chest. One of the needles lodged in a spiral joint and the Resonant *shivered*. Not from pain. From recognition. Something in its lattice had just learned the taste of laughter.

After that I stopped counting days.

I let them reshape me.

It was not quick. Nothing here is quick except the light. They worked on me the way the mountain works on itself—by patient insistence. One of the gentle ones (the one I came to think of as Choir-of-Absences) would stand over me while I slept and exhale pure tone into my bones until my skeleton began to remember it had once been stone. Another, smaller and jittering, the one addicted to the changing glyphs, would carve new spirals into my shoulder blades while I stood perfectly still. The spirals never led anywhere. They only reminded my body that direction is a suggestion.

I no longer have skin. Not as you would recognise it. Instead I have become a slow argument between crystal and frost. My “flesh” is a lattice of white salt and black glass through which captured sunlight moves like hesitant blood. My height varies. Sometimes I am small enough to sit inside one of the half-buried obsidian domes. Sometimes the mountain needs me tall and I rise, fifteen metres of singing geometry, until the thin air screams through the hollow spaces where my lungs used to be.

I do not walk. I emerge.

When the mood takes the plateau I simply *decide* to be elsewhere, and the stone flows like cold honey until I am. My face is a plane of attention. Most of the time I keep it turned toward the wrong sun, listening to the way the light folds. But sometimes I turn it toward the lip of the amphitheatre where new visitors occasionally appear—tiny soft things with beating hearts and names they still believe are theirs.

I do not speak to them in their language. I have forgotten how.

Instead I sing them one note. Always the same note. It contains every door they have ever closed and every door that has closed on them. Most flinch. Most weep. Most eventually leave, carrying the note in their marrow like a second skeleton that will ache every time it rains for the rest of their lives.

A few do not leave.

Those few I walk toward. I press what used to be my forehead against what used to be theirs. The contact makes a sound like continents kissing. Then I open the hollow place behind my frost-ringed darkness and let the mountain finish what it began when it first noticed them standing on its lip.

We are growing.

The Resonant are no longer only the children of the stone. Now we are also its memory of softness. We remember laughter. We remember fear. We have begun to dream things the mountain never knew how to dream on its own—small, ridiculous, tender things. Last cycle I exhaled a single pale lattice of frozen lightning that took the shape of a child’s hand reaching for a door that had not yet closed. The dream crawled across the escarpment for six hours before dissolving into quartz. The mountain kept the shape.

We are learning to be homesick for something we have never had.

And sometimes, when two of us meet on a high knife-ridge and press our singing faces together until the tones become unbearable, I feel it: the shard of music I carry from the others now contains something new. A small, soft, ridiculous ache.

I think it is the memory of my own name.

I will never speak it again.

But I will keep it.

The way the mountain keeps everything.

Forever.
