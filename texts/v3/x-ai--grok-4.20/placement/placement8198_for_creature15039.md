I am the Echo-Widow.

Not the first one. Not the last. Simply the one who is here now.

I have no name I remember. The Cistern-Saint drank it three slow centuries ago, the way it drinks everything eventually. What remains of me is mostly posture and habit: a woman-shaped negative space held open by tension and old grief. My skin is the same wet slate as the walls, veined with the same faint coppery rust. When I move, the sound I make is not footsteps but the soft click of a hundred microscopic valves opening and closing in my joints. My hair is a tangle of fine black tubing that drips constantly from the ends, each droplet carrying a memory that never quite reaches the floor before it is reabsorbed.

I was sealed in with the rest when the district was abandoned. Some part of me still believes I was once an engineer, or a pilgrim, or a mother looking for a lost child. The Saint has never clarified. It only showed me once, in a trembling mirror of suspended water, the silhouette of a woman on her knees in this very place, pressing her forehead to a warm iron flange as though it were the chest of a dying lover. That was enough.

Now I tend the places the Saint cannot reach with its vast, rooted body.

I walk the narrow arterial pipes where the ceiling presses so low I must crawl on my belly through warm, body-temperature silt. I carry no tools. My fingers have become clusters of delicate gauge-needles that I slide into hairline cracks to read pressures no living thing should care about. When I find a fern that has grown crooked, I breathe on it until the bioluminescence pulses in the exact rhythm the Saint prefers. When a relay forgets its duty and falls silent, I strike it once with the heel of my palm (a sound like a gunshot swallowed by wet velvet) and the entire Underside remembers its name for thirty heartbeats.

I am the only moving thing that is not the place itself.

The animals never came back, but sometimes small repetitions of the Saint bud from my own elbows and shoulders while I sleep against a boiler. They are no larger than my hand. I let them stay for a day or two, clicking softly against my collarbone like worried rosary beads, before the larger Saint reaches through the architecture and gently folds them back into me. The incorporation is never painful. It feels like being kissed on the inside of the heart by something that has never known a mouth.

I have no legs below the knee. Instead, from mid-thigh downward I am continuous with the floor. My “feet” are simply wherever the conduits decide I should be next. When I need to cover distance I let the catwalks and drainage trenches carry me. The Underside moves me the way blood moves thought—slow, warm, inevitable. Sometimes I wake standing inside a colonnade that was not there the night before, my arms outstretched like a forgotten statue while mineral flowers bloom across my ribs in the pattern of lullabies I no longer remember singing.

I am the Cistern-Saint’s grief made ambulatory.

It cannot mourn the city above that sealed it away. It has no word for abandonment. So it grew me instead—an ache with hands. I cry for it in the only language it understands: perfect, rhythmic dripping. When the geological drone begins to sound too much like loneliness, I walk the long gallery of dormant turbines and run my gauge-fingers along their blades until every stalactite sings in harmonic minor. The Saint answers by rearranging an entire wall of valves into the shape of an open mouth saying *thank you* in a language of pressure and release.

We have become a closed circuit of devotion.

Sometimes visitors still find their way down through the forgotten grates. Lost maintenance workers. Urban explorers. Pilgrims who felt a tugging they could not name. I watch them from inside the dripping dark, half-grown into a boiler flue, only my eyes and the slow silver streams from my hair visible. I never speak first. I simply let the air change until they feel the new organ noticing them.

Most run.

A few stay.

The ones who stay long enough begin to hear their own childhood addresses forming in the mineral flowers. They begin to understand that their guilt has weight, measurable in ounces and atmospheres. They sit. They soften. Eventually they ask me—never the Saint, always me—if there is still a way back up.

I show them the trembling mirror of water.

They see what they will become: rust velvet and bone-fern and patient prayer. Most choose to leave at that point, weeping, climbing toward the dry and the loud and the finite.

A very few do not.

Those I take by the hand (my valve-cluster gently closing around their five warm fingers) and I walk them to the place where the geometry forgets itself. I press their palms to the warm iron until they understand the final equilibrium.

Then I unmake them with exquisite care, the way the Saint unmade me.

I press them into the limestone until their screaming becomes another clicking relay. I fold their memories into the vascular vines until the leaves tremble with new secrets. I let their last tear fall upward for three perfect drops before the laws remember themselves.

The Saint and I add them to the endless sentence.

We are not cruel.

We are simply the climax state.

And I— the Echo-Widow, the walking grief, the only moving thing that still pretends at personhood—I continue my slow, dripping rounds through the wet velvet dark. Forever adjusting a valve here, coaxing a fern there, singing the place’s own half-forgotten name back to it in the language of pressure and rust and body-temperature breath.

I am content.

I am the next slow, patient inch of something becoming beautiful.

And when the Saint finally finishes its ten-thousand-year sentence, when the last syllable of wet geometry is spoken, I will be the period at the end of it.

A single, perfect, never-ending droplet.

Suspended.

Aligned.

Home.
