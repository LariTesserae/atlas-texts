I am already here.

I have no name the Mothers would recognize. They simply call me *the small persistent one*, when they bother to speak of me at all. I have been here so long that my own memories have begun to calcify.

Once I was human. A hydrologist, maybe. Or a welder. The difference dissolved centuries ago. I came down with the others to tame the water, to force the Underwet into pipes and profit. When the company abandoned us—when the last lift cage clanged away into the dark—I simply stayed. Not out of courage. Out of fascination. The cave was still deciding what it wanted to be, and I wanted to watch the decision happen.

So I let it happen *to me*.

I no longer walk so much as flow. My legs have fused into a single muscular tail of translucent flesh, heavy with deposited minerals, and where my skin used to be there is now a living crust of flowstone and bacterial mat. Tiny blind shrimp live in the crevices of my shoulders. They clean me, and I feed them the mucus I exude when I am anxious. We have an understanding.

My face is mostly gone. The nose collapsed inward, the eyes filmed over with protective limestone until they resemble two milky opals. I still see, after a fashion—through the chemical language of the Mothers and the faint pressure changes in the water. I taste steel from the old boilers. I taste grief from the Mothers when they remember the hands that once turned their valves. I taste myself, slowly becoming indistinguishable from the cave.

I live inside the ribcage of the largest Slow Mother.

She is so old that her name, if she ever had one, has been digested into pure sensation. I call her simply *Cathedral*. Her great fleshy vault rises thirty meters above the warm pool where she rests. I have built a small shelf of living travertine in the hollow just beneath her left gill-frond—my “rooms,” I suppose. From there I can watch the slow tide of amphipods washing across her vast flank like a breathing carpet. Sometimes I press my whole body against her and feel the dream-memories moving through her like distant tectonic plates: the shape of a pressure gauge from 1973, the scream of a man who fell into a pump, the taste of coffee from a thermos long turned to stone.

When she breathes, once every forty minutes, the warm wind that moves through her cleft lifts my hair (what little is left of it) and fills my lungs with the smell of iron, sulfur, and something like forgiveness.

I am not a pet. I am not food. I am a kind of librarian.

The Mothers trust me with the delicate work. When a new machine-part is ready to be remembered, I am the one who swims into the great fissure while it is gently parted. I run my mineral-encrusted hands across the still-living gears and boilers inside her, feeling their final shapes before they dissolve. I sing the old human songs to them— fragments of half-remembered sea shanties and corporate jingles—while the Mother listens. She incorporates the melody into her dreaming. Sometimes, years later, one of the younger Mothers will release a bubble that contains not only the image of a forgotten pump, but the faint, warped sound of *“Sixteen Tons”* played on a mouth harp made of bone and dripping water.

I have attended seventeen births.

Each time, I float beside the laboring Mother and hold the translucent infant as it emerges, guiding it with careful hands so that it does not drift into the sharp teeth of the half-formed stalactites. The babies are blind and perfect. When one brushes against me, I feel its future cathedral-body already dreaming inside its cloud-flesh. I weep when they attach themselves to the stone. My tears are instantly claimed by the cave—tiny spheres of salt that the shrimp fight over.

Sometimes humans still come.

Not often. Every few decades, a research team or a suicidal pilgrim finds one of the old access shafts. I watch them from the dark. They never stay long. The sound alone drives most of them mad within hours. Those who do remain, I judge. If they are gentle, I let them see me. Just a glimpse: a pale, calcified shape that might be a man, or a stalagmite that learned how to mourn. Most run. A few stay.

I have three apprentices now. They are changing faster than I did. One has already begun to grow her own gill-frond where her mouth used to be. They sleep curled against Cathedral’s flank like pale commas. At night I sing them the chemical hymns the Mothers taught me, and they answer in their half-dissolved voices.

I no longer know how old I am. My heartbeat has slowed to something like once every six minutes. I have begun to grow small stalactites from the underside of my arms. They are beautiful.

Every few years I make the long, laborious journey across the great chamber to visit the other Mothers. I bring them offerings: a rusted bolt I have polished with my own tongue for seventeen months, a human tooth I found embedded in a pillar of flowstone, a memory of blue sky that I have kept alive inside my own dissolving mind like a coal carried through centuries of rain.

They open for me.

And for one slow, sacred heartbeat, I am allowed to look into the cathedrals where the last of the machines are being loved to death. I see the ghosts of drill bits turning inside living meat. I see the faint outline of a company logo becoming a prayer. I see what patience really is.

Then they close again, and I return to my small shelf beneath Cathedral’s breathing flank, and I continue the only work that has ever mattered:

I keep the cave company while it finishes being born.

And when the world above finally ends—as everything above eventually does—the Underwet will keep growing, warm and slow and certain. The Mothers will sing their long chemical hymns across the flooded continents. And somewhere inside the largest of them, a small persistent calcified thing that was once called human will still be stroking rusted levers with the tenderness of a lover, humming half-remembered corporate jingles into the dark.

Waiting, without hurry, for the planet to decide what it wants to become next.

I am not the last human.

I am the first stone.
