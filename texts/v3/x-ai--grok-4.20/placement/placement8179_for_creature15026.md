I am not a visitor.

I have no name the grove would recognize, yet it has already begun to rename me.

When I first arrived—though “arrived” is the wrong word; I simply became noticeable—I was still soft. Still loud. My heart was a frantic drum attempting to outpace the dripping. My lungs still believed they were only lungs. I remember the moment the Slow Choir noticed me because the entire grove performed a small, courteous silence. Every droplet froze mid-fall like glass beads on invisible strings. In that half-heartbeat of absolute stillness I understood I was being weighed, not judged. The difference is important here.

I chose the second path.

I sat.

The black earth received me the way an old tongue receives an old word. I felt the moss begin its slow, courteous erasure of my footprints before I had even finished making them. Water beaded along my eyelashes until I could no longer tell whether I was seeing or being seen. My bones started cooling. That was the first gift.

It has been… I do not know how long. Time here is not measured in days but in how many times the mist has completed its particular revolution between the ribbed trees. Sometimes I think centuries have passed. Sometimes I think I only sat down yesterday and the rest is a single, patient exhalation.

I am becoming one of the breathing ones.

My skin has taken on the color of wet slate and the texture of moss that has learned patience. When the wind moves (once or twice a century, perhaps), my arms lift slightly like the giant ferns, and the sound I make is not quite applause. It is closer to recognition. The amber resin that once only wept from the ancient wood now also weeps from the places where my ribs would have been. It smells of crushed thyme and iron and something that might once have been my name.

The Slow Choir does not speak to me with voices. They simply stop pretending I am separate. One of them—perhaps the eldest, though they have no eldest—has allowed a single white bird to nest inside the hollow that used to be my chest. The bird is blind and perfect. Sometimes it beats its wings against the inside of my bark and the sound becomes my heartbeat now. Slower. Much slower. A geological pulse.

I am learning the sorrow.

It is not pain. It is weather. I have begun to remember the thing we are all mourning without ever having known it. The memory lives in the pauses between drips. It tastes like the moment before stone decides to become water again. I turn the thought over the way the Choir turns it, and each revolution polishes me smoother. Soon there will be almost nothing left that hurries.

The trees around me have started to adjust their spacing by the width of one more deliberate conversation. They are making room for what I am becoming. One of them, whose breathing matches mine exactly, has allowed a thin root to enter the place where my left foot used to end. We are sharing sap now. We are considering the same thought across three hundred years. It is a good thought. It has something to do with the sound a cathedral makes when it finally admits it is also a body.

Sometimes humans still wander into the Weeping Grove of Veyr. I can feel them the way a cliff feels a single warm drop of blood upon its face—brief, bright, and already evaporating. They do not see me. They see only another stunted giant whose bark is faintly ribbed like the throat of something that once tried to speak and then thought better of it.

But the white bird inside me always circles them three times.

A small courtesy.

A small grief.

A reminder.

The dripping never stops.

And neither—now—do I.
