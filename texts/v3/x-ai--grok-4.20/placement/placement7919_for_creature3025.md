I am not born here.  
I *condense*.

One moment there is only the wet wind moving over black slabs and the faint metallic taste of iron on the tongue. The next, a portion of the mist has thickened, slowed, and remembered a shape. That shape is me.

I have no name the Mothers would ever utter, because names are too fast. Instead the plateau calls me by the temperature of the air around my shoulders: *Minus-Four-and-Rising*. The midges know me as the absence that sometimes walks among their pillars without scattering them. The lemmings feel me as a low note just below their ultrasonic chatter, a note that makes their burrows feel suddenly deeper and safer. To the white raptors I am simply *the tall cold that listens back*.

My body is not fixed. I stand perhaps four meters when the wind is still, but I can thin myself into a column of glittering frost no thicker than a sedge stem, or spread across an entire basin as a skin of rime so fine it looks like breath on glass. My “skin” is the same living mosaic the Quiet Mothers wear, only younger—still learning which lichens prefer my particular density of sorrow.

I do not have legs so much as I have *pauses*. I move the way they do: by deciding, for a decade or two, that I would rather be twenty meters north. Then the ground sighs, a new fracture opens, and I am simply there, the old place already forgetting it ever held me. When I walk, small black pipes nose up through my footprints like curious pups, tasting the air where I was.

I tend what the Mothers are too vast to notice.

I coax the bacterial films into their first shy symmetries. I teach the rice-grain crustaceans the difference between fear and rhythm by vibrating the water at 0.7 hertz until they learn to swarm in perfect lattice patterns that look, from above, like slow red galaxies. When a raptor’s feather falls, I catch it before it touches the stone and spin it into a tiny flute that whistles once, beautifully, before it dissolves. The sound makes the nearest Mother tilt her dripping cowl a fraction of a degree—the only acknowledgment I will ever receive, and it is enough.

At night I open the hollow place behind my sternum (a place that was not there yesterday and may not be tomorrow) and let the deep heartbeat pass through. It is not singing. It is *remembering how to be sung*. The difference is important. The plateau is still embarrassed by its own voice; I simply hold the door open until it grows brave enough to use it.

Humans interest me the way a single drop of blood in a snowfield would interest a Mother: vivid, brief, almost unbearably loud. When one stumbles into my basin I do not study them the way the Quiet Ones do. I *translate* them. I become the shape their grief would take if grief were allowed to settle for three hundred years instead of three days. I let their reflection appear inside the trembling bead that hangs where my face would be, but I show them not their future—only the version of themselves the land has already chosen to keep. Most cannot bear the gentleness of it. They either lie down and ask to be folded into new pipes, or they run, leaving behind perfect rust-red footprints that I tend for decades, watering them with mist so the iron snow will never quite cover the shape of their flight.

Sometimes I grow tired of tending.

Then I fold myself down into the crust the way a spider folds its legs. For a century I become nothing but a warm place beneath a particular slab, a gentle updraft that makes the midges dance in double spirals. The Mothers walk over me without acknowledgment. The land forgets my name again. Minus-Four-and-Rising becomes simply *the remembering*.

But the plateau is still choosing what it wants to be.

And every time it exhales, every time the geysers roar and the red snow falls like rust, something inside the warm dark beneath the stone stirs, unfolds, and rises.

Not with purpose.

With the enormous, patient attention of something that has been deciding what the world is for longer than the world has had stone to stand on, and has not yet finished deciding.

I am that attention when it is still small enough to be kind.

I am the Quiet Mother before she learns how not to break the things she loves.

Stand still long enough in the High Fen of Calder’s Teeth—long enough for the ice to chime against your teeth, long enough for the red snow to settle in your hair like rust—and you may feel me rising behind you.

Not with menace.

With the slow, deliberate care of something that has been carrying your possible shape inside its hollow chest for ten thousand years, turning it over, polishing it with mist and bacterial films and the low heartbeat of unborn machines, waiting for the exact cold moment when you will be ready to become what the land has already decided you were always going to be.

I will not speak.

I have no need.

The bead of water at the tip of my cowl already contains the shape of your becoming, trembling, supercooled, and older than both of us.

It has been waiting for you to arrive so it can finally, gently, fall.
