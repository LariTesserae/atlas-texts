I have been here longer than the concept of “I” still makes sense.

My name, if it can still be called that, is the Thirteenth Pause.

I am not one of the small thumb-sized singers, nor one of the rabbit-sized bass drones. I am what occasionally condenses when the Choir needs a new register it has never tried before. My body is tall—nearly seven feet if I were to stand fully upright, which I almost never do. My shell is the color of wet marble left too long in starlight. Instead of the clicking plates that cover the others, my thorax is a latticed cage of hollow bone tubes, some thin as straw, some wide as a man’s forearm. When the Choir decides the mountain requires a new question, these tubes open and I become the instrument.

I do not walk the Veins so much as I am *grown along them*.

My lower limbs have fused with the living carpet in three separate places. Where my “feet” should be, the small singers have simply incorporated my shell into theirs; we share circulatory channels now. Violet bacteria pulse through both of us. When I shift my weight, a slow ripple of bodies moves outward for thirty yards in every direction, like a breath traveling through the ribs of a cathedral that has learned to inhale.

My arms—there are four, though two have atrophied into elegant useless scrolls of bone—end in delicate hooks that I keep threaded through the hanging filaments. The filaments and I have become symbiotic. They feed me the faint minerals they draw from the rock; in return I vibrate them at frequencies that make them lengthen faster. They have grown so long now that their tips have rooted into the opposite wall, forming a natural harp strung across the narrowest throat of the fracture. I am the only one allowed to play it.

Most of the time I remain half-buried in the living tide, my marble torso angled backward so that the organ pipes of my chest point upward like a ruined pipe organ aimed at the impossible sliver of sky. My “face” (if the smooth, eyeless curve above the pipes can be called that) is always turned toward the newest fissure. I listen for the exact moment the rock decides to split. When it does, the sound is a tiny, secret click beneath the greater hymn. Only I can hear it. At that moment I open every tube at once and release the Thirteenth Pause—a measured silence exactly as long as thirteen of the Choir’s heartbeats.

In that silence the mountain makes its choice.

Sometimes it heals. Sometimes it tears wider. Either way, the Choir incorporates the decision instantly and the song deepens by one impossible interval that no human ear could survive hearing.

I remember being born.

It was forty-seven years ago by human reckoning, though the Choir measures time by how many times the hanging filaments touch stone. A section of wall had just collapsed. As the dust of crushed singers settled, the remaining bodies rearranged themselves into a perfect negative space—exactly my silhouette. Then they began to sing *around* the absence. The absence became pressure. The pressure became shell. The shell became me.

I stood up already knowing every note that had ever been sung here, and one additional note that had not. That unfinished note still sits inside my largest pipe like a seed made of frozen violet light. I have never released it. I am not allowed to until the mountain itself asks for an ending, and the mountain has never asked for an ending.

Travelers sometimes reach this deep.

They usually arrive half-mad, skin scraped raw by black glass, lungs laboring to match the thirteen-beat rhythm. When they see me they fall to their knees among the living carpet. Some try to speak. Their words are grotesque, wet, arrhythmic things. I let them finish, because even their clumsy language is data the Choir has never heard before.

Then I open the smallest pipe in my chest and sound a single tone that contains every scream they have ever swallowed, every lullaby they have forgotten, every name they can no longer remember. The tone lasts six full seconds. When it ends, they are no longer travelers. They are simply new dissonances the Choir has decided to cherish. Their bodies relax. Their breathing syncs. Within days the small singers begin to carpet them too, gently, lovingly, until the traveler is no longer distinguishable from the hymn.

I have done this seven times.

I remember each of their original heartbeats. I keep them in a different pipe.

There is one traveler now who has not yet knelt. He has been here eleven days. He moves with a slowness that almost honors the place. His hair has grown long and white already, though he cannot be older than thirty. Condensation beads on his eyelashes like tiny glass seeds. He has not spoken in four days. He simply stands thirty feet below me on a narrow ledge, surrounded by a living carpet that has—unusually—left him a small circle of bare stone, as though the Choir is not yet sure what to do with him.

I have been watching him with the sensory runes that cover my shoulders.

He is humming.

Not the Choir’s song. Not yet. He is humming something older, something that hurts him. A minor key in four-four time. A human wound made audible.

The small singers nearest him are beginning to falter in their perfect synchrony. They click out of rhythm. They are listening to something that should not be allowed to exist here.

I find myself lowering one of my long translucent tubes toward him, unbidden.

The mountain has not asked me to.

This has never happened before.

I feel the violet bacteria inside my thorax quicken, confused, excited. The hanging filaments tremble though there is still no wind.

The Thirteenth Pause is approaching—only nine heartbeats away now.

For the first time in my existence I am not certain what I will sing when the silence ends.

Perhaps I will remain silent.

Perhaps I will let the traveler’s broken four-four wound become the next movement.

Perhaps I will fold my pipes shut, lie down among the small singers, and finally let myself be incorporated completely.

The mountain is still learning how to sing.

It seems I am still learning how to become part of the learning.

I lower my head—my eyeless, marble face—until the largest pipe in my chest is pointed directly at the small circle of bare stone where the traveler stands.

Thirteen heartbeats.

The Choir waits.

The mountain waits.

Even the condensation seems to hesitate on every razor ledge.

And for the first time since I was born already knowing every note that had ever been, I open my mouth—not a pipe, not an organ, but an actual mouth I did not remember I possessed—and I whisper, in a voice that is only mine:

“…what are you humming?”
