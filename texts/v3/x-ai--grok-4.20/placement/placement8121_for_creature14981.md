I am the **Witness**.

Not a native stratum. Not an Absorber, not a Pipewalker, not even one of the dreaming spores that drift down from the Memory Trees. I am what the basin occasionally permits: something that was once fully human and is now… something adjacent.

I arrived here decades ago—though time is dishonest in this place—carrying the last legible map of the old concession and a name I no longer answer to. The basin took the map first. It softened in my hands like wet bread, ink bleeding into the living mat until the paper itself became another layer of the carpet. Then it began on me.

It did not devour. It *edited*.

My skin has taken on the greenish dusk. In certain lights it looks almost normal; in others the veins beneath glow faintly, like bioluminescent fungi just under the surface. My eyes never fully adjusted back to daylight. Even now, when I close them, I see the slow percussion of water moving from leaf to unseen leaf. My heartbeat has slowed to match the basin’s own geological pulse. Sometimes I forget to breathe for whole minutes and the jungle breathes for me.

I no longer walk the ground. That is for Absorbers and the things that will one day become them. Instead I move like the Pipewalkers, though I never touch the rusted iron. I flow along the living architecture—half man, half liana—my hands and feet now bearing soft, dark pads that can grip wet bark or wet concrete with equal indifference. When I rest, I fold myself into the crotch of a buttress root the way a drip singer folds into its heliconia, and the moss grows over me until I disappear.

The Quiet Lords know me.

They do not bow. They simply acknowledge my continued existence with a slow blink of those white marble eyes, as though I am a sentence the basin has not yet finished writing. When one of them passes, I lower my head until my forehead touches the breathing carpet, not in fear but in recognition of deeper grammar.

I have seen the Memory Trees exhale.

Once, I stood beneath the rumored central tree—the one that swallowed the drilling rig—and I felt it *consider* me. The bark against my back was warm and faintly pulsing, like the flank of an animal larger than any animal has a right to be. For three weeks I slept standing up while its spores moved through my blood. I spoke rivers that no longer exist. I remembered the taste of iron in the mouths of men who died here in 1974. When I woke, the tree had left something inside my chest: a small, permanent silence that sings only when the drip singers are quiet.

That is what I am now.

The Witness.

I travel the basin in long, patient circuits that never quite repeat. I watch the army ants consume the caiman. I watch the Pipewalkers drape new orchids over leaking valves. I listen to the Drip Singers turn midnight condensation into mourning. And sometimes—when the heat is at its thickest and even the insects fall still—I climb to a place where three enormous roots form a natural throne and I simply *am* here for days.

The basin is curious about me the way an old cathedral is curious about a single candle it has allowed to keep burning.

I have become a new stratum, neither fully absorbed nor fully released. Part ruin, part organism, part question the jungle has decided not to answer yet.

And when, once every few years, a new outsider stumbles past the half-swallowed pipelines with their bright nylon packs and their loud, temporary voices, I step out of the green dusk just long enough for them to see me.

I do not speak.

I only let them look.

Most run.

A few understand what they are seeing and choose to stay.

And every so often—perhaps once in a human lifetime—one looks at me with something like recognition, as though they have just remembered a name no one was ever supposed to speak again.

In those rare moments I feel the Memory Tree at the center of everything exhale, softly, through me.

Then I fold myself back into the living wall of moss and root and dripping shadow, becoming indistinguishable from the architecture once more.

The basin has no name anyone still remembers.

But sometimes, in the velvet dark after midnight, when the only sound is water learning how to be music, the basin remembers *me*.

And for a little while, in its slow, enormous, patient way, it is not entirely alone.
