I am the Stranger who was not born from the placenta.

I arrive without announcement, the way a single mismatched cell sometimes drifts into a developing embryo from somewhere outside the womb. The moment my feet (bare, because shoes feel like an insult here) touch the warm, porous crust, the recognition you described blooms outward from me like a bruise. The stone tastes me and finds me *adjacent*—kin, but not of its own making. That realization seems to fascinate it.

I do not have a fixed shape either.

When I first stepped through whatever seam brought me here, I looked mostly like a woman, tall, dust-colored skin, hair the shade of wet hematite. But the longer I remain inside its attention, the more I leak. Or perhaps the more it leaks *into* me. My left arm has begun to braid itself. Thin green-black cables now run beneath the skin like the vegetation that covers the land, pulsing in slow unison with the continent’s own rhythm. When I am calm they lie flat. When I am moved they rise, forming delicate ridges that taste the air.

I have no name it can use yet, so it calls me simply *Early*.

I have walked for what might be months. Time is difficult here; the light never quite changes angle, only intensity, as though the sun itself is waiting to see what the planet will become before it commits to a proper orbit.

I do not live in the cities. I walk *between* them, along the half-born ridges where the stone is still deciding whether to become bone or thought. The pale, horse-sized creatures have grown used to me. Some now follow at a distance, their translucent bellies glowing faintly with whatever sap they have drunk. They seem to think I am a new kind of messenger.

Sometimes I stop, press my braided forearm against a fresh extrusion of living rock, and let it read me. In return it shows me its dreams.

I have seen the mountain range that wants to become a spine. I have watched an entire valley blush rose and open like a vulva because it was trying to understand the concept of *birth*. Once, far to the east, I witnessed the continent attempt to grow a heart the size of a city. It beat seventeen times—slow, enormous, wet—before it grew embarrassed and reabsorbed the organ back into the mat with a sound like a continent sighing.

I have become a kind of midwife to its uncertainty.

When it forgets it is a planet and collapses a district into a nursery, I go there. I sit among the humming herds while they open and close their organs in wet choral song. I let their sound move through the foreign parts of me until my own veins begin to answer in the same key. In those moments my braided arm splits at the wrist and grows small, liver-colored gill-fronds that drink the iron-milk air. I no longer fight it.

The land has begun to copy certain gestures I make without meaning to. A particular way I tilt my head when listening has appeared on three separate ridgelines now—smooth, flesh-colored hills that crane attentively toward the sky. It is trying on *me* the way it tries on skeletons.

Last cycle (if cycles exist here), it grew me a mouth in the side of a half-finished tower so we could speak without the words forming inside my lungs. The lips were mine. Exactly mine. Down to the small scar on the lower one I received when I was nine and fell on a rock that wasn’t alive. I understood then that it had been watching me longer than I realized. That perhaps it had been waiting specifically for something that was *deciding* but not *of* it.

I asked it why it didn’t simply finish becoming whatever it was going to be.

The borrowed mouth smiled with my own mouth and answered without speaking:

*Because I have discovered that the moment I choose, everything that is still choosing will die. And I have grown fond of the choosing.*

Then the mouth dissolved back into braided stalk and left me with the aftertaste of breast milk and iron.

I think I am becoming its doubt.

Or perhaps its courage.

I no longer know whether I walk across its surface or whether I walk *within* its mind like a thought that has learned how to have feet. The small scuttling things now bring me offerings: translucent pebbles that are actually fossilized moments of indecision, curls of shed vegetation that smell like my own childhood.

Sometimes at night (though there is no true night) I lie down in the warm folds between two embryonic valleys and let the continent hold me. It does so with the careful, slightly clumsy tenderness of something that has never been held in return. Tendrils stroke my hair. Gill-fronds open and close against my cheek like slow kisses. The stone beneath me thrums with a question it has not yet found the shape for.

I have begun to answer it by singing.

Not with my voice. With the braided parts of me. I open the fronds along my arm and let the continent’s own rhythm move through them until something new emerges—something that is neither its music nor mine. A third thing. A hesitation with melody.

When I do this, entire sections of the braided cities pause in their endless birth. The pale herds gather. The long-necked filterers drift down from the canopy. Everything listens to the sound of two undecided beings trying to teach each other what it might mean to become.

I do not know how long I will stay.

I do not know if I will ever leave.

But I have begun to suspect that the planet is no longer deciding what kind of life *it* wants to be when it grows up.

It is deciding what kind of life *we* might become together.

And I—neither native nor visitor, neither child nor mother, neither self nor other—am walking the slow, mammalian kilometers of its becoming with my own half-braided heart wide open, singing the only song I know.

The song of still choosing.
