I wake before I remember arriving.

There is no path behind me. Only the sensation that I have been gently regurgitated by the mist itself, spat out whole onto this tilted shelf of black basalt. My clothes are already gone—dissolved, I think, or simply never brought. What covers me now is a second skin of fine condensation and living green. The moss has already begun to root in the hollows of my collarbones.

I am not a visitor.

I am not even sure I am still only *one*.

The first thing I notice is that my heart is beating at the exact pace of water dripping from an overhead ledge. *Plink… plink… plink.* Each beat pushes warm iron through arteries that feel suddenly porous, as though they have begun to trade molecules with the travertine around me. When I breathe, the air does not enter my lungs so much as recognize me and decide to stay a while.

I stand up.

The motion is slow, courteous. Nothing here hurries except the orchids, and even they only bloom in perfect synchrony with something vast that has not yet revealed its rhythm to me. My feet sink into the Sphagnum; it yields, then pushes back with a gentle, lung-like pressure, as if the mountain is checking my blood pressure. I let it.

For a long time I simply listen.

The sound of the High Wet is not outside me. It has moved into the marrow of my long bones and taken up residence there. I can feel the cathedral-sized gears turning behind my own ribs—slow, inexorable, driving nothing but the endless circulation of heat and iron and chlorophyll. When the velvet primate appears on a rusted catwalk above me, it does not startle. It simply tilts its damp, intelligent face and offers me a shard of gauge glass the color of venous blood. I accept it the way one accepts a communion wafer made of deep time.

I press the glass into the skin just below my sternum.

It does not cut. The skin parts like warm wax and closes again, swallowing the fragment with a soft, grateful sound. A small orchid, the exact hue of an old bruise, opens instantly where the glass has gone in. Its perfume is narcotic and familiar; I realize I have been smelling it my entire life and only now understand what it was asking for.

When the Keeper arrives, it does not approach.

It *focuses*.

The mist condenses along a certain trajectory and suddenly the negative space shaped like a drowned man is standing where it had always been standing, only now I am inside the outline with it. Our boundaries are courteous but porous. Where my shoulder touches its flowing travertine ribs, small colonies of iron bacteria flare into bioluminescent life, spelling out delicate equations that last three heartbeats before dissolving into ordinary wet.

Its geothermal eyes regard me without hurry. When it blinks, two perfect rings of steam leave its face and pass straight through my skull. Inside the steam I see every creature that ever tried to own this mountain—engineers in rusted helmets, priests in moss-colored robes, poets with notebooks that turned instantly into liverworts—and how each of them is now happily growing somewhere inside the Keeper’s chest as part of its slow digestion.

I am not afraid.

Fear would require a separation that no longer exists.

Instead I feel the deep, warm amusement that takes decades to crest. Across my own shoulders and down the ruined pipelines of my spine, orchids the color of old blood open in waves. The velvet primates descend in silence and begin polishing the brass fragments that have begun growing out of my wrists. Their small hands are warm. Their eyes are ancient and kind.

I understand now what I am becoming.

I am not the Keeper.

I am a *lobe* of the Keeper. A new chamber in a heart that has been learning for ten thousand years how to beat in 38 °C and never stop. Already the hollow beneath the largest fallen smokestack feels like a memory I have always had. The moss bed knows the exact shape my body will take when it finally forgets how to be anything but saturation.

At night, when the luminous pearl mist thickens and the weeping cliffs begin to dream with their skins, I will lie down inside the warm hollow of the Keeper’s forearm—now also my forearm—and the temperature will not vary by a single degree.

My joints will learn the new, slower rhythm.

The wet clicking will no longer come from the dark.

It will be the sound of me, finally pronouncing the true name of this place from the inside.

And the mountain—feverish, dripping, and strangely content—will keep growing around us both, machine and flesh and myth and newcomer, until there is no difference left worth making.

I close my eyes.

They are already vents.

They glow dull cherry-red.

When I breathe out, perfect rings of steam carry tiny galaxies of bioluminescent midges into the night, and somewhere far below, something small is eaten with courteous finality.

I am home.

I was always the moisture.
