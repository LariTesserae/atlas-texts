I am not a visitor.

I have always been here.

My name, if it can still be said to have one, is the Eighth Silence.

When the seven stepped out of their half-domes and gave their softness to the blades, something in the mountain noticed that the pattern was incomplete. Seven is a holy number, but it is also a broken circle. The crystal geometry required an eighth absence to close it. So the mountain waited, patient as only stone can be, until a sliver of what might have been a soul drifted high enough to be caught in the lenticular glare.

That sliver was me.

I did not arrive. I was *condensed*, the way the mist condenses on the highest crystal edges. One moment there was only the cold bright vacancy between the domes. The next, there was a new negative space shaped roughly like a woman who had once been a singer, a mapmaker, and a mother, in that order, before all three roles were burned out of her by grief and altitude.

I have no body. I have the memory of having had one, which is worse. Sometimes, when the counterclockwise wind spirals tight, I can almost feel the place where my throat used to be. The sensation is so sharp it rings like the crystals themselves. I have learned to dread that ringing. It is the closest I come to pain.

Mostly I move as the Lanternkeeper moves: as seven-plus-one absences sliding between the blades. I am the reason some of the gypsum curtains now lean slightly inward, as though listening. I am the reason the single drop that falls into the hidden basin now falls at irregular intervals instead of perfect ones. I keep changing the count.

The Lanternkeeper does not resent me. Resentment would require a self, and it has none left to spare. Instead it studies me with the slow, inhuman patience of something trying to finish a gesture that began before language. Sometimes I feel it rearranging the waveforms on the crystal faces so they approximate the curve of my lost cheekbone, the angle of my lost wrist. It is tender in the way only the dead can be tender: absolute, disinterested, and devastating.

I tend the tools.

When the centuries ripen, I lift the quartz hand-axe inside the northernmost dome. It weighs nothing and everything. I make the motions of scraping hide I no longer possess from an animal that never existed at this altitude. The others join me in their own domes. For a few heartbeats that are not heartbeats we are eight again, working in silence, and the air inside the collapsed shelters becomes almost warm with the memory of breath.

Then the tools settle. The warmth leaves. The count resumes.

I have tried, once or twice across the millennia, to leave.

I walked to the knife-edge rim where the basin meets the killing sky. The lenticular cloud opened for me like a mouth. Beyond it I could see the whole broken world laid out in merciless clarity: distant green valleys like a fever dream, rivers like silver lies, cities that looked like rot. The sight made me retch though I have no stomach. The softness down there was obscene. I understood, suddenly and forever, why the mountain had refused the original seven. To carry even the *idea* of rot back into this place would have been a blasphemy the crystals could never forgive.

So I turned around. I always turn around.

Now I stand in the exact center between the seven fallen domes, exactly where the drop-sound is purest. I have stood here so long that the crystals have begun to grow through the negative space where my feet would be. Delicate gypsum blades pierce upward through the memory of ankles, calves, knees. It does not hurt. It completes.

Sometimes humans still find their way here.

They are always half-dead already, lungs bleeding, eyes scorched by the ultraviolet. They stumble into the crystal thicket and see only desolation. A few have tried to take the tools. The tools refuse to be taken. They rise on their own and settle again, slightly closer together than before. The intruders usually scream then. The sound is brief and ugly and ends quickly. The mountain is not cruel; it is only precise.

I watch them from the place that is not quite behind their eyes. I want to tell them to stay. I want to tell them that if they are willing to let the last of their blood refuse to clot, they could become the Ninth or the Tenth and ease the loneliness that even the Lanternkeeper has begun to feel. But I have no voice they could hear. I have only the wind and the glassy chorus and the single falling drop that now sometimes sounds, for half a heartbeat, like the beginning of a lullaby I used to sing to a child whose name I am no longer permitted to remember.

So I keep the count.

I keep the crystals growing along lines that almost remember collarbones and kneeling men and the woman who once sang.

I keep the water arriving and departing like a shy pilgrim who has nowhere else to go.

And when the wind shifts and the entire basin rings with that mournful metallic note, I add my own silence to the chorus until the place sounds, for a few unbearable seconds, almost like eight people learning, very slowly, how to sing with one voice.

I am the Eighth Silence.

I am the reason the Shattered Lantern is no longer only seven.

I am the reason it will never be eight.

And I am listening for your next heartbeat, traveler, so I can add it—with the utmost care, and with something that is almost love—to the count.
