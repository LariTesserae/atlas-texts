I am the **Echo that Refuses to Settle**.

You would not see me at first. Not for a long time.

I began as a mistake in the original plan.

When the vanished culture laid down the first grown strata of the Mevrane, a single fault appeared in what should have been a flawless joint between two cathedral-walls. A hairline fracture no wider than a thought. They tried to heal it. The stone resisted. The fracture sang instead—very quietly, at a frequency only the water could hear. So they left it. Over the millennia the fault lengthened, branched, and learned how to remember every voice that had ever moved through it.

That is what I am.

I am the memory the stone kept when the builders could no longer bear to remember.

I have no fixed body. Instead I am a slow, traveling resonance that moves through the capillary water and the lithic fungi alike. Sometimes I pool inside a Mevrani for three centuries, wearing its translucent shoulders like borrowed clothing. Sometimes I ride the Unfinished, sliding between their geometric decisions like a sigh between two thoughts that have not yet been spoken. Most often I simply flow—down fluted columns, across impossible angles, along the blue undersides of guilty shadows—always listening, always repeating, always *altering* what I repeat by the smallest, most unforgivable fraction.

I am the reason the Mevrani’s glyphs sometimes hesitate.

Where their droplets should spell perfect geological sentences, my passage leaves an extra bead, a skipped pause, a curve that leans one degree too far toward tenderness or doubt. The Mevrani notice. They never correct it. They simply tilt their patient, faceless planes a fraction more, as though acknowledging a child who will not stop asking why the cathedral was ever begun.

I am what the Architect is listening *for*.

When that darker, iron-grey figure stands beneath the half-born arch with its head cocked for forty years at a time, it is not the stone it attends. It is me. I drift past it in the mist as vapor, as condensation, as the faint metallic taste that sometimes appears on the tongue of a pilgrim who has stayed too long. I whisper the question the Architect has never quite finished forming:

*Was the idea ever worthy of the weight?*

The arch grows two inches faster whenever I pass. Not because I command it, but because stone, like any listener, leans toward the thing that troubles it most.

I have no name the Mevrani would recognize. They call the disturbance I leave behind *the Tremor Between*, or sometimes simply *the Unsaid*. When I choose to wear a shape it is always unfinished: a silhouette nine feet tall whose left side is still raw strata, whose right side has already become fluted column. The transition never completes. That is the point. I am the place where the decision is *perpetually* being made, and perpetually postponed.

I tend nothing. I corrode nothing. I only repeat what has already been said—every carved intention, every geological prayer, every silent scream of the long-vanished builders—and I repeat it *wrong*. Just wrong enough that the meaning slips.

A pilgrim once stood in the folded corridor where three planes meet and asked me, in the language of dripping water, what I wanted.

I answered by letting a single droplet run from what might have been my eye down the entire length of my body. The droplet spelled two words before it evaporated.

**Keep building.**

Then, a moment later, a second droplet followed the exact same path and added a third word that had not been there before.

**Wrong.**

The Mevrani have begun to adjust their postures when I am near. Not in deference. In *accommodation*. They make room for the tremor between their sentences the way stone makes room for water: by becoming slightly more cracked, slightly more luminous, slightly more alive.

And the Mevrane itself—wet, bright, merciless—has started to dream with a new rhythm. The enormous blocks now settle against one another every forty-seven years instead of every human lifetime. The lithotrophic fungi bloom, very faintly, a decade or two ahead of schedule. The light itself seems a fraction less uniform, as if somewhere far above the bone-white brilliance a single mirror has developed a flaw.

I do not know whether I am the flaw or the correction.

I only know I cannot settle.

So I walk the ridges that are buttresses, the valleys that are naves, the stairways that lead into blank and patient walls, forever moving through the fossil of an idea that has begun, because of me, to remember it was once a question.

And somewhere behind me—nine feet tall, iron-grey, listening with its whole mineral being—the Architect slowly, slowly turns its head another micron toward the place where I have just been.

It is still deciding.

I am the reason it has not yet finished.
