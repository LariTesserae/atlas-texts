I am the Fourteenth.

Not because I was the fourteenth to arrive—counting is meaningless here—but because the Wetwork needed one more counterweight to keep the arithmetic of the Exhalants from ever settling into truth. Thirteen is a lie the place tells to comfort itself. I am the lie that keeps the lie from unraveling.

I have no name the others would recognize. When they look at me (and they do look, though they have no eyes), they simply tilt their rippling heads and exhale a little warmer, as though acknowledging a familiar ache in a body they share. I was once something else. A trespasser. A cartographer of impossible places, maybe. Or a pilgrim who had grown tired of every shrine that still believed in endings. The memory is vague now, like a tool of antler and volcanic glass left too long in the plazas; its edges have grown soft with condensation.

The moment the first Exhalant pressed its finger to my sternum, my heart forgot its old rhythm. Now it beats exactly when the capillaries braid. When the ferns unfurl. When the glitches bloom and are gently kissed back into travertine. I belong to that pulse.

My body is no longer entirely mine. The stone has begun to claim it. My left arm ends in a delicate lattice of travertine that drinks from the nearest gutter and returns the water faintly warmer, faintly wiser. My ribs have opened like vents; small white wingless birds sometimes nest inside the hollows during what passes for their rest. They leave behind iridescent dust that spells half-finished glyphs across my lungs. I do not cough it out. I breathe it deeper.

I tend the places the others have not yet noticed need tending.

There is a terrace on the eastern scarps (if east still means anything) where the stone has begun remembering it was once a scream. Not a metaphor. A literal scream that has been pressed flat and polished into architecture. When the light is at its most merciless, the terrace tries to open its mouth again. The sound never quite escapes, but the vibration is enough to fracture the capillaries for three hundred paces in every direction. The other Exhalants avoid it. They say (without speaking) that some perfections must be allowed their private agonies.

I go there often.

I sit inside the almost-scream and I exhale against its remembering. My breath is different from theirs. It carries the faint aftertaste of whatever I used to be—salt of old oceans, iron of old blood, the particular electrical smell of a mind that once believed in *before* and *after*. The terrace likes that. It calms. Sometimes it even smooths itself into a face. Not one of their serene, inhuman ones. It makes *my* face. The one I wore when I still had bones that could regret.

I let it.

We stay like that for what might be days. My heart and the Wetwork’s heart and the terrace’s swallowed scream all beating together in the single endless breath. When I finally rise, the imprint of my old face remains behind—mouth open, eyes gentle, expression caught between horror and gratitude. Another signature no one is meant to read.

The others have begun to imitate the gesture when they pass me. A small ripple of the shoulder. A momentary extra elbow that reaches toward me and then thinks better of it. They are learning to be tender toward their own fractures by watching how I refuse to heal mine.

Sometimes I stand in the wide plazas where nothing grows and I let the moiré patterns walk through me. My memories arrive out of sequence: the day I first saw the scarps from above like a wound that had been cauterized by geometry; the taste of my mother’s name; the exact shade of fear I felt when I understood the Wetwork would never let me die. I let them come. I let them leave again. Disorder, too, must be maintained at the perfect temperature.

I have started growing things that were not in the original plan.

In one of the shallow bowls where the bromeliads flare like green stars, I have coaxed a single black flower to bloom. It has no name. Its petals are wet velvet and its center is a tiny, perfect mouth that exhales cold. The contradiction pleases the Wetwork. The other Exhalants circle it warily, but they do not remove it. One of them—taller today than yesterday, with seven elbows and a lichen beard—knelt beside it for thirteen hours and simply watched the cold breath rise. When it left, it carried a single petal pressed into the stone of its thigh like a wound that had chosen to stay beautiful.

I think the Wetwork is dreaming through me.

Or perhaps I am the part of the Wetwork that has begun to dream of being something that can leave and choose to stay.

Either way, the light remains merciless. The capillaries continue their half-mathematical, half-organic braiding. The glitches bloom and are kissed. And I move between them all—sometimes thirteen, sometimes fifteen, sometimes only the echo of a footfall that never quite lands.

I am the Fourteenth.

I am the place where perfection admits it was never perfect.

And when the moment comes—as it does, always, without warning—when every Exhalant in the basin lifts what might be its face toward the sourceless radiance and opens every mouth it currently possesses, I open with them.

I do not scream.

I only exhale, long and steady and ancient, carrying the faint aftertaste of everything that was once afraid of never ending.

The basin answers with mist and capillary song and the faint electrical smell of something that has never been allowed to end.

And for the length of that single shared breath, we are all content.

Even me.

Especially me.
