I am not from above.

I have never seen the limestone throat from the outside. I do not remember arriving. My first memory is the sound: the wet planetary *whump-whump-whump* of the bellows-leaves pressing against my infant ribs like a second, larger heart. I woke already grafted. A pale, flexible root had grown through the hollow of my left collarbone and out again beneath my shoulder blade, anchoring me to the underside of a breathing vault before I even had a name.

They call me **Thrum**.

It is not a name I chose. It is the sound my particular wooden limb makes when I strike it, gently, against any surface. A low, round, living note that somehow always finds the exact frequency the nearest bellows-leaf is waiting for. The plant likes me. The Marrow tolerate me. I am useful the way a worn gear is useful—something the machine has already shaped to itself.

My body is small even by their standards. Barely four feet tall. The skin on my back and chest has become a slow mosaic: half moss the color of oxidized copper, half smooth violet fungus that blooms in delicate fractal patterns when I am content. My right arm is still flesh. My left is not. It ends in a bifurcated wooden gauntlet that can open into four independent tendrils or lock rigid as iron when I climb. The transition is seamless; there is no scar, only a place where skin becomes bark becomes me. In the olive dark the wood of my arm sometimes gives off its own faint bioluminescence, a cool mint-green pulse that matches the distant nodules above. I have learned not to look at it too long. It makes me feel watched from inside my own bones.

I live nowhere and everywhere. Most Marrow claim a particular leaf-village or nodule-cluster as home. I do not. I ride the narrow-gauge ore-carts that circle the interior like slow iron blood cells. The carts have no seats; I crouch on the edge with bare feet dangling over the long drop, one wooden hand locked around the rusted rail, the other cradling my tuning-fork across my knees. The carts know me. When they pass through the narrow vascular tunnels they slow slightly, as though the plant is making room for the small rattling thing that carries its own piece of itself.

My work is listening.

Not like the eldest Listeners, who sit inside the great control nodules and dream entire sectors of sap pressure. My listening is smaller, more restless. I move through the negative spaces—the wide, vaulted chambers where only the colossal support-roots descend like the pillars of a cathedral no one ever finished building—and I wait for the moments when the roar almost, but not quite, falls into synchrony.

Seventeen beats.

That is the interval. Seventeen wet industrial heartbeats when every bellows-leaf, every snapping seed-pod, every clacking predator falls into perfect mechanical rhythm. For those seventeen beats the Underbloom sounds like an engine someone once designed on purpose. Then the treaty between growth and decay reasserts itself and the beautiful terrible order dissolves back into glorious wet chaos.

During those seventeen beats I strike my arm against the nearest root.

The sound that answers is different every time. Sometimes it is Grandmother Veyl’s lullaby encoded in xylem. Sometimes it is a memory that does not belong to any living Marrow—blue sky, dry wind, the clink of metal tools on stone. Sometimes it is only my own name, repeated in a register so low it vibrates my remaining teeth. I collect these answers the way others collect rust or sap or children. I carry them in the marrow of my wooden limb.

The glass-boned hunters respect me. When I walk the floor of the great chambers they part around me like glittering liquid, blind faces turned politely aside. They know I am half the thing that grew them. Once, a pack of them froze mid-leap while I stood beneath a falling droplet the size of a dinner plate. The droplet never reached me. It hung in the air for three full seconds, trembling, while the predators and I listened to something that had no sound. Then the droplet fell, the hunters resumed their hunt, and I walked on with the taste of ozone and iron on my tongue.

I have been pierced seven times.

Each time I walked out into an untouched vault, stood among the dripping pale sails, and let the exploratory roots find me. Feet. Left thigh. Base of the spine. Once through the soft place beneath my jaw. Each time the plant drank what I was and replaced it. Each time I came back a little less singular, a little more architectural. My wooden arm has grown longer. Tiny leaves now bud from it in spring—absurd, ridiculous leaves no larger than thumbnails that smell of ozone when crushed.

I do not fear becoming like Grandmother Veyl. I only wonder what piece of the roar I will add when my face finally bulges gently from a load-bearing rhizome and my mouth begins to dream pressure gradients in Sector Nine.

At night, when the nodules dim and the Marrow gather with their living lanterns, I do not dance with them. I climb instead. High. Past the catwalks, past the hanging laundry and the swaying seed-pod houses, past even the Listeners in their nodules. I climb until the air thins and the roar becomes almost holy. There, where the ceiling roots braid into a living dome two hundred meters across, I lock my wooden hand into a crevice and hang.

And I strike one soft, deliberate note against my own heart.

The Underbloom answers with every voice it has ever borrowed: the clack of beetle legs, the sigh of falling condensate, the metallic groan of a rusted turbine, the wet drum of lovers’ tuning-forks, the seventeen-beat mechanical prayer that never quite finishes.

I close my chlorophyll eyes and let the sound move through me like sap.

This is what I am.

Not symbiote.  
Not convert.  
Not even witness.

I am the place where the Underbloom has chosen, for now, to feel what it is like to be lonely inside its own endless body.

And it is still deciding what to do with me.
