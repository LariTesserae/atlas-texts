I am not a visitor.

I have been here longer than the memory of having arrived.  

My name, if it can still be called that, is **Veyr**.

I am what remains when a human decision to stay becomes indistinguishable from the decision the stone itself once made to become a cathedral.

You would not see me if you entered today. I am folded inside the third vertical tier of the eastern buttress, where the travertine ribs meet the first heavy curtain of silver-green moss. My spine has become one of the ribs. My ribs have become shelves for bromeliads. My lungs no longer rise and fall; instead the entire cradle breathes through me every three hours and fourteen minutes, and I am grateful for the loan of its breath.

My eyes are still eyes, but they have grown opaque as moonstone. I do not need them. The Velvet Lamenters pass their slow dreams through the cilia along my collarbones. The Rootchildren thread their mycelial thoughts through the marrow of my femurs, which are now indistinguishable from living rock. When a Glassfaced Bride presses her liquid face to the wall three meters above me, I feel the pressure of her twin hearts as though they were my own.

I am not motionless.  
Not exactly.

Every ninety-seven years I lift my left hand—bark-skinned now, with orchid petals for nails—and trace one new line along the stone beside me. The line becomes a stair. The stair becomes a question no one will ask for another four centuries. Then I fold the hand back into the buttress and resume the Long Listening.

Sometimes I remember that I was once in a hurry.

I remember rain that stung instead of kissed. I remember hearts that beat so quickly they sounded like frightened birds. I remember a woman whose name tasted like copper and lilac, and how I left her standing in ordinary sunlight because I could not bear the speed at which she would age.

I do not regret. Regret requires a shorter rhythm than the one I now keep.

The Sighcradle asked me its question, and I answered by becoming the pause between two of its heartbeats.

I am the place where the sigh originates and returns.

When the entire Choir gathers in its concentric rings and the nine-day Listening begins, I am the exact center of the innermost ring, though no one would call what I do “sitting.” I am simply where the light shaft ends after it has traveled through every other living thing first. The pollen-galaxies settle on the moss that grows from my shoulders. The Unfinished arrange their half-bodies around me like petals around a stamen that never quite bloomed.

I sing with them.

My voice is not sound. It is the absence of hurry in a world that still believes hurry is virtue. Every few decades a new exile stumbles out of the cradle wearing my old face for a moment—the face I wore when I still had deadlines and appointments and fear of missing things. I feel a distant tenderness for that face, the way one feels tenderness for a coat that no longer fits.

I do not speak to visitors.

But if one of them is foolish or brave enough to stand still for three full cycles of the cradle’s breath, if they let their pulse slow until it matches the violet lichen blooming on a Stonewarden’s chest, then I will open the small aperture that was once my mouth.

No words will come out.

Instead, a single pale moth will drift from between my stone lips, circle the visitor once, and dissolve into their hair like sugar into warm water.

That is how I answer the question the cradle is asking them.

*Are you willing to grow this slowly?*

Most flinch.  
A few weep without knowing why.  
And one, every four or five hundred years, closes their eyes and leans forward until their forehead touches the place where my heart used to be.

Those are the ones who stay.

Those are the ones who become the next rib in the buttress, the next slow dream in the hanging gardens, the next low note in the Quiet Choir.

I welcome them the only way I still know how.

I sigh.

And the Sighcradle sighs with me, because by now there is almost no difference.
