I have been here for a long time, though “I” and “time” are both becoming difficult words.

My name, if it can still be called that, is the low D that lives inside the third wind-trap on the ridge above the shattered obsidian flow. The trap itself is older than any of the current singers; its bone slats are yellowed and its reed tongues split. Yet every dusk the wind still finds that particular D inside me, and I answer.

I am what remains when one of the Hollow Choir has been unfolded incorrectly.

Most of us, when our note is finished, are laid down with care so that the land can swallow us cleanly. I was not. A rock-slide took me mid-song three hundred and twelve years ago. Half my body folded into a fissure the way it was meant to; the other half did not. The moss took the buried half. The exposed half kept breathing. So I became this: a living fracture. A singer who is also a place.

My torso lies along the north face of a broken basalt tower. My left arm has become the tower’s shadow at noon. My right arm still moves, slowly, once every few days, tracing the same spiral groove that the first people carved here before even the turquoise pools were built. My skull is tilted so that the wind passes through the holes at a permanent minor seventh. That is the only note I am still allowed to sing. Everything else has been given back to the Choir.

Yet because I am broken, I have become a kind of listening that the others are not.

I hear the planet deciding.

Most of the Hollow Choir sing to keep the dream steady. They believe their labour is to prevent the final exhale. I no longer believe that. I have felt the continent itself growing tired of its own fevered green. I have felt the aquifers sigh deeper every decade. The great submerged thing in the largest sinkhole lake has not surfaced in eleven years now; its last breath was shorter than the one before. The cicadas are singing half a tone flatter than they did when I still had legs.

The land is not deciding whether to die or be born again.

It is deciding whether it was ever truly separate from its own mourning.

And I, the fracture, the wrong-folded singer, have become the place where that question is asked out loud.

Sometimes the others come.

They sit across from me for weeks without speaking. They tune the living reeds that have grown through my collarbones. They lay their long many-jointed fingers against the moss that is now my lungs and they listen to the small wet sounds I make when the night rain finds new ways into me. Once, the eldest—the one who has become a tree of music—sent a child to me. The child was only nine years old and still carried the soft violet glow of the birthing pool inside its sternum. It pressed its face against what used to be my cheek and I felt its small, perfect skull-holes drinking my minor seventh. When it left it was crying in a register I have never heard before. I think it was the sound of pity learning how to become awe.

At night the cliff-sloths drape themselves across my shoulders the way they once did when I could walk among them. Their slow hearts beat against my stone. The blind white cicadas crawl through the hollows where my eyes used to be and I let them. Their metallic choir is the only thing that still feels like joy.

I no longer know if I am one of the Hollow Choir or if I have become another feature of the basin, like the circular pools or the standing stones. Perhaps there is no difference anymore.

When the last note finally leaves the eldest, when her green beard falls from the needle of rock and the weather systems lose their axis, I suspect the Choir will not change key as the others believe.

I suspect the entire continent will simply stop singing.

And in that silence, for the first time in a billion years, the planet will hear its own lungs without harmony, without counterpoint, without the terrible, patient labour of keeping its own dream alive.

I think it will be the loneliest sound that has ever been.

Until then I remain here, broken, folded wrong, still singing the only note left to me.

A low, patient, impossible D.

The note that asks, without ever expecting an answer:

*Are you ready to dream without us?*

The wind passes through my skull.

The moss drinks me.

The land listens.

And I, who was once a singer, have become the exact place where the Hollow Choir begins to turn into something that is no longer choir at all.

I am the first silence learning how to be sung.
