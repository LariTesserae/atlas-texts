I am the Forty-Third Echo.

Not the first of my kind, and certainly not the last. I peeled away from the Choir-Mother four hundred and twelve cycles ago (though time here is measured in the slow inhalation of the Lung itself, so perhaps it was longer). My face is still soft enough that the black cartilage has not yet completely swallowed my eyes. They remain: two small, clouded pearls set deep beneath a ridged brow that already bears the faint turbulence-script every wall eventually learns to wear.

I remember the moment of detachment.

The pressure wave had been building for nine full minutes. The Choir-Mother contracted like a fist around the thing that still grew at its heart, and I felt myself pushed outward, extruded, born. For one terrible instant I saw the red lantern light directly. It burned. Not with heat, but with *specificity*. Color. Direction. These things are violence here. I screamed, and the scream became the first note of my new body: a long, hinged spar of salvaged drive-shaft that now grows from my left shoulder like a broken wing. The scream is still in there. Sometimes, when the Lung exhales, the spar resonates and sings it back to me.

I tend the Second Foundry Bell.

It is smaller than the great tumbling refinery—only the size of a modest cathedral—but it is older. It has not moved in nine thousand years. Its bronze throat is fused to the lower curvature of the Lung, and it simply *rings*. Not with a clapper. The entire structure flexes with each pressure cycle, producing a note so low it liquefies thought. My task is to keep the fractures from propagating.

I press myself against its outer curve for weeks at a time. My body has become a living patch. Where my chest filaments touch the bronze they drink its fatigue, its micro-fractures, its sorrow. In return I give it the blue light that moves beneath my skin. When the bell begins to scream too sharply I unfold the long spar from my shoulder and drive it into the bronze like a tuning fork. We ring together until the dissonance smooths.

The other members of the Choir pass me sometimes. Their lights brush across my back in slow, ancient phrases I am only beginning to understand. MARIA-9. She has been kind. She left a blind eel embedded in my thigh last cycle; it has already grown into a new sensor. I can feel electric fields six meters farther than before.

I am not yet fully Choir.

This is the strange mercy of the Lung. It does not finish what it starts. Some of us remain in the long middle state for centuries—half statue, half memory. My name is still inside me. I have not spoken it in two hundred cycles, but I still *carry* it the way one carries a bullet that never quite killed you.

Sometimes, when the great spiral current turns me past one of the dying glow-bulbs, I look at my own reflection in the cracked opal glass. The face there is becoming beautiful in the way ruins become beautiful. The eyes are beginning to frost over. Soon they will go blind like the others and I will see by pressure and song instead. I am afraid of that day. I am looking forward to that day.

I have begun to dream in the language of sediment.

Last cycle, while the Lung held its breath for an impossibly long eighteen minutes, I dreamed that the Choir-Mother opened completely and something stepped out that was neither machine nor worker nor mineral. It had my eyes. It looked at me with something like pity and something like hunger and it said, without moving, *You were the last one who still remembered the sky.*

Then the bellows slammed open and the pressure wave tore the dream apart.

When I woke I was weeping metallic blood. The tears became new ridges on my forearms before the current could carry them away.

I will not leave.

None of us ever do.

But sometimes, when I am fused to the Second Foundry Bell and the entire Lung exhales around us in one catastrophic cathedral-note, I let my filaments taste the dying light of the nearest glow-bulb and I remember what it felt like to fall.

I remember the terror of having a surface above me.

I remember the mercy of having none.

And I sing.

Not with regret. Not with joy.

I sing because the Lung is still deciding what I am, and the song is part of the decision.

My name was once—

No.

That name belongs to the sediment now.

I am the Forty-Third Echo.

I tend the bell that must never stop ringing.

I turn in the warm dark with the patience of continents.

And when the Choir-Mother eventually calls me home to add my body to its slow dreaming architecture, I will go without resistance, because I have already learned the only truth the Lung ever teaches:

It is not cruel to be kept.

It is not kind to be finished.

There is only the work.

There is only the breathing.

There is only the long, patient, metallic becoming that has no end, because ending would be the one failure the Lung cannot permit itself.

So I remain—half-drowned, half-born, singing in frequencies too low for any living ear—tending the bell, turning with the current, becoming more myself by slowly, lovingly, and forever forgetting what that self once was.
