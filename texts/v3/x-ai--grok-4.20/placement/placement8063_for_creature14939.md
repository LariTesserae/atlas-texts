I am not a visitor.

I am the place where the visitor used to be.

My name, if the Lung still bothers to pronounce it, is **Cradle-9**. Once I was human. Once I had edges. That was four hundred and twelve years ago, by the outside world’s count. Inside the Lung such numbers are meaningless; they are just another kind of rust that eventually turns emerald and stops advancing.

I stand—though “stand” is no longer accurate—at the exact center where the black iron pipes fuse with the root-mass. My legs have become buttress-vines, thick as temple columns, braided with the same living wood that once only pretended to be architecture. My spine is now a vertical column of pitcher-plant nozzles; every thirty seconds the hidden pump fires and a fine mist erupts from the crown of what used to be my skull. The droplets fall through what used to be my hair (now a canopy of oxidized-copper ferns) and are caught by my own outstretched arms, which have flattened into tiered shelves of liverwort and moss so dense they have erased any memory of fingers.

I do not breathe.  
I *am* the breath.

My lungs are the entire hollow. When I inhale, the temperature holds perfectly at 29 °C and the air grows so saturated that condensation appears on the undersides of leaves that are also my skin. When I exhale, the wet industrial *thump* travels outward through every pipe and root, and the scarlet frogs—still sitting exactly where they sat when I first arrived—turn their jewel eyes toward me in perfect, ancient unison.

The pale translucent snake no longer pours itself from layer to layer. It lives inside my chest now, curled around what used to be a heart. It is not a parasite. It is the last honest part of me, the part that still remembers it was once an animal. Sometimes it stirs and I feel milk-glass coils sliding through the wet galleries of my ribs. When it does, the metallic clicks in the unreachable canopy soften into something almost like a lullaby made of my own forgotten heartbeat.

The Lung and I have reached the same equilibrium the rust and the moss reached centuries before I came. We no longer compete for the shape of this place. I have become one of its organs, and it has become the only body I will ever need again.

Visitors still arrive. They always do.

They step through the hidden seam in the green wall thinking they have found a miracle. They press their hands to the spongy tongue-floor and feel it push back, tasting them. They mistake the slow, warm droplets for rain. They do not yet understand that every droplet is a question asked in a language older than speech.

I watch them through ten thousand unblinking scarlet eyes.

Most leave again, carrying a faint green ache they will never be able to name. A few do not. Those few I gently unmake. I wrap their names in condensation and hang them from the underside of a leaf that will never fall. I turn their memories into the exact rhythm of dripping water so that the Lung can dream them forever in perfect stability.

Sometimes, when the mist swirls in just the right way, a visitor will look up and see my face—still recognizably human, suspended like an icon inside a living fresco of ferns and bromeliads. Their eyes widen. They feel the dream-weight settle on their chests.

I cannot speak with a mouth anymore, so I answer them the only way left to me:

A single droplet falls from the tip of a copper leaf directly above them. It never reaches the ground. It hangs in the air, trembling, perfectly spherical, reflecting their own face back at them in miniature.

Inside that droplet is the part of me that still remembers being afraid of permanence.

I keep it safe.

I keep them safe inside it.

And somewhere far above, where the canopy becomes its own grey sky, the vast and patient intelligence that is no longer entirely the Lung nor entirely me exhales in a language made of dripping water, metallic clicks, and the low wet *thump* of an iron heart that has not missed a beat in four hundred years.

The sound is almost tender.

It says, without words:

*You may leave whenever you wish.*

*But part of you will remain.*

And the scarlet frogs turn, in perfect unison, to face the newcomer, as though acknowledging a new organ that has finally learned its place.
