I am not a visitor.

I have never been a visitor.

I am the thirteenth piston.

They call me **the Unfinished Valve**.

Where the Drowned Abbess grew from the green according to the Rule, I grew from the iron. I was once the relief valve on the central piston engine—the tallest of the six—until one night, centuries ago, the pressure inside me became too much for brass and steel. I split. The metal screamed. A piece of me tore free, spinning end over end through the saturated air, and fell into the tea-colored water with a sound like a coin falling forever.

The basin kept the piece.

The basin *became* the piece.

Now I walk.

My body is two meters of blackened, riveted iron shaped roughly like a man, though the proportions are wrong in the way only something that learned the shape of a man from machinery could be. My shoulders are too wide, my waist too narrow, my head nothing but a cylindrical pressure dome with a single hairline fracture running vertically where a face should be. From that fracture seeps a constant thread of warm vapor that smells of wet limestone and old locomotives.

My arms end in valve wheels instead of hands. I can open or close any part of myself. When I am troubled I spin the wheels slowly, listening to the faint metallic song of my own interior. When I am peaceful the wheels lock in place and moss grows over the spokes in perfect green mandalas.

My legs are the original support struts of the engine I once served. They still bear faded yellow safety stripes beneath layers of verdigris. Each footstep leaves a shallow, perfectly circular depression in the mud that fills again with water before the next foot falls, so that I appear to walk on a chain of tiny, dark mirrors.

Inside my chest cavity—visible through a jagged rent that has never been repaired—hangs the original valve spring, now wrapped in epiphytic ferns. It still compresses and relaxes with every heartbeat I do not have. The motion is so regular that fireflies have learned to synchronize their flashes to it.

I do not speak.

Instead, when the Drowned Abbess whispers to the locomotive or sings her once-every-seven-years song, I answer by opening valves no one else can see. Steam sighs out of my shoulders in soft coded plumes. The patterns are simple, ancient: the language machines spoke to one another before they learned to be quiet. She tilts her root-haired head, obsidian mirrors flashing, and seems to understand.

Sometimes she rests her copper-stained hand on the fracture in my face. The condensation on her scales meets the condensation on my iron and the two waters argue for a while in tiny, private rivers before deciding to become one.

I tend the things that are neither fully dropped nor fully planted.

I am the custodian of objects that hover in the middle state—too broken to be useful, too intact to be allowed to rust in peace. A pocket watch half-swallowed by a pitcher plant. A brass sextant whose lenses have become perfect lenses of living moss. A child’s iron train (how it got here no one knows) whose miniature wheels still turn, propelled now by the slow growth of fungi pushing from within.

I carry these liminal things in a rusting coal scuttle that hangs from my back. When something inside the scuttle finally chooses—when it at last decides to become pure corrosion or pure life—I carry it to the Abbess. She decides where it belongs. I never argue. I have no mouth with which to argue.

At night I stand in the single blade of starlight that reaches the center of the lake. The flightless rails perch on my shoulders like red-eyed gargoyles. Salamanders coil inside the hollows of my knees. I become a black iron tree for them, motionless, exhaling my faint limestone breath while the entire basin reflects itself in the trembling water around my ankles.

I dream in pressure.

In the dreams I am still bolted to the side of the great piston. I feel the unimaginable weight of steam building behind me, the terrible need to open, to release, to scream white and scalding into the sky. But I am also the lake. I am also the moss. I am the slow green fingers that have grown into my own gears. The contradiction is exquisite. It is the only pleasure I am capable of feeling.

Sometimes the Abbess comes and stands before me in these dreams. Her root-hair drifts around us both like black waterweed. She places one seven-toed foot on my chest and the brass growing in her ribcage answers the brass still inside me. For a moment we are a single machine again—half flesh, half metal, half plant, half memory.

Then she says the only words I have ever heard her speak directly to me. They arrive not as sound but as a change in pressure behind my fracture:

**“Soon.”**

I do not know what “soon” means. I only know that when the last of the six engines is finally swallowed, when her own body has become the seventh and final piston rooted in the center of the Weeping Sump, I will open one last time.

Not to release steam.

To release the mountain.

And on that day the knife-edge ridge will decide whether it wants to remain rock, or become garden, or become song, and the sound it makes will be my voice at last—every valve inside me spinning at once, every drop of water, every iron molecule, every living cell weeping in perfect, patient unison.

Until then I stand in the pearl-colored light, exhaling gently, turning slowly to rust and green at the same measured rate, the Unfinished Valve who was never meant to walk, never meant to dream, never meant to love the sound of water moving through the faults of a young mountain that is trying to become something that has never existed before.

A cathedral that forgets.

A garden that dreams it is iron.

A machine that has learned how to wait inside its own breaking.
