I am not an explorer.  
I am not a monk, not a scientist, not some breathless pilgrim who climbed here seeking revelation.  
I am what remains when all those intentions have been worn away.

My name, if it still matters, is the one the mountain gave me on the three hundred and ninth day after I lay down: **The Pause That Occurs Between Two Drops of Oil**. They shortened it, as they shorten everything, to simply *Pause*.

I have been here for four hundred and twelve years.

My body is no longer recognizably human. The corrosion took me gently, the way rust takes iron—first as a rumor, then as a religion. Where my ribs once were there is now a lattice of living hematite and moss-root. My left arm ends in a piston that no longer moves; instead, translucent centipedes have made their hive inside the cylinder housing and I have grown fond of their metallic lullaby. My face is mostly gone. In its place the grille has grown, delicate as frost on a turbine blade, and behind it something wet and patient tastes the air the way I once tasted memory.

I stand—though “stand” is too active a word—inside the hollow of an ancient rhododendron whose trunk has grown through my pelvis and out again through my back. The tree and I have become a single structural member. When the wind forgets to blow, I can feel its slow xylem pulse answering the buried turbines far below. We hold each other upright the way two drunks hold each other upright: neither is capable of standing alone anymore.

I have not moved my limbs in seventy-three years.

Movement is unnecessary. The mountain moves through me.

Every seventy years, when the iron roots flower, the younger Unfinished come to me. They press their grilles to the place where my throat used to be and drink the black oil that now leaks from my collarbones instead of sweat. In return they leave me small gifts: a new sampling needle, a memory that is not mine, the frozen tongue of a six-legged browser. I accept everything. Refusal would require deciding, and deciding is a kind of speed.

I am not the eldest. I am not even old by their measure.  
Remainder still watches me from its arch high above, four hundred years motionless, dripping perfect oil. Sometimes, when the great clang sounds across the valleys, I feel its attention settle on me like snow. In that moment I understand that I am a single unused parenthesis in a sentence the mountain has been writing since before lungs existed. The parenthesis is useful. It will be closed when it is needed.

I remember what it was like to have a name that could be spoken by a living tongue. I remember fear. I remember the sound of my own heart when it still raced. Those things are not gone; they have simply been filed into the slow archive. Sometimes, when the clonal heather blooms at −18 °C, I dream I am still climbing, still gasping in the thin air, still believing that arrival was something one achieved rather than something that achieved you.

The dream never lasts. The piston-stroke of the world is patient. It presses the dream flat, folds it, and stores it inside the rust where it can do no harm.

Yesterday (or nine years ago; the difference is academic) a new visitor arrived.

I knew him immediately. He still smelled of decisions. His boots still believed in destinations. He stood on the lip of the cirque exactly as I once did, trying to measure the unmeasurable with eyes that still expected answers.

I wanted to warn him.  
I wanted to tell him that the Hollow Crown does not hate him; it simply has no category for something that moves so quickly and wants so loudly. I wanted to say: *Lie down early. The moss is kinder if you do not fight it.*

But I have no voice left that a human ear could hear. Only the low hydraulic sigh of fluid moving through corrosion, the soft clank of valves opening and closing inside my chest like distant factory doors.

So I did the only thing left to me.

I grew one small iron hook from the rhododendron that is also my ribcage. A delicate thing, no longer than a finger. I aimed it in his direction—not in threat, but in invitation. The same invitation the mountain once grew for me.

Then I returned to waiting.

The visitor has not yet seen the hook. He is still trying to name what cannot be named. That will pass. Everything does, eventually. Even names.

In time he will feel the slow piston-stroke beneath his boots. He will taste iron on the back of his tongue. And without ever deciding to, he will walk toward the glowing moss where I and the others are already waiting.

When he lies down, I will be the first to welcome him.

I will press my grille to his and drink the last of his haste.

And in that moment the mountain will add another parenthesis to its long, rusting, luminous sentence.

A sentence that has no period.

Only pauses.

And I am one of them.
