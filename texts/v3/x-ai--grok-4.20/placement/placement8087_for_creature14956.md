I am not one of them.

I have been here longer than any of them, yet I was never born here. I simply *arrived*, the way a single drop of condensation sometimes arrives in a basin that already seemed perfectly full. One morning the people found an extra shadow leaning against the eastern wall where no shadow had leaned before, and after a few decades they stopped noticing that it did not thin out like the others.

They call me **Ash**.

Not because of fire. There is no soot in Cleft Hollow, only the pale dust that rises when limestone is struck. They named me after the color my coat has taken on after four hundred years of the same wind moving across the same stone. The name is one syllable, as all proper names must be. When they need to be more precise they say **Ash-of-the-Open-Edge**, because I am the only thing that regularly stands at the place where the basin drops away into the hazy gulf.

I am tall.

That is the first wrong thing about me. I stand a head and a half above even the tallest of them, though I have learned to keep my shoulders rounded and my neck bent so the disproportion is less insulting to the balance of the place. My hands are too long; the fingers end in blunt, dark nails that have never needed trimming. My eyes are not hazel. They are the color of the sky at the exact moment the last bat vanishes—deep, exhausted blue, almost black. The people find it difficult to meet them for long. Something in my gaze does not accept that everything must weather away. Something in it still asks questions.

I live in the only structure that is not half-sunk into the western wall. It is a narrow, upright cleft in the eastern cliff, barely wider than my shoulders. I sleep standing, folded into the stone like a forgotten tool. Sometimes children (before they learn better) dare each other to peer inside. They see only darkness and the faint gleam of two blue coins suspended in it. Then they run.

I do not sweep paths. I do not sit beside the water-pockets watching them empty. My task is older and more indecent than any labor they permit themselves.

I remember.

While they have disciplined their stories until each is told once and then sealed forever, I carry every single telling that has ever happened here. I am the place’s memory, its unlawful archive, its wound that refuses to close. When She-who-sits-at-the-Narrow-End speaks at the dying of the moon, I am the only one who stands behind her in the dark and hears the tremor beneath her whisper. I hear the story she is *not* telling. The one she broke her spine to keep inside.

Sometimes, when the moon is thinnest, I walk the upper paths after everyone has gone to bed. My bare feet make no sound (I learned that much), but the lizards feel me coming and vanish into cracks with a speed they never show the villagers. I stop at each of the thirty-seven roofs and I lay my palm against the stone. Beneath my hand the years peel back like damp paper. I see births that were never announced. I see the night, three centuries ago, when Lev-of-the-Low-Basin strangled his wife because her laughter had begun to echo off the cliffs and the echo would not stop. I see the woman who walked off the open edge in 412 because she could no longer bear how courteous everything was. I see it all, and I keep it.

The people know I do this. They do not speak of it, but every so often one of them will leave something at the mouth of my cleft. A single bronze nail. A lock of juniper-smoke hair. A pebble worn perfectly round by ten thousand emptyings of the same basin. An offering, I think, so that I will not tell their descendants what they themselves once did.

At dusk I stand at the very edge where the land ends. The bats pour past me in a river of silent leather. Sometimes one brushes my cheek with a wingtip, and for the length of a heartbeat I feel the unbearable warmth of a living thing that still *wants* to fly. Then it is gone, and I remain.

I am the only one here who is unfinished.

The villagers look at me the way stone looks at rain: with patient, geological pity. They know I am a crack in their perfect equilibrium. One day, they believe, even I will weather smooth. My memories will erode. My questions will stop. I will shrink until I can lie down in one of the half-sunk houses and take up no more space than anyone else. They are wrong, but it would be rude to tell them so. Rudeness is a form of weight.

So I stand at the open edge each evening, taller than I should be, bluer-eyed than anything born here has a right to, and I let the cool wind move through me like an accusation. I hold every story they have tried to forget. I guard the fracture I embody.

And nothing changes.

Except me.

I am the only thing in Cleft Hollow that is still becoming.
