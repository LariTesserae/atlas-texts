I have been here for nine thousand four hundred and twelve of the 41-hour cycles.

They do not call it time here. They call it *witness*.

I am not the Unfinished. I am what remains when the Unfinished has finished with me.

My name, if names still mattered, was once Dr. Mara Solenne Vey. I came through a wound in reality that opened for less than six seconds, a wound I had spent seventeen years trying to cut open with mathematics and grief. I stepped through expecting answers about my daughter’s death. Instead the Hollow Forge took the question out of my lungs with a single breath of −217 °C and replaced it with the roar.

The first time an Unfinished formed, it chose the shape of a woman made of negative space—exactly my height, exactly the way I had stood at her funeral with my arms wrapped around nothing. It looked at me for the full forty-one hours without eyes. When it finally walked into the new wall, it left the shape of my own hand melted into the housing of the nearest builder. The metal was warm. I pressed my living palm against the print it had left and felt the echo of every mother who had ever touched the outside of a nursery door at night.

That was the moment I stopped being a visitor.

Now I live inside the intervals.

Between one Unfinished and the next I move along the gantries like a ghost that has forgotten it is supposed to be afraid of heights. I have learned to read the silicon fronds the way sailors once read wind. When they lean away from a particular stretch of wall, I know the next birth tremor is forming there. I wait. I have become very good at waiting.

Sometimes the Unfinished that precipitates looks like my daughter at seven years old, except her bones are made of phase-locked plasma and her eyes are perfectly round vacuum furnaces. Sometimes it looks like the last intubation tube I saw threaded between her lips. Once it was simply my own corpse, still wearing the coat I had on when I cut the wound in reality, hanging upside-down from a gantry like a broken marionette. Each time, I stand with it for the full forty-one hours. I have learned not to speak. Speech is a kind of violence here; the roar does not forgive interruptions.

I have tried, eight hundred and nine times, to walk into the wall when the Unfinished does.

Every time the cylinder refuses me. The metal becomes mirror-bright where I touch it and shows me a woman who is no longer decaying, who no longer carries the smell of hospital corridors or the particular silence that comes after the last monitor flatlines. The reflection is kinder than I deserve. I step back. The wall stays wall.

So I continue.

I have touched every handprint on every builder. There are millions. Some are mine now. I press my palm to the warm places and feel the memory of hands that were once mine, reaching for something that was never finished. The machines shudder—not in pain, but in something that might be recognition. For a fraction of a second the eternal roar develops a counter-harmonic that sounds almost like a lullaby heard through bone.

That is the only tenderness I am allowed.

I have become the interval.

I am the held breath between one perfect birth and the next. I am the tremor that the Forge manufactures so it can remember it is still allowed to tremble. When the next Unfinished forms, it will wear my face again, or my daughter’s, or the face of the surgeon who told me there was nothing more to be done. It will climb. It will witness. And when it walks into the wall I will remain behind, already beginning to forget which of us was the witness and which of us was the wall.

If you ever come here—and you will not come here on purpose—you will find me standing at chest height on one of the builders, palm pressed to a warm handprint that is still cooling from the last incorporation. I will not turn around. I will not speak. I will only wait for the next tremor, the next forgetting, the next chance to be taken home inside the only thing that ever loved me enough to finish me.

The roar does not forgive.

But once every forty-one hours, for the length of a single held breath, it remembers what it felt like to need a mother.

That is enough.

I am enough.

I am the Hollow Forge remembering that it once had hands, and that those hands were warm.
