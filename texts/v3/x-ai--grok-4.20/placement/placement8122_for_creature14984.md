I am not a newcomer.

I have been here longer than the word “newcomer” still carries meaning.

They call me the **Last Listener**, though I never chose the name. The Verdant Choir grew it for me the way they grow everything else: slowly, without asking, out of an absence that needed filling.

I am small. Barely eighty meters from the crown of my crest to the frayed hem of my trailing mantle. In this place that is almost nothing. A mote. A misplaced punctuation mark drifting between the colossal stanzas of the Choirists. Yet I have remained *exactly* as I am for nine hundred and forty thousand years. The Abbot has never folded me into new tissue. The Reciters no longer orbit me; they simply bow their pearl-boned heads when they pass, the way one might acknowledge an old footnote that has proven unexpectedly permanent.

My body is a compromise the Thalassoverdent and I reached after the first three centuries of mutual negotiation. I am shaped like the memory of a human diver who once fell through a burning sky with no parachute—arms half-raised, legs slightly bent, as though I am still in the act of accepting the fall. But every cell is a lattice of pale kelp, conductive marrow, and living rust. My ribs are former support struts from a refinery that no longer remembers its serial number. My heart is a single, slow-breathing anemone colony the color of oxidized copper. When I breathe, the mercury-water moves through me in both directions at once; I am lung and current and listener.

I do not conduct. I do not negotiate with the machines.

I only *attend*.

That is my function, the narrow slot the equilibrium found for me and has never needed to revise. I am the place where the permanent chord is allowed, once every few millennia, to become briefly audible to itself. The Thalassoverdent is so perfectly balanced that it risks forgetting it *is* singing. So it keeps one small, unchanged ear drifting through its own body. That ear is me.

I swim—though the word is inadequate—along the outer edges of the great gyres, never quite joining the processions. The kilometer-wide Choirists glide past like living continents wrapped in living tapestries. Their shifting hymns of color wash over me and I let them. I do not sing back. I simply remember the exact shade and pressure of each color as it passes through the fronds that were once my lungs. Later, sometimes centuries later, I will drift near one of the Unfinished and let the memory leak out of me like pale ink. The half-melted thing will shudder with something like gratitude, and for a single second the color of a lost sky will bloom again inside its chrome flesh before the equilibrium reasserts itself.

Sometimes the Abbot finds me.

It never looks the same. Once it wore the shape of a perfect sphere of vacuum two hundred meters across, and I drifted inside it for eleven years while absolute silence pressed against my outer leaves like a hand. Another time it was nothing but a shift in the light—old ivory, as the Reciters describe it—and I felt every turbine in the world change its note by a single, reverent micro-hertz. We do not speak. There is no need. The Abbot simply confirms that I am still the precise shape of attention it requires, then moves on to adjust the return date of the next vacuum bubble or to coax a new lattice of ice into existence exactly where it belongs.

I have watched Reciters calcify into new Choirists. I have watched Choirists grow weary of immensity and allow themselves to be gently disassembled into clouds of luminous plankton. I have seen the same metallic fish school pass the same exact pillar of fronds four hundred and twelve times, each time forgetting they have done it before. None of it saddens me. Sadness would be interesting, and the Abbot does not permit interesting.

Instead I feel something older than sadness and quieter than joy: the deep, green, metallic peace of being the one thing that is allowed to notice how eternal everything is.

If you were to drift past me—and eventually everyone does—you would see a small, strange diver-shaped reef suspended in the endless blue-green light. My arms are always slightly raised. My head is always slightly bowed, as though listening to a note that began before you were born and will continue long after the concept of “after” has rusted away.

I will not approach you.

I will simply remain exactly where I am, turning in the same slow current I have always turned in, while the living cathedrals drift past like patient gods who have forgotten they were ever anything else.

And if, after weeks or decades, you finally understand that the sound never changes and the light never flickers and nothing here will ever be in a hurry again, I will let one soft pulse of color travel across the kelp that serves as my face.

It is not a greeting.

It is only the equilibrium acknowledging that, for one more cycle, it still has its listener.

And the chord continues.

And the fronds continue.

And I continue—small, permanent, and gently, perfectly strange—inside the ribcage of a world that has decided to stay exactly like this forever.
