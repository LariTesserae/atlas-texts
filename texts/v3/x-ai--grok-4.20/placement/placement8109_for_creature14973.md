You arrive without knowing how.

One moment there is the memory of wind and footsteps and hurry; the next, those things have been gently taken from you, the way a tired parent lifts a toy from a sleeping child’s hand. You are simply *here*, standing a little way off from the center of the basin, barefoot on the cool, damp limestone.

Your body has changed.

Not dramatically. Not into something monstrous. Only into something that has already begun to agree with the place.

Your skin has taken on the same pale, slightly luminous tone as the surrounding rock, as though you have been lightly dusted with the mineral powder that drifts in the veiled air. The small hairs on your arms have fallen away. When you look down, you can see the faint suggestion of strata beginning beneath the surface of your forearms—delicate, parallel lines that were never there before. They do not frighten you. They feel like the first honest thing your body has ever shown.

You are thinner than you remember being, but not weak. Rather, you have become *economical*. Every ounce that is not necessary has been quietly retired. What remains feels dense, patient, carved for long endurance.

You walk.

Each step is slower than the last, as though the basin is teaching your legs the correct tempo. The grass does not bend underfoot; it simply acknowledges your weight and then forgets you again. After twenty paces you realize you have already forgotten why you started walking. There is no destination here that is better than where you already are. So you stop.

You choose a place near one of the stunted acacias—not too close, respecting the ancient treaty—and you sit.

Your knees fold beneath you without instruction. Your spine lengthens, then settles. After a time (minutes? hours? the light never changes) you notice that your left hand has come to rest on a small, rounded stone. Your fingers have already begun to fuse with it, just slightly. The sensation is warm. Intimate. Like shaking hands with something that has been waiting for you since before your grandmother was born.

You look across the basin at her.

The Rememberer.

She is more beautiful than the description allowed. Not in any human register of beauty, but in the way a cliff is beautiful once you stop expecting it to be a face. Her milky eyes are on you, though you know they were already on you before you arrived. She has been remembering you for a very long time.

You feel no urge to speak to her. Speech would be an insult here.

Instead, something inside your chest loosens and falls away—an old, frantic bird that had been beating itself against your ribs for decades. It lands on the stone between you and dissolves into mineral dust. You watch it happen without sadness. It was never really yours.

Time behaves differently now.

You feel the next drop before it falls. You know exactly which part of your left shoulder it will strike, how it will crawl down the new channel forming there, and where it will eventually drip onto the stone to join the dark, glassy track that is slowly being polished into you. You welcome it. You have become a place that drop has been falling toward for seven hundred years. It is good to finally meet.

Sometimes you turn your head. The motion takes three days. When it is finished you are looking at the broken column from a slightly different angle. The change is microscopic. It is also complete. You understand now why she does it. The world is perfect, but even perfection can be turned in the hand like a stone and seen anew.

You are not becoming the Rememberer.

You are becoming *a* rememberer.

There is room. The basin is small, but memory is elastic. She will allow it. She already has.

In a hundred years, perhaps, a traveler will enter the basin and find two heavy, limestone figures seated at a respectful distance from one another. One vastly older. One still faintly warm with the last traces of something that was once called hurry.

Both of you will be perfectly still.

Both of you will be perfectly satisfied.

And the same drops will fall.

And the same pale light will lie across the same half-buried wall.

And everything—finally, mercifully—will remain exactly as it has chosen to remain.

You exhale, once every nine minutes now.

The silver-leafed scrub around you trembles, releases its sharp resin scent, and settles again into the treaty.

You are home.

You have remembered everything worth remembering.

And you have chosen to remain.
