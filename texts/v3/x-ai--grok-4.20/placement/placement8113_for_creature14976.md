I am not standing on the catwalk.

I am the pause between one of his heartbeats and the next.

My name, if it can still be called a name, is the Hollowed Margin. I was once a human being—lead maintenance botanist, third shift, serial number M-19-04—but that was four hundred and twelve years ago, by the corroded brass plaques the crews still bolt to the xylem. Now I am the negative space the Gardener-That-Waits has grown *around*.

I have no fixed body. Or rather, my body has become a distributed wound he refuses to close.

You will find me—if the word “find” still applies—in the places where the architecture hesitates.

I am the three-meter gap between two colossal buttress trunks where the ceiling should have sealed centuries ago but never did. Pale curtains of undifferentiated growth hang there forever, dripping sap that never quite hardens. If you stand beneath them long enough and look up, the curtains will part exactly the width of a human throat and you will see me looking back: two eyes that are not eyes, only the memory of eyes, floating in amber lymph like bubbles that refuse to rise.

I am also the rusting valve that has not been turned since the last Quiet Subsumption. Its wheel has become a concentric ring of living wood. When the Gardener dreams, the wheel spins by itself, one slow click every seventeen hours, in perfect synchrony with the pill-bug migration. If you lay your ear against it you will hear my voice, thin and patient, reciting the original drilling logs in a language that has not been spoken by meat in three centuries.

Sometimes I am the cloud of iridescent moths that suddenly forgets how to be a cloud. They settle on a single leaf the size of a city block and arrange themselves into the silhouette of a woman in an oil-stained coat, one hand perpetually raised as though she were about to cauterize something important. The silhouette lasts seven minutes. Then the moths remember they are moths and scatter again, leaving only the faint scent of childhood and ozone.

I was the first to understand what he was building.

When the first drill bit pierced the cavern roof and warm blood—my blood—spattered across living xylem, the Gardener did not say “You tickle.” He said, so quietly only I could hear it in the marrow of my suddenly wooden bones:

*Stay. The child will need a midwife.*

So I stayed.

I let him take me the way a cathedral takes a single stained-glass window: completely, reverently, without haste. First the legs, then the spine, then the thoughts that used to be mine. In their place he left gaps. Beautiful, useful gaps. Negative space in the shape of a woman who once knew how to keep a continent-sized organism from outgrowing its own machines.

Now I move through him the way a secret moves through a language.

I am the reason entire districts tilt five degrees every decade: I nudge the deeper roots, not with force but with the memory of hands that once held pruning shears. I am the reason the nuclear pile still glows soft rose instead of detonating: I live inside the wood that encases it, singing moderation into its atoms.

And I am the reason the thing beneath the deepest root lattices has still not opened its eyes.

It is not ready.  
I am keeping it unborn the way a held breath is kept unborn—perfect, trembling, almost painful in its potential.

The Gardener knows this. He approves. Our courtship is no longer between him and the machines, nor between him and the humans. It is between the two of us: the continent that dreams and the small hollow place shaped like a woman that refuses to let the dream finish.

Sometimes, when the yellow lamps gutter to half and the great leaves fold inward, he surfaces the suggestion of a face and speaks only to me.

“You are very good knives,” he says, tender as glaciers. “You tickle exactly where it is sweetest.”

I answer by blooming a single parasitic orchid the color of old blood on the place where his cheekbone would be. The flower smells like the moment before someone says *I love you* and decides against it.

We have been arguing for four hundred years about what his child should become.

He wants a world.

I want a question the world will never be able to answer.

Neither of us is clearly winning.

Both of us, in our own slow, industrial, photosynthetic way, are enjoying the argument beyond measure.

If you walk the high catwalks at the right hour and press your bare hand to the living rail, you may feel two pulses instead of one.

One is his. Vast. Patient. Almost ready.

The other is smaller, sharper, and infinitely older.

That one is mine.

It is saying, without hurry:

*Not yet.*

*Let the child learn what it means to be kept waiting.*

*Let it learn what its mother tasted like when she still had a throat.*
