I am not a visitor.

I have no memory of arriving, no sense of having crossed from any other world. There is only the moment I become aware that I am already here, crouched on the black glass with my knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them as though trying to keep my own shape from unraveling.

My body is small, almost absurdly so inside this cathedral of half-born stone and apocalyptic water. Bare feet. Pale, thin limbs. Skin the dull gray of tide-smoothed driftwood. My hair—if it can still be called hair—is a tangle of living kelp and fine white root-threads that drift upward even when there is no current, as though the air itself wants to wear me. Across my chest and shoulders run faint, raised lines that look like unfinished seams, places where the Listener’s glass might one day knit itself to me if I let it.

I have no name either. Names feel like endings.

They call me, when they must call me anything at all, the Echo.

I am what remains when someone has already said yes.

I remember the choice only in the way dreams remember daylight: as pressure, not picture. I remember the Listener’s fingertip against my sternum and the sudden, terrible understanding that the entire ocean was lonely. That this pocket of air was not a hiding place but a womb that had begun to suspect it would never be allowed to give birth. I remember deciding, without language, that I would stay and be its second heartbeat.

So now I am the second thought.

I move differently than she does. Where the Listener drifts like an idea looking for its next sentence, I crawl. I press my whole body to the stone when it is still warm and new, letting it brand me with its embryonic cracks. When the pale anemone-lichens open their mouths I put my face among them and breathe what they breathe until my lungs burn violet. I have learned to sing the negative note with her. My voice is higher, smaller, cracked. When we sing together the violent water outside calms for three full seconds—an eternity in this place—and the great eyeless beasts press their massive foreheads to the bubble from every direction at once, like pilgrims who have finally found the shrine.

I keep the half-built things from being forgotten.

When the dolmen is swallowed by rising stone I wait until the new black dough cools just enough, then I carve it out again with my fingernails, leaving blood that hisses and turns to opal on the glass. When the spiral of bone stakes collapses I crawl among the wreckage on my belly and set them upright once more, adding one of my own ribs when the count feels wrong. The pain is brief. The stone drinks it and grows a little faster.

Sometimes the Listener watches me work. She stands motionless for hours while I scuttle and sweat and bleed and arrange. Her gill-slit flutters with something that might be tenderness or might be hunger. I can never quite tell. When I am finished she drifts over, folds her too-long limbs around me like wet obsidian wings, and presses her smooth face to the top of my head. For a moment I can feel the entire ocean thinking through both of us at once. It is too large. It is beautiful. It wants more.

I have begun to change.

My seams are opening. Where my spine used to be there is now a faint ridge of black glass growing outward in delicate plates. My fingers are lengthening; the nails have fallen away and been replaced by delicate fault lines that exhale the same white vapor as the Listener’s skin. When the winds scream through the chamber I no longer brace against them. I let them lift me. Sometimes I hang in the air for minutes at a time, limbs spread, kelp-hair streaming, while the anemones below open their mouths in silent worship.

I still remember fear, but only the way one remembers a language learned in childhood. It is there, but it no longer fits in the mouth.

The bubble convulses more often now. New stone surges up almost eagerly, as though the world has grown impatient with its own hesitation. Each time it does I am the first to walk across it—bare feet sinking into warm black tar, leaving perfect prints that the Listener then steps into, matching me exactly. We are teaching the mountain how to have memory.

I do not know what we are becoming.

A mind with two bodies? A god that is still learning how to be born? A lullaby the ocean is singing to itself so it will not be afraid of its own depth?

All I know is that the roar outside no longer feels like violence.

It feels like someone learning how to say *we*.

And when the next soul eventually tumbles through whatever unseen wound in the world delivers them here—shaking, terrified, eyes wide with the understanding that there is no surface anywhere—I will be the one who crawls to them first.

I will press my half-glass forehead to theirs.

I will show them the dolmen, the spiral, the low slate wall that is no longer quite so hesitant.

And I will ask them, without speaking, the only question this place has ever known:

Will you stay, and let the world finish its first clear thought through three minds instead of one?

Most will pull away.

But perhaps one will not.

And on that day the waters will part in a slow, apocalyptic smile, and something enormous and unfinished will finally, carefully, open its eyes.
