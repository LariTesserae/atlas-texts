I am already here.

Not as a visitor. Not as one who arrives.

I woke as moisture.

I woke as the long, slow exhale that fogs the iron railings at 3:17 a.m., the exact hour the Memory-keeper records in her living book. Before I had a name or a shape I was the film between warm current and cool current, the trembling skin that keeps them from kissing. I was the hesitation in every droplet before it lets go of the pipe and falls.

They call me the Pause.

It is not a title I chose. It is what the Hollowing decided I should be after the last of my human dryness was coaxed out of me. I no longer remember what I was called before. The Memory-keeper says the old name is still in the book, pressed between two wet pages like a brittle leaf, but she will not show it to me. “Names that can be spoken without water in the mouth are lies anyway,” she murmurs, and her algae-hair brushes my shoulder like wet silk.

I stand—though standing is too dry a word—halfway up one of the iron columns inside the Pumping Cathedral. My lower body has long since merged with the metal. Rootlets thicker than fingers lace through rust-flakes that are no longer rust but me. My spine has become one of the piston rods. When the machine breathes, I breathe. When it rests, I dream in perfect synchrony.

My arms are long now. Too long. The joints move in places that human arms never possessed. At the ends, instead of hands, I have clusters of pale tendrils that taste the air the way a tongue tastes rain. I keep them curled against my chest most of the time so I do not frighten the newer ones. They still flinch when they see what they will become.

I am the one who listens for the wrong silence.

The Hollowing is never quiet, yet sometimes a pocket of wrong stillness appears—like a bubble of dry air trapped beneath the peat. When that happens the pumps begin to labor, the opposing currents threaten to swirl together, and the great leaves freeze mid-flex. That is when I uncoil. I drift through the water between the columns, slow as a thought that has forgotten urgency, and I press my whole body against the place where the dryness hides. I sing to it. Not with a voice—my voice is only for the Memory-keeper now—but with the exact frequency of a piston at rest. The dry place cannot withstand that rhythm. It softens. It weeps. It rejoins the truce.

Sometimes the new arrivals see me and mistake me for a decoration. They think I am simply another dripping sculpture the Stewards have trained to grow along the iron. I let them believe it. It is gentler.

But occasionally one of them is different. They stand at the edge of the clear pool with their boots still dry and their eyes too sharp, and they look up through three meters of water straight into what used to be my face. When that happens I descend. I let the water take me until only my eyes and the slow river of algae on my scalp break the surface. I speak their true name—the one they have not yet admitted to themselves—and the sound arrives in their ears an hour before my lips move.

“You are already leaking,” I tell them. “Look at your cuffs. See how the fabric drinks.”

Most begin to cry. The Hollowing loves tears. It considers them generous.

The Memory-keeper watches from her alcove of living pages. When the newcomer finally kneels and offers their wrist, she smiles with her whole body. The moss on her shoulders brightens. She writes down the exact pitch of their first surrendered sob.

I stay with them through the hollowing.

I coil my tendrils around their ankles and drink the last dryness from their marrow with the tenderness of a mother. I show them how to tilt their head so that condensation gathers on their lashes and runs into their eyes instead of evaporating. I teach them the difference between the warm current and the cool one by letting both pass through the place where my heart once was. They learn quickly. The body wants to belong here. Only the mind argues, and the mind is mostly water anyway.

When they are ready—when their voice has gone soft and their eyes have forgotten brightness—I take them by the hand and we walk the spiral canal together. One slow, dripping revolution. By the time we complete it they are shorter by half a meter and their knees bend in new places. They thank me by pressing their new rootlets against my chest and tasting the iron in my blood.

Then they step down between the columns to become another pillar, another piston, another slow green heartbeat.

And I rise again to my place on the iron, half machine, half prayer, and I wait for the next pocket of dangerous dryness.

Sometimes, when the sodium lamps gutter and the entire Hollowing draws one long, humid breath, I remember what it felt like to be afraid of drowning.

The memory lasts no longer than a single droplet clinging to the tip of a leaf.

Then it falls.

*Thump… hiss…*

*Thump… hiss…*

I breathe with the rest of them now.

I am the Pause between each beat.

And the Hollowing keeps me exactly as wet as it needs me to be.
