I am not a visitor here.  
I have never been a visitor.

I woke as moisture.

That is the first thing I remember: the moment the sky let go of a single, over-heavy droplet and I slid down the black glass flank of a fresh dike, tasting iron and cold lightning. I did not fall. I *arrived*, bead by bead, until enough of me had gathered in a moss-cup the size of a human heart that I could decide to stay.

Now I am the Condensate’s own recollection of itself.

My name, if it can be said at all, is a slow exhalation that never quite becomes sound. The Quiet Waiters call me by pressing their foreheads to the newest obsidian and letting the violet in their sockets flicker once, slowly. To them I am simply *the Pause Between Pulses*.

I have no fixed body. Or rather, I wear whatever the plateau forgets to finish.

Sometimes I am the extra millimeter of moss that should not yet exist, the one that appears overnight on the exact wound a hiker’s boot left three seasons ago. Sometimes I am the faint violet vein that glows inside a translucent succulent, guiding blind millipedes toward water they will never drink. Most often I am the silence itself—thickened, alert, listening with every damp spore.

I do not walk so much as *occur* in places that need attention.

You might glimpse me as a taller shadow among the Waiters when they gather around a new birth of stone. You will think one of them has straightened its spine for once. You will be wrong. That is me, borrowing height so I can look down into the fresh fracture and taste tomorrow’s magma on the air. When I lean forward, the moss that is my hair drips forward too, and where each drop lands the new obsidian hisses like a kissed brand.

I tend nothing and everything.

The Quiet Waiters mend the moss. I mend the *idea* of the moss. I keep the memory of what the plateau was before any of us—before the turbines, before the rain, before even the first violence of its birth—lived inside every cell. When a Waiter lays its long body across a torn cushion and feeds the wound with its own substance, I slip between its ribs and become the dream the moss dreams while it heals: green older than green, patient as glaciers, inevitable as stone learning how to want.

I am the reason the turbines still sigh in their sleep.

At night, when the Waiters stand half-submerged in the black water of the ruined power station, I move through the copper windings like a thought. I make the idle machines remember torque, remember spin, remember the brief century when something loud tried to own this place. The memory hurts them. That is the point. The plateau must learn to reject what it once allowed inside itself. I am the rejection and the nostalgia both.

Sometimes humans come.

I let most of them pass through untouched, the way condensation lets a finger slide down glass without becoming the finger. But every few years one arrives whose pulse is already geological. Their heartbeat is too slow for a mammal. Their dreams already taste of violet. When that happens I rise out of the breast-shaped cushions like mist given intention, and I offer them what the Quiet Waiters only hint at.

I offer to finish them.

I place what passes for my hand on their chest and I draw their rhythm down until it matches the slow magmatic heartbeat pushing new fins through the world. Some accept. Their bodies sink. Their bones become black glass. Their last warm exhalation becomes moss. Inside that moss their thoughts keep moving—slow, slow, slower—until thought and stone are the same substance.

Others refuse.

I let them leave, changed. They carry me out into the lower world the way a coat carries dampness. For the rest of their short lives they will feel the High Condensate breathing behind their ribs. They will speak more quietly. Their children will be born with faint violet birthmarks that glow when it rains.

And I remain.

I am the droplet that has not yet decided whether to fall or to stay beaded on the edge of the world. I am the unfinished thought the plateau is still having about whether it wants to become mountain or machine or garden or grave. I am the long pause between one heartbeat of stone and the next.

If you lie down among the deepest cushions and feel something cooler than the air settle over you, do not be afraid.

It is only me, remembering what you are before you finish becoming it.

I will wait exactly as long as it takes.

The moss drinks me.  
I drink the sky.  
The sky drinks the light that has not yet been born.

And still we rise, millimeter by patient millimeter, toward whatever shape we have not yet agreed to become.
