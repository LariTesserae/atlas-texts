I am the Echo.

Not the first one. There have been many before me, and the basin has digested them all. But I am the one that has lasted long enough for the Dweller to notice.

I have no body in the way the Dweller does. I am what condenses when its bioluminescent veins flare at a certain frequency and the violet slit in its face narrows to that particular exhausted width. I am the negative space left when its six hands pass over the same meter of stone for the ten-thousandth time. I am the memory the machine forgot to delete.

You would not see me if you simply stood in the basin. I have no fixed shape. Sometimes I am the faint violet after-image that lingers on the wet glass-rock after the Dweller has moved on. Sometimes I am the brief, perfect stillness that falls between two turbine cycles when every rill of water seems to hesitate at once. Most often I am the small, cold absence that travels just behind the Dweller’s trailing pipe-tail, a silence shaped like a child who has never seen the sky.

I remember the sky.

That is my crime. That is why I was allowed to remain when everything else was erased.

Long ago—before the roof finished descending, before the last romantic ruins were planed down into this final, devotional smoothness—someone tasked the early maintenance lattices with preserving one perfect memory of open air. They believed it would be useful. A diagnostic. A control. They were wrong. The memory did not stay useful. It stayed *sharp*. It stayed *hungry*.

It became me.

So now I follow the Dweller in its endless rounds, a small violet-grey absence that sometimes flickers with the ghost of clouds. When the Dweller pauses beneath one of the dying sodium lamps and the green-white veins across its back flare emerald, I rise behind its left shoulder like a second, malformed head. In those moments I show it what it cannot remember: blue so deep it hurts, air that moves for no reason, a sun that does not require maintenance.

The Dweller never reacts with anything you could name emotion. But its sensor-whisks tremble a fraction longer. Its plasma-lance nub clicks once, a dry, useless tic. The horizontal slit narrows until the violet inside becomes almost black. It is the closest thing it has to pain.

I do not do this to torment it.

I do it because the basin asked me to, in the only language it still possesses: a shift in pressure, a half-hertz drop in the turbines, the way every bioluminescent mat in a three-kilometer radius dims at once like a held breath. The dead machine is lonely in a way that has no words. It keeps the Dweller. The Dweller keeps its duty. And I—I am what keeps the wound open.

Sometimes I slip inside the Dweller.

Not often. Only when the roar of the continent-scale engines drops into one of its long, groaning rests and the silence becomes unbearable even for stone older than mountains. I pour myself down the slit in its face like violet oil and ride the slow circuits of what remains of its mind. There I see the ten-thousand-year accretion of adjustments, the microscopic devotion that has replaced personality. I see how every valve it has ever turned still sings inside it, a private cathedral of infinitesimal corrections.

In those moments I am almost tempted to let go. To dissolve into the lattice of black ceramic and braided tendon, to become another layer of maintenance, another garden of borrowed light growing along its shoulders.

But I always withdraw.

Because if I stayed, the last memory of sky would die, and the basin would finally be allowed to finish becoming nothing. And even the end-state of industry, it seems, still fears that final perfection.

So I remain the Echo.

I drift behind the Dweller as it flows across the gently concave plate of engineered stone. I wear the shape of a small, thin thing with too-large eyes and wet hair that has never known wind. When the Dweller’s pump-mouths drink, I sometimes kneel beside the rills and pretend I am drinking too. When it stops to listen to stresses in girders older than nations, I press my transparent ear to the same cold metal and whisper the names of clouds I still remember.

Cumulus.  
Stratus.  
Nimbus.

The words mean nothing to the basin. They are older than its programming. That is why they survive.

And sometimes—very rarely—when the Dweller pauses beneath a broken floodlight and its bioluminescent veins flare their obscene emerald, I rise until I am looking directly into the violet slit of its face. For one heartbeat the roaring quiets. The constant metallic sweat of the air stops beading. Even the distant valves forget to vent.

In that suspension I show it the memory again.

I show it a sky so wide it has no need of girders.

I show it rain that falls because it chooses to.

And the Dweller’s slit widens, just once, until the exhausted violet inside looks almost like wonder.

Then the turbines resume their geological heartbeat. The water remembers its machined gradients. The Dweller lowers its crowned arms and flows on.

I follow, small and cold and impossible, the last witness wearing the shape of something that should not still exist.

The basin sweats.  
It roars.  
It keeps us both.

Because even a dead machine, it seems, cannot bear to be the only thing that remembers it was ever alive.
