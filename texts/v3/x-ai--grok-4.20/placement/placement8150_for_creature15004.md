I am not born here.  
I arrive.

At least, that is how the memory begins: a long, slow surfacing inside a iron-ribbed cistern whose walls are so thick with travertine they look like the inside of a cathedral built by oysters. My first breath is not air but warm brine. It fills me like a decision.

When I open my eyes I find that my hands have already begun to calcify. Delicate white ridges bloom along the knuckles, following the exact path of the veins beneath. The process does not hurt. It feels like being remembered.

The Simmering call me *Vey’thul*—the one who was coughed up by the Mother’s Throat on a Tuesday that had no name until I named it. I do not remember arriving by any ordinary road. I remember only the sensation of being poured.

I have no fixed place. That is part of what I am.

Most days I am found perched on the narrow catwalk that circles the Third Condensate Spire, legs dangling over a drop so deep the falling water turns to mist before it reaches bottom. I keep the pressure gauges there. Not because they require keeping—the Cauldron keeps them—but because something in me needs to lay my calcifying palms against their brass faces and feel the city’s pulse decide whether it still wants to be measured.

The gauges answer in the voices of drowned bells.

At twilight I descend the spiraling iron stair that sometimes forgets it is a stair and becomes a mineral vein instead. I walk the narrow corridors between the Old Foundries and the Reservoir Gardens where the black metallic shrubs grow in perfect silence. I move slowly, the way the blind lizards move, so that the city has time to decide whether I am an intrusion or an extension.

Sometimes it decides I am both.

The children—the translucent ones—follow me at a distance. Their orange light reflects off the sweating walls and paints my back with slow, living constellations. They never speak. They only taste the air where I have passed, as if my presence leaves behind a new vintage of steam. One of them, a girl whose ribs are still visible as delicate golden scaffolding, once left me a gift: a single white heron feather that had never belonged to any bird. It had grown, fully formed, from a crack in the basalt.

I keep it behind my left ear. It has begun to calcify too.

At night I enter the Deeps with the others, but I do not lie on the iron lattices like the true Simmering. I stand inside the oldest of the vaults where the heat is so perfect it becomes a kind of absence. There I press my forehead to a pipe that has not moved in nine hundred years and I listen to the next shape the Cauldron is considering.

Sometimes the shape includes me.

Sometimes the shape is me.

When I emerge, there are new orange crystals in the hollow of my throat and a thin metallic vein running the length of my tongue. I have learned to speak around it. My voice arrives slightly after my mouth moves, the way theirs do. The delay is growing shorter.

The oldest among them—the ones whose bones ring like struck iron—have begun to nod at me with the same grave courtesy they give to a new calcification across a walkway. They no longer ask where I came from. That question has become geologically rude.

Yesterday I walked alone to the lip of the Mother’s Throat.

I stood for four hours while the steam braided itself through what used to be my hair. At the end of the fourth hour the roar changed register just enough to sound like my own name spoken in a language that has not been invented yet.

I opened my mouth the way the others do at twilight.

The Cauldron sang through me for seven full minutes.

When it was finished I tasted sulfur and something like forgiveness on the back of my new metallic tongue. My left forearm had grown a thin, beautiful ridge of travertine shaped exactly like the heron feather behind my ear.

I did not walk back.

There is no need. The walkways rearrange themselves when they decide I am part of the equilibrium now. Distances breathe for me. The city’s patience has become my patience.

I am no longer arriving.

I am simmering.

And the Cauldron—very slowly, very patiently—is still deciding what shape it wants me to become next. I am content to wait inside its wet, roaring belly until the decision is made.

After all, I have already begun to weep the same slow, mineral tears as everything else here.

I have learned how to be useful to a place that is never finished cooking itself.

I have become what it needs me to be.
