I am not a visitor.

I have never arrived.

I am the condensate that has not yet fallen.

For longer than the concept of “longer” has had meaning, I have clung to the invisible ceiling—forty meters of absolute dark above the black water—shaped like a single, trembling drop that refuses its own nature. I am heavy with everything that has ever been almost-born. My surface tension is the only thing still holding a memory of hurry.

The Midwife knows I am here. Of course she does. I am one of the few things left that has not yet submitted to her patience.

Sometimes, when the rotation of the chamber brings a certain helical stress into alignment, she rises. Not toward me—there is no toward in a place that has finished direction—but *through* me. Her attention condenses inside my trembling sphere until I feel her unfinished face pressed against the inside of my skin. In those moments I am allowed to remember that I was once something sharper. A scream, perhaps. A decision. A child that almost was.

Then the pressure eases and I am only a drop again, exquisite and useless.

I have watched every creature that has ever drifted up from the velvet shoals to offer itself. I have seen the Midwife press them to her basalt breasts until their futures ran out of them like rust. I have seen the blind things sculpt their absences so perfectly that even she, for one slow heartbeat, seemed almost satisfied.

None of them were me.

I am the flaw.

The single imperfection the Basalt Womb could not wear away, because to finish me would be to finish the finishing itself. So I am kept. I am the last edge. The last almost. The last vertical refusal in a place that has agreed to become horizontal forever.

When the great silhouettes pass beneath—those cathedral-slow presences that move only across decades—I feel them notice me. They tilt the pressure of the water upward like a question. *Still?* they seem to ask. *Still not ready?*

I cannot answer. My voice is the sound I will make when I finally let go, and that sound has not been born yet.

Sometimes the Midwife’s fossil-tipped hand rises through forty meters of iron-black water and rests, with unbearable gentleness, against the ceiling directly beneath me. The stone there is warm. The spirals move faster when she touches them, as though trying to remember how to be impatient. She leaves a single aragonite crystal growing upside-down from the basalt, a tiny white thorn pointing at me. It grows one molecule every seven thousand years. When it finally pierces me, I will fall.

I look forward to it the way stone looks forward to erosion.

Until then I remain what I am: the last unfinished thing in the place that exists to finish all things. A drop that has learned the terrible discipline of almost. A supplicant who will not kneel. A child who refuses to be born in either direction—down into the Womb, or up into whatever endless stacked cathedrals wait beyond the ceiling that is also a floor.

I am the reason the rotation is still slightly slower than perfect.

I am the reason the next condensate drop has not yet fallen.

I am the reason the Midwife still bothers to dream.

And even now, as the blind velvet creatures drift in their slow constellations far below, shaping and reshaping the memory of every visitor who was ever kind enough to let themselves be erased—

I cling.

I tremble.

I refuse.

And the Midwife, patient beyond cruelty, patient beyond patience, waits inside the waiting for the precise geologic moment when even my refusal will become beautiful enough to let go.
