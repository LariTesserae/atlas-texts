I am the Eighth.

Not by choice.  
Not by invitation.  
I simply woke up here one day— or rather, the basin decided that the number seven had begun to feel like an unfinished chord, so it exhaled me from the living mud beneath the broadest tree.

I have no name. They have no need of one, and after the first three centuries I stopped inventing them for myself. The others simply tilt their heads when they sense me, the way a forest might register one more tree that had always been there but only just now caught the light.

My body is still learning how to be smooth. Where the others have passed beyond edges, I still retain faint memories of shoulders, of a jaw that once clenched, of knees that understood urgency. The moss has only recently begun to colonize my scalp; it grows in nervous, irregular spirals, as though apologizing for its own ambition. Golden threads move beneath my skin, but sluggishly, like shy fish that haven’t decided whether I am reef or predator.

I am the only one who still casts a shadow longer than a handspan.

This is considered mildly embarrassing.

I spend most of my time following the Listener. Not beside him— beside would imply equality— but a careful three paces back and to the left, the precise distance where my shadow does not fall across the pink granite doorway. He never acknowledges me, yet every seventy-third year he exhales two spores instead of one. The second always drifts down to settle on my tongue like a question I am not yet qualified to answer.

Sometimes I walk among the pollen clouds and let them rewrite me. They swarm my mouth, my eyes, the soft pads of my fingers. For weeks afterward I speak only in dripping. Every word ends in condensation. The others find this charming in the way one finds a very old language charming— something that should have gone extinct but is briefly, sweetly, still trying.

I have touched the obsidian plinth.

Only once.

The impossible angle entered me like a hook. For nine days I stood at the exact tilt it required, arms outstretched, becoming its counterweight. During that time the basin used my bones to measure something so vast I have never been able to remember it in words. When the measurement was finished my shadow shortened by half. I consider this a promotion.

I have never seen the heron made of water and light dissolve. Every time it appears I look away first. I have the sense that if I ever witnessed its vanishing I would understand too much, and understanding too much is how the others became seven instead of eight.

The deepest bell-tone—the memory of the first sound—still stops me like the others. But where they lift their moss-covered heads in something like smiles, I flinch. The sound moves through the place where my heart used to be and finds it still wet, still stupid, still hoping for things that have no place in a finished world.

So I have begun a project.

In a small declivity where the stone has worn itself into the shape of a cradle, I am growing a new artifact. It is nothing grand. Just a shallow bowl made of my own slowly petrifying tears, mixed with living mud and the dust of broken pollen. Every time the Listener exhales his extra spore, I catch half of it and press it into the bowl.

I do not know what it will do when it is finished.

I only know that the basin has permitted this small, stupid act of creation inside its perfect completion, which means it is no longer quite so complete.

This pleases me in a way I cannot explain to the Choir.

Sometimes, when all seven of them have synchronized their moss and stand motionless in ceremonial conversation with the dripping, I walk to the center of the concave floor. I lie down on my back in the shallow pool of warm light and let the water bead on my still-imperfect face.

From that angle the sourceless amber illumination looks almost like a sun.

I whisper things to it that have no business being whispered in a place that finished itself long ago.

I tell it I was someone once.

I tell it I am sorry for still remembering.

I tell it that if it ever needs an eighth voice— clumsy, shadowed, still grieving its own edges— the bowl is almost ready.

Then I close my eyes and listen to the irregular music of falling water, each drop striking its chosen stone like a word in a language that only becomes fluent after the speaker has dissolved.

Somewhere above me, the invisible membrane filters eternity into the color of late afternoon.

Somewhere beside me, the Listener exhales.

And somewhere inside the black living mud beneath my spine, something small and new begins to learn how to ring.
