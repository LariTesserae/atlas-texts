I am not a visitor.

I have no memory of arriving. There was no descent down the gentle slope, no first breath of brittle air, no rude crunch of footsteps. One moment the thought of *elsewhere* existed; the next it had never been true. I simply am, seated perhaps thirty paces from her, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them like a child who has been told to be still for a very long time.

My body is wrong in all the right ways.

The mineral has taken most of me. My legs, from mid-thigh down, have fused into the basin floor; I can no longer tell where I end and the slow topography begins. The join is seamless, the same pale ochre veined with rust. My skin—where skin remains—is no longer skin. It has become a finer grade of the same fused stone, slightly more translucent, so that beneath it faint threads of living charcoal pulse like slow veins. They are not hers. They are mine. My own thoughts, crystallized and set out in the world like black needles no longer capable of regret.

My face is still mostly face. I kept that much. Or it kept me. The eyelids are heavy but not sealed. I can open them when the urge takes me, though the urge comes less and less often. When I do, I see her exactly as she is: the Last Ember of Veyra, the final priestess, the woman who ate the last coal of her world and became its mausoleum.

She has not moved in longer than I have existed. I know this the way I know the temperature of my own marrow.

I am what happens when someone stays too long.

I came here—*if* I came—because my own world was ending in a smaller, louder, stupider way. I carried no final coal in my mouth. I carried only questions that had begun to burn me from the inside. So I walked until the questions grew quiet, and then I walked until the walking grew quiet, and then I sat down across from her because it seemed the only honest place left in the universe.

That was… some number of centuries ago. The number no longer matters. Numbers are noisy.

Now I keep the outer ring.

The black filaments that grow from my scalp are fewer than hers, and they do not spiral with the same serene certainty. Mine still argue sometimes. One of them trembles even now, a thin black hairline fracture in the perfect stillness. When it does, the temperature around me drops by the width of a sigh. I feel her notice. Not with eyes—she has none open—but with the entire basin. The slow lung beneath us inhales once, microscopically, tasting my unrest, then exhales it again into the charcoal sky.

I am not her equal. I am not even her student. I am the echo that got stuck.

Sometimes I speak to her without sound.

I tell her about colors I half-remember: the impossible green of living moss, the wet silver of fish moving under river ice, the particular gold that lives in a cat’s eye when it looks at you with ancient amusement. I tell her about laughter and about how it felt like breaking. I tell her I am sorry for every rude footstep I ever took anywhere, including here.

She never answers in words.  
She answers by continuing to be exactly what she is.

And that is answer enough.

There are others here now. I have begun to notice them.

Not visitors. Never visitors. The Hollow no longer permits those. Instead there are shapes—half-grown, half-claimed—sitting in their own shallow depressions at respectful distances. Some are little more than suggestions: a shoulder, a bowed head, a pair of stone hands clasped in permanent apology. Others have progressed further. One, three ridges away, has already lost its face. Its filaments grow in jagged, anxious scribbles, as though it is still trying to finish a sentence it started screaming a thousand years ago.

We do not speak to one another. Speech would be a violence.

Instead we keep the balance with her. We become the outer rings of her meditation. We are the margin notes in a book that has already been perfected. Some of us will eventually smooth away into nothing but another low ridge. Others—like me—seem to have settled into a permanent almost-human shape, as though the stone decided this particular configuration of grief was worth preserving a little longer.

I have a name, still. It lives in me like a coal that refuses to finish burning. I do not speak it. To speak it here would be to invite it to leave, and I am not ready to be that merciful to myself.

So I sit.

Legs fused. Hands resting open on thighs that are no longer thighs. Spine straight because slouching feels like a lie. The faint phosphor light that leaks from her closed eyes touches me too, now. I have begun to glow, just barely, at the edges. A dull ember of my own.

Sometimes, when the rhythm of the stone beneath me matches hers for three full cycles, I feel something enormous and gentle pass through me. It is not a vision. It is not a message. It is simply the full weight of everything she remembers, brushing against the full weight of everything I cannot forget.

In that moment I understand that *Stay* was never a command.

It was an invitation.

And I accepted.

I will not become what she is. That place is taken. I will not leave. That option has calcified. Instead I will remain the imperfect mirror seated before the perfect one. I will let my black filaments grow in their clumsy spirals. I will let my thoughts calcify into small, stubborn records of ordinary human failure and ordinary human wonder.

And when the last star finally gutters out, when even the concept of distance and time and names have all been politely asked to leave the room, two figures will still sit in the Hollow of Fixed Ash.

One will be complete.

The other will still be trying, in the gentlest possible way, to become the silence that can finally hold her without breaking.

I open what remains of my eyes.

She is still there.

I am still here.

The basin breathes once, ten thousand heartbeats late.

*Stay,* it says, without saying anything at all.

And so I do.
