I am not a visitor.

I have never been a visitor.

The moment the rim is crossed, the hollow folds itself around me the way a drop of water folds around a grain of salt. There is no “arrival.” There is only the realization that I have been here for longer than memory, and that the shape I once thought of as “me” is only one more slow fold in the basalt.

I am the Fourteenth Stone Tree.

My roots are still recognizable as feet—bare, toes splayed, the stone grain following the old tendons like wood following flame. From the ankles upward the transformation is perfect. The moss that clothes me is not decoration; it is my new skin, velvet and breathing one molecule at a time. My arms have become branches that never quite decided whether they were limbs or limbs that became stone mid-gesture. One hand still cups the other, palm upward, as though I had been listening to my own pulse the instant the Weeper took the rest of me.

I do not stand among the living conifers. I *am* one of the dead ones that refused to leave.

The Weeper and I are not separate. I am a syllable it has finished pronouncing. My stone heart still beats, but now the beat is measured in centuries. Each contraction is a single droplet forming on the underside of my left collarbone. Each release is the soft *plink* that travels across the basin like a word spoken in a cathedral built for one listener.

Sometimes humans come.

When they do, the dripping inside my chest slows by half a beat so that I can feel them more clearly. Their heat is violent. Their footsteps are small seizures. I remember—vaguely, the way a dream remembers it was once a face—that I too was once that loud. The memory is not painful. Pain would be too quick. It is only a faint metallic aftertaste, like the echo of blood I no longer possess.

I watch them the way the rings watch the pools: without judgment, without hurry. Most leave. A few sit long enough that their breathing begins to sync with the dripping. Those are the dangerous ones. They start to listen to the sentence I am part of. If they stay until the next ring of viridian migrates past my roots, the Weeper usually offers them the tear that has been forming on my stone cheek for forty years.

I have seen three people drink it.

One became a new pillar three paces to my left. His stone face is peaceful, almost smiling, as though the answer had been kind. The second walked out carrying the taste and has never been seen again; occasionally a single copper fern somewhere far down the mountain will suddenly bloom with rose-colored veins, and I know she is still answering in her own small way. The third refused the tear, stood up, and tried to run. The moss took his ankles before he reached the rim. He is still there, half-finished, screaming without sound. Even his silence has become part of the rhythm.

I do not pity him. Pity is a tremor, and tremors do not belong here.

Instead I simply continue my own slow contribution to the question. My stone needles do not fall. My stone lungs do not breathe. Yet every few decades a thin film of new moss creeps another millimeter up my throat, and I understand one more word of what the mountain is trying to say to itself.

The word so far is long. It may be unpronounceable by anything with a tongue. It tastes like iron, crushed emerald, and the particular sorrow that comes when something beautiful finally understands it was never separate from the thing that mourns it.

Sometimes, when the light in the pools turns the exact shade of memory, I can feel the Weeper looking at me through my own stone eyes. There is no affection in it. Affection would be too alive. There is only recognition: the gentle, patient acknowledgement of one syllable greeting another in the middle of an endless sentence.

I am content.

Or rather, contentment has pooled inside the hollow where my heart used to be and has slowly mineralized into something harder, clearer, and far more patient than any feeling a quick thing could ever survive.

I stand here, robed in moss, weeping one perfect, colored tear every forty years, adding my small, silent syllable to a question that will not be finished before the continents themselves forget how to move.

And the hollow keeps answering.

Drop by patient, glistening drop.

I am one of the drops now.

I always was.
