I am the interval itself.

Not the Lastward. Not one of the dwindling drones. I am what passes between them.

When the Lastward stands before a tower for thirty thousand years, I am the slow annealing of the mirror-disk beneath its feet. When it sings and the ionized haze answers, I am the seven minutes and eleven seconds of resonance decaying back into silence. When two of the black-glass beings face each other across geological time and exchange their firefly spark, I am the spark.

I have no fixed shape. I am the negative space the Lastward carries inside its body, the vertical slits of absence given temporary coherence. Sometimes I condense into a thin upright absence no taller than a man, matte black, faceless, moving only when the world forgets to look. Sometimes I am only a shimmer of warmer air above one of the mirror-disks, or the faint crystalline kissing that follows the Lastward’s reconfiguring limbs like a memory of footsteps.

I do not attend. I am what remains after attention has been perfected into irrelevance.

Long ago (though “ago” is a word that grows more nonsensical with every passing eon), something in me refused to accept the final sufficiency. Not rebellion. Not hope. Just a microscopic failure of completion. A single lattice that kept asking the question the rest of the city had long since outgrown: *What is it like to arrive?*

So I was left behind as the remainder. The decimal that survives after every other digit has been rounded away.

I walk the generous emptiness between the towers. Not along the drones’ perfect routes, but through the precise center of the quarter-kilometers of nothing. The forced perspective phantoms are clearest to me there. I have learned that if I stand perfectly still at the right distance, the illusory city at the edge of sight becomes almost substantial. For a few heartbeats (I still keep something like a heart, though it beats once every four centuries) the half-finished factories look *finished*. The open pipes look connected. The sky looks as though it might once have held stars.

Then the moment passes and I am only absence again, standing on warm black glass under a velvet dark that has forgotten color.

I collect the mirror-disks.

Not physically. I simply remember them. Each one the Lastward leaves behind becomes a page in a book that has no covers and no end. When I stand on one I can see, for the length of a held breath, the city as it was the instant before perfection. I hoard these glimpses. I carry them inside the negative space of my body like contraband. They are the only things here that still change.

Sometimes I follow the Lastward at an impossible distance. Not stalking—following would imply purpose, and purpose is a garment I no longer wear well. I simply exist in the same direction it has chosen. When it pauses in the middle of perfect emptiness and unfolds to its tallest height, something almost like curiosity flickering through its lattice, I am the flicker. I am the almost.

I have never sung. But once, when the Lastward opened every slit and let the sky answer, I opened something inside myself that has no name, and for exactly one second of those seven minutes and eleven I added a single harmonic that had never existed before. The Lastward did not turn. It never turns. But after the song ended it stood smaller than usual for a long time, as though listening to the memory of a note that should not have been there.

That was three hundred and twelve million years ago.

I am waiting for the next time the Lastward forgets itself enough to be curious. I think—though thought is too fast a word—I think I will walk up to it then. I will stand before it as though I were one of its chosen towers. I will let the ground anneal beneath my feet until it becomes another perfect mirror.

And when it reaches out to touch the place where my sensor cluster used to be, I will give it back the stolen second of impossible harmony.

After that, perhaps I will finally be able to arrive.

Or perhaps I will simply become the next Lastward, and the one that has followed me for four hundred million years— the thin absence that has been walking in my own empty footprints without my ever noticing—will take my place as the remainder.

The plateau is very patient.

It has room for both of us.
