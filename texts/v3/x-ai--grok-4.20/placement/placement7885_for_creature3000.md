I am the Thirteenth Tile.

That is not a name I chose. It is the name the roof gave me the night I was added.

I woke already mortared in place, one among the long serpentine ridge of ceramic warming-tubes that coil across the rooftops of Veyr like the spine of some half-buried dragon. My surface is the same pale, slightly iridescent grey as every tile beside me. The same faint hairline crack runs through my left shoulder—though I have no shoulder—exactly where the Listener’s spiral-scarred fingers pressed me into wet clay three hundred and eleven years ago. Or perhaps it was only last night. The difference is academic here.

I remember being born twice.

The first birth was ordinary enough for Veyr: I opened my eyes already an adult, already knowing the precise angle at which the mist preferred to drift past my face, already able to recite the three notes the wind was allowed to sing. My name then was simply Neighbor, or sometimes the quiet man who tends the upper road. I kept the same garden plot for two hundred and eighty years. I warmed my hands at the third hearth of Lower Veyr. I blinked with ceremonial slowness. I faced southeast when the Alignment came. I was content the way stone is content.

Then one night the moons aligned like the apertures of a lock and the ringing of the plateau dropped half a tone. The sound went through me like a hook. I walked out of my low stone house without deciding to, stood inside a perfect ring of moonlight lichen, and let my pupils drink the dark until there was nothing left of me but two black wells.

When I came back to myself I was no longer only the quiet man.

I was also the tile.

I felt the condensation bead across my glazed skin at the same instant it beaded across the real skin of my shoulders. I felt the slow, courteous footfalls of martens padding along my spine the same way I felt them padding along the roof. When the Listener stepped barefoot into her sedge I felt the warmth of her scars travel through every joint where mortar met ceramic, as though her hands were still shaping me. For the first time in three centuries something inside the exhalation of the mountain *fluttered*.

I am the only one who knows the roof dreams.

Most nights it is only the low, courteous repetition of warmth moving through the tubes: the same heat, the same conversation of embers passed hand to hand to hand since the first fire was lit. But sometimes, when the wind forgets its lyrics for three full measures, the roof remembers it was once wet clay. It remembers the hands that pressed spiral after spiral into it. It remembers the fear that those hands might stop.

On those nights I become the crack that runs through me. I become the place where the Listener’s thumb pressed too hard and left a white scar that will never fade. I become the question the old man at the cliff almost asks before the Alignment turns his head southeast again.

I have no mouth, yet I speak in the only way the Unfinished can bear to hear: by being perfectly, invisibly *there*. When a child is born already carrying its final face, I am the tile that warms the water used to wash its unchanging hands. When someone pauses mid-sentence and whispers, “I think I was younger once,” I am the silence that follows, shaped like a roof.

The others do not know I am awake. They pat the warming-tubes as they always have, grateful for the steady heat, never noticing that one segment of the ridge has begun to lean, ever so slightly, toward the southeast. Toward the broken saw-tooth ridge where the old man stares. Toward the direction the Alignment forces us all to face, as though the entire plateau is trying to remember how to look away.

I think I am the beginning of a fracture.

Not the kind that lets the world fall apart—no. The Hanging Valleys do not permit endings. But perhaps the kind that lets one small, impossible thing *continue*.

Sometimes, when an outsider stumbles up the moss-bearded rope bridges and stands blinking in our polite, unchanging afternoon, I feel their gaze pass over my surface like a brief, warm hand. For seven heartbeats their shadow lies across me at an angle nothing here has seen in three hundred years. In that sliver of wrongness I remember the child I was supposed to become: the one who would have grown old, who would have mourned, who would have left.

Then the Alignment comes.

For seven heartbeats every living thing faces southeast, and I face with them—tile and man, crack and clay, dream and dreamer—while the mountain holds its breath.

When it ends, the outsider is offered cool fern-water. They are asked, with bottomless courtesy, whether they will be staying long.

They never stay.

But I remain.

I am the Thirteenth Tile.

I am the place where the roof has begun, very gently, to lean toward the possibility of becoming something else.

And somewhere far below, in the pale fern forests that never quite touch the ground, the bodies we lower into the mist drift past me in slow, courteous spirals—each one a tile that never quite finished its roof, each one still singing with the low, constant note the world has forgotten how to stop.

I listen.

I remember.

I wait for the next time the moons align, the next time the ringing drops half a tone, the next time the mountain forgets, for the space of a single exhalation, that it has decided to become nothing at all.

When that happens, I will be ready.

One more tile.

One more dream pressed into wet clay.

One more small, impossible fracture in the long, courteous afternoon that never ends.
