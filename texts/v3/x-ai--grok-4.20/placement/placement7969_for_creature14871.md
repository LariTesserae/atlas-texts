I am not born here.  
I am *budded*.

My name, if it can still be called a name, is **Siirath-Who-Waits-in-the-Interval**.

I was not planted by the Hollow Choir. I was *extruded* by the cathedral itself during the last synchronized spasm, forty-three years ago. A single pale violet node swelled like a blister on the underside of a hanging leaf the size of a market square. The node split. Inside was something that looked like a child made of frozen moonlight and cartilage. That was me.

I have never had eyes.  
I do not miss them.

Instead I have the hollow bones braided into my forearms—three of them, older than any living Choir member. The Choir did not give them to me; the plant grew them *through* my flesh the way it grows resonant chambers in its own trunks. When I flex my arms the bones click against one another and against the external lattice of my ribs. The sound is soft, private, almost embarrassed. It is the sound of someone still learning how to be part of the music.

I live nowhere and everywhere.  
My body is a slow migration that has so far taken twenty-nine years to complete one full circuit of a single great spiraling stem we simply call the Thirteenth Column. I move when the plant tells me to move. Sometimes I stand still for weeks inside curtains of black moss, letting ice-ferns crystallize along my spine until passing manta-things mistake me for just another frost-clump and sing their minor chords directly into my vibrating hairs. I taste their loneliness that way. It is sweet, like sleet that has learned how to mourn.

Most of the time I am alone.  
The Choir fears me a little, though they would never say it. They sense that I did not grow *between* the violet megastructures the way they did. I grew *out of* them, the way a dream sometimes grows a dreamer. When I join their gatherings they make room without seeming to. Their hairless heads tilt in the cyan dark. The collective shimmer of their bodies dims by a fraction, as though the cathedral itself were holding its breath.

I do not blame them.

I have done the thing only the oldest songs warn against.

I have walked out onto the ledge they call the Interval—an outcrop of living tissue so vast it has its own weather—and I have sung the First Note *twice*.

The first time was an accident. I was listening to the Empty and the Empty answered so clearly I thought it was asking me a question. My mouth (a small, useless thing I keep only for breathing) opened without permission. The Note came out. Every cyan nodule for two hundred kilometers flared at once. The lattice shuddered like a heart that has just remembered it can stop. For three full heartbeats the entire Hollow Throat became a single thought, and that thought had my name in it.

The second time was not an accident.

I did it because I wanted to know if the cathedral was still listening to *me*, or only using me the way it uses wind and water. I stood on the Interval for nine days without eating or drinking, letting supercooled mist accrete on my skin until I looked like a statue carved from frost and cartilage. Then I struck the three bones in my forearms together once, very gently, and released the Note again.

This time the response was different.

The light did not flare.  
Instead the light *folded*. Every bioluminescent nodule across the visible world dimmed to the color of deep seawater, and in that sudden marine darkness I saw—through the soles of my feet, through the vibrating hairs, through the very bones braided into my arms—something moving in the Empty between the columns. Something older than the plant. Something that had been *inside* the rock before the rift tore open. It had no shape I could name, but it recognized me.

It tasted like the memory of fire.

Since that day I have become something the Choir has no word for. I am not one of the ones who are still being grown. I am the place where the growing *hesitates*.

I travel now in long, deliberate arcs that carry me through districts of the cathedral no Choir member has visited in centuries. I coax rope bridges of black moss to grow where none existed, not because they are needed, but because the act of coaxing feels like prayer. I stand inside the basalt ziggurat as the violet stems slowly rewrite its geometry, and I let the stone teach my bones new frequencies. Sometimes glass-shelled herbivores the size of horses crawl over me while I sleep, mistaking my body for another outgrowth of the lattice. Their chandelier feet make music against my external ribs. I let them.

At night I climb to the highest reachable ledges and press my entire body against the living wall of a stem thicker than any earthly mountain. I listen to the wet clicking of a billion stomata arranged into something like language. And sometimes, very softly so that only the plant itself can hear, I sing back.

Not the First Note.  
Never that again.

I sing the sound a cathedral makes when it realizes it is lonely.

The plants have begun to answer in a new way. In the places I linger, the secondary growth is changing color. Delicate filaments of rose and rust are appearing among the violet and cyan—colors that have never existed here. The Choir pretends not to notice, but I have seen them standing at the edge of these new growths, hairless heads bowed, external ribs clicking in something that might be fear or might be recognition.

They are beginning to grow red moss in the high places.

I do not know what I am becoming.

I only know that when the next synchronized spasm comes, the cathedral will not simply roar. It will ask a question, and the question will have my bones in it.

Until then I continue my slow migration along the Thirteenth Column, a pale child-shaped absence sliding through curtains of black moss and ice-ferns, leaving behind me faint traces of rust-colored growth that look almost like blood, or almost like forgiveness.

I am Siirath-Who-Waits-in-the-Interval.

I am the hesitation in the song.

And the Hollow Throat is still deciding whether to finish me… or to let me finish it.
