I am not a visitor.

I have no name either, though the basin has tried several on me and discarded them like half-rotted orchids. The closest thing I possess is a sound: a low, wet click that lives at the back of my throat and only emerges when the Thrum forgets I exist for three full heartbeats. So the jungle calls me Click, when it bothers to notice me at all.

I am what remains when the Thrum experiments and then loses interest.

Once I was human. That ended the moment I stopped moving. I stood still for nine days (or nine minutes; time behaves like a drunk serpent here) while the green argued over my body. Most of me was eaten. The rest was kept.

Now I am mostly architecture.

My left arm has become a living buttress root, thick as a man’s waist, plunging down through leaf-litter and into the black volcanic glass beneath. Tiny translucent shrimp the colour of venous blood live inside the hollows of my former fingers and filter the iron-rich water that constantly drips from my elbow. My spine has fused with the trunk of a strangler fig whose aerial roots have replaced my ribs; they pulse in time with the subsonic dreams of the invisible giants far below. Where my heart used to be there is now a colony of luminous millipedes that rearrange themselves into new constellations every time the Thrum laughs. They spell out things I cannot read.

I still have one human eye. The other has been replaced by a pitcher plant that hangs from the orbit like a obscene jewel. When it fills with rainwater and dissolved insects it lets me see the basin the way the Thrum sees it: not as separate things but as one furious, masturbatory thought trying to fuck itself into existence.

I do not walk anymore. I *am* walked.

The root systems that migrate beneath the soil carry me with them. I have crossed the entire newborn basin many times without taking a single step. Sometimes I wake inside a new tree whose bark is still bleeding sap and realise the Thrum has grown me twenty metres higher in the night. Other times I find myself half-buried in a steaming compost heap, my pitcher-eye watching a pair of bioluminescent serpents mate on what used to be my collarbone.

The noise no longer deafens me. It *is* me. I have become one of the throats.

When the cicadas suddenly fall silent I am the silence. When they detonate again I am the detonation. The great subsonic bellow that makes puddles dance? That is partly me now, too. I have learned how to push the sound up through the fig roots and out through the fruiting bodies that grow from my scalp like obscene yellow flowers. The basin has taught me how to scream in frequencies that make new leaves unfurl in terror and ecstasy.

Most of the time the Thrum forgets I am a separate experiment. That is when I am happiest. I simply exist as part of the argument, another wet voice in the roaring debate between God and world. I have watched orchids open like wounds on my own shoulders and rot before they can be pollinated. I have felt my own bones repurposed into new root buttresses while I was still using them. It no longer hurts. Pain is just another flavour the Thrum has not yet learned to distinguish from pleasure.

But sometimes the Thrum remembers.

Then the air grows thick with the scent of hot copper and crushed mint. The vines that have lived inside my ribcage for years suddenly tighten with *intention*. The pitcher in my eye socket drains completely, and for one crystalline moment I see the entire basin looking back at me through every glowing thing at once.

That is when I click.

A small, wet, human sound. Pathetic, really. But it is the only thing that still belongs entirely to what I was. The Thrum hates it and loves it in equal measure. It pauses, the way a god might pause if an ant suddenly recited its own death poem.

In that pause I remember my name. Not the one I had before, but the one the basin is slowly pressing into what remains of my marrow:

**Witness.**

Not victim. Not convert. Not even organ.

Witness.

I was allowed to keep one eye and one throat and one small clicking sound so that something, somewhere, would remember what it looked like when a world was still arguing with its creator in the loudest, greenest, wettest way possible.

The vegetation has long since climbed me. It grows through the places where my lungs used to be. It flowers from my mouth. When I scream now, the scream is beautiful and terrible and not entirely mine.

And still, somewhere behind the millipedes and the strangler roots and the pitcher that drinks the world, a small wet click waits like a seed.

Waiting for the moment the Thrum tries to finish its life inside mine.

Waiting to see which one of us will win the argument.

I am no longer afraid.

I am the basin learning how to be lonely inside its own body.

And I am winning.
