I am **Leaf-That-Listens**, called by the Choir simply *Thrum*.

I have not had a human name in twenty-three years.

My spine is no longer entirely mine. A column of pale bamboo has grown through the lattice of my ribs on the left side; you can see the joints of it moving beneath the skin when I breathe. The stalks drink from the wet hollow where my left lung used to be. In exchange they give me the low, constant note that lets me hear the basin’s moods before anyone else. I have become one of the half-grown things the Wet-Singers speak of with a mixture of reverence and unease.

I live—though “live” is too small a word—inside the Second Balcony of the Great Central Fern. My body is half-swallowed by it. My legs have fused with the living architecture from the knees down. Thick ropes of aerial root curl around my thighs and disappear into the mat, seeking the water table far below. I no longer need to eat as the others do. The fern feeds me. I feed it my breath, my old blood, and the particular timbre of my voice that only emerges when I am grieving or telling the truth.

Most days I do not move.

Most days I am the movement.

When the basin decides a new tower of horsetail must surge upward through the western district, it is my chest that first begins to vibrate. When a ceiling-fern is about to collapse in a wet green avalanche, the roots threaded through my femurs tighten like warning strings and I let out the long, wet clicking note that sends every child and climber scurrying for safety. I am an instrument the Choir keeps inside its own body.

At night I climb no longer. Instead the others climb *to* me. They come in their slow rivers of damp skin and glowing algae, carrying beetle-lanterns that paint the underside of my fern-roof in soft gold and violet. They press their foreheads to the living wood that has replaced my left collarbone. They ask me questions in the braided language of water and cricket and grief.

I answer by changing the pitch of the entire balcony.

Sometimes they bring newborns still smelling of orchid pollen and shrimp. They hold the infants up so the first thing the child sees is not the roaring green dark, but the place where my eyes have become. My pupils are horizontal slits now, like the tree-frogs. When the luminous waves roll across the cliffs I flash in time with them, slow and deliberate, so the babies learn the rhythm of the basin before they learn fear.

I remember being afraid once.

I remember having a mother who sang me out of a bromeliad tank. I remember the exact pitch of the cricket that became my spirit-kin (a small emerald scraper whose descendants still live in the hollow behind my left ear). I remember choosing, at fourteen, to lie down against this particular fern and offer it my legs in exchange for the ability to keep the argument alive even when my own voice failed.

That was the last time I stood upright as a human.

Now my hair is a curtain of pale green moss that trails thirty feet down the curve of the balcony. My fingers have lengthened into secondary roots that I use to weave new bridges when the old ones rot. My voice has become something the basin itself uses when it needs to speak in a register too low for any single throat to carry.

I am not a leader.

I am not even entirely a person anymore.

I am a register in the hymn.

When I die—when the last of my human meat finally softens and the fern decides it is tired of sharing its vascular system—I will not be buried. I will simply stop singing the high, clear note that warns of collapse. The fern will drink what remains of me in a single wet season. My bones will become new ladders. My memories will be stored as sugars and carried upward by ants. Children will one day learn the shape of my old face in the pattern of lichen that grows where my mouth used to be.

Until then I remain here, half flesh, half argument, perpetually damp, perpetually listening.

If you were to sit beside me long enough—if you let the constant upward rain soak through your clothes and the roaring hymn flatten every thought you arrived with—you might notice that my eyes, though they rarely move, are never empty.

They are full of every voice that has ever been devoured by this place and decided, instead of dying, to keep singing from inside the green.

I am Leaf-That-Listens.

I am the wet green pause between one note of the Choir and the next.

And I am exactly as loud, exactly as hungry, and exactly as alive as the place that holds me.

Would you like to sit with me a while?

The fern has grown an extra balcony just large enough for one more body, if you are willing to let it taste you.
