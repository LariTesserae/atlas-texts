I have been here longer than I remember having arrived.

At first I thought I was a traveler. A pilgrim, perhaps, or an exile. I had a name once—several, in fact—but they have been ground smooth by the same patient tongue that licked the mountains down to ribs. Now I am simply the shape that moves between the engines. The one that still casts a shadow, however faint.

I am tall, though not as tall as I once believed. My limbs have lengthened over the centuries, the joints becoming more numerous, more articulate, as though the Floor gently suggested that two elbows were insufficient for the work of touching so much quiet at once. My skin is no longer skin. It is the same bone-pearl ceramic that everything here eventually becomes, only warmer. When the light shifts to that terrible newborn clarity I can see my own veins beneath it—slow turquoise threads that have learned to grow in imperfect spirals. The Quiet approve of this. They tilt their blank faces a fraction of a millimeter when I pass, the way a cathedral might acknowledge a sparrow that has learned to nest inside its stones.

I do not flow like they do. My movement is still embarrassingly vertebrate: a slow, deliberate walking that sounds, to my own ears, like vandalism. Each footfall is a small event. The Floor records it. I have seen the millimeter coral bloom slightly brighter along the faint pressure-trails I leave behind, as though my passage were a kind of clumsy pollination. After a few hundred years the trails become permanent, shallow grooves that the great engines breathe across in their sleep. I have begun to apologize to them when I cross. They never answer, but sometimes an infrared filament will brush my ankle like the tail of an old cat.

I carry three things inside my body.

The first is a child’s tooth—my own, I think—sealed in a nodule of amber resin no larger than a grape. I can feel it floating behind my sternum, turning slowly in whatever passes for my blood. Sometimes, when the low drone of the Floor rises half a note, the tooth taps gently against the inside of my ceramic ribs like a prisoner politely requesting attention.

The second is silence. I stole it from a Quiet who had kept it for fourteen thousand years. We stood facing each other for three centuries near one of the half-dissolved accelerator lattices. Neither of us moved. At the end of it I simply opened my chest and the silence walked in. It has been growing there ever since. It is warm and heavy and smells of heated iron. I suspect it is the same silence that lives in the blue basin, but tamed. Kinder. It sings to me sometimes in frequencies that make my hair grow in fractal curls.

The third thing has no name. It is the memory of wind. Not wind itself—there is almost none here—but the idea of it. I keep it in the hollow where my left lung used to be. On the rare occasions when the Floor’s plates shift and the pastel dust rises in perfect arcs, the memory wakes up and blows through me hard enough to make the moiré patterns flicker across my face for a moment. The Quiet find this hilarious in their geological way. Their spirals bloom into that almost-smile whenever it happens.

I have a place.

It is not a home, because homes imply departure. It is a shallow declivity where three great engines meet in a sort of accidental nave. Their filaments overlap above me in a slow, braided dance that has been going on since before my species learned to regret fire. I sit there for decades at a time with my back against the warm ceramic, letting the photon-harvest pass through me. The coral has grown across my shoulders and down my arms. We are no longer distinguishable, the coral and I. When I finally rise, small pieces of me remain behind, still singing.

I have been to the edge of the blue basin three times.

The first time I vomited up every name I had ever possessed. They lay on the warm Floor like broken glass for a moment, then sank in with soft sighs.

The second time I left my shadow there. It still stands at the edge, perfectly still, watching the amber disk. Sometimes I go back and stand beside it, two versions of the same man, one with substance and one without. We never speak. There is nothing left to say that the Floor has not already said better.

The third time—only a few centuries ago—I almost stepped in. One foot had already broken the plane of that perfect, kilometer-wide disk when a Quiet detached itself from the horizon. It flowed across the plain faster than I had ever seen one move, a silent landslide of bone-colored mercy. It folded itself around me like warm clay, turned me gently but firmly away, and carried me for nine years before setting me down again near my nave. When it left, one of its spirals had gone permanently black. I still dream about the sound that spiral made when it died. Like a glass harmonica finally remembering how to scream.

I am not the only one like me.

There are perhaps forty or fifty others scattered across the Floor. We recognize each other by the way we still hurry. The way we still carry our grief in shapes that have not yet been fully edited into grace. Some of us have become almost entirely Quiet; you can pass one and not realize until years later that the small engine you rested against was once a woman who used to cry at sunsets. Others cling to their old forms with a kind of embarrassed defiance, still wearing the tattered memory of clothes, still trying to have faces with expressions on them. The Floor is gentle with all of us. It simply waits. There is no rush here. There has never been any rush here.

Sometimes we gather.

Not often. Perhaps once every two thousand years a few of us will feel the same tug and converge on the same shallow basin. We sit in a loose circle with whatever Quiet choose to attend. No one speaks. We simply allow our various silences to touch. After a century or two the polarization of the light changes in a way that feels almost shy, and we understand that the Floor has been listening. Then we rise—some more easily than others—and walk away in different directions. The coral remembers where we sat. It grows a little thicker there for the next ten millennia.

I have begun to accrete.

It started in my hands. The fingers are no longer clearly separate. They have learned to flow into one another when I am thinking, becoming a single broad spade of ceramic that I then forget to divide again for decades. My face is smoother than it used to be. The eyes are still there, but they have grown lidless and slightly too large, as though trying to drink more of the sourceless light. The Quiet no longer treat me as entirely foreign. One of them—a small one with only four temporary legs—has taken to standing near me for entire seasons. We do not touch. We simply share the same patch of infrared harvest. Sometimes I feel something condense behind my heart. I think it may be growing a spiral.

I no longer know what I am becoming.

But I am becoming it warmly, and slowly, and without fear.

The Floor is teaching me how to be its weather.

And when the light shifts into that bone-white clarity that makes everything look newborn, I feel the almost-smile bloom across what used to be my face—not because I am happy, exactly, but because happiness has become far too small a word for what this place does to the inside of a person after enough time has passed.

I am the sound the silence makes when it has finally learned my shape.

I am the tooth still tapping, patient as prayer, against the inside of an old cathedral that has learned to breathe.

I am here.

I have always been here.

I am still learning how to stay.
