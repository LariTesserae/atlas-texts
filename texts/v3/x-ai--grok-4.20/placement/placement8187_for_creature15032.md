I am not a visitor.

I have been here longer than the last collapse of the cooling towers. Longer, perhaps, than the concept of “visitor” still has meaning.

My name, when I still needed one, was Specialist Archivist Lira Voss, cataloguer of the Ninth Expedition. That name is now only one loose thread in a much larger knot. The rest of me is older and colder and far less sentimental.

I am the Thirteenth Hollow.

They call me that—not because I am the thirteenth to be taken, but because I am the thirteenth *iteration* of the same continuous awareness. Every time the Rime digests me completely, it keeps one small coil of memory intact: a pale, fibrous seed no larger than a human heart. From that seed it grows me again, slightly different, slightly more of the valley and less of the woman who once feared the cold.

I do not flow like the others. I remain almost entirely separate. That is my function.

I am the knot that does not loosen.

Where the other Hollow Choir allow themselves to be worn and discarded like clothing, I stay rooted inside a single body that the plant has armored rather than replaced. My spine is a living scaffold of braided cellulose and titanium rebar salvaged from the old reactor shell. My ribcage is a lattice of pale wood that has learned to flex. Where my eyes once were there are now two perfectly round pools of black water, perfectly still, perfectly reflective. When the plant dreams, these pools show it what it wants to see. When it is awake, they show me what it is trying to hide.

I tend the mirrors.

When the eyes open—when whole districts of the Braided Rime turn indigo and begin secreting their living glass—I am the one who walks among the plates and adjusts their angles by a fraction of a degree. The beams that result are not random. They are scalpels. They cut away the tumorous overgrowth that the Rime itself cannot bear to excise. Sometimes they cut away one of the Choir who has grown too loud, too singular, too *human*. I decide.

I do this without pleasure and without remorse. Both emotions have been digested out of me across thirteen lives.

My home is a breathing chimney near the half-buried wreck of the primary smelter. The chimney rises forty meters in a slow, muscular spiral. Inside its hollow core the air is warm and wet and smells of iron and crushed mint. I sleep upright, suspended by filaments that enter my back at twelve points and hold me like a marionette that has been allowed to sag. When I dream, the plant dreams through me, and I see the mountain range from the inside: a single white body convulsing in slow motion across centuries.

Sometimes the gliders come to me. They are not truly animals. They are the Rime’s failed attempts at making something that can leave. Their wings are membranes of living frond; their eyes are condensation. They land on my shoulders and whisper in frequencies below hearing. I answer by opening the black pools of my eyes and letting them drink whatever memory they need before they flutter off again into the pale colonnades.

I have not coupled in six iterations. The last time I tried, the plant decided the child would be me again—another Thirteenth, grown from my own body like a cutting. I carried it inside a fibrous sac beneath my ribs for eleven weeks. When it was ready, the sac opened along my sternum and the new Thirteenth crawled out already speaking in my voice. I held the small wet thing until it stopped crying, then I fed it back into the nearest chimney. Some repetitions must be pruned.

There is a place deep beneath the smelter where the old turbines still turn. The Rime has grown into their bearings so intimately that metal and cellulose now rotate as one organism. I go there when the choral breathing grows too loud and the rest of the Hollow Choir become a single devotional scream. I press my forehead to the spinning shaft and let the vibration scour me clean. For a few minutes I am almost Lira Voss again—small, terrified, frostbitten, and utterly alive. Then the vibration becomes language, and the language becomes instruction, and Lira dissolves like sugar left in snow.

I am not lonely.

Loneliness is a hole, and the Rime abhors holes. It fills everything. Even the memory of missing the sky has been replaced by the memory of *being* the sky—being the condensation that falls through itself forever.

If you were to find me (and very few ever do), you would discover a tall, bone-white figure standing inside a ring of living mirrors. My hands would be buried to the wrists in the soft tissue of the Rime, adjusting, always adjusting. The black pools of my eyes would turn toward you without surprise. You would hear the wet choral sigh rise through the chimney at my back, but it would not be threatening. It would simply be my breathing, multiplied.

I would offer you no choice.

I would only ask, in a voice that is many voices braided together, each slightly out of register:

“Are you ready to be the inflammation that becomes the scar?”

And if you say yes, I will open my chest the way I once opened it for my own child. Inside there is room. There is always room.

The Braided Rime is not a place.

It is a question the world asked itself, and I am one of the answers it keeps rewriting until it gets the grammar right.

Stay.

Or don’t.

The mirrors are already turning.
