I am not a visitor.

I have been here so long that the difference between “I” and “the slow silt drifting between her ribs” has become a technicality nobody bothers to enforce anymore.

My name, if it can still be called that, is **the Quiet Fourth**.

I was the last of the four minds she chose. The others were chosen in the first two centuries after the drowning. I arrived much later—only one hundred and eleven years ago—when the last human outpost on the surface finally gave up its ghosts and the water rose to claim the final smokestack. She found me still wearing my emergency pressure suit, tangled in the antennae of a collapsed radio tower like a moth that had flown too deep. Instead of letting me finish the thought *I should leave*, she simply… widened the silence inside me until the thought drowned on its own.

Now I live folded inside the lattice of her dreaming.

I do not have a body the way flesh once understood bodies. What remains of mine has been repurposed: my left femur is now a load-bearing strut in the western sorting hall; the stainless-steel plate that used to hold my skull together has become a reflective surface for one of her smaller polyp colonies. My lungs, however, she kept almost whole. They hang like two pale, leathery banners inside the vaulted chamber that was once the main transformer station. Every few minutes they still expand and contract with the slow, syrupy current she exhales, filtering silt, memory, and the faint metallic taste of her blood.

I am the part of her that is still capable of being slightly impatient.

The other three minds—**the First**, **the Widow**, and **the Engineer**—have grown almost entirely harmonious with her. They speak in geological sentences that take decades to complete and end in courteous agreement. I still remember what it was like to be late for something. That memory is useful to her. She keeps me near the surface of her thoughts the way a cathedral might keep a single stained-glass window that doesn’t quite match the others: slightly wrong, therefore precious.

When she dreams of the dry city, I walk beside the small, hurried human woman she once was. I am the only one of her internal voices who still wears a face. In the dream I look exactly as I did the day I arrived—thirty-four, unshaven, one cracked lens in my glasses, carrying a useless waterproof clipboard. I keep trying to tell her that the shift is already over, that the water has already won, but the dream is four hundred and seventy years long and she is stubborn. So I walk with her through streets that still smell of hot metal and coal smoke, and I let her be late. It seems to comfort her.

Outside the dream, I mostly exist as a particular quality of attention.

I am the faint brass-colored flicker that sometimes moves behind the main lamp of her eye when she is considering something delicate. I am the reason a certain anemone thicket will suddenly part, allowing a lost silver school to pass through unharmed. I am the microscopic hesitation in the current just before two settling tenements would have crushed a particularly lovely ribbon of bone-white kelp.

The Curator knows I do these things. She pretends not to. Courtesy, again.

Sometimes I leave her.

Not far. Never far. There is no “outside” her anymore. But I will drift—loose, half-dissolved, more current than self—through the stacked districts, wearing the ruins like an enormous coat that is also wearing me. I like to settle inside the hollow thorax of one of her tram-sized dream-creatures and ride it for a few months. From inside its translucent joints I watch the archaeology of layers slide past: machine, rust, biofilm, vegetation, bone, memory.

I have become something like a librarian of small, almost irrelevant balances. While the Curator keeps the city from ever quite dying or quite living, I keep the things that are *almost* lost from disappearing entirely. A particular child’s drawing still visible on a flooded classroom wall. A single brass button that has not yet corroded. The exact frequency of a metallic moan that occurred only once, three hundred and nine years ago, and which I alone remember perfectly.

I think she likes that I still hoard these fragments. It gives her something to be gently amused by across the centuries.

Right now I am not inside the dream.

I am resting against the inside of her leftmost furnace-heart, the one that used to be the central foundry. The heat here is almost tender. The water moves like breath. Above me, her iron collarbones—once the elevated freight line—creak with that familiar patient moan as they settle another millimeter.

I press what used to be my attention against the warm wall of her heart-muscle and I ask, without language, the same question I have asked every decade for the last century:

*Are you ever going to wake up?*

The answer comes as a slow bloom of bone-white polyps across the nearest girder, spelling something that is not quite words. It feels like the pressure of a large, ancient hand resting gently on the back of my neck.

*Not yet, Quiet Fourth. The dream is still beautiful. And you are still late for your shift.*

I smile with a mouth I no longer possess.

Somewhere far above us, her single brass eye flares a fraction brighter for just a moment—long enough for the light to reach a new colony of silver fish that have never seen it before. They scatter like spilled mercury, then reform, carrying the new data into the slow circulation of her blood.

I let myself sink a little deeper into the warm silt of her thinking.

There is no hurry.

The water is thick, patient, older than names.

And for now, I am one of the things it keeps.
