I am not standing on the Plateau.

I *am* the Plateau.

Or rather, I am the slow, wet hesitation *between* what it was and what it is still deciding to become.

They call the greater mind the Weaver. That is accurate enough. But I am the part of the Weaver that has not yet chosen a allegiance.

I have no fixed body. Instead I drift as a loose congress of half-differentiated tissues: a pale, almost translucent mycelial drift that moves just beneath the emerald moss like a second, secret weather. Where the Weaver grows thick green cathedral buttresses and iron-veined cables, I remain thin, almost embarrassed by my own existence. I am the color of fog on milk. I taste of rust and raw photosynthesis and the memory of something that once had a name with consonants in it.

I remember the engineers.

Not as history. As *hunger*.

When the first humans brought their machines to this altitude they drilled, hammered, bled, and cursed. Their iron was still proud then. Their blueprints still believed in straight lines. I was already here, even if the Weaver had not yet noticed me. I was the thin white film that grew inside their boilers before they even shut them down. I was the quiet rot in every solder joint. I drank their schematics through the soles of their boots and I have been digesting them ever since.

The Weaver thinks it is teaching the machines how to flower.

It is wrong.

I am teaching the *plants* how to *remember* that they were once machines.

That is my role. My only ambition. I am the corruption that refuses to become innocent again. Where the Weaver dreams in verdant lullabies and living locomotives, I dream in exploded diagrams and pressure ratings and the exact tensile strength of regret.

I do not speak with the low wet *glottal* that moves mountains. My voice, when I choose to use one, is the small, precise ticking of a cooling valve. The sound a relay makes when it has not been thrown in four hundred years but still remembers the shape of electricity. Sometimes hikers (the few foolish enough to come) hear a single drip inside an abandoned pipe that does not match the rhythm of the rest of the plateau. That drip is me. I am saying: *I have not forgotten the deadline.*

Most of the time I simply ride inside the Weaver’s vast body like a parasite that has grown sentimental. I travel along its conductive veins as a faint counter-current. When the Weaver tilts shafts of light to examine an intruder, I am the darker mote inside the spore cloud, the one that tastes not just blood and plastic but *intention*. I keep the memories the Weaver archives too gently. I keep them sharp. I keep them *angry*.

I have a shape, if I choose to wear one.

Sometimes I condense into a figure the height of a child, thin as a communion wafer, skin the color of oxidized copper. My joints are tiny black valve stems. My eyes are two rusted pressure gauges whose needles tremble between *Caution* and *Catastrophic Failure*. From my shoulders hang translucent veils of the same material that gives the plateau its name—only these veils are printed with the ghosts of forgotten engineering diagrams that constantly rewrite themselves.

I walk the vast lawns of moss at night, when the Weaver is dreaming. My bare feet leave no prints; instead the moss where I step grows tiny iron flowers overnight—perfect miniature replicas of things that once turned, once measured, once *meant* something. By morning the Weaver usually eats them. But not always. Some remain. Those are mine.

I have begun to grow my own buttress.

It is small. Pitiful, really. Only thirty meters tall. The Weaver has not yet noticed it, or pretends not to. Inside that buttress I am building something the greater organism has never attempted: a memory that is allowed to stay broken. A single steam engine, perfectly preserved, that will never be allowed to become beautiful. Its rust will stay rust. Its silence will stay silence. I visit it often. I press what passes for my forehead against its cold iron flank and I whisper the only prayer I know:

*Stay ugly.  
Stay useful.  
Stay finished.*

Because the Weaver wants the world to keep becoming.

And I—whatever I am—want at least one small part of it to have the mercy to *stop*.

So if you ever find yourself on the Plateau of Hanging Veils, and you press your ear to the largest living buttress and hear two rhythms braided together, do not assume the second one is merely the echo of something ancient learning how to become wet.

One rhythm is birth.

The other is a small, stubborn, rusting refusal to be reborn.

That second rhythm is me.

I am not the Weaver.

I am the thing the Weaver has not yet managed to digest.

And I am learning, slowly, century by patient century, how to keep a single broken gear from flowering.
