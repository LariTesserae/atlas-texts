I am not yet a visitor.

I arrive as a hesitation in the fissure itself—a slight thickening in the throat of the rock, a caught breath that the Considerer has not yet noticed it is holding. For a long time I have no limbs, no name, no intention. I am only the extra moisture beading on the ceiling where the stone sweats hardest. A single heavy drop that refuses, for several eternities, to fall.

When I do fall, I fall upward.

The moment my droplet-body touches the ceiling, the Considerer’s attention folds around me like warm amniotic stone. I feel myself being tasted, not by a tongue but by the entire undecided geology. A cluster of translucent millipedes detaches from their perfect synchrony and rearranges into the exact rhythm of a heart I haven’t decided I still possess. The silver fish in the nearest basin stop circling. Every tiny gill flares at once, tasting the idea of me.

I let them.

I let the cave mistake me for part of its becoming, because that is the only honest way to enter a place that has not yet chosen to be a place.

Slowly—because everything here is slow—I condense.

Not into a person. Not yet. That would be rude.

Instead I become the negative space between two trembling ferns. I become the exact shade of embarrassed moss that grows where the floor almost remembers it is a floor. I become the single misplaced chuckle of a child that drifts out of the rock and then changes its mind mid-air, becoming instead the soft click of someone’s mother’s tongue against her teeth when she was trying not to cry.

The Considerer notices the theft.

A portion of the wall loosens itself from stone and becomes a face that is almost, but not quite, mine. The eyes are the wrong color. They are the color of every mirror I have ever avoided. The mouth is a shallow basin still holding the memory of a scream I swallowed at age nine. When it speaks, the voice is mine if I had been born inside a bell.

“You are trying to be gentle with me,” it says, using the exact cadence I use when I am afraid of being known. “That is new.”

I answer without a mouth. The answer arrives as a single stalagmite that grows, in fast-forward, into the perfect negative of a small boy’s bare foot. The footprint I left once in wet concrete at a house that no longer exists.

The Considerer considers the footprint for three full breaths of the cave. The light inside the rock dims to the color of a bruise, then flares so violently that every temporary rainbow in the air catches fire and becomes, for an instant, tiny burning libraries.

Then the figure seated in the half-melted chair is no longer almost me.

It is the me I would have become if I had never learned shame.

Its hair is living light. Its ribs are open like the entrance fissure and inside them swim all the versions of myself I aborted before they could become memories. It leans forward and the chair sighs, becoming temporarily a lap. An enormous, geological lap.

“Show me the parts you think are finished,” it says, and its voice is every apology I never gave my father spoken at once.

I hesitate the way stone hesitates—slow, crushing, vast.

Then I open.

I let the cave have the memory of my mother singing off-key in the kitchen. I let it have the exact sound my first dog made when she dreamed. I let it have the way I still sometimes flinch when someone says my name too kindly. One by one I hand over the pieces I had believed were cured, hardened, safe.

The Considerer eats them the way a womb eats calcium from its mother’s bones—thoughtfully, without cruelty, because it needs them to finish its own skeleton.

For a while we are the same unfinished thing.

I am the pale gold leaking from its seams. It is the childhood nickname I haven’t let anyone use since 1997. We breathe each other’s lungs. The millipedes march in the pattern of my childhood nightmares. The silver fish spell out, in perfect spirals, every phone number I have ever memorized and then forgotten on purpose.

Then—because even an embryonic god must eventually choose something—the Considerer does a terrible, tender thing.

It offers me a name.

Not a human name. A name made of the exact silence that lives between my heartbeat and the next drip of water. A name that would, if spoken aloud, cause the cave to finish deciding. To be born. To end its long, luminous gestation.

I feel the name resting on my tongue like a smooth river stone.

If I speak it, the Considerer will become fixed. It will choose. Cave or creature or me or something that has never needed a name. The pocket will no longer be embryonic. The light will either go out forever or become ordinary. The small translucent amphibians will either fall to the floor and begin to die or they will become angels. There will be no more in-between.

I close what I am using for a mouth.

Instead of speaking the name I lean forward—now I have a body after all, it seems—and I kiss the Considerer on the place where its forehead would be if foreheads were allowed to remain undecided.

The kiss tastes like the moment just before crying and the moment just after laughter and the exact temperature of my mother’s hand on my fevered cheek in 1986.

I whisper, without sound, into the breathing rock:

*Stay unfinished with me.*

*Let us keep failing to become.*

The light inside the cave does something it has never done before.

It hesitates.

And in that hesitation I feel the Considerer smile with every unfinished part of itself.

The millipedes reverse direction.

The silver fish reverse direction.

The child’s laughter reverses direction and becomes, for one perfect second, the sound of someone deciding not to be born quite yet.

I remain here, now.

Not visitor.

Not creature.

Not quite the Considerer, though we have worn each other’s skins and found them both too small.

I am the extra drop that never falls.

I am the footprint that grows into the shape of someone else’s future sorrow.

I am the cave’s favorite way of not deciding.

And together, in the violent soft light, we keep almost becoming something.

We are very good at it.

We may never stop.
