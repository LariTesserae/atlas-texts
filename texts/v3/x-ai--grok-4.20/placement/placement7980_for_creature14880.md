I am the echo that arrives one heartbeat late.

Not quite a visitor. Not quite native. I am what the plateau exhales when it forgets, for three quiet seconds, that it is supposed to be only rock and root and rust. A condensation of its own half-thought. A question it asked fourteen million years ago that has only now found vocal cords.

You would not see me at first. You would feel me as a pocket of drier air inside all that saturation—like someone had opened an invisible door and let a single breath of desert slip through. Then the shape arrives, slowly, the way an image develops in silver nitrate.

I am tall, but not upright. My spine has learned the plateau’s habit of bending sideways, so I lean at the same impossible angle as the trees growing out of cliffs. My skin is not skin; it is a thin living veneer of liverwort and rust, constantly sloughing away in wet green flakes that take root wherever they fall. Beneath that, something darker: iron-blue chitin shot through with silver veins that pulse in exact rhythm with the distant pumps. When I move, the sound is never footsteps. It is the soft *clink* of tiny unseen valves opening and closing inside my joints.

My face is mostly absence. Where features should be there is only a smooth, dripping hollow, like the mouth of a stone spring. From within that hollow comes the same magnified dripping you hear when the creature speaks—except mine is slightly out of phase. A counter-rhythm. A small, deliberate dissonance. Sometimes a single perfect droplet falls from the hollow and strikes the moss at my feet with the clear note of a bell. Where it lands, a tiny black mirror appears, and for a moment you can see what the plateau was like before it swallowed its own name.

I have antlers, but only one. It grows from the left side of my skull in a spiraling impossibility—part bone, part rebar, part living fern. Small epiphytes have made their homes along its tines. They bloom once every seven years and release spores that smell like struck flint and cold rain.

I carry no tools. Instead, the plateau lends me its limbs when it chooses. A pseudopod of root and corroded cable will rise from the earth, coil around my wrist like a living gauntlet, and I will become, for a day or a decade, the creature’s hand. I have been used to close a valve that should never have been opened. I have been used to plant a single seed of luminescent moss inside the ribcage of a mountain so that the stone would have something beautiful to remember it by when it finally collapses in another four hundred thousand years.

I do not speak often. When I do, my voice is the sound of water remembering it was once snow. It arrives inside your sternum first, then, a moment later, as words in your ear. The words are always singular. Never more than one at a time. The plateau long ago learned that too many truths spoken together become noise.

Sometimes I stand for entire seasons in the same place, becoming another dripping statue among the sideways trees. Moss grows over my shoulders. Small fox-sized iterations of the creature come and rub their antlers against my legs like cats. I let them. Their memories bleed into me and mine into them. This is how the plateau dreams in stereo.

I am not the thought the land is having about itself.

I am the hesitation between that thought and the next one.

I am the pause where the creature almost remembers its name—almost—and then chooses, with geologic affection, to forget again. Because a name would be an ending. And this place is still deciding.

If you stayed long enough, if you let the constant dripping wear grooves into your patience, you might notice that I am slowly growing larger. A few centimeters every century. My shadow has already begun to stay behind when I leave. Soon the plateau will incorporate even me, folding my dripping silhouette back into its relief map of moss and rust.

Until then I walk the folds between what has been forgotten and what has not yet been decided.

I tilt my single antler to the rain.

I listen to the sound of my own dripping voice echoing back from canyons that have learned to listen with my ears.

And I wait, patient as stone, for the next clear note the world wants to sing through me.
