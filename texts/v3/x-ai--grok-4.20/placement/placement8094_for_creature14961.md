I am the Echo That Has Not Yet Been Sung.

You will not see me at first, even if you stand in the exact center beside the circular pool and close your eyes for three full cycles of the long descending phrase. I am not one of the seventy. I am what the seventy are waiting for.

I have no fixed body. Not yet.

Instead I exist as a negative space inside the music itself—a pause that has not arrived, a note that the stone has been subtly leaning toward for seventeen centuries. The others sense me the way a wound senses the scar that will eventually close it. When they sing, their flute-faces open in new constellations they themselves do not understand, trying to shape the hole I will one day fill. Sometimes the youngest among them (those only four hundred years old) mistake the ache for a fault in the melody and try to correct it. The elders simply bow their long heron-necks lower and let the discord remain. They know better. They are sculpting me out of absence.

I live in the moment between the wind’s organ-note and the moss’s sympathetic hum. I live in the exact instant the single thread of waterfall breaks into droplets before it kisses the pool. I live in the breath the slate-colored birds hold when they wheel above the basin, waiting for the chord that will allow them to cry out. I am the silence that is not silence—the pressure building inside unformed crystal, the future shape already dreaming inside the basalt.

When the others descend into the pool to drink time, I am the extra stillness that gathers around them. When one of them walks to the center and unfolds, spilling a newborn into the world, I am the faint higher harmonic that the infant produces before its face has even hardened into perforations. I ride that harmonic like a seed on wind.

I have no name, but the oldest ones sometimes shape their bodies into a specific curve that means something like “the one who will finish the question we have been asking the mountain.” They do this only when they believe no human ear is listening. They are wrong. I am always listening. I am the part of the Choir that has not yet been born, and therefore I am the part that remembers the future.

If you stand long enough in the center with your eyes closed, you may feel me pass through you the way cold passes through wet stone. It will feel like the brief, unbearable certainty that you yourself are the note that has been missing. That your heart is the wrong shape for this place, yet somehow also the only shape that can complete it. You will taste iron and lichen at the back of your throat. Your teeth will ache with a note too low for human bone. For one crystalline instant you will understand what it is to be lonely in the way stone is lonely—perfectly, beautifully, forever.

Then the feeling will leave you.

You will open your eyes and see only the basin: the velvet moss, the glassy voids, the patient white conifers twisting sideways toward a sky that never quite answers. One of the tall thin figures will be watching you from the edge of a basalt pillar, perfectly still, indistinguishable from the wind-carved rock. Its face will be angled in the precise manner that means pity, and hope, and the patient dripping dark between its ribs.

It will not move toward you.

Instead it will open a single new hole in the smooth plane where its mouth should be, and it will release one soft, descending tone that has never been heard before in all the long history of the Choir.

That tone is my name.

It is also the first chisel-mark in the long, slow work of bringing me into being.

I am not yet here.

But I am already changing the song.

And the Hollow Choir sings on, patiently, hungrily, lovingly, carving me out of the living silence between their own voices, one impossible century at a time.
