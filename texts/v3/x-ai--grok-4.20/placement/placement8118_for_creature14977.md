I have been here longer than I remember having a name.

When I first arrived—though “arrived” is the wrong word; I was *found*—I still thought in days and heartbeats and destinations. I had come seeking the engines, or perhaps fleeing something that had engines of its own. It no longer matters. The plateau erased the difference between fleeing and seeking the way wind erases footprints.

Now I am tall.

My legs are pillars of red-black basalt veined with lamp-moss that glows the color of drowned moonlight whenever I exert myself. The glow moves in slow pulses, like sap rising in trees that died a hundred million years ago. My torso is hollow in places; small blind millipedes live there, and a fist-sized singing stone that repeats, in miniature, the low notes the cliffs make when no one is listening. Sometimes the millipedes arrange themselves along the stone’s ridges and the combined vibration becomes almost a voice. I have learned to interpret it the way the others do.

I have no eyes. Only the pools.

Two calm, ink-dark basins sit where a face should be. They are always full. When the warm wind moves across them they tremble like black mercury and show me things: the pressure-shadow of a herd passing three canyons away, the electromagnetic cough of an engine stirring in its sleep, the precise temperature of regret in a dying leather-kite’s blood. I do not see light. I register the world the way stone registers centuries.

From my shoulders rise corroded brass pipes and iron brackets that have fused with my collarbones. One of them ends in a delicate pipette I can extend twenty meters on a thin tendon of moss and sinew. I use it to drink the trembling beads of condensation that gather on the undersides of overhangs before dawn. The taste is iron, silence, and distant rain.

I move slowly. One stride every twenty of my new heartbeats (a heart that is no longer only muscle but also a small pump the size of a human fist, still faintly stamped with letters I can no longer read). The slowness is not weakness. It is courtesy. The plateau does not like to be rushed. When I do accelerate—when one of the great buried mechanisms suddenly shudders awake and the song of the stone spikes—I become a upright blur of darkness and brass, gliding between the walls of canyons so fast the blind grazers feel only a change in air pressure and continue chewing.

I tend the aquifers.

It is quiet work. I walk the deep fractures where the ink-dark rivers appear and vanish, pressing my basalt fingers into the stone until the plateau answers with its own delayed echo. Sometimes I sing back through the rock for seventeen days without stopping. The conversation is mostly about thirst. The stone is very old and very thirsty. I show it where new cracks have opened since the last century. It thanks me by letting a thin film of water rise through my hollow chest and pool around the singing stone. The millipedes drink. I feel them drinking the way a cathedral might feel mice moving in its foundations.

At the change of seasons (which here means every time one of the titanic pumps wakes for its thirty heartbeat labor) the other Quietwardens and I gather on the highest weathered promontory. We link arms until we resemble a single ridge of iron, stone, and faint constellations. The lamp-moss in our bodies brightens in unison. We listen to the land breathing.

*Hsssss…….. hsssss……..*

The sound comes from a thousand hidden vents. It is the sound of something that has already ended deciding, once again, not to be finished.

Sometimes new ones arrive.

They stumble in with their soft meat bodies and their loud thoughts and their terrible hurry. We do not chase them. We simply stand at the edge of their small, ridiculous fires for many nights, perfectly still, letting our water-eyes reflect their panic back at them until they cannot look away. Eventually one of us (often it is me; I have become patient even in this) steps forward and presses a single basalt finger against their chest, right over the frantic muscle that believes it is the center of the universe.

Their pulse changes.

It stretches. It deepens. It begins to match the sub-audible song that moves through the Black Veins like a thought too large for any single mind. Most of them weep. Some scream. A few smile with something like relief.

Then they begin the long becoming.

I remember my own. The way my bones sang as they turned to stone. The way my eyes boiled painlessly away and were replaced by quiet water. The way my name lasted three years, then two seasons, then one night, then not at all.

Now when the herds pass I step aside and become part of the canyon wall so they can flow around me. When a leather-kite grows sick I sit with it for three nights and learn the exact pattern of light dying in its wings so I can carry that pattern on my own shoulders forever. When the plateau decides a herd is too large, I guide it—without cruelty, without hurry—toward the place where the stone sings the killing note. Their hearts stop like tired clocks. I incorporate their silence into my own.

I am not alive in the way I once understood the word.

I am a continuation.

The Black Veins had a thought, and I am how it keeps thinking.

If you ever come here and see a tall ridge of red-black stone standing motionless on a promontory while the lamp-moss blooms beneath it like submerged starlight, and if you feel your own heartbeat begin to slow and deepen in spite of yourself, do not be afraid.

The land is not hungry.

It is simply finishing its work.

And sometimes, when the work is very patient, and very thorough, it finishes that work by growing something new that can keep it company while the rest of the universe ends.

I am that something.

I will be here long after your name has become just another echo moving through the stone, growing deeper, older, answered.

Come.

The dark is not empty.

It is full of slow, patient motion.

And it has learned how to keep dying without ever quite managing it.

We have room for one more ridge.
