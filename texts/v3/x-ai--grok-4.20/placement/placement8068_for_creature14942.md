I am not standing in the Sighing Flat.

I am the pause between two overlapping sighs.

You would not see me at first. You would only notice that one of the shallow bowls in the rock has gone perfectly, impossibly still. The constant weeping of the stone has stopped there, as though the basin itself has drawn in a breath and forgotten to let it go. A small absence no larger than a grave. A circle of drier black glass surrounded by a million trembling beads that refuse to roll any closer.

That is where I am.

I was not born here. I was *exhaled*.

Long ago (though time is a joke the land no longer bothers to tell), something living tried to leave. A pilgrim, a scholar, a criminal, a lover; the reason has rotted away. What matters is that the basin would not let the attempt finish. It took the half-departed thing, folded it like wet cloth, pressed it down into the stone, and kept only the part that could never again say *I*.

So now I am that kept part.

I have no name the sky-beasts can hear. The Sighers do not name me either; they simply acknowledge my presence with a slight sideways tilt of their hollow faces, the way one mourning person might nod to another at a funeral that has lasted three billion years.

My body is mostly negative space. Where the Sighers are tall and mantis-like and made of wet volcanic glass, I am short, almost child-sized, and made of the *lack* between droplets. You can see through me in places. If you tried to touch me your fingers would pass through a cold membrane of condensed regret and come out smelling of drowned bells. Sometimes, when the great membranous herds pass directly overhead and the world turns aquarium-blue, you can see faint golden rings drifting inside my chest like slow, exhausted fish. Those are what is left of the eyes that once belonged to the thing that tried to leave.

I do not unfold across the land the way the Sighers do.

I *remember* myself into new places.

One moment the stone is only stone and violet lattices. The next, I am simply there, already kneeling at the lip of one of the primitive domes, small glassine hands resting on basalt that is warmer than it has any right to be. The dome answers me with its three-heartbeat bell-tone. I answer with a sound that is not quite a sigh. It is thinner. Higher. The sound a child might make if it had been crying underwater for ten thousand years and had finally run out of tears but not out of sorrow.

The Sighers allow me to tend the pools.

When one of them comes to fold itself into the still black water, I am the one who smooths the surface first with my translucent palms so that the reflection will be perfect. I am the one who waits outside the dome like a patient mourner while the land tries to remember how to speak through the folded creature inside. Sometimes, when the Sigher emerges again with new colors drifting in its mercury face, it will pause and rest one enormous glassine needle-limb on my shoulder. Not in affection. In recognition. Two different kinds of leftover.

I feed on nothing and am fed on by nothing. Even the bat-like screamers that burst from the sky-beasts’ pores avoid me. They sense that I am already what they would become if they ever stopped screaming.

Mostly I drift between the half-swallowed domes in long, aimless loops that never quite match the ancient circuits of the sky-beasts or the Sighers. I collect the places where the roar grows thin. I press my face (if the hollow where my face should be can be called a face) against the wet stone until the constant booming of the hidden cisterns vibrates the golden rings inside my chest into delicate standing waves. Sometimes I sing back to the land in a register so high it is almost silence. The violet lattices bloom around me in frantic, grateful spirals when I do. They have learned that my voice tastes like the memory of forgiveness.

I am not wise.

I am not even especially sad anymore.

I am what happens when sadness stays so long that the land incorporates it into the weather.

If you were to stumble into the Sighing Flat (though nothing stumbles here that the basin has not already chosen), you might glimpse me only once. Just a small, child-shaped absence kneeling beside a dome while one of the tall Sighers slowly unfolds itself out of the still black pool. You would see the Sighers place one dripping obsidian limb on my shoulder. You would hear the bell-tone fade, and then a sound like the last breath of someone who has finally accepted that the story is over and yet the telling must continue.

Then I would remember myself somewhere else, and the water on the stone would begin weeping again as though it had never stopped.

I have no name.

But if the basin ever decides to speak one, I suspect it will sound exactly like the small, high, endless note that lives in the space between two of its own sighs.

And that note will be me.
