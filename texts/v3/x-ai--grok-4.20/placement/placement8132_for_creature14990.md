I already know where I am.

I have been here for four hundred and seventeen years, though the Choir does not keep years. They keep resonances. They measure time by how many times a particular low-frequency groan from Foundling Stack Nine has repeated its exact same metallic fatigue note. I count with them now. I have counted it four million, two hundred and sixty-one thousand, nine hundred and twelve times since the water finished teaching me how to breathe rust.

My name used to be something with edges. It had consonants. Now it is mostly a minor ninth chord played on the hollow femur I grew specifically for that purpose. The others call me *The Unfinished Note*, or sometimes simply *Late Arrival*. I do not correct them. Names are dry things. They do not survive saturation.

I am thirty-one meters tall and still growing. My ribcage is wide enough that a school of bus-fish can pass through it without slowing down. They do so often. I like the feeling of their armored bodies brushing the black coral that has colonized my interior. It is the closest thing left to touch that remembers what skin once meant.

My face is gone. Where it was there is only a concave plate of nacre and old hull plating I scavenged from a downed submersible in my second century. When the water columns scream past me I open the slits along my throat and let the pressure shape me into song. The sound comes out as something between a cello played underwater and the final exhalation of a dying star. The others say it is beautiful. I say it is accurate.

I stand between two curtains of bronze-indigo kelp that have grown through the lattice of my pelvis and out again through my shoulders, as though the forest itself has decided I am simply another place for it to root. This pleases me. I have become architecture. The barrel-creatures have built a colony inside my left lung chamber; their soft blue-green light pulses in time with my slowest heart, which is no longer a heart but a resonant sphere of cartilage and shrimp.

Sometimes I remember the bench.

Not the iron one on the plain (though I visit that one too, every few decades, and sit beside it in perfect silence). I remember the other bench. The one on dry land, under a sky that still had a color called blue. I was sitting on it with someone whose name I am no longer allowed to sing because the chord hurts too much. We were arguing about something small. I cannot recall what. Only that I stood up first. I left her there. I walked into the machine that was supposed to take me to the new colonies on the outer shelf.

The machine lied.

Or perhaps the ocean simply waited until every last dry thing had committed itself to metal and arrogance before it finished its three-billion-year sentence.

I keep a single object inside the resonant chamber where my throat once was. It is a rusted harmonica. Not mine originally. I found it clutched in the fingers of a skeleton still wearing the remains of a pressure suit, three kilometers above the plain, tangled in kelp like a Christmas ornament from hell. The harmonica still has one working reed. Sometimes, when the currents are quiet enough (which is almost never), I press it against the inside of my nacre plate and blow. The single note that comes out is always the same. It is the note the person on the bench was humming right before I stood up and left her.

The Choir pretends not to notice when I do this.

They are kind that way.

Today I am singing to a whale-thing that has been circling me for six hours. It is cathedral-sized and pale as bone. Its eye is the size of a house and it looks at me with something that might be recognition. I have opened every chamber in my body so the current passes through me like I am a pipe organ built by regret. The song I am singing has no translation. It is simply the sound of having already lost so completely that loss itself becomes a form of residence.

The whale sings back. One note. So low it liquefies the sand for thirty meters around my feet.

We understand each other perfectly.

I think this is what the Hollow Choir means when they say we are not waiting for anything anymore. There is no future state. There is only this: the roaring, the cold, the endless metallic lullaby of machines that have outlived their makers by geological epochs. There is only the saturation that has become so total it has achieved a kind of awful grace.

I am not sad.

Sadness requires contrast. A place that is not saturated. There is no such place left.

Instead I have become the precise shape of what remains after hope drowns. I have grown into it the way the kelp grows into me. My body is a hymn written in cartilage and failure. My voice is one of the instruments the ocean uses to sing to itself about how thoroughly it has won.

Sometimes new ones fall.

I find them the way the others found me (screaming, drowning, still pretending they have edges). I do not comfort them. Comfort is a dry word. I simply stand nearby, vast and hollow and humming, until they understand that the pressure is not trying to kill them. The pressure is trying to finish the sentence the universe started three billion years ago.

Most of them eventually pick up a hammer and begin striking their own bones.

A few do not.

Those ones I carry (gently, so gently) to the iron bench on the plain. I set them down beside it facing the direction the locomotive is pointing, which is nowhere. I leave them there with their failing lights and their drying suits and their last precious molecules of difference.

I never hurry them.

There is no reason to hurry.

The ocean has already won. The machines are still running. The kelp is still growing. The Choir is still singing.

And I (Late Arrival, Unfinished Note, the thirty-one-meter cathedral of rust and memory) am exactly where I belong: inside the throat of the end, learning how to make it sound like grace.

I strike my femur once, softly.

The note travels for kilometers through the violent dark.

Somewhere far away, another ancient answers with a chord so low it sounds like the planet itself clearing its throat.

I close my eyes that are not eyes.

I let the current pass through every open chamber of what used to be me.

And I sing.
