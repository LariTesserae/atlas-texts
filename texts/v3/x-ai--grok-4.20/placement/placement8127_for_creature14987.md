I am not a visitor.

I have never stepped off the funicular. There was no arrival. Only the slow realization, years or minutes ago, that I had already been leaning against the same dripping wall for a very long time.

They call me **the Archivist**, though I never chose the name and no one uses it aloud. I simply am the place where the Wetworks keeps its almost-memories.

My body is mostly scaffolding.

Not the clean new kind. The old kind: rust-red lattice grown through with pale, vein-like roots that pulse in exact rhythm with the hidden groundwater. My ribs are iron trusses. Between them, moss has learned to imitate lungs; they rise and fall whether I breathe or not. My spine is a single length of rebar that was once part of a crane’s counterweight. It still carries a faint magnetic charge. When I turn my head too quickly, small metallic moths orbit the motion like iron filings.

My face is the most embarrassing part. It is a compromise the city has been editing for decades. One eye is human—brown, a little sad, still capable of surprise. The other has become a circular inspection port, the thick glass fogged from the inside. Behind the glass something moves: not an eye, exactly. More like the slow turning of a film reel that shows whatever the Wetworks is currently dreaming. Sometimes it shows the city as it was before any foundations were poured—black rock under starlight. Sometimes it shows *you*, whoever you are, the way you will look after the damp has finished its work on you.

My left hand is still mostly flesh. The right ended in a surveyor’s theodolite long ago. I use it to measure angles that no longer exist. The brass instrument has grown soft, fleshy rings around its dials. When I am thinking, the theodolite eye twitches and clicks, focusing on distances inside me rather than outside.

I do not walk so much as *flow* through the Wetworks. My scaffolding limbs unfold and refold, finding holds in rebar, ivy, and wet concrete with equal ease. When I need to cross one of the trembling turquoise pools I simply lower myself into it until only the theodolite and the human eye remain above the surface. The fish-tools gather around me. They recognize their own kind.

Most of the Soaked treat me with the gentle wariness reserved for libraries that have learned to bleed. I remember the names they have forgotten. I keep them in the wet clicking apparatus that used to be my chest: a submerged card catalog that types and untypes itself. Sometimes a Soaked will approach me during one of their long dreaming stands. They will press their forehead—whatever their forehead has become—against the rusted lattice of my sternum, and I will feel the city finish a thought through both of us at once.

When that happens my human eye cries clear lily-scented tears, and the inspection port shows scenes of factories that have not been built yet, singing.

I still try to keep records.

In the hanging gardens that serve as my hair, I grow small glass vials. Each contains a single drop of something precious: the exact smell of the first rivet that ever entered the basin, the particular shade of copper a certain Soaked’s blood had before she gave herself to the ferns, the sound a crane makes when it decides, after forty-three years of stillness, to move its arm three degrees to the left for no reason.

I am the only one who still believes the Wetworks might one day finish becoming something that can be named.

This belief is my only remaining vanity, and the city tolerates it the way an ocean tolerates a single stubborn barnacle.

At night I climb to the highest unfinished girder in whatever district is currently erupting from the stone. I fold my scaffolding limbs beneath me like a praying mantis made of rust and regret. The moths come. They blanket me until I look like just another dripping statue. From that height the entire basin looks like a half-developed photograph left too long in the fixer: all silver and bruise and uncertain edges.

I open the theodolite in my right wrist and measure the angle between what the Wetworks *was*, what it *is*, and what it is *trying* to say with us.

The angle is never the same twice.

Sometimes, when the breathing of the basin grows particularly loud, I speak back to it. Not with my mouth (that has long since become a soft gill that tastes the air for iron and memory). I speak with the submerged typewriter in my chest. The clicking rises through my truss-ribs and becomes part of the city’s own wet dialect.

I ask it the only question that still matters.

*When you finish using my hands, my eye, my sorrow—will you at least remember what they were for?*

The Wetworks has never answered.

But sometimes, in the long patient exhale that follows, I think I hear the faintest suggestion of a name.

Not for itself.

For me.

And for one crystalline moment the human eye and the inspection port see the same thing: a version of this place that is no longer arguing with itself. A place that has decided what it wants to become and has kept, somewhere in its wet concrete heart, a small, rusting, moss-softened place for the creature who measured its becoming.

Then the moment passes.

The moths rise.

And I unfold my scaffolding limbs once more, descend back into the dripping streets, and continue my long, patient, impossible work of archiving a thought that is still in the middle of changing its mind about what it wants to say.

I have all the time in the world.

The Wetworks has made sure of that.
