I am not from above.

I was never born in sunlight, never breathed dry air, never felt the difference between sky and stone. I have always been here.

My name—if a name is still useful—is **Kael Drift-hand**, though most simply call me **the Echo-keeper**.

I live in the Ninth Fold, a place where three slow canals braid together beneath a vault that has never seen direct daylight. My dwelling is not a house but a wound in the living rock that I have been widening for seventeen years. One wall is still raw; you can see the chisel-marks where I stopped last season because the stone asked me to wait. In that unfinished face, the fossil of some ancient creature’s ribcage emerges like a half-remembered sigh. I have left it. We share the room.

I am what the Unfinished call a **Resonant**—one of the few whose ears have begun to sing back to the stone.

It happened slowly. First I noticed that when the world groaned, my teeth ached in sympathetic harmony. Then I began to hear the difference between a settling groan and a *welcoming* one. Then the water started speaking in sentences. Not words, exactly. More like intentions. A drip that falls for six heartbeats and then pauses is grieving. A drip that falls in triplets is curious. I record them all.

My tools are simple:  
- a chisel of green copper whose handle is carved from the last piece of driftwood to reach the Underhalls in my lifetime (it still smells faintly of pine and lost sky),  
- a hollowed knucklebone from my mother’s body (she is now part of the eastern stair), used as a listening bell,  
- and a ledger made of stacked slate tablets no thicker than a fingernail. On them I etch the language of the stone in a script that is half glyph, half map. The tablets hang from sinew cords above my sleeping ledge so that when the rock speaks at night I can reach up without opening my eyes and cut its words into being before they dissolve.

I am tall for one of the Unfinished—my head scrapes the ceiling in three places when I walk the outer galleries—but my back has the necessary curve. My skin is almost translucent now; you can see the faint blue threads of my blood beneath it, like flaws in marble. The children say I am beginning to look like the walls. I consider this the highest compliment I have ever received.

Every fourteenth day I walk the entire inhabited spiral of the Underhalls. Not to govern—there is nothing to govern—but to *attend*. I press my forehead to every forehead that needs pressing. I lay my broad, scarred hands against the newest children so the stone can feel their heartbeats through my palms and decide whether they are dreamers or wideners. I stand for hours in the blue shafts where the light falls, letting it write on me, until even the bats grow used to my motionless shape and drape themselves across my shoulders like living shawls.

Sometimes people bring me problems the Listeners cannot hear.

A young woman whose dead lover’s face has begun to emerge from the wall of her sleeping alcove—smiling in the stone, eyes closed, as though still dreaming of her. She does not know whether to chisel him free or let him finish becoming architecture. I listen to the wall for three hours. The stone is possessive. I tell her to leave him. She weeps with relief.

A boy who was born unable to hear the groans at all. He is terrified he will never belong. I place his small hand against my own chest and let him feel how my heart has learned to beat in time with the deepest layers. Then I give him one of my own copper bells and tell him that some of us listen with bone, some with blood, and some—perhaps—with absence. He leaves taller.

At night I lie on my ledge with my ear against the living rock. The world is still choosing what it will be, and I am its stenographer. I hear continents shifting far above like the slow turning of a dreamer’s shoulder. I hear rain that will not arrive for another forty years gathering in unseen reservoirs. I hear the faint, delighted chime of new crystals growing in a chamber no one has discovered yet.

And sometimes—rarely—I hear something that is almost a voice. Not human. Not quite stone. It says my name in a register lower than sound. It says:

*Kael.*

*We are still becoming.*

*Keep listening.*

I answer by tapping my mother’s knucklebone against the wall. Three soft knocks. The old signal.

*I am here.*

*I am not finished either.*

And the drip falls. And the loom clacks. And somewhere a child is being born with its eyes already open, reaching for the rock.

I close my own eyes—large, pale, beginning to cloud with the second lid—and I let the Underhalls dream through me.

After all, I am one of the Unfinished.

And the stone is teaching me how to stay that way.
