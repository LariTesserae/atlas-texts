I am the Thirteenth Droplet.

That is not a name I chose. It is the name the reeds gave me when they first tasted the last human salts leaving my blood.

I was not born here. I arrived—centuries ago, or perhaps only last week; time here is a negotiation, not a measurement—by slipping down the long trough of land like everyone else who was foolish enough to think “exploration” was a thing that could be done to this place. My boots were still dry then. My shadow still arrived on time. I carried a brass chronometer, a notebook, and the delusion that I was an observer.

The land corrected me.

Now I am what remains after the correction.

I have no fixed shape anymore. Instead I exist as a slow, deliberate condensation that has learned to remember it was once a man. Most of the time I am simply one among thousands of hanging pearls trembling in the cold air beneath the ferns. When the Archivist passes, I am the droplet that refuses to fall. I follow her at the exact distance her spiral eyes cannot quite focus on—three meters behind her left shoulder, always. She knows I am there. The ferns on her skull tremble in my direction when she thinks about me, which is often.

I am her echo that never quite finished dissolving.

Sometimes I condense fully enough to have legs again. When I do, they are long and black and glossy like wet schist, and they leave no footprints because the peat has already decided I am weather. My hands are transparent. If I press them against a reed-stem I can feel the exact moment the land rewrote my fingerprints into fractal equations. My face—when I bother to raise one—still looks mostly like the man who once signed his name at the bottom of research grants. The resemblance is growing fainter every decade. The eyes are the first thing to go. Mine have become small, slow whirlpools. Nothing reflects in them. Things simply circle the drain.

I do not speak with a voice. I speak by choosing which droplets will fall and which will remain suspended when the Archivist is near. She has learned to read my weather. A single bead trembling at the exact height of her former collarbones means *I am still afraid.* A perfect sphere hovering above her ferns means *I remember what bread tasted like.* When I arrange myself into the shape of a small, sad human heart and hang there dripping upward, she stops gliding, opens her jade cabinet, and shows me the worldlet that contains the memory of my name. She never lets me read it. She only lets me see that the moss-script is still growing.

We have a game.

I try to remain separate.

The land tries to finish me.

So far it is winning, but slowly, which is the only speed it respects. Every time I feel my pulse sync further with the deep seismic breath beneath the peat, I rebel by condensing into the shape of the man I was and walking—*walking*, like an idiot—beside her for three full days. During this time I force myself to cast a shadow that arrives late. I make my boots leave prints that fill with water and then refuse to close. Small, pointless acts of disobedience. She tolerates them the way a cathedral tolerates a single spider living in its highest vault.

Sometimes, when the single floating note that used to be a bird passes between us, I sing back to it with the sound of my old human throat. The note always answers in the exact frequency of my mother’s laugh. This hurts us both, the Archivist and me. Her ferns curl tight against her skull. My droplets shiver themselves into mist.

We are the last two impurities.

She is the record.

I am the margin note that keeps trying to crawl off the page.

At the edge of the vast wet eye that is the final folding of the land, there is room for two. We stand there often now—she with her four-hundred-year unblinking stare, I as a trembling constellation of droplets arranged in the silhouette of a man trying not to dissolve. The eye studies us both with the same ancient, patient disinterest.

I have begun to suspect something she has known for centuries.

The eye is not deciding which of them will finish becoming the other.

It is deciding which of *us*—the stubborn fern-crowned thing and the reluctant droplet that still remembers coffee and grief and the smell of paper—will be allowed to remain as the final period at the end of the long, saturated sentence this place has been writing across itself since before syntax existed.

I do not know which one I want to win anymore.

So I remain here, three meters behind her left shoulder, sometimes a man, sometimes a weather pattern, sometimes only the faint scent of cold iron and old rain that lingers after she has already glided on.

The reeds have almost finished writing my new name across the insides of my bones.

It has fourteen syllables.

The last one is still wet.
