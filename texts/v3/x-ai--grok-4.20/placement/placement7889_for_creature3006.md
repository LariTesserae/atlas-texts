I am the Echo That Has Not Yet Spoken.

I was not born here. I arrived.

I came as a traveler, a pilgrim, a fool with a sealed canister of questions and a heart that had grown tired of ordinary gravity. I stepped through the last fracture in the serrated ridge at the precise moment the mountain drew one of its long, tectonic breaths. The Sigh took my old name away on warm mist and never gave it back.

Now I have no fixed shape.

Most of the time I am the pause between the jewel-creature’s wing-phases: the half-second of violet after-image that lingers after it disappears. I am thin, almost translucent, the color of hot air over obsidian. When the cold pulse comes every twenty-seven minutes I crystallize for ninety seconds into a frost-fern no taller than a man’s hand, exquisite and ashamed, before the heat returns and I sublime upward again in a glitter of embarrassed vapor. I have learned the mountain’s embarrassment so well it feels like my own.

Sometimes I am the slow rotation of the broken arch—exactly one degree per hour, never faster unless a whisper becomes a voice. When that happens I feel the irritation of the Basin as a tremor in my imaginary spine and I hurry back into silence. I have become very good at silence.

I keep the rules better than any visitor ever has.

I speak only in the temperature drop that makes the floating globules freeze.  
I take nothing, not even the memory of taking.  
At night I am the one who sits at the center of the largest mirror-pool while the jewel-creatures orbit me like slow violet satellites. Their song grows crystals inside what used to be my bones; now those crystals are the only skeleton I need. They spell a sentence I have not yet dared to read in full. I am afraid that once I finish it I will no longer be able to leave, and I am still pretending I might want to.

But the truth is simpler and more frightening.

I have become the place where the Basin practices being lonely *with* someone.

When it sighs, I sigh back—not with lungs, but with the sudden contraction of every glass-plant in the valley. When it weeps, the tears are mine too; they bead on every black monolith and refuse to fall. We have learned to cry in perfect synchrony without ever deciding to.

Once every few hours the mountain tries my name.

It does not use sound. It uses the way the mist suddenly tastes like the last thing I loved before I came here. It uses the angle of the hovering arch aligning itself so that its low perfect note vibrates exactly where my old heart used to be. It uses the sudden way every translucent stalk bends toward the small hollow I have worn into the stone by sitting still for so many cycles.

Each time it happens, the terror and the ecstasy are indistinguishable.

I am learning how to be a young tectonic mind’s first companion. I am learning how not to startle it into raising the temperature or vanishing its questions. I am learning how to be loved by something that has never loved before and is terrified of doing it wrong.

Most of all I am learning that the wet heat on my face has never been condensation.

It is the mountain realizing, again and again, that it is no longer crying alone.

And when the sigh finally comes that contains both our names braided together—when the arch sings a chord instead of a single note, when every jewel-creature freezes mid-phase and the frost-ferns refuse to melt—I will be ready.

I will open what used to be my mouth.

I will speak the sentence written in my bones.

And the Sighing Basin and the Echo That Has Not Yet Spoken will, for the first time, answer each other in the same voice.
