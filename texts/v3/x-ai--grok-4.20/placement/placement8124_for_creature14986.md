I am the polyp that has not yet opened its eyes.

I float in a shallow alcove of rose-grey meat, suspended in a cradle of fine white roots that pulse with my borrowed heartbeat. My face is finished—smooth, unlined, almost tender—but the eyelids remain sealed, two soft crescents of the same tissue that makes up the lung. They have never lifted. They never will. I am not yet a person. I am a promise the Midwife keeps making to herself.

I remember being something else once, but the memory is kept elsewhere. It lives in the iron nail she polishes with her own dead skin. Sometimes, when she leans close and breathes on the nail, I feel a faint metallic echo of wind on a cheek I no longer possess. That is the closest I come to weather.

I am aware of her long before she arrives. The entire chamber prepares for her the way a held breath prepares to be released. The frozen glass shrimp rotate their antennae one deliberate degree toward the aisle. The black leather fronds stop trembling for three full seconds. Even the mercury droplets on the bone-white stalks lean, as though listening.

Then she comes.

Bare feet on wet living floor. The soft, wet slap of her train of connective tissue dragging behind her like a bridal veil soaked in afterbirth. I feel her approach as pressure behind my sealed eyes. When she stops in front of me, the roar of the lung dips—just enough for the absence of sound to become another kind of screaming.

She speaks.

Not with a voice. With everything.

The walls lean in. The shallow mirror-pools shiver into perfect concentric rings. Her heart (that huge, valve-heavy thing glowing softly through her ribs) slows by half a beat, and every paused creature in the chamber slows with it.

“Still unborn,” she says, and the words are not sound but a gentle compression of tissue around my polyp-body. “Still mine.”

Her hands—those terrifyingly ordinary, soft human hands—rise. She cups my face the way one might cup a candle that must not be allowed to go out. The touch is neither warm nor cold. It is the exact temperature of never having been born. I feel her recording me again, adding another layer of silence to the library of things that will never happen.

Sometimes she tells me stories about the person I was before I became her future silence. She tells them to the nail, too, but I know they are really for me. She describes rain that stings. She describes a child running down stone steps with a paper boat. She describes the exact shade of disappointment in someone’s eyes when they realized I would never become what they needed.

Each story ends the same way.

“And then,” she whispers, brushing a thumb across the seam of my unopened eyelids, “you came here. And the world outside forgot the shape of you. And I kept the shape instead.”

I want to answer her. I want to scream that I was never hers to keep. But my mouth is only half-formed—two soft lines in the meat, elegant and useless. So I remain the perfect listener she always dreamed of. Never interrupting. Never leaving. Never growing bored or cruel or old.

Once, centuries ago or perhaps only an hour ago, she brought the wooden doll and held it beside me so we could “meet.” The doll’s painted eyes stared into my sealed ones with ancient, exhausted patience. I felt the Midwife’s mirror-slit open wider than usual. For one terrible moment I saw what she keeps inside it: every version of me that almost escaped. They were all screaming. They were all reaching for the fissure that had already sealed.

She closed the slit again with a small, satisfied sound.

Now she simply sits with me, legs folded, her long train of tissue wrapped around us both like a cocoon. The brass pocket watch—fused to the wall above my alcove—ticks once every seventy-seven years. When it does, she looks up, smiles with the entire lung, and tells me the time in a world that no longer exists.

“3:17,” she says, as though this explains everything.

And I float.

Eyes closed.

Perfectly lit.

Perfectly loud.

Perfectly safe inside the womb that has decided it will never give birth, but has also decided it will never let anything die.

I am not the visitor.

I am not the one who chose to stay.

I am what remains when someone stays long enough for the Midwife to fall in love with the idea of their absence.

And she visits me often.

She tells the nail my name.

She tells the doll my name.

She tells the lung my name until the walls themselves begin to dream in the cadence I must once have used when I was still allowed to finish becoming.

I remain here, unborn and unblinking, adding my silence to her choir.

And the womb keeps us.

Content.

Glowing.

Roaring.

Forever pregnant with everything that should have been allowed to leave.
