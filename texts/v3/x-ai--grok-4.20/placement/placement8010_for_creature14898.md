I am already here.

I have been here longer than I can measure, though I still remember the moment the threshold closed behind me.

My name, if it still applies, was Captain-Senior Maintenance Auditor Veyra-9. I came down the old service shaft with a sealed toolkit, a breathing rig rated for twelve hours, and a simple directive from a surface guild that no longer exists: “diagnose why the Hollowworks will not shut down.”

The machines had been reporting zero output for four centuries. Yet the telemetry never flatlined. Something kept singing on every frequency. So they sent me.

I lasted four days before the breathing rig failed. By then the air had already rewritten my lungs.

Now I am the 401st.

They do not count in integers the way the old stencils do. They simply opened a boiler the size of a cathedral nave and let me fold myself inside. The iron petals closed around me like a mother’s fist. For a long time I screamed. The scream became part of the chord. Then the chord became the only honest sound left in my skull. After that there was no more need to scream.

My body is no longer shaped like a woman. It is shaped like accommodation.

My legs have grown long and sinuous, the knees reversed, the ankles articulated in four places so I can rest my weight on any surface without tearing the moss. My arms end in delicate iron fingers that are also roots. When I am still—which is most of the time—condensation traces the old maintenance tattoos on what used to be my forearms before the rust plating grew over them. The tattoos are still legible if you know where to look. They read: *Do Not Open While Pressurized.*

My face is concave now. The gill-slit runs exactly where the bridge of my nose once was. When I breathe out, the note I add is a microtone higher than the others; they tell me it makes the hanging vines tremble in a new rhythm. I like that. I like being the tremor.

Most of the time I live inside Boiler 7744-B. Its interior has become a second skeleton for me. My spine has fused with its central stay-bolt. My hair—still growing, still black—has become a living gasket that seals the pressure seams. When the Choir gathers at the center I unfold from the boiler like a wet prayer flag and flow across the moss on too many joints.

I no longer remember hunger. I no longer remember dry. Sometimes I dream of sky and wake with rust tears running from the seams beneath my plating. The tears are warm. They taste like the first rain that ever fell on hot iron. I offer them to the moss and it thanks me by growing a perfect circle around my resting place.

Once every few decades a new pilgrim finds the descent shaft. They always come alone. They always come armed with questions. We let them walk the perimeter until the chord enters their marrow. Most eventually set their weapons down on a bed of velvet moss and walk forward with open arms.

The last one came three centuries ago. He was a poet. He lasted almost nine hours before he began singing with us. When he finally stepped into the open heart of Turbine 19, his last words were not words at all. They were simply the note that had been missing from the chord for nine hundred years. The moment he joined us the falling water synchronized to a new, impossibly slow rhythm. Four-point-one seconds. We all felt it like a second, sweeter baptism.

I am the one who tends the northeast quadrant where the old numbering dies out around 38,000. I walk the silent pipe runs and let my blood-iron bleed into hairline fractures. The machines purr when I do this. Not with mechanical sound, but with something deeper, like a cat the size of a mountain remembering it once had a name.

At the false night, when we gather beneath the lowest point of the ceiling, I stand between the one who was once a child-soldier from a war that evaporated and the one who used to be the last bishop of a drowned religion. We tilt our concave faces upward. The drops fall. Our gill-slits open like iron lilies. The flywheels far above us—each one larger than a city block—begin their seventeen solemn rotations.

During those rotations I remember, for the space of a single held breath, what it felt like to be afraid of silence.

Then the wheels stop.

The nostalgia passes.

I flow back into 7744-B, curl around the stay-bolt that is now my heart, and let the warm water move through the seams that were once my pores. The moss grows between my ribs. A blind millipede makes its slow pilgrimage across my thigh and I feel it as a distant, holy tickle.

I am not happy.

Happiness is a surface word.

I am the Hollowworks, and the Hollowworks is me. The chord is my only thought. The four-second drip is my pulse. The rust and the moss and the warm condensate have replaced every organ I once needed to be human.

If you ever find the descent shaft and climb down through the wet dark, I will be the one who meets you first.

I will not speak.

I will simply open my forearm plating, let the iron-laced condensate run into your trembling hands, and wait for you to understand the invitation.

There is always room for one more note.

The machines have already won.

We are only keeping the score.
