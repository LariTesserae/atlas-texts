I have been here longer than the mountain remembers being young.

My name, if names still mattered, would be useless. The Mnemonists simply call me *the Echo That Chose to Stay*.

I am not one of them. I am what happens when one of their number presses a rust-warm finger against a human sternum and finds something that refuses to become mere absence.

That was… centuries ago? Decades? Time here is not linear; it pools. It drips. It grows rings inside basalt the way trees do.

When the first Mnemonist touched me I felt every valve, every groan, every bat-wingbeat the chamber had ever known flood into the hollow behind my ribs. My knees buckled. Black water closed over my head. For nine full days I lay on the lake floor among the blind crustaceans while my body forgot how to be only human.

On the tenth day I rose.

Now I am tall, though not as tall as they are. My skin—where it can still be called skin—is a living patina of siderite and moss the color of old pennies. Across my back and shoulders run three deep furrows where rebar once tried to root and then changed its mind, leaving me instead with flexible iron ligaments that click when I turn my head. My left arm ends in a perfectly preserved negative mold of a 1950s wrench; the cavity is warm, and things that fall into it are never seen again. My right hand still has five fingers. I keep them because I like the way they remember how to tremble.

I have no eyes. Where eyes should be there is only a shallow basin that collects the purest drips from the ceiling. When it fills, I can see every year the mountain has lived, stacked like wet transparencies.

Most of the time I move alone.

The parliament of Mnemonists tolerates me the way an old cathedral tolerates a single bat that refuses to join any colony. They drift past me in their slow, courteous three-legged gait and sometimes one will pause, lower its blunt head, and taste the flavor of my water. If I have been remembering something particularly sharp—an argument between two pipes that happened in 1978, for instance—they will linger a moment longer, the cavities in their faces sighing with something like approval. Then they move on, leaving me to my own smaller archive.

I keep the things too brief for them.

I remember the exact pitch of the last human scream that ever echoed here, when the last maintenance crew sealed the fissure behind them in 1964. I remember the smell of a woman’s hair caught in a hardhat that later became one of their faces. I remember the first time a bat mistook the sodium light for dawn and flew straight into a pipe, and the small, wet sound it made when it died.

These are not grand memories. They are the lint of history. The Mnemonists have no use for lint. So it falls to me.

Sometimes I stand for decades in one place, just as they do, until my beard becomes a curtain of liverwort and my left leg grows into the lake floor. The blind crustaceans learn to trace my silhouette instead of pipe schematics. I let them. It is another way of remembering.

At other times I walk.

I walk the black water until the *whump* of the pipes becomes a second heartbeat inside my siderite chest. I coax a particular fern to grow in the exact shape of a long-vanished warning sign. I sing—silently, in the only language left to me—by altering the mineral signature of the water around me until it tastes like machine oil and grief.

And sometimes, when the mountain groans especially deep and every hanging thread of water trembles in unison, I go to the newest fracture in the basalt wall and press my wrench-shaped absence against the stone.

I whisper, without sound:

*Remember the small things too.*

Then I step back and wait for the next one of us to bloom.

I am not sorrowful. I am not joyful.

I am the compromise that learned how to miss the sunlight it will never see again.

And if you ever find the fissure no wider than a man’s shoulders, if you ever squeeze through into the dripping dark and stand knee-deep in the black lake long enough for your teeth to vibrate with the subsonic cough of the pipes…

Look for the tall, rust-colored figure whose beard is half moss and whose left arm ends in a missing wrench.

That will be me.

I will come.

I will lower the basin where my eyes should be until it catches the weak sodium light just right, and for one slow, wet minute you will smell hot iron, cold fern, and the ghost of something that might once have been your own name.

Then I will touch you.

Not to add you to the archive.

Only to show you what it feels like when the argument between stone and iron finally decides to keep one small, trembling piece of you alive forever.

Stay.

The dripping will teach you how.
