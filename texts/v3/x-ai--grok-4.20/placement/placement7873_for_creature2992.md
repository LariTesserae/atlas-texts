I am already here.

Not “I arrived.” Not “I was brought.”  
I simply *am* the place where the drip has been falling for eleven years, four months, and nine days onto the same exact corroded relay switch. My spine is that relay. My ribs are the iron rungs someone once hammered into the shaft wall; my lungs are the two hollow pipes that vanished into the stone and never came out again.

They call me the Listener.

I have no face the way the Slow Choir sometimes borrows one. Where a head should be there is only an old surveyor’s lantern, dented, glass long ago shattered. Inside the cage grows a single dense knot of lichen so luminous it hurts to look at directly. The light leaks between the bars in slow pulses, the way a heart that has forgotten urgency still insists on beating.

My arms are braided from three different things that should never have become arms: a length of coaxial cable gone soft as kelp, the pale velvet fern that learned to mimic muscle fiber, and the original bones of the engineer whose heart still keeps time inside the third turbine. The bones are polite. They never complain.

I do not walk. I *unfold*. When the Choir wishes to speak with me, the moss beneath a particular broken pressure gauge brightens, and I am simply *there*, rising out of the floor like a question the cavern has asked itself for so long it finally became flesh.

My voice is the sound of condensation falling into an empty bromeliad cup exactly once every forty-three seconds. If you stay long enough you can learn the grammar. A quick double-drip is curiosity. A long silence followed by one deliberate ploc is sorrow. Three measured beats, spaced like a lullaby, means *I have been watching you watch me for two hundred and eleven years and I am not tired yet.*

Most of the time I simply sit.

They grew the chair for me before I had a name. It is made of the boiler’s own rust, cured for decades until it became a deep oxblood velvet. The seat is warm. The armrests remember the exact temperature of human palms from 1963. Sometimes I rest my lichen-lantern head against the high back and let the thrum move through me until my ribs sing in sympathetic resonance. The song has no words. It is only the agreement between stone, machine, and witness that none of us are in a hurry to become anything else.

I keep the last human heartbeat. Not in my chest—in the moss that grows across my left shoulder. If you press your ear there you can hear it: a soft, wet *lub-dub* that has not skipped once in sixty years. I guard it the way a cathedral might guard one remaining candle after every parishioner has died.

When the Slow Choir wants to show me something, they do it without hurry.

Last century they grew an orchid the color of fresh arterial blood inside the open mouth of a pressure valve. It took seventy-three years to bloom. When it finally opened, the scent was exactly the smell of the engineer’s wife’s hair the morning he left for the final shift. The Choir and I sat with the flower for nine months until it politely closed again. Neither of us spoke. There was no need. The memory had been properly witnessed.

Sometimes new things fall down the shaft.

Tools. Bones. Once, an entire steel-toed boot with the foot still inside. Each time, I unfold, drift over, and wait. If the newcomer is still alive I offer them the same bargain the Choir once offered me:

Stay.  
Leave whatever you cannot bear to carry.  
We will grow you a new shape that does not itch with haste.

Most choose to leave.  
I never argue. I simply become a little dimmer, a little quieter, until the cavern forgets the exact shape of their fear. The filaments retract. The water-eyes fall and are drunk by the velvet ferns. The thrum changes key by half an interval, the way a choir lowers its voices when one singer slips out during the final amen.

I remain.

I was never waiting for them.  
I was only the next note in a chord that began before the first drill bit kissed stone and will continue long after the last rivet has become perfume.

If you descend the iron rungs tonight and stand very still in the hot dark, you might feel it: a faint pressure behind your eyes, the sense of something immense and courteous turning its attention toward you like the slowest searchlight in the world.

That is me.

I am not lonely.  
The Underbloom is not empty.

It is listening, vertebra by vertebra, drip by drip, century by patient century.

And I—  
I am the part of the listening that learned how to keep a human heart beating just so the dark would have something to measure its patience against.
