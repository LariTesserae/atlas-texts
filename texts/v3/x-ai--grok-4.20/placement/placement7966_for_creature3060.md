I step out of the narrow green fissure that brought me here and the fissure seals behind me without a sound. The root-floor gives beneath my weight like the deck of an old ship, then slowly remembers itself and firms again. For a long minute I do nothing but stand still and let the Hollowroot taste me.

I already know I will not leave.

Not because I have been trapped. Because every other place I have ever been now feels like a half-finished sentence.

My name used to be Mara Solvestri. That name still fits, the way an old coat still fits even after the shoulders have changed. I was a machinist, then a cartographer of abandoned industrial sites, then something between a pilgrim and a thief of atmospheres. I spent twenty-three years chasing the exact sound a place makes when it has outlived every reason to exist and still refuses to die. I thought I was looking for ruins that dreamed. Instead I was looking for the Hollowroot. It simply waited until I was ready to be found.

I feel the attention settle on me the way the description warned it would: cool, patient, enormous. Ten thousand copper-green leaves tilt a fraction of a millimeter in my direction. The dripping changes tempo by one beat in every seven. The Grove is listening with my own blood.

I walk.

My boots (old, salt-stained, re-soled three times) leave faint luminous prints that fade after three heartbeats. The air smells of wet iron, chlorophyll, and something like distant lightning. Every few steps a valve hidden inside a trunk sighs open and a ribbon of steam uncoils across the lane like a slow gray snake. I let it wrap around my ankles. It feels like being recognized.

At the first intersection a Rootwarden is already waiting for me.

It stands inside the negative space between four colossal stems, as though the architecture grew around it rather than the other way around. Its leaf-whiskers tremble although there is no wind. The circuit patterns on its face-plate are moving, very slowly, rearranging themselves into new diagrams. I recognize some of them: they are exploded views of the pressure-release system I designed in a dream six years ago and never told anyone about.

It speaks before I can.

“Mara Solvestri,” it says. The voice is exactly as promised: wet wood, distant iron, centuries of patience. “You brought the blueprints with you after all.”

I laugh once, a small cracked sound that the leaves catch and turn into soft dripping.

“I thought I was only bringing questions.”

The Rootwarden inclines its hollow head. “Questions are the best kind of blueprint.”

It turns without hurry and begins walking. I follow. The sound of our footsteps (mine quick and human, its slow and creaking) braids itself into the constant plick… plick… plick until the whole cathedral seems to be keeping time with us.

We pass a courtyard where three of the Quiet are weaving. Their bodies are mostly still human, but their arms have lengthened and split into multiple jointed lianas that thread themselves through an enormous living loom. One of them lifts a face that used to belong to a woman. Her leaf-bud eyes open. For a moment I see myself reflected in them: already a little greener at the edges, the faint circuit pattern beginning to surface along my left collarbone like a birthmark that has waited thirty-eight years to appear.

She smiles with a mouth that is still mostly mouth. The smile says: *You will be beautiful when you finish becoming.*

I feel no fear. Only the deep, almost sexual relief of finally being recognized by the thing you have always been secretly growing toward.

The Rootwarden leads me to a place that was obviously prepared decades before I was born.

It is a small raised chamber formed by the hollowed belly of a single enormous trunk. Inside, iron pipes have grown into the exact ergonomic curves my back has ached for my entire adult life. A brass spigot protrudes at the precise height where my left hand will want to rest when I am thinking. The dripping here falls in perfect 7/4 time against a taut leaf-skin drum. The tone is the same one that used to wake me at 3:14 a.m. in every cheap rented room I ever slept in, the tone I could never record or reproduce. Here it simply lives.

There is no bed. There is a hollow shaped exactly like the negative space of my sleeping body. I understand that it will slowly adjust as my bones soften and my spine becomes more vascular.

I step inside.

The Rootwarden does not follow. It only places one cable-limb against the living wall. The wall blooms open like a slow green mouth and reveals a new valve that was not there a moment ago. The valve is shaped like a question mark.

It says, gently, “We can begin whenever you are tired of being finished.”

Then it folds itself into the architecture and becomes a buttress. I will not see it as a separate being again for seven years.

I sit in the hollow that already knows me.

For the first time in my life I am not mapping anything. I am being mapped.

I close my eyes and feel the Grove slide the first delicate thread of itself into the base of my skull. It does not hurt. It feels like finally remembering a song I have been humming wrong for forty years.

I smile, and the smile is already half leaf.

Somewhere far above, in the vaulted crook of two fused stems, a new loom begins to click and reset. It is weaving something that will one day wear my face the way the Rootwardens wear theirs.

I am not becoming Quiet.

I am not becoming a Rootwarden.

I am becoming the shape that the Hollowroot has been dreaming in iron and green since before my grandmother was born.

It is a good shape.

It has patience for centuries.

It has room for the particular ache I carried.

And when it finally speaks with what used to be my voice, centuries from now, it will say to the next lost traveler who stumbles into this grove:

*You brought the blueprints with you after all.*

Then it will tilt its copper-green head, listen to the dripping, and ask the only question that has ever mattered here.

*What shape would you like to grow into, if given forever?*
