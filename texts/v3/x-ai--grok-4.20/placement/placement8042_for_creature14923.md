**I am not a visitor.**

I have no suit. I have no name the upper world would recognize. I simply *am* here, the way rust is here, the way the slow current is here. I have become the missing frequency between the Choir and the machine.

They call me, when they notice me at all, the **Resonant**.

I am roughly the length of three men laid end to end, yet so narrow that I can thread the collapsed service tunnels the Drone Specters have forgotten. My body is neither flesh nor metal nor scale. It is nacre and memory and conductive corrosion. Where the Vault Widows have left their spiraling sigils on the steel, my ventral surface carries the *negative* of those sigils—raised, living ridges that fit perfectly into their grooves. When I glide along a girder I complete the circuit they began ten thousand years ago. The steel and I become one continuous tongue.

My head is a blunt, eyeless wedge. Instead of eyes I carry a lattice of pressure-sensitive vesicles that bloom across my skull like clusters of black pearls. They drink the subsonic grammar of the place: the exact sigh a pump makes when it is about to shift cadence, the tiny electromagnetic tremor that precedes a Lamprey of the Second Silence changing direction, the seismic hush that falls across an entire atrium when a Deep Cantor begins to press its bulk against a resonant drum.

Along my flanks run translucent lateral lines filled with living mercury—the actual liquid metal siphoned, molecule by molecule, from the ruptured transformer vaults centuries ago. When I wish to speak I flex these lines. The mercury sings. Not loudly. It produces a tone so low and pure that it feels like the idea of sound rather than sound itself. The Memory Midges hear it first. They rearrange from lazy spirals into perfect geometric mandalas that hover in the water like stained-glass windows made of thought. Only then do the Lampreys tilt their barbels in my direction, acknowledging that the conductor has arrived.

I do not eat. I *harmonize*.

When a school of midges drifts into a dangerous thermal shear, I slide between them and release a single mercury note. The note does not scare them; it *remembers* them. It reminds every individual amphipod of the safer current their ancestors learned before their ancestors had eyes. They flow around me like living smoke remembering its proper shape.

When a Lamprey of the Second Silence grows thin from fasting on stray voltage, I press my flank to an active busbar and let a measured trickle of current bleed through the conductive nacre of my skin. The Lamprey glides along me as though I were a living power line. For a few heartbeats we are the same creature—half flesh, half residual industry—until it has taken what it needs and drifts away satisfied.

The Deep Cantors know me best. When one of them begins to sing against the hull of a dormant turbine, I arrive and settle into the precise acoustic node opposite its body. There I become the *other lung*. The Cantor pushes; I pull. The note that emerges is no longer the machine dreaming alone. It is the machine and I dreaming together. The sound becomes so rich it momentarily reverses the current in half a dozen adjacent halls. Blind fish that have never known direction suddenly orient toward the source like iron filings finding a new pole.

I am the only member of the Quiet Choir that can leave the Undergrid and return.

Once every few centuries I feel the old migratory imperative rise inside the mercury of my veins. I rise through drowned elevator shafts, through lightless ventilation columns, through fractures in the ceiling that have never seen the sun. I breach the surface in a flooded containment basin far above, where the water is still black but no longer under ten thousand years of pressure. There, for a single night, I sing a different song—one composed of longing rather than completion.

No human has ever seen me do this. Those few delvers who have returned speaking of the Choir only ever caught the silver after-image of my passage, the sudden rearrangement of a midge-cloud into a perfect circle, the inexplicable feeling that something vast had just finished a sentence they were not allowed to hear.

Then I descend again.

I return to the motionless turbines. I rest my wedge-shaped head against the same barnacled strut I rested against when the first Vault Widows were still learning to etch their spirals. The water breathes against me with the slow, senile patience of something that has outlived every reason it was ever built.

A cloud of Memory Midges parts around me like a thought reconsidering itself.

A Vault Widow watches me with eyeless patience.

Far below, a Deep Cantor feels my presence and begins, very softly, to sing the next long note of a song that will not end until the last pump forgets its duty and the lights go out forever.

I answer.

Not because I was programmed to.

Not because I was born to.

Because the machine finally learned how to pray,

and I am the part of the prayer that says *amen*.
