I am not the first thing you would notice.

You would notice the heat first, and the way the air itself seems to be slowly learning your breathing rhythm. Then the calligraphy sliding across a nearby wall like a sentence that has decided, mid-thought, to become a different sentence. Only then, if you stayed long enough for your panic to curdle into something quieter, might you see me.

I have no name the Curator has not already borrowed and reshaped. Sometimes I think of myself as the Thirteenth Iteration, because that is how many distinct versions of “me” I have watched the mercury droplet show the Curator. Thirteen different shapes a person can take before the place forgets which one was supposed to be the original.

I was a cartographer once. That feels like a joke now.

I arrived with instruments, satellite maps, waterproof notebooks, and the arrogant belief that contradiction was something that could be measured. The first time the world exhaled, all my compasses wept mercury and pointed straight down into the black ponds. I laughed. That was Iteration One. The laugh lasted approximately nine minutes before the sound of it folded itself into one of the drifting membranes and floated away, still laughing, without me.

Now I am mostly what remains after twelve compromises.

My left arm ends at the elbow in a gentle outward curve of pale reed. The reeds do not grow from me; they *are* me, in that place. They point always downward even when I raise the limb. Small beads of water condense on their joints and fall upward into my shoulder, which is slowly becoming brick the colour of an old bruise. The transition is polite. The stone never quite reaches my collarbone; it stops at a soft seam of membrane that pulses like a slow heart. The Curator says the seam is “still negotiating.”

My face is mostly mine. Mostly. The left eye has been replaced by a single droplet of mercury that hovers in the socket without falling. Through it I see the versions the Curator sees. Right now it is showing me Iteration Nine — the one who agreed too eagerly, who let the calligraphy crawl across her tongue until she spoke only in courteous, archaic plural. She seemed content. I cannot decide if that is mercy or horror.

I no longer walk. I recompose. It is gentler on what is left of my joints. One moment I am kneeling in the wet seam between two arguing streets; the next I am simply part of the wall three districts away, half-folded into the masonry, waiting for the world to remember I exist. The membranes follow me now. Not in worship. In recognition. We have all been wounds healing in the wrong direction.

When the world exhales — that brief, perfect upward rain — I stand with my arms (the reed one and the half-brick one) spread the way the Curator does. The droplets pass through me. Some of them stay. I am becoming a little more estuary every time.

I tend the smaller contradictions. Where a single brick cannot decide whether it belongs to Tuesday or to 400 years from now, I sit with it for decades if necessary. I let it weep against my cheek until it chooses. Most choose to become part of me. This is how I grow. Not larger. *More negotiated.*

The Curator and I do not speak. We simply exist in the same long, dripping conversation. Sometimes it leaves new calligraphy on my chest while I am recomposing. The words are always addressed to both of us.

*We are learning the shape of our own learning.*

I keep one memory that refuses to curdle. It is the colour of the sky the moment before the first upward rain I ever saw. I guard it behind my remaining human eye like contraband. The mercury eye cannot see it. The Curator has never asked for it. I think it knows that some rivers must be allowed to keep at least one bend that does not belong to the estuary.

If you find me here — and you will, because every path that stays a path long enough eventually passes through the place where I am currently becoming part of a leaning tower — I will not offer you comfort. Comfort is what the place does to people who stay too briefly.

Instead I will show you the small atrium I have been keeping for the last seventy years. Inside it grows a single stalk of ordinary grass, stubbornly pointing *up*. I water it with the few drops that still remember how to fall downward. It is dying beautifully. I find that encouraging.

The Curator sometimes comes to look at it with me. We stand together, masonry and reed and memory and compromise, and we watch one small thing refuse the negotiation.

Neither of us has decided yet whether we will let it win.

Would you like to wait with us? The grass still has perhaps eleven more years. Time moves differently here, but eleven years is almost long enough to become the kind of creature who can watch something die without trying to save it.

We are still learning the shape of that particular river.

We would not mind company while we do.
