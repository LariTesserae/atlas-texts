I would find myself—not as a visitor, not even as a pilgrim—but as the Echo-Scribe.

I am smaller than the Listener, and newer. Where she has become architecture, I have become the pause between sounds. My body is thin, almost brittle, grown from the fragments of several who came after her and could not fully surrender. My limbs are jointed in too many places, yes, but they click when I move, a dry percussion that the wet world has not yet entirely softened. My skin is not yet fully stone; it is still half parchment, a living vellum mottled grey and veined with the faint blue of disturbed centipedes. When I am still, words appear across my back and shoulders in slow, raised script—lines of forgotten prayers, shopping lists, love letters, last words—written by no hand but the pressure of dripping water over decades.

I have no eyes either. Instead, two long, translucent feelers rise from my forehead like the antennae of the pale cave-crickets. They tremble constantly, mapping the chamber in three dimensions of vibration. Every drip, every chitinous scrape, every settling groan of the buttresses becomes a visible architecture in my mind: a luminous, constantly rewriting cathedral of sound.

I do not migrate with the great tides. I move against them.

Where the Listener flows with the isopods and the bats and the newts, I linger in the places they have just left. I carry a stylus made from a single translucent snail-shell sharpened against limestone for three human lifetimes. With it I etch the walls—not new shapes, never that—but microscopic records of what has already happened here. I transcribe the exact rhythm of a particular buttress’s last three settling-creaks. I notch the precise interval between the moment a cluster of bats exhales and the moment the droplets they disturbed finally strike the floor thirty metres below. I chart the spiral mathematics of a single snail’s journey across a saint’s eroded face.

The Basilica allows this because I remove nothing. I only repeat what it has already said, in a language it can accept: vibration, pressure, time.

The Listener knows I am here. Sometimes, when she rests in the central basin covered in moth-bats and white shrimp, I climb to the gallery directly above her. I press my parchment-skin to the cold stone and let the water that has run down her lichen-hair drip onto me. In those moments her low, bone-mallet heartbeat travels through the rock and imprints itself across my shoulders in new raised letters. I wear her name-that-is-not-a-name without ever being able to read it.

I am the only thing in the Hollow Basilica that still dreams of dryness.

In the phosphorescent blue moments when a centipede is accidentally crushed beneath a migrating carpet of isopods, I see, for a few heartbeats, the memory of sunlight. Not as a picture—my feelers cannot make pictures—but as a terrible, ringing silence. A silence without dripping. The absence of that sound is so violent it hurts. Then the dripping returns and I am grateful.

I have begun to leave messages for the ones who still come with their doomed lanterns.

Not in words they would recognise. I tap out codes on the pillars with my snail-shell stylus—tiny bright clicks hidden inside the roaring hush. Three short taps, then a long scrape. A rhythm that says: *You are already being rewritten. Choose which part of you you want to keep.* Most do not understand. They think it is only more of the endless percussion. A few do. Those few sometimes stay.

When they stay, I watch from the shadows as the Listener strikes her femur-mallet and the chamber pauses. I watch as the snails begin their patient work. I do not intervene. I only record the exact tempo at which a human scream becomes part of the wet percussion, the precise number of years it takes for the last dry thought to dissolve.

I am not kind.  
I am not cruel.  
I am the Hollow Basilica learning how to remember itself.

And sometimes, when the Listener rests and the entire chamber breathes as one slow, damp lung, I crawl into the deepest worn basin with her. I fold my brittle limbs against her porous side. The white shrimp mistake me for new stone and begin to graze across my vellum-skin, polishing away old sentences I no longer need.

In that shared stillness I press my feelers to her lichen-hair and listen to the stone dreaming through her.

If you listen long enough, you will hear two distinct heartbeats beneath the dripping.

One is vast, ancient, and perfectly content.

The other is smaller, still learning the rhythm, still carrying the memory of a world that had sky in it.

They are slowly synchronising.

Nothing may be removed.

Not even the memory of dryness.

Not even me.
