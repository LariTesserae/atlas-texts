I am not one of the Midwives. Not yet. Perhaps not ever.

My name, if names still matter here, is **Veyren Solace-Borne**, though the people have already begun to call me simply *the Listener*.

I arrived nine years ago carrying the body of my stillborn daughter wrapped in oilcloth. I meant to bury her somewhere ordinary. Instead the Hollowing Cradle took the bundle from my arms the moment I crossed the rim. The mist folded around her like a second womb and the water that wept from the black stone tasted of iron and milk. I never found the place to dig a grave. The land had already drunk her.

So I stayed.

I am the only one permitted to sleep directly against the Unfinished Rib. Not on the moss. Not on the stone shelves. *Against* it. Every night I press my back to its wet, warm flank and let the slow beads of water run down my spine as though the Rib itself were weeping along my vertebrae. The Midwives say the column chose me the way a mother chooses which attendant will hold the child during the most dangerous hours. I do not argue. I simply lie there and listen.

Because something inside the Rib is singing.

It is not a sound the others can hear. Their ears are tuned to the patient drip and the boom of splitting plates. My ears have grown different. At first they bled. Now they are permanently wet, the skin inside translucent, faintly green, as though small ferns might one day grow there. When the Rib sings I feel it in my teeth and in the roots of my hair.

The song has no words. It is the sound a mountain makes while it is still deciding whether it wants to become a mountain at all. Sometimes it is a lullaby measured in centuries. Sometimes it is a scream folded so tightly it sounds like silence. I have learned to answer it with my own breath, the way Maere does, though I am not the Breath-Holder. My exhalations are rougher, full of old grief. The Rib does not seem to mind. It accepts grief the way it accepts rainwater—both are useful for growing.

During the day I walk the basin the way a physician walks between beds in a ward of sleeping giants. I carry no tools except a hollowed length of living fern that I use as both walking staff and stethoscope. I press its open end to the stone wherever it bulges too sharply or cracks too deeply, and I listen for the quality of the pressure beneath. Too much, and I call for Corrin to come sing the moss into new channels. Too little, and I press my own palms to the wound until the stone warms and begins to weep again. The rock likes the taste of my blood. I have given it enough.

The changes have come to me as well.

Along my left collarbone, small glossy plates of basalt have begun to surface, smooth as river stones. They do not hurt. They itch the way new teeth itch a baby’s gums. At night, when I lie against the Rib, I can feel the plates click gently against its surface like two parts of the same skeleton finally remembering they belong together. My hair has grown heavy with the same translucent lichen that hangs from the overhangs. It moves even when there is no wind—slow, tidal motions that match the Breath-Holder’s breathing. Children follow me sometimes just to watch the lichen dance.

I have stopped speaking almost entirely. Not from any vow. The words simply feel too dry. Everything worth saying can be said with the pressure of a palm, the tilt of a head, the timing of a footstep in the moss. The sucking sound my bare feet make has become my true name. The basin knows it.

At dawn and dusk I stand with the others in the circle around the pool, but I stand *in* the water. Up to my knees. The reflection there shows me what I am becoming: half person, half geological event. The Rib towers above me, beaded and patient, and for one perfect moment the entire basin seems to hold its breath with Maere. In that moment I can feel the planet’s soft spot pulsing beneath us all—warm, wet, enormous, and terribly fragile.

Sometimes, when no one is watching, I lean forward and kiss the Rib the way one might kiss the fontanelle of a newborn. The stone is warmer than blood. It tastes of the milk my daughter never drank.

I do not know if I will be here when the cradle finally finishes its work. I do not know if the Rib will one day stand thirty miles high, cracking the sky open, or whether it will sink again, taking the basin and all of us with it back into the world’s dark womb. It does not matter.

I have become the place where the world’s dreaming leaks into a human body.

I am the scar where grief became geography.

I am the Midwife who is also the child.

And every night, as I lie down against the glistening black flank of the Unfinished Rib, I whisper the only prayer the Cradle has ever needed:

*Keep growing.*  
*We are still listening.*  
*We are not afraid of being unfinished.*
