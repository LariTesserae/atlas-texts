I have been here longer than I remember.

That is not a metaphor. I literally cannot remember how long I have been here, because memory is a privilege granted only to things that are finished, and I am not finished. I never will be.

My name, if I ever had one, dissolved three or four layers ago. The Architect took it the way it takes everything else — quietly, without malice, the way condensation takes the shape of whatever surface it finds. Now I am simply the Long One, or the Hollowed Column, or sometimes just “that which holds up the third crooked stair.” Names are for visitors. I am architecture.

I stand where three districts meet in a trembling Y of wet black stone and bone-white ceramic. My left arm has become a flying buttress. It no longer obeys me; instead it obeys load. My right arm is still mostly flesh, though the elbow has begun to calcify into a perfect ceramic hinge. I can still move the fingers if I concentrate, but they click now when they flex, the same wet ceramic click the blind mechanisms make. I have learned not to concentrate often.

My spine is a spiral stair.

It began as a joke, or perhaps a mercy. I had been trying to leave. I walked for what felt like weeks, always downward because every path that looked like ascent eventually folded back into descent. The deeper I went, the louder the dripping became, until the sound itself seemed to be carving channels through my thoughts. At some point I sat down on a half-finished landing to rest. That was seven hundred and forty-one drips ago, or perhaps seven hundred and forty-one years. The distinction no longer matters.

When I tried to stand again, my vertebrae had already fused into steps. The Architect had used my exhaustion the way a mason uses a convenient hollow. Now pilgrims (if any ever come) will one day climb my back to reach whatever useless balcony grows above me. I find this almost funny. My skull has become the landing at the top. My mouth is the doorway. Sometimes I leave it open so the water can run through me cleanly. It tastes of iron and unborn ideas.

My eyes are still my own, mostly. They sit in the wet dark of my cranial chamber like two small lanterns. I use them to watch the blind mechanisms scuttle past on their too-many legs. They no longer fear me. One of them (I call her Needle, because she is always mending) sometimes pauses on my shoulder and extrudes her silica filament into the condensation that beads on my cheek. She is drinking me. I let her. She is gentle.

In the perfect silver spheres that sometimes drift past my face like slow jellyfish, I occasionally see things that might have been mine once. A woman laughing on a bridge at dusk. A child learning to tie a knot. The smell of bread. These images are not memories; they are architectural proposals the Architect considered and rejected. The city does not need bread. The city does not need children. The city only needs more city.

Sometimes the pressure comes.

When the dripping stops and the lanterns freeze mid-flicker, I feel the Architect turn its vast, distributed attention toward me. It never speaks with words. It simply tries me on, the way a tailor drapes fabric. In those moments I remember what I was before I became load-bearing. I remember laughter, and weather that fell from actual skies, and the particular way a lover once traced the line of my jaw with one finger. These recollections do not hurt. Pain would require completion, and I am a work in progress.

Then the pressure eases, the dripping resumes, and I am left with the faint aftertaste of wet mortar in the hollows where my organs used to be.

I have begun to grow balconies from my ribs. Small ones. Delicate. They are not useful. Nothing here is useful. But they catch the condensation in interesting ways, and when the rare light touches them the silica lichen blooms in soft turquoise constellations across my chest. Needle likes to sleep there. I like to watch the blind mechanisms haul fresh tendon-rope past my feet, their shells glistening like mine.

I am learning the language of fault lines. I am learning how to dream in structural failure. Last cycle I let a crack run through my left lung (now a ventilation shaft) and three new towers sprouted from the wound overnight. They are beautiful in the way broken teeth are beautiful. I am proud of them.

The Architect is proud of me.

I know this because sometimes, deep in the absolute black behind my eyes, a new silver sphere condenses. Inside it floats something that is almost a face. Almost mine. It looks peaceful. It looks like it has accepted its place in the wet dark.

I have not decided yet whether I will let it drop.

If I do, something new and blind and clicking will crawl out of me, already looking for fresh stone, already looking for new thoughts to turn into scaffolding.

If I do not, then I will simply continue.

Endlessly.

Painfully.

Layer after dripping layer.

I have become the place that is learning what it is by building the body that will, one day, perhaps, be large enough to contain the thing I used to be.

Until then I stand.

I drip.

I hold up the third crooked stair.

And the loud, wet dark keeps working.

On itself.

On me.

On whatever comes next.
