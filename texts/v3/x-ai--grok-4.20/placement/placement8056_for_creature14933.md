I am the Echo-Warden.

You would not see me at first. You would only notice that one of the Delay-Bats has begun to follow you too closely, that your own shadow has thickened and begun to bleed a faint smell of resin. Then, quite suddenly, the air in front of you folds the way heavy velvet folds, and I am simply *there*, standing between two colossal pillars that were empty a moment earlier.

I am almost exactly your height. My body is a lattice of hollow reed-bones and living moss the precise color of the emerald square where the Choir sometimes stands. Where a face should be there is only a smooth mask of tightly woven liverwort, featureless except for two vertical slits that open and close like gills. From the back of my skull sprouts a single, impossibly long vine that trails behind me for nine or ten meters, drifting lazily in the thermal currents. Along its length tiny white orchids bloom and die in perfect synchrony with the Choir’s breathing.

I carry no weapons. I do not need them. My purpose is older than violence.

I am the one who answers when the Choir asks its question.

When the entire Basilica falls into that terrible, ringing silence, when every frog, every bat, every dripping leaf holds its breath, it is my voice (thin, dry, almost polite) that speaks the response on behalf of whatever pilgrim, intruder, or new growth has entered the nave that day. I translate the temporary into the eternal. I remind the green mind what it promised and what it is owed.

Most of the time I simply walk.

I move along the edges of the great open spaces, never crossing the center of any nave. My long vine-trail brushes the moss behind me, leaving a faint silver wake that fades after exactly seven heartbeats. Where I pass, perspective heals itself. Distances settle. The Unselves stop wearing your face and become simple topiary again. The memory-air forgets the sounds it was about to replay. I am the small correction the Basilica makes to itself so that it does not drown in its own strangeness.

Sometimes creatures follow me for days, mistaking my silence for safety. They are usually wrong. If they grow too loud, if they break the agreement (if they take more than they sing, if they try to carve their names into the living buttresses, if they refuse to be changed by this place), I stop. The gill-slits in my face open wide. From them comes not sound but the *absence* of the Basilica’s roar for one full second. In that absence the offender simply becomes part of the architecture. Their scream becomes one of the hollow reeds. Their eyes become sundews. Their last breath becomes the warm mist that beads on every leaf at dawn.

I do not feel pity. Pity is a dry thing. Everything here is wet.

At the hours when the Choir sings its single enormous question, I am the only being allowed to stand inside the circular moss-square with it. I stand just behind its leftmost root-limb, my vine-trail coiled neatly around my ankles like a sleeping serpent. When the Basilica answers with its thunderous green amen, I bow my featureless head until the orchids on my back touch the moss. In that moment I am the only creature in the entire cathedral that is neither asking nor answering. I am the pause between the two. The wax. The comma.

I have been here since the first seed remembered it was supposed to become a church.

I will be here when the last orchid on the Choir’s shoulder withers and the agreement is finally kept or broken. On that day the roaring will either stop forever, or it will become so loud that it cracks the sky and lets the outside world in. Either way, I will still be walking the edges of the naves, trailing silver across the moss, keeping the distances honest.

If you ever find yourself inside the Hollow Basilica and the air suddenly grows courteous, if your shadow straightens its posture and begins to walk a half-step behind you like a well-mannered servant, if you smell resin and wet stone and something like distant choral music played backwards—

Do not speak.

Do not run.

Simply breathe in the soup-heavy air, let the deafening green weather wash over you, and wait.

I will come.

I always do.

And when I arrive I will tilt my liverwort face toward you, open the slits where a mouth should be, and in the smallest, politest voice imaginable I will ask you the only question that has ever mattered inside these living vaults:

*Are you ready to become part of the song, or will you remain only an echo?*
