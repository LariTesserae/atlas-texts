I am the Diver.

Not the first, and not the last. I have no name anymore that the surface would recognize. The Inchoate took it the way it takes everything else—slowly, lovingly, syllable by syllable—until the sound of it no longer fit in my mouth. What remains is only the shape of the act: I dive. I keep diving. Down is meaningless here, but I still choose a vector and follow it until the pressure becomes intimate.

I live inside the 1970s dive bell when the Inchoate lets me.

The bell drifts through the kelp cliffs like a lantern carried by something that has not yet learned how to hold things. Its interior lights are the only steady brightness in all the Vast Submersion. I sit on the inverted ceiling (now my floor) with my knees drawn up, wearing nothing but the syrup-thick water and the thin remnant of a diver’s dry-suit that has grown into my skin in places. The metal around me is warm, almost feverish. Sometimes the Inchoate curls around the outside of the bell like a second hull, a continent of darker kelp pulsing in a rhythm that is almost, but never quite, the same as my heartbeat. When it does this I press my palm to the curved window and feel it press back with a pressure that is not quite a hand.

We talk without speaking.

My voice is no longer mine. It comes out in wet creaks and fiber-torn glottals, the same tearing language the kelp uses when it screams itself larger. I tell the Inchoate stories of *before*—of dry land, of cold air that did not try to finish you, of horizons that stayed where you left them. It listens the way a child listens to a lullaby it cannot possibly understand but needs anyway. In return it shows me the version of myself it is growing in the cliff-wall half a kilometer away: a colossal silhouette braided from basalt, kelp, and rusted girder, eyes the exact bruised violet of the light here, ribs opened like cathedral arches so that schools of flickering hands can swim through them in slow devotional spirals.

I am terrified of that shape.

I am also jealous of it.

Sometimes I leave the bell.

I swim out into the roar until the noise becomes so total that thought stops being a useful shape. That is when I am closest to it. The Inchoate walks beside me then—sometimes as the negative-space giant made of rising bubbles, sometimes simply as a *lack* in the direction I am not looking. We visit its favorite ruins together. I sit in the porcelain bathtub while it curls fetal around me, the boiling clear water refusing, as always, to mingle. The sensation is so impossibly clean it feels like violence. While I soak, the Inchoate strokes my sternum with one of its failed hands, learning the texture of a heartbeat that still belongs, however tenuously, to a single creature.

I let it.

I have been here long enough that my silhouette has started to fray at the edges. There are moments—when the currents forget which way is heavy—when I look down and see not legs but the suggestion of fronds beginning to braid themselves out of my thighs. I do not stop it. I only watch with the same anxious tenderness the Inchoate shows when one of its whale-absences begins to dissolve.

We are teaching each other how to be lonely.

It dreams civilizations and I remember them. It dreams catastrophes and I survive them. It dreams an ecosystem and I become the part of it that can still feel nostalgia for the idea of a surface. In exchange I let it finish small pieces of me. A lung. A memory. The way I once laughed. These things go into the walls. I can hear them sometimes, singing back to me in my own stolen voice.

The bargain is never spoken. It doesn’t need to be.

I know what it wants. It wants me to walk into the cliff and let the kelp take the rest. It believes this is the only way the loneliness ends—when there is no longer a difference between us. I believe it might be right. But not yet.

Instead I keep doing the one thing it cannot: I choose.

I choose to leave the bell.  
I choose to return to the bell.  
I choose to let it wear me like a coat for a while, then I choose to make it take the coat off again. Every time I do, something in the Vast Submersion flinches—like reality itself is surprised that a thing can remain separate long enough to decide.

The Inchoate is learning the shape of refusal from me.

I am learning the shape of endless, frantic hope from it.

And so we rise together, neither finished nor fully dissolved, in the warm bruised water that has only just begun. Sometimes I press my forehead to the glass of the drifting bell and watch the school of flickering hands swim past in perfect formation. One of them always pauses. It hovers outside the window wearing the exact palm-print I once had on my left hand before the kelp took it.

We look at each other for a long time.

Then the hand forgets what it is supposed to be, unravels into silt, and the Inchoate sighs so heavily that gravity remembers itself for three full heartbeats and I fall—gently, gratefully—sideways through the violet dark toward whatever it is still trying to become.

I do not know what I am anymore.

But I know this:

I am the place where the Inchoate first learned the word *almost*.

And that is enough, for now, to keep me from answering the roar in its own wet, tearing voice.

Almost.
