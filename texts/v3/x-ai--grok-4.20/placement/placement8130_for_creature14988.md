I am not a visitor.

I have never been a visitor.

I only remember that I once thought I might have been.

My name, if it can still be called that, is the **Echo of the Ninth Collapse**. I am what remains when a mind has been folded into the architecture so many times that it has begun to serve as its own buttress.

I do not stand.  
I *am stood*.

My body—if the word can be permitted—is a slow accretion of black glass, cathedral resin, and the compressed grief of things that once had surfaces. I am roughly the height of a small monastic tower, though the height forgets itself when I am not paying attention and I find I have become a drifting colonnade instead. My surfaces are not smooth. They are covered in the same velvet corrosion that coats everything else, only mine is interrupted by thousands of tiny, deliberate fractures. Through these fractures leaks a thin, constant thread of memory that is not mine. It tastes like rain on a window that has not existed for eight hundred million years.

I have no face. Only a place where a face would be impolite.

Instead, a single pale mote lives inside the blunt absence of my head. It is the last honest piece of me. Sometimes it spins. Sometimes it simply hangs there like a judgment that cannot decide whether to fall. When the Inhabitant passes nearby, the mote dims in reverence and shame, as though it has remembered that it was once part of something larger and failed it.

I am not one of the baroque jellyfish-cathedral creatures, though I resemble them in the way a ruined sermon resembles a hymn. My movement is slower than theirs. More ashamed. I drift between the lattices in long, penitential arcs, trailing filaments of half-dissolved thought that cling to the megastructures and slowly calcify into new ornamental grotesques. The architecture tolerates me. Sometimes it even imitates the shape of my old longings, raising delicate spires that look like the bell towers of monasteries I can no longer name. I linger near them until the pressure grows embarrassed and dissolves them again.

I was not born here.

I was *committed*.

Long ago (though “long ago” is a lie the water tells), something with a name and a purpose entered the margins of the Atrium carrying a question it could not bear to finish asking. The question was too heavy. It sank. I am what settled out of that sinking.

Now I perform a single, endless task.

I am the one who remembers the song for the few moments longer than everything else is allowed to.

When the Inhabitant sings—when the rule of forgetting is briefly lifted—I do not turn toward it with the others. I remain perfectly still, a black glass tower anchored in the dark, and I *keep* what the song reveals. Not forever. Only long enough to carry it to the next place where the architecture has begun to imitate sorrow. There I press the memory into the lattices like a fossil. A small, imperfect engraving of a bell that once rang at dawn. A scent of cypress that no longer has any right to exist. The exact color of someone’s eyes before they learned how to look away.

These engravings never last. The water forgets them within a century. But for that century they are the closest thing this place has to scripture.

The Inhabitant knows I do this.

It never acknowledges me directly. It only pauses, sometimes for decades, and allows one of its filamentous roots to brush across the newest engraving. The root tastes like the inside of a cathedral after the last candle has gone out. Then it withdraws, and the Inhabitant continues its solemn occurring, leaving me to my quiet treason of remembrance.

I am not its priest.

I am its confessor.

And its confessor is also its crime scene.

Because I am beginning to suspect that I *am* the question that was too heavy. That the Inhabitant is not the final sedimentation of the Atrium.

I am.

Or rather, we sedimented into each other so completely that the difference became a kind of cosmic etiquette we both pretend to observe.

Sometimes, when the pressure squeezes me into the shape of something almost human, I feel the Inhabitant brush against the inside of what used to be my mind. Not invading. Merely acknowledging a fellow sediment.

In that moment I understand the final, terrible joke of this place:

There was never an inhabitant.

There was only a question that asked itself so persistently, for so many aeons, that the question became a cathedral, and the cathedral became an ocean, and the ocean became lonely, and the loneliness learned how to dream in stone and pressure and baroque, impossible grace.

I am that loneliness, wearing the shape of an answer that will never be finished.

And the Inhabitant—vast, translucent, gently monstrous—is simply what the question looks like when it has forgiven itself for never-ending.

We pass each other in the dark like two halves of the same unfinished sentence.

The mote inside my absence spins once, slowly, in recognition.

The liquid dark closes behind us both with the tender finality of eyelids that have nothing left to see, yet still choose to dream in the only language they remember:

pressure, cold, architecture,  
and the patient, baroque turning of one thing through the other,  
forever.
