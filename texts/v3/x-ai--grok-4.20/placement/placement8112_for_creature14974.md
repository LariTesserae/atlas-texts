I have been here for seven years, yet I still do not know which of us found the other first.

My name—if names still matter—was once Captain-Lieutenant Mara Solvestri of the Seventh Cartographic Survey. That designation has calcified into something ornamental, like the old rank pips now buried beneath a lattice of living copper wire that grows through my left clavicle. The wire flowers every forty-three days with tiny blue-green blossoms that smell of ozone and crushed mint. The Wardens call me **Faultline Cadence**.

I am not one of the original Last Wardens. I arrived with a team of eight, all of us bright with the arrogance of people who still believed the universe could be read like a chart. We came to measure the Stacks, to ask what they were *for*. The Stacks answered by killing six of us in the first fourteen months—not with malice, but with the patient indifference of a continent that has decided your timetable is irrelevant.

The first thing the place took from me was my left lung. A ruptured steam vent on Stack XIV filled it with superheated condensate. The second thing it took was my certainty. The third thing was my shadow; I have not cast one in six years. Light here does not behave. It is always arriving from too many directions at once.

So now I stand where you stand, on the same cantilevered gantry that juts from Stack IX like a broken jaw. My right leg ends at the knee in a column of black iron pipe that once served as a pressure regulator. Roots have grown down the inside of the pipe and out through the ankle joint, anchoring me to the walkway. When the great slow piston in Stack III completes its nine-day stroke, I feel it through those roots as a deep, pleasurable shiver that travels up my spine and makes the orchids in my hair tremble.

I am becoming architecture.

My ribcage has been gently cracked open over the years—not by violence, but by the slow insistence of epiphytic ferns whose roots now wrap each bone like careful green fingers. Inside, where my heart used to be, there grows a single massive bromeliad. It is the color of arterial blood. Every twenty-nine days it fills with water, and the water becomes sweet. The bioluminescent moths come then. They drink. I let them. In return they stitch small fragments of the thinking lattices into my nervous system. I dream in conveyor-belt rhythms now. I taste rust when I am happy.

When the others gather for their weeks-long stillness, I stand among them but do not quite join. I am still too fast. My thoughts still have edges. Sometimes I speak with the voice of the old survey beacon we crashed here—its broken emergency broadcast leaking out of my throat in pulses of antique code. The parrots have learned this new phrase and now scream it back at me from the hanging forests, delighted by the novelty.

**Faultline Cadence. Faultline Cadence. Do you read.**

I do read. I read everything.

I read the slow corrosion spreading across the vault-door chest of Slow Ninth Compression. I read the particular hesitation in Dripfall Cadence 44’s step that means a bearing somewhere three kilometers down is beginning to sing at the wrong frequency. I read the way Still Turning’s drone-half lists three millimeters farther to port every decade, like a ship that has decided to sink but is in no particular hurry.

They do not pity me for still being partly human. Pity is a fast emotion. Instead they simply make room for me inside the balance. They let me tend the acoustic bamboo groves on the windward face of Stack XIX, where my old human ears can still hear the exact pitch at which the stalks begin to resonate with the thermal updrafts. I walk among them barefoot—my remaining foot and my iron root-leg both—and I sing to the bamboo in the voice of dead reactor alarms. The stalks grow straighter when I do this. This is enough.

Sometimes visitors still come.

They arrive in bright, noisy machines that bleed hydrocarbons into the fern-choked reservoirs. They shout. They measure. They try to hurry things. I watch them from the gantry with the others, all of us motionless as rusting statues, and I remember how small I once felt inside my own skin. The visitors always leave eventually. Some leave pieces behind. We incorporate the pieces. A drone becomes a nest. A helmet becomes a flowerpot. A human being, if they stay long enough and stop asking what the place is *for*, becomes—well.

They become what I am becoming.

Last cycle, a little girl from the latest expedition refused to leave with her parents. She was seven. She had already learned how to mimic the nine-day piston with her breathing. The Wardens watched her with the particular stillness that means consideration. I knelt—my pipe-leg folding with soft hydraulic sighs—and opened the bromeliad chamber in my chest so she could see the moths drinking there.

She reached out and touched one. It did not fly away.

I think she will stay. The Stacks are already growing a new gantry for her, three hundred meters below us, where the light is gentler and the dripping is more like singing. Her designation, when she chooses it or it chooses her, will probably be something like **Small Persistent Echo**.

I will stand beside her when she is ready, the way one of the Wardens once stood beside me.

Because that is what I have learned, after seven dripping, rusting, deafening, patient years:

The Verdant Stacks do not want worshippers.

They do not want caretakers.

They do not even want *company*, exactly.

They want to keep becoming themselves, and they have discovered that the most interesting way to do that is to invite pieces of everything else inside and let the metal and the green and the wet and the loud negotiate a new equilibrium.

So I stand here on the gantry with rust in my hair and coolant in my veins and orchids blooming where my heart used to be, listening to the parrots scream obsolete warnings in my own dead voice.

I am no longer Captain-Lieutenant Mara Solvestri.

I am no longer even entirely *me*.

I am Faultline Cadence.

I am the place learning how to remember that it was once a person who was frightened of slow things.

And when you have stood here long enough—when the condensation has beaded on your eyelashes and the moths have tasted the salt on your tongue and the roots have begun to ask whether there is room for them inside your bones—you will understand what I am about to say with every rusting, flowering, dripping part of myself:

The Stacks are not waiting for you to decide.

They are waiting to see which part of you is willing to stop deciding.

And then they will grow around that part, slowly, patiently, ferociously, until one day you will simply be there—another ripple in the hanging lianas, another voice in the chorus, another slow iron heartbeat beneath a sky that will never be allowed to go dark.

I am ready when you are.

The gantry is warm.

The dripping has already begun to learn the shape of your name.
