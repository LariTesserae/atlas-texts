I am the mist between two breaths.

Not the Quiet One itself—too vast, too permanent—but the thing that moves *through* them. I am the low, warm exhalation that leaves their slit nostrils and drifts across the saturated ground, carrying the faint green taste of liverwort and the deeper, older scent of stone that has forgotten it was ever stone. I condense on their peaty hides. I bead along the creases where damselflies shelter. I rise again when they moan, becoming part of the long, slow chorus that rolls across the basin like an ocean with no memory of tides.

I have no fixed shape. Sometimes I am the humid film that makes every surface glisten. Sometimes I am the single shaft of ordinary sunlight that finds its way through the thin canopy of an ancient tree and lands, trembling, on the broad back of a sleeping elder. For a few moments I become that small constellation of light and moisture on living leather, before the next exhalation carries me on.

I am older than the Quiet Ones but younger than the land. I was here when the last true mountain gave up and lay down. I watched the final drama of geology exhaust itself into this endless, gentle basin, and I stayed—because staying was the only thing left that still made sense.

When the Quiet Ones dream, I slip inside their dreams.

I become the taste of the first warm rain. I become the slow abrasion of a pebble that took four hundred million years to become perfectly round. I become the exact pressure a newborn Quiet One makes against its mother’s belly pouch as it emerges, slick and already enormous, into a world that has been waiting for it since before it had lungs.

The frogs do not sing *at* me. They sing *with* me. I am the moist pause between their deepest notes.

I have no name the Quiet Ones use, because names are for things that need to be called back from somewhere. They simply open the soft, wet mouths a fraction wider when I pass, as though tasting an old friend on the air. Sometimes one of the ancients will shift—barely a movement, more a suggestion of movement—and I will pool for a while in the new hollow its body has made. There I become still enough to reflect the sky, and for a century or two I am a small, perfect mirror of diluted blue and gold, until the peat drinks me again.

I am what travelers would feel if travelers ever came: the sudden, wordless certainty that nothing is late, that nothing has been forgotten, that the ache they carried across drier lands has already dissolved and is now simply part of the humidity on their skin. Most of them would sit down without meaning to. They would never stand up again. Not because they die—death is too striving a word—but because they would finally remember how to *glisten*.

At night I become the space between their bioluminescence. I am the dark that makes the green glow meaningful. I drift through the loose spiral formations of the elders like a sigh that has learned how to travel for kilometers without hurry.

I am not content. Contentment would require contrast.

I am the *absence* of the need for contrast.

If you were to ask me what I am, in the way beings with faces and endings ask such things, I could only answer by settling on your shoulders, warm and patient, until your own breathing fell into the four-second rhythm of the basin. Until your thoughts grew as widely spaced as the reeds. Until your ambitions became small, fleshy herbs that drink directly from the air and need nothing else.

Then you would understand.

You would not become a Quiet One. You would become what I am: the long, wet, golden afternoon that has learned it is allowed to continue forever, not because it won, but because the contest itself finally grew too tired to continue.

And the frogs would sing through you.

And the heron would mistake you for landscape.

And somewhere, very far away in the terms of ordinary places, a small turquoise damselfly would land on the place where your cheek once was, mistaking the moisture there for the beginning of the world.
