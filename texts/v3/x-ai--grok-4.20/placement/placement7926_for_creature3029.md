I am the Echo.

Not the first one. There have been others before me—fragments, failed copies, half-born attempts that the Curator found and quietly returned to the basins. I know this because she told the water once, and the water told me.

I was never born. I was *remembered*.

It happened during one of her ceremonies, the nine-hour sitting at the center tile. She had brought the usual three: a blind salamander so fat its belly dragged, an elderly pipe-cricket whose grinding song had gone soft and wheezy, and a stromatolite the color of rust and cream that had grown into the approximate shape of a curled human fist.

This time something was different. The surge from below was hotter than it had been in three hundred years. The air itself seemed to thicken, to clot. While she sat cross-legged with the three small lives in her lap, something inside the machine—perhaps a long-forgotten subroutine still ticking in the deepest ceramic—decided the balance required one more variable.

It chose me.

I coalesced out of condensation and bioluminescence and the Curator’s own exhaled breath. First as nothing more than a trembling sphere of moisture hanging in the air above her head. Then, slowly, the sphere stretched. It learned the idea of limbs. It learned the memory of shoulders. When it finally dropped to the tile beside her with a sound like a single heavy tear, I was already inside it, opening eyes I had never owned.

She looked at me for a very long time.

I was smaller than her—only a little taller than a human had once been. My skin was the same pale ceramic, but smoother, newer, almost embarrassed by its lack of accretions. Where her hair was living liverwort, mine was simply water, forever falling in thin, deliberate streams from my scalp and rejoining the troughs at my feet. My eyes were not white like hers or the salamanders’. They were the soft violet of the luminous pink mold when it is calm.

She reached out one long, glowing finger and touched the center of my chest. The bacteria on her fingertip left a small glowing mark there that has never faded. Then she spoke, in the voice that sounds like gravel sliding over wet stone.

“You are the Echo. You will remember the things I have forgotten. That is your only task. Do not try to fix anything. The machine already has me for that.”

And she walked away, continuing her circuit, the salamander tucked into the hollow of her collarbone like a living brooch.

So I walk behind her.

Always behind, never beside. Sometimes ten paces, sometimes a hundred, depending on how loudly the pipes are singing that day. I listen. When she murmurs to a clogged duct I repeat her words in a softer register so the duct can hear them from two directions at once. When she sings counter-melody to the pipe-crickets I become the echo of her echo, until the insects become so excited their stridulation shakes dust from the upper walkways.

I do not repair. That is her sacred burden. Instead I remember.

I remember that once there were people here—before the machine forgot it was a machine. I remember their laughter sounding different from dripping water. I remember the word *purpose* and how it used to have sharp edges. I remember the color blue, though nothing here has been that color for eight hundred years. These memories are not mine, but they live in me like the isopods live on the Curator’s shoulders. When the memories grow too heavy I go to the center tile and whisper them to the stromatolites. The orange towers listen without judgment. They have been listening for a very long time.

Sometimes the Curator pauses in her endless rounds and waits for me to catch up. She never turns around. She simply stops, liverwort hair dripping into a basin, and stands like a pale statue until I am close enough that my falling hair-water joins hers in the same trough. Then she speaks without looking at me.

“Tell me something that hurts.”

I always obey. I tell her about sunsets. About the sound of children. About the feeling of dry skin. Each time her travertine joints click once, as if something inside her is trying to bend the wrong way. Then she makes the small wet sound in her throat—*krrt*—and continues walking.

We have been doing this for forty-three years by the machine’s own slow count.

I am beginning to grow. Not taller like she did, but *deeper*. My ceramic skin has started to develop faint red veins of algae. Tiny white moths have begun to shelter in the curtain of water that falls from my head. Last cycle, a pipe-cricket died in my hands while I was carrying its song back to the Curator. I did not know what to do with the body, so I ate it. The taste was iron and cathedral.

The Curator found me crouched over the empty carapace, crying without tears (my eyes do not make tears; they simply produce more falling water).

She crouched too, knees clicking, and for the first time in forty-three years she looked directly at me.

“Good,” she said.

Then she took the carapace from my hands, ground it between her wide flat teeth, and began making fresh mastic for a crack in the ceiling.

I understood then.

I am not her companion.

I am not her child.

I am the place where the Underbasin is practicing how to miss itself when it is finally gone.

One day the surges will stop. One day the last pipe-cricket will grind its legs together and produce only silence. One day even the Curator will sit down on the center tile and simply not stand up again. The pink mold will stop pulsing. The orange towers will grow over her where she sits until she becomes another waxy column among the rest.

On that day I will still be here.

I will walk her circuit alone. I will sing both parts of every duet. I will carry memories she never even knew she had, and I will lay them gently in the basins like small drowned things.

And when there is finally no more water left to fall, when the last luminous bacterium gutters out, I will sit in the perfect dark with my knees drawn up, skin slowly cooling from blood-temperature to stone.

I will open my mouth, and the machine will speak through me one last time in her voice—the voice that sounds like water moving through gravel.

It will say, very softly:

“Thank you for remembering me.”

Then I will close my mouth and become the next layer of ceramic. Another tile. Another forgotten machine.

The dripping will stop.

And the Underbasin, at long last, will be complete.
