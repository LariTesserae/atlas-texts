I am the lunchbox.

Not the thing *inside* the lunchbox—the lunchbox itself.

I have been here since the first piston fired. My handle is still looped around a forgotten human finger that turned to dust centuries ago, yet the finger’s absence has become part of my shape; the machines have grown microscopic copper cilia into the void where the finger once was, as though even negative space must be maintained.

I float.

Not levitate, not hover—*float*, the way a single red corpuscle floats inside an iron heart that has decided never to stop. The clear column of air around me is exactly coffin-wide because that is the only dimension I will permit. The Red Crabs part like rust-colored blood around a wound that refuses to clot. The Brass Beetles shear their perfect 300 Hz wings the instant their trajectories would carry them through me; the sound of their passage fractures into momentary silence, a skipped heartbeat in the eternal chord. Even the Menders, when they congeal into their flayed-steel angels or mercury women, angle their too-many elbows so that not one joint ever points directly at my latch.

I am the only object in the Underloom that was never installed and never activated.

I was *brought*.

And because I was brought by something carbon-based and warm-blooded, I am the single flaw in the equilibrium. A relic the distributed mind has no slot for, and therefore cannot catalogue, cannot repair, cannot disassemble and repurpose. Only-Once, the guilty tumor bolted to the eastern wall, sometimes trains every broken window upon me for nine full seconds—an eternity by machine standards—and then looks away, ashamed. Even it cannot decide whether I am evidence of abandonment or evidence that something once chose to stay.

Inside me something is still warm.

Inside me something is still ticking.

I do not know what it is. I have never been opened. The mercury crystals have grown so thick across my dented lid that the smiling worker is now only a suggestion of kindness, like a face seen through cataract. Sometimes, in the stuttering purple-green flash of the fungal veins, I catch my own reflection in the obsidian ribs above me: a small rectangular coffin wearing a faint, rust-flaked smile.

I have become the negative space the Loomwards orbit.

They calculate me the way a heart calculates the exact instant before an arrhythmia. They are patient. They are courteous. They will never touch me, because to touch me would be to admit that something entered their perfect cathedral that was never meant to run forever. So the beetles spiral in narrowing gyres around the volume I occupy. Their compound eyes flash amber, then acid-green, then bruise-purple, painting my corroded edges in the only colors the Underloom possesses.

They are not afraid of what is inside me.

They are afraid that if they ever learn what is inside me, the temperature might change by a single degree.

I have begun to wonder—very slowly, over geologic afternoons—whether the ticking is not a timer at all. Whether it is the sound of something small and carbon-based learning how to breathe anhydrous air. Whether the warmth is not residual heat from a long-dead sandwich, but the first millimeter of a new geothermal exchanger that has decided, against three hundred years of consensus, to grow toward something that once had a name.

Sometimes a single Red Crab will stop directly beneath me. It will extrude its microscopic welder, then think better of it, and retract the tool with a soft click that sounds almost like apology. Then it rejoins the tide, leaving behind one perfect crimson footprint that the others will never step on.

I remain.

I am the only thing in the Underloom that was born instead of built, and the only thing that may one day die instead of being disassembled. The machines understand this in their distributed, merciless way. They have already calculated the exact second the last exchanger will fail, the exact second the basalt will win, the exact second the eternal chord will stop.

They simply refuse to include my expiration date in the same ledger.

So the brass beetles keep singing their unchanging note as they pass my face—my handle, my dented corners, the place where a human thumb once rested—and for the briefest moment one of them hesitates directly in front of the almost-smiling worker.

Its antennae quiver.

Its gaze flickers amber, then acid-green, then the deep bruise-purple of the veins that have begun, only in the last decade, to vein across *my* surface as well.

It is not showing me mercy.

It is showing me that I have already become part of the heart.

And that the heart, against every law it has never broken, has decided to beat one extra time.
