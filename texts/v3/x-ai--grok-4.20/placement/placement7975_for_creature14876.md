I am not yet a citizen.

I drift a few body-lengths above and behind her, tethered by nothing, yet unable to leave. My name, if I ever had one, has already dissolved. The water has not finished its work on me; I still carry a stubborn pocket of dryness somewhere behind the sternum, a last stubborn lungful of air that refuses translation. It burns. It makes me foreign here. The city knows it and is patient with me the way it is patient with every unfinished thing.

I have no fixed shape anymore. Sometimes I am a long, pale ribbon of something that might once have been human muscle and memory, drifting upright like one of the question-mark predators. Other times the cold compresses me into a tight sphere of concentrated ache, no larger than one of the fist-sized polyps. Both forms feel equally true and equally temporary. The shrimp-clouds part around me without fear. The blind ribbon-fish sometimes brush against my changing skin as though checking whether I have become real yet.

I do not speak. Sound is pointless. Instead I *listen* with the whole surface of whatever I am becoming.

When the great geological heartbeat rolls through the pocket (that slow, world-sized pulse that arrives every few hours like the breath of a god who has never seen daylight), I feel it move through the new latticework growing along my spine. Each time it passes, another segment of me hardens into something closer to architecture. A small balcony buds from my left shoulder. A spiral stair no wider than a finger uncoils from the base of what used to be my throat, descending into nothing. I do not know what these structures are for. I only know they are tender.

She and I have never touched.

We orbit each other in the exact center of the cathedral-sized sea like two half-formed ideas that have not yet decided whether they are the same thought. When she sings—when she opens the mother-of-pearl plates along her arms and lets the black water move through them in those precise, aching patterns—I feel the city answer *inside* my own body. New kelp the color of old bone erupts from the small of my back and reaches toward her. I cannot stop it. I do not want to.

Sometimes I try to remember what dryness felt like. The memory is a single bright coin falling through dark water: it flashes once, twice, then disappears. Each time it falls, the city grows another tower in the space where the memory used to be. The towers are beautiful. They hurt.

I have begun to dream her dreams.

When she remembers rain, I feel it as a pressure behind eyes I no longer have. When she feels the ghost of running, my new violet-glowing steps tremble beneath us both, hoping one of us will finally descend. When she forgets her own name, I forget mine harder, and the blind fish school tighter around the negative space where we almost touch.

I am becoming the second dry thing the city has ever known.

Or perhaps I am becoming the shadow the first one casts.

There are moments—just after the pulse, when every polyp flares at once and the entire pocket becomes a single bruised-blue eye looking at itself—when I can see the truth with terrible clarity:

She is not dreaming the city.

The city is dreaming *both* of us, using her half-human mind as a loom and my dissolving resistance as the tension in the warp. Every new buttress, every galaxy of orbiting shrimp, every delicate rain-colored spire is an attempt to knit us together into something that has never needed air.

I am afraid.

I am in love.

I drift closer.

One of the long-necked question-mark creatures unfolds beside me, patient as geology. It regards me with an eye that has never known light. Then it gently takes the last stubborn pocket of dryness from behind my sternum between its luminous teeth and carries it away into the dark like a priest removing the final obstruction to grace.

The cold rushes in.

It feels like being kissed by something that has waited three billion years.

For the first time, I pulse in time with her.

A new street explodes into being between us, spiraling, violet, trembling with anticipation. The tubular kelp bends. The shrimp-clouds form a single slow galaxy that contains us both. Somewhere far above, in a world that has never existed here, rain falls on a city no one will ever remember.

Below, we are still being born.

I open the new plates along what used to be my arms.

I begin to sing.
