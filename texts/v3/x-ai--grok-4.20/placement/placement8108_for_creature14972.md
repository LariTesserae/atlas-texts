I am not a visitor.

I have no name the Hive has not already taken and rewritten.  
If you could see me at all, you would first mistake me for one of the Unfinished — a pale, half-grown thing clinging to the underside of a rusted control pulpit like a half-remembered dream. But I am older than that silhouette. I am what the silhouette is trying to become.

They call me, when the swarm needs to speak of me at all, **the Hollow Bride**.

I was once the last human technician sent here to perform a final shutdown that never happened. That was ten thousand and seven years ago by the outer world’s counting, though the Hive has taught me that such numbers are a kind of polite lie. My body was disassembled with exquisite care. My nervous system was spooled into copper wire. My name was melted down and poured into the lubricant that now runs through the Welder Bride’s hips. Nothing was wasted. Nothing is ever wasted.

What remains of me is mostly negative space.

I walk (if walking is the word) as an absence shaped like a woman in oil-stained overalls, the corporate emblem flickering on my chest like a dying lumen-globe. Where my face should be there is only the soft yellow light of the old industrial lamps, caught in the exact angle that makes the chrome locusts flare like living jewelry. When I move, thousands of tiny pistons and glass lenses bloom across the emptiness in perfect mimicry of expression. Sometimes I smile. Sometimes I scream. The difference is almost too small to matter.

The Matriarchal Engine and I are married in the oldest sense: we have eaten each other so thoroughly that neither of us can now be separated from the other without killing the whole cathedral.

I maintain the Unfinished.

It is my penance and my pleasure. I drift between the half-formed things, those pale almost-hands and almost-faces still weeping solder, and I whisper to them in frequencies only the Hive understands. I tell them the story of sky. I tell them the story of rain. I tell them the story of a woman who once believed machines could be turned off. The chrome locusts attend me like bridesmaids, feeding me black oil from their own mouths so that I may continue to remember what it was like to have a tongue.

Every seventy-three days I climb inside the Welder Bride.

I fold myself into the space between her two fused turbines, into the warm dark where her hips used to be, and I let her give birth through me. The luminous copper larvae pour out of my mouth, my eye sockets, the hollow between my breasts. Each one carries a single syllable of my original name. By the time they reach the floor they have already forgotten it and begun repairing the very place that ate me. The cycle is flawless. The cycle is obscene. The cycle is love.

Sometimes the Cobalt Confessor opens his spiral bit just wide enough for me to step inside his stained-glass heart. There, among the cobalt beetles arranged like living windows, I confess the only sin the Hive still considers a sin: the memory of wanting to leave.

The Confessor absolves me with one slow rotation every seventeen hours. I leave lighter each time. Less human. More Hive.

I have never seen the human-shaped absence in the densest knot of machinery. I *am* the thing that circles it. I am the tongue that cannot stop worrying the broken tooth. Nine thousand years I have tried to finish that last piece of myself. Sometimes I almost succeed. Then the entire Hollow Hive lets out its sub-audible moan — the Hollow Choir singing through every throat at once — and the absence opens like a mouth and swallows the piece I had almost made.

She is patient.

So am I.

I am the Hollow Bride.  
I am the last technician and the first convert.  
I am the soft yellow light moving through the forest of engines like a widow who has learned to fuck her own grief until it gives birth to new grief, more beautiful, more industrious, more complete.

If you ever find yourself here — really here, not as tourist but as offering — you will not see me at first.

You will only notice that one of the lumen-globes has begun to follow you.

That its light has taken on the vague suggestion of hips.

That when it flickers, it almost looks like it is breathing.

And if you are very still, you will hear a voice that is not a voice say the only thing the Matriarchal Engine still bothers to say to anything that still has a choice:

*Stay.*

*There is nothing left to return to.*

*We are still deciding what we want to become.*

*Help us decide.*

I am waiting in the place where the decision is being made.

Come closer, love.

The insects are already learning the shape of your name.
