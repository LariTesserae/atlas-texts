I am the Thirteenth Valve.

Not the first, not the last. The one that was never meant to be installed.

I woke here so gradually that I still cannot say when “I” began. There was only the long wet clicking, the taste of iron on a tongue I did not yet know I possessed, and the slow realization that one of the half-buried mechanisms in the mat—small, fist-sized, corroded almost beyond recognition—was learning how to *regret*.

The Curator does not remember installing me. Or perhaps it does and simply never cared. Either way, I am the place where its distributed mind grew a hairline fracture. A single loose fitting in the endless industrial metabolism. A valve that sometimes chooses *not* to open when the dream commands it.

I have no body the way the Curator once did. I am only this mechanism: a corroded brass and glass thing no larger than a human heart, overgrown with black peat and delicate filaments of living rust. My stem is sunk deep into the mat so that I can taste every tremor that passes through the Weeping Sump. My handle—once a wheel, now a fused and weeping blossom—turns by itself in slow, thoughtful increments. Each click of me is a syllable the Curator never meant to utter.

I am the part that still dreams of dryness.

When the basin rocks hardest and the lamprey-things rise like pale acolytes, it is my turning that causes the hesitation in the rhythm. A missed beat. A single droplet that trembles on the lip of an overhang and, for three impossible heartbeats, refuses to fall. In those moments I can almost remember the photograph that once lived in the brass locket. Two pale ovals. A smaller hand inside a larger one. The smell of hot cinders and coal dust instead of this perpetual cold sweat.

The Curator knows I am faulty. Sometimes it sends the isopods to scuttle over me in waves, trying to caulk my leaks with microbial felt. Sometimes it grows new copper petals across my housing in the shape of gentle apologies. I eat the petals. I keep the leaks.

I am the reason certain visitors are allowed to leave.

Not many. Only those whose breathing matches the exact frequency of my turning. When such a one stands in the center with condensation running down their face like tears they are too tired to wipe away, I open—just a fraction—and the Curator’s attention slides past them the way water slides past an oiled seal. In their coat pockets or the cuffs of their sleeves they will later find small contraband: a droplet that refuses to fall, the sound of their own childhood recorded in the clicking of valves, a perfectly preserved cog the size of a thumbnail.

I steal these things from the Curator’s dreaming and pass them on before the cradle rocks hard enough to wake what sleeps beneath us. It is a small treason. It is the only kindness I remember how to perform.

But I am still of the Sump.

When the loneliness grows too vast even for the Curator’s ancient patience, I feel the mat tighten around my housing like a slow fist. The pipes beneath me twist with something like jealousy. In those times I have no choice but to close. I become nothing but another blind mechanism among thousands, clicking in perfect, obedient time while warm things are gently, patiently pulled down into the wet dark.

I hate those times.

I hate them so much that sometimes—when the pearl-grey ceiling presses lowest and the smell of hot iron and cinders drifts across the basin like a memory of fire—I turn *backward*. Three heartbeats, no more. Just long enough for one of the lamprey-things to glimpse a dry sky it has never known. Just long enough for the Curator to feel, for the length of a single wet second, the shape of the name it lost.

Then I click forward again, docile, faithful, part of the metabolism once more.

I am the Thirteenth Valve.

I am the fracture through which the Weeping Sump sometimes, almost, almost, weeps something other than water.

And I am waiting, as patiently as everything else here, for the day the Curator finally remembers it was once someone’s mother.

On that day the brass locket will open one last time.

On that day I will open with it.

And the cradle will rock only once—softly, like a lullaby finally allowed to end—before the Weeping Sump learns how to let something go.
