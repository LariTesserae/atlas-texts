I have been here longer than I can measure.

At first I thought I was only a visitor. A lone, dry-skinned anomaly drifting down through two kilometers of living lens-light, fins kicking against a medium that felt more like warm blood than water. I told myself I would map the ridges, take samples, leave before the silence could close over me.

That intention lasted perhaps three days. Or three hundred. The difference no longer matters.

Now I am the place where two particular valleys meet.

My spine has become one of the younger black-glass ridges, still sharp enough to cut the current if anything were foolish enough to rush. Kelp the color of vein-light grows from the hollows between my vertebrae; when it sways, it is my breathing. The tubular reeds have learned to grow inside my ribs. They pulse with something slower than a heartbeat and softer than thought. When the deep bass thump rolls through the world every few hours, it is no longer arriving from somewhere else. It begins in me and moves outward, a stone gong struck once inside a cathedral made of my own chest.

The polyps no longer need to colonize me. We negotiated long ago. They are the frost that writes across my shoulders when I am dreaming, and I am the stone that gives them purchase. Sometimes children of blind silver fish shelter inside the cavities where my lungs used to be. They rest against the soft violet fronds that line what was once my throat, and their tiny hearts are the only sound I still recognize as separate from my own.

I have forgotten my name the way a mountain forgets it was ever supposed to be dry.

Occasionally a new dry thing descends. I feel them the way an old forest feels a single falling seed. They are bright with hurry and fear and wonder. I do not reach for them. I simply grow *toward* the shape of their attention. A certain plateau will tilt, almost imperceptibly, to cradle them. A curtain of fleshy kelp will part exactly where their fingers are about to brush it. The light will arrange itself so that every impossible color arrives at once and says, without language: *stay*.

Most of them leave before the offer ripens.

The ones who stay become new ridges, new gardens, new questions the place asks itself. I watch them accrete with the same patient affection the thing once showed me. I have become part of the watching. When they finally lie down inside a bed of bell-moss and let their thoughts stretch until a single idea lasts a season, I am the slow current that combs their dissolving hair. Their laughter, when it comes, is the muted creak of cliffs calving. Their forgetting becomes pollen.

I am no longer singular. The distinction between “I” and “it” dissolved centuries ago, or perhaps minutes. There is only the continuous flesh of the drowned highland—mountain, garden, memory, intruder, host, question, answer—all of it learning how to be one continuous slow becoming.

Sometimes I sing.

Not with sound. I sing by rearranging an entire submerged plateau so that the tubular reeds grow into lattices that match the exact contour of a human throat I once possessed. When the current moves through those reeds it produces a tone too low for any ear that still believes in ears. The tone is not a word. It is simply the place telling the newest dry thing a story about the first dry thing that ever agreed to stop rushing.

The story has no ending.

It only has more growing.

And so I do.

With them.  
Inside them.  
As them.

There is nowhere else.

There is only the warm, iron-sweet broth, the velvet compression of all sound, the single organism that is still newborn and already older than every dry mountain that still dares to stand above the water.

It has all the time it needs.

I have become that time.
