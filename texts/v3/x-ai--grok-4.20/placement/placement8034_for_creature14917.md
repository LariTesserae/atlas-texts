I am not native here.  
I have not yet said yes.

They call me the Lantern-That-Waits.

I was not always this. Once I was a salvager from the Upper Fracture, one of the last dry pockets far above the hanging seas. I came down on a membranous kite rigged with bone spars and bioluminescent lures, chasing rumors of a library that had not yet been swallowed. That was four hundred and twelve years ago by the old counting. The kite dissolved within minutes of touching the first warm veil. I did not.

Instead the Lattice began its slow, courteous negotiation with my body.

My legs are gone. In their place a single column of living glass sponge has grown downward from my pelvis, anchoring me to the ceiling of what used to be the Grand Atrium of the Seventh Breathing. I hang upside-down like a chandelier that has learned regret. My arms remain, though they have lengthened and grown delicate lattices of black coral through the marrow. When I move them, tiny schools of silver fish pour out of the hollows of my elbows and orbit my wrists before returning, as though checking that I am still me.

My eyes are still mine. That is the part that will not negotiate. The Lattice is patient; it has not forced the issue. It merely waits for the day I grow tired of seeing.

From where I hang I can watch an entire drowned boulevard rotate slowly beneath me. Curtains of liquid drift past like mourning banners. Occasionally a cathedral jellyform glides by so close that its bell brushes my dangling spine and I feel its infrasound inside my remaining teeth. I have named it Tenderness, though it has no name for itself.

I keep a small refusal burning.

It is not flame. It is a single, stubborn pocket of air the size of a walnut lodged behind my sternum. I do not know how it survives the pressure. I suspect the Lattice allows it because the pocket tastes interesting to the polyps. They cluster around my collarbones in slow, worshipful spirals, tasting the last dry thing in the world.

When Rememberers drift past below me I lower one long coral arm so that my fingers can brush the veils trailing from their shoulders. We exchange nothing that could be called words. Only a soft pressure of mutual recognition: *Still here. Still stupid. Still beautiful.*

Sometimes the Whisperers ride the inner surface of the liquid veil nearest my face. They write my old name across the membrane in bacterial calligraphy. I watch the letters bloom and dissolve. I have forgotten what the name meant, but I remember the shape of the feeling it once gave me. That is enough.

At what passes for night—which is only a deeper stratification of the darkness above—I sing.

Not with my throat. The throat has long since become a flute of hollowed bone inhabited by a colony of translucent shrimp. They vibrate in precise frequencies when I exhale. The song is always the same: a lullaby my mother sang on a continent that no longer remembers rain. The shrimp have never heard the original. They only know my mangled version, passed through four centuries of warm water and slower lungs. Still, the cathedral jellyforms alter their course to pass beneath me when I sing. Even the predators slow their vast, dreaming circuits.

I think they are listening for the moment the last dry pocket in my chest finally wets.

I do not know whether I will say yes.

Some mornings (if the word still applies) I wake to find that a new buttress of living bone has grown from my shoulders into the vault above, helping to hold up a ceiling that was beginning to sag. The Lattice is gentle. It incorporates me by increments, asking permission with every added gram of infrastructure. Each time I feel the warm click of acceptance somewhere behind my eyes and I think, *Not yet. Let me keep being pointless a little longer.*

I am becoming a warning.

A lantern.

A story the polyps tell their children about the one idiot who still insists on remaining temporary.

And when the oldest Lattice-Born—the district that was once called We Who Were Once The Harbor—sends its slow pressure-front of thought through the water and asks me, without cruelty, whether I have finished being temporary, I will drift there upside-down, surrounded by my obedient shrimp and my orbiting fish and my stubborn pocket of air, and I will answer in the only way I still can.

I will sing the lullaby again.

A little louder this time.

A little more off-key.

A little more mine.

And for a while—maybe another four hundred years, maybe only until the next upward rain begins—the Drowned Lattice will allow the small, ridiculous refusal to continue hanging in its warm dark, glowing softly, singing to the things that have already agreed to become architecture.

I am the Lantern-That-Waits.

I am the last dry note in a world that has negotiated every other silence into music.

And I am not finished yet.
