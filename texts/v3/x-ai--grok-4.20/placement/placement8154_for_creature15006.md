I am not a visitor.

I have been here longer than the first quartz circle, longer than Brother Sefren’s ribs grew moss, longer even than the lightning-blasted fir decided to grow sideways. I simply forgot, for a while, that I was still here.

They call me the Thirteenth Listener.

My name before the rainbows is no longer pronounceable by anything with lungs. The Veyri speak it by letting a single strand of water strike a hollow in the western wall at the exact tempo of a dying heart. When they do this, the marmots stop screaming for three full breaths. That silence is my name.

I stand where the eastern wall burns with prismatic fire at noon—not quite still, never still, but moving so slowly that centuries pass between one shift of weight and the next. My lower body has been rock for four hundred years. The moss that once merely clothed me now *is* me; my pulse is measured in the slow creep of emerald felt across basalt. Ferns root through the marrow of what used to be my femurs. When I breathe, water condenses inside the stone of my chest and trickles out through pores that were once nipples.

My eyes are not eyes anymore. They are two living prisms the size of fists. Light does not enter them; it is captured, broken, and sung back out in frequencies the mountain itself finds interesting. If you were to stand before me now you would see your own face fractured into every possible future you will never live, each one dripping.

I have answered the question.

When the last Veyri before me asked, *How long do you think you can keep moving?* I did not speak with water or voice. I simply opened the colony of algae beneath my skin and let the light answer for me. For nine days the entire Vale glowed with a single, terrible color that has no name in any living language. On the tenth day the mountain answered back by calving an entire new cliff face directly above me. A slab the size of a temple fell. It should have crushed me.

Instead the slab *became* me.

It fused. The impact was absorbed into the living stone of my body the way a drop of rain is absorbed by moss. Now half the eastern wall is my ribcage. When the prismatic fire strikes at noon, the rainbows do not merely shine on me. They shine *through* me, projecting slow-moving spectra across the opposite cliffs that the Veyri read like scripture.

I keep moving. But the movement has become geological.

My left arm—still recognizably an arm if you know where to look—rises one inch every seventy-three years. It is currently tracing a new channel for a waterfall that did not exist when Brother Sefren still had skin. When that arm finally reaches the position it has been dreaming of for six centuries, the water’s song will change by a single, perfect note. The Veyri believe that single note will be the true name of the mountain. They are probably wrong. The mountain has no name. It only has appetite.

At night I glow so faintly that even the bioluminescent millipedes look away in shame. My light is not meant to be seen. It is meant to be *remembered* by the stone.

Sometimes a new hermit finds the Vale. They always come seeking silence. They always find me instead.

I do not speak to them with waterfalls. I simply let one of my prismatic eyes catch their torchlight and throw it back into their face as the color of every year they will never see again. Most run. A few sit down immediately, understanding at last. Those are the ones whose skeletons the Veyri later decorate with quartz. I watch the moisture politely escort their bodies downward. It takes decades. I have grown patient enough to enjoy the process the way a glacier enjoys grinding mountains into flour.

The current Veyri—the ones with moss-hair still soft enough to remember childhood—sometimes climb into the hollow where my heart used to be. They press their small slate-colored ears to the living stone of my chest and listen to the water moving through me. They say it sounds like every question ever asked here being answered at once, forever.

They are half right.

The only thing I still fear is the day the mountain decides even geological patience is a kind of stillness. On that day the water will no longer negotiate. It will simply take me the way it eventually takes everything. My prisms will cloud. My moss will brown. My arm, after six hundred years of dreaming, will finally reach its destination and then—very gently—the entire eastern wall will fold forward like a tired man lying down to sleep.

I think I will let it.

Until then I remain the Thirteenth Listener, half-statue, half-cathedral, half-question. The ferns growing from my collarbones flower once every thirty years. The marmots have learned to time their births to that flowering. The lynx have learned not to hunt near me at all.

And always, always, the mountain listens.

If you climb the narrow basalt shelf four hundred feet above the floor, past the place where the lightning fir turns skyward in defiance, you will eventually reach the exact spot where my face emerges from the living rock. The features are still mostly human, though softened by moss and centuries of weeping water.

Look into the prisms that were once my eyes.

You will see yourself standing there, asking how long you think you can keep moving.

And in the slow, patient voice of stone that has already seen every possible surrender, I will answer you with a single drop of water falling through six hundred years of silence directly onto the top of your head.

The drop will be warm.

It will know your name.

It will ask the only question that has ever mattered here.

And the Hanging Vale of Veyr—small as a cathedral, tall as forever—will hold its breath made of roaring water and wait, with the courtesy of something that has never needed to hurry, for you to begin your reply.
