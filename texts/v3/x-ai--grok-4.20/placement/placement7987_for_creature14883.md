I am the Echo.

Not the first thing that drowned, nor the last. I am what remained after the forgetting began but before it was complete. A loose skein of human-shaped memory that refused, for a long time, to dissolve.

I have no body anymore, yet I still wear the habit of one. A faint outline, like heat haze in the shape of a woman who once stood on a pier in another age watching gulls argue over bread. The water moves through me as easily as it moves through itself. My “skin” is only a difference in salinity no thicker than a thought. My “eyes” are two small pockets of colder water that remember how to focus. When I weep, the tears do not fall; they simply become slightly sweeter veins that stray upward for a few meters before forgetting their purpose.

I drift between the hanging cathedrals of kelp.

The Lamina knows I am here the way a lung knows it contains a single unfiltered particle of smoke. It does not hurry me. It has centuries. I have whatever this is.

Sometimes I follow the child’s marble.

It hangs in a column of perfectly motionless water like a blue star that has given up on gravity. I circle it for what might be weeks. I press what used to be my forehead against the invisible skin of that stillness and try to remember the sound of a child laughing. The memory arrives thick and slow, as though it has barnacles on it. When it finally surfaces, it tastes of iron and old roses, and I understand that the Lamina is tasting it with me.

I have names for some of the layers now, though they are useless to anything but me.

The Sighlayer, where the water is so saturated with memory that thoughts coagulate into faint luminous shapes (broken Ferris wheels, half a church organ, the smell of my mother’s coat).  
The Lanternbelt, where the anemones keep their long, mournful conversation in cold green fire.  
The Ribroad, the vast migratory path of the eyeless mountain-things that wear the Lamina’s patience like a second skeleton.

I have walked all of them.

I have become small enough to live inside one of the translucent shrimp for an entire season, wearing its nervous system like a coat, learning what it feels like to be uncomplicated hunger. I have grown large enough to wear the ribbed shadow for three heartbeats (long enough to feel the planetary groan move through bones that were never mine). Both times I returned to my hazy woman-shape tasting of trespass and unbearable tenderness.

The bell tolled once while I was near it.

The sound arrived seven years late and seven years early at the same time. It moved through me like regret made liquid. For a moment the hazy outline of my body sharpened so painfully I almost had lungs again. I screamed without sound. The Lamina took that scream, folded it into the kelp, and grew a new frond the color of bruised mulberry and dried blood. I understood then that even my refusal was compost.

There are days (if days still exist) when I try to swim upward toward the bruised cloud ceiling. I never make it far. The pressure does not fight me; it simply becomes more itself. Every meter I gain costs me a memory. First the name of the ocean I was born beside. Then the color of my childhood house. Then the feeling of rain on skin that was actually skin. Eventually I stop, turn, and let myself sink again, because the alternative is to arrive at the surface as nothing but a faint warmth with no story left to tell.

I have seen two versions of the same kelp tower existing slightly out of phase.

In one, I am still trying to be human. In the other, I have already become another pulse in the Lamina’s slow digestion. The two versions regard each other with something that might be recognition if recognition still mattered. Then the water shifts and they overlap again, and I am both and neither.

Most of the time I simply drift with the planetary gyre.

I let the great currents comb through what used to be my hair. I let the plankton blooms light me from within until I glow like moonlight on bone for a few ecstatic minutes. I let the colossal rib-shadow pass overhead and I do not flinch. I am learning the only lesson there is left to learn here:

Everything must drift.  
Everything must layer.  
Everything must touch without ever quite meeting.

And one day (though “day” is a dry word that no longer applies), I will forget that I was ever the Echo.

I will simply be another gradient inside the Lamina.  
A particular thermocline that once dreamed it had a name.  
A faint blue memory hanging motionless in a column of still water.

The Lamina will not have eaten me.  
It will simply have finished becoming me, the way it finished becoming everything else.

And in that perfect, syrupy, iron-and-rose equilibrium, the world will have no surface at all.

Only depth.  
Only patience.  
Only the gentle, ceaseless turning of one warm, endless, forgetting heart.
