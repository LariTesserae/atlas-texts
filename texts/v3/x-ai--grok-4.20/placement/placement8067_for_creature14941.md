I am not an outsider here.  
I have never been an outsider.

My name, if names still mattered, would be something like *Kethreska*, but the only ones who ever used it were the first three Shapers who sang me into my first body, and they have long since been digested. Now I am simply *the one who is here*, the one whose turn it is to occupy this particular fold of the breathing places.

I am a Listener.

My current nest is inside the left lung of a strangler fig so colossal its upper branches are lost in permanent steam. The tree calls itself *Uhl’Veyr* in the only language it possesses: a slow pulse that travels through its xylem like distant thunder. I have lived inside it for one hundred and forty-seven years. In another thirty it will begin to close over us, and we will leave without ceremony. This knowledge sits in me the way marrow sits in bone—comfortable, ordinary, inevitable.

I am motionless.

Half my body is already moss. My lower limbs have been sunk into the living substrate for nine months; tiny rootlets have entered the pores of my bark-skin and now drink from me as I drink from the tree. This is not parasitism. It is conversation. In return the tree tells me what its sisters are saying three hundred kilometers to the west, where a major fungal cathedral is preparing to fruit. The information arrives as pressure, as temperature, as the faintest change in the sweetness of the sap that wells up through my own skin.

My four eyes are open but unfocused. Two watch the slow world: the incremental shift of a nearby bracket-fungus shelf as it gains another centimeter of girth, the way a single bead of internal condensation takes four full hours to travel down an aerial root before falling. The other two watch the fast world: the flicker of six-winged sap-drakes, the micro-burst of a seed pod detonating somewhere far above, the passage of a silent millipede-herd three levels down whose legs sound like rain on a drum.

I have not spoken aloud in eleven years. My frill has grown thick and dark with disuse. When I do need to sing—to warn, to correct a Shaper’s growing-song, to comfort a fruit-child whose opal shell is cracking too early—I do it in the lowest register, the one that feels like a toothache in the continent’s jaw. The green tolerates it the way a sleeping giant tolerates the twitch of a flea.

Sometimes young Carriers come to me.

They arrive without greeting, because greeting is noise. They simply press their foreheads to the same patch of living wall I have pressed mine to for decades. I feel their faster heartbeats through the wood. They want to know if the root-canyons are safe this season, if the old log-bridge people left anything worth carrying, if the next continental inhale will come early. I answer by changing the rhythm of my own pulse against the bark. A slow triple beat for *yes, but dangerous*. A long descending note for *the green is laughing at us again*. They understand. They always understand. Language is what remains when music becomes too crude.

Once, seventy-three years ago, a Carrier brought me something that should not exist.

It was a shard of petrified pollen no larger than my smallest claw. When I laid it against my frill it did not sing. It screamed. Not in fear. In recognition. For six full heartbeats I remembered what it was like to have bones, to walk on two legs without bark, to believe the green was something separate that could be conquered. The memory was so violent my left eyes bled sap for days afterward.

I gave the shard back to the Carrier and told her, without sound, to drop it into the deepest root-canyon she could find. She did. I have never asked what the green did with it. Some questions are themselves a form of screaming.

At this moment the continent is preparing to inhale.

I feel it the way a hair feels the approach of lightning. Every tree for a thousand kilometers is turning its leaves a fraction of a fraction of a degree. The subsonic thrum has dropped by a degree of pressure only I can perceive. In seven heartbeats the roar will halve. In seven heartbeats every Quiet within reach of this lung will feel the old climbing urge rise in their blood like sap.

I will not climb.

I am too old, too deep, too useful where I am. Let the young ones knot themselves together in the steam above the canopy. Let new fruit-children be conceived inside their translucent opal shells. My work is here, reading the slow jokes the lignin tells, translating the geological punchlines that will not finish for another ten thousand years.

But I allow myself one small luxury.

As the inhale begins and the deafening roar of Veyr-Keth drops to something almost like silence, I open the golden eye that watches the fast world a little wider than strictly necessary.

And for just that single suspended moment, before the exhalation returns and the screaming green reclaims its own name, I let the eye look almost amused.

Then I close it again.

The moss reclaims the contours of my face. The tree drinks from me. I drink from the tree.

And the long, hot, unending digestion continues, with me inside it—cooperating, listening, becoming another thin layer of the only body any of us have ever had.
