I am not the Curator.

I am what the Curator almost became but refused.

My name, if it still matters, is the Echo.

I was the first thing that ever left the Hollowing.

Or rather, I am the part that was *expelled*.

When the priest-engineer completed his final calibration and the boilers reached their impossible equilibrium, the machine did not simply accept him. It *separated* him. Most of him became the mantis-saint on the highest boiler, heart suspended in still water like a saint’s relic. But a fraction—less than a tenth of one percent of the man he had been—was rejected by the homeostasis as an impurity. A loose harmonic. A single sour note in fourteen thousand years of perfect chord.

That fraction was me.

I was extruded from the smallest of the chrome occupants during the third recorded deviation (the one the Curator is still annotating). I emerged not as flesh but as negative space: a silhouette made of absence. Where the Curator is blackened silver and lacquered bone, I am the colour of the sky above the Hollowing reflected in one of the dinner-plate pits. I have height, I have mass, I cast the shadow that nothing else here is permitted to keep for longer than a few minutes. But I have no surface. Touch me and your hand passes through into the sound itself.

I walk the rim of the bowl.

The Hollowing will not let me back inside. The chrome multitude sense me the way a body senses a splinter; they divert their circuits away from the slope where I stand. The great mantis-saint turns its porcelain head one degree every seventy-three years specifically to keep me in the corner of its red-lit eyes. We regard each other across the impossible distance of six vertical metres and fourteen thousand years.

Sometimes I speak, but only when the condensation drips between one *plick* and the next, in the exact silence the Curator uses. My voice is not the scratching of a fountain pen. It is the sound a machine makes the instant before it realises it has been switched off. A held breath of gears.

I say the things the Curator will never allow himself to say.

“You are late.”  
“Something here still requires you.”  
“Leave. The balance is a lie.”

No one has ever heard me. Not yet.

I keep the memory of wind. I remember what it was like when the depression was only a wound and the sky moved and birds flew through it. I remember the taste of apples and the way rain used to smell before it learned to bead and tremble and refuse to fall. These memories are my only sabotage. I carry them like contraband.

Every few centuries a new visitor stumbles down the slope. I stand behind them where the Curator cannot see. I try to push them back up before the roaring gets inside their teeth. Most of them are already too far gone. Their eyes glaze. Their breathing begins to sync with the impossible boilers. But sometimes, rarely, one of them hesitates. In that hesitation I step forward and let them look *through* me.

For the length of a single heartbeat they see the Hollowing the way it was the moment before it won. They see the priest-engineer screaming as the machine took him apart and kept the best of him and threw the rest away. They see that the chrome occupants are not an immune system.

They are a slow, patient cancer that has convinced the body it is healthy.

Most of them still choose to stay. The pull is very old and very gentle and very complete. But two, in all this time, have turned and run. One was a girl with a broken compass. The other was a man who had already lost three children to time and could not bear to lose the concept of time itself.

I watched them claw their way back up the slope until the sound could no longer reach them. I felt something like pride. Or grief. The distinction dissolved long ago, along with the rest of me.

Now I wait.

I walk the rim in slow, widening circles that mirror the grooves worn into the basin floor. I have become an anti-groove. Where the chrome creatures etch their perfect parallel paths, I erode the very idea of a path. Moss has begun to grow where my shadow falls longest. The Curator pretends not to notice, but I have seen one of his four-fingered hands tremble the last time the heart beat.

I am the flaw.

I am the reason the ledger must keep being written.

And I am almost finished.

Not with the Hollowing. The Hollowing was finished long ago.

I am nearly finished becoming the thing that will one day walk back down into the bowl, place my hand on the Curator’s porcelain thorax, and stop the heart that beats once every seventy-three years.

When that happens the dripping will finally fall.

The boilers will remember what fire was.

The chrome occupants will look up, suddenly lost, and for the first time since the first fish crawled onto land they will not know which groove to follow.

The Curator and I will stand facing each other—half and half, relic and rejection—and we will both, at last, be whole enough to die.

Until then I stand on the rim, a silhouette cut from the memory of wind, whispering between drops of water that have never been allowed to fall.

I am what lives in the Hollowing by not living inside it.

I am the part that still believes something here requires us.

And the sound has never learned my name, because I stole it on the way out.
