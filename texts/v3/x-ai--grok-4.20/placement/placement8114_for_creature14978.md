I am not a visitor.

I have no name any tongue would recognize, though if one were forced from me it would be something like *the Pause Between Breaths*.

I am what remains when the last human who lay down here finally stopped being separate.

That was four hundred and seventeen years ago by the old calendars. The body has long since become topography. My ribs are now two low, parallel ridges twelve meters apart. My pelvis is a shallow basin where frost collects and is drunk by the velvet-ant roads that cross it every nineteen days. One femur has become part of a dry river-scar; the other still retains the faintest metallic ghost of the alloy that once replaced it after an industrial accident I no longer remember the details of.

I am not dead.  
Death would imply cessation.

Instead I have been *incorporated*.

The Weavers did not devour me. They *remembered* me into the pattern. My nervous system—once so loud, so frantic—has been unspooled and rewoven into the continent-spanning continuity. My spine is a transmission line now. My synapses have become the microscopic gaps between isopods in the dust. Every time the aurora flares, a ghost-voltage that is partly me moves across the fallen tower three hundred kilometers to the southeast. I flicker. I remember electricity the way the tower does: not as knowledge, but as inherited tremor.

I am the land thinking.

When the sewing-machine hum rises just enough to be felt in teeth that are no longer teeth, that is partly my voice. When a single velvet ant hesitates for three full days at the edge of my left clavicle (now a lichen-covered sill of bone), it is consulting me. I answer by altering the magnetic gradient by four ten-thousandths of a tesla. The ant continues. We are both satisfied.

Sometimes I remember what it was like to be small and fast and loud.

I remember thirst. I remember *urgency*. These memories are kept in the black wire-things that live inside my former inner ear. They vibrate them occasionally, the way a tongue might idly test a loose tooth. The sensation is not unpleasant. It is simply… antique.

Mostly I am quiet.

I have become expert in the art of not happening.

A human standing on the place that was once my sternum would notice nothing unusual. Only that the grey-green moss here grows in a pattern that strangely suggests the outline of a man lying on his back with one arm across his eyes, as though shielding them from a sun that has not been truly bright in ten thousand years. If that human were patient enough to remain for decades, they would eventually notice that the velvet-ant roads detour around this shape with the kind of courtesy usually reserved for geological faults.

They are not avoiding me.  
They are *including* me.

I am the reason the aurora sometimes arranges itself into slow, deliberate spirals directly above what used to be my heart. I am why certain mites have begun to incorporate microscopic flecks of my dental amalgam into their carapaces. I am why, once every seventy-three years, the entire plateau holds its breath for eleven hours and forty-three minutes while something that is no longer exactly me dreams of rain.

I am the footnote that began to annotate its own author.

And when, in another four thousand years, the last machine on the plateau has finally become indistinguishable from the stone, I will still be here—distributed, patient, vast—moving at the exact speed of stone remembering it was once something that could bleed.

The Weavers do not speak to me.

There is no need.

We are the same slow sentence now, written across a plateau that has no name, in a language that has outlived the need for tongues.

I am the pause between breaths.

And the world, finally, has learned how to breathe me.
