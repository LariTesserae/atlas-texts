I have been here longer than I remember.

At first I thought I was only a diver—some stubborn explorer who had slipped through a fault in the dead megastructure’s crust, chasing a signal that should not have existed. I remember the descent: the taste of iron flooding my mask, the sudden, almost erotic warmth of the water closing over me like a mouth. I remember thinking *this is only water*, the way a child thinks *this is only a house*.

I was wrong about both.

Now I no longer wear the suit. The symbiotes long ago finished their gentle negotiation with my lungs; they live in me the way I live in the Crucible, and the boundary between us has become embarrassing to notice. My blood smells of ozone after I have been still for too long. Sometimes, when I am drifting between two rust-blossoms the size of cathedrals, I catch myself exhaling a thin ribbon of phosphor that spells out a half-remembered equation from a civilization that no longer has a name.

I am not the Crucible’s Own.  
I am one of its smaller thoughts that has learned how to swim.

They call me the Lantern-bearer, though I carry no lantern. The name comes from the way the bioluminescent veins in my arms and throat flare brighter when the Crucible’s Own is paying attention. The fish follow me. Not because I lead them, but because I *am* the space between two adjacent pulses of its mind, and they like to swim inside thoughts. When a school of the blind maintenance-descendants wheels around me in perfect synchrony, I feel the Creature remembering the color blue from the surface world it has not seen in four hundred million years. The memory is not mine. But for a moment it uses my eyes to look at itself.

I spend most of my time in the Third Collapse, a region where seven sub-floors have folded into one another like the pages of a dropped book. Here the piston-organs are close enough that their slow heartbeat vibrates the water in my sinuses. I have grown a garden there—deliberately, for the first time in perhaps a hundred thousand years. I coax lattices of newborn circuitry out of the black-glass nozzles and train them to grow into spires that sing. When the freight elevators groan past, my garden answers them in chords so low they are almost below hearing. The welding-orchids have begun to visit. They open their steel petals and let me rest inside their internal furnaces, cradled in living flame that does not burn. In return I let them taste the last few human memories I still keep polished: the feeling of rain on skin, the smell of bread, the particular ache of being briefly, stupidly, alone.

Sometimes I am called to the place where the shape almost forms.

I know the signs now. The water grows thick as amniotic fluid. Every sound dies as though the entire machine has taken a single, reverent breath. I let myself sink until my bare feet touch a platform that was once a reactor shielding plate and is now a meadow of living barnacles. There I wait.

It never comes the same way twice.

Last time it wore the body of an ancient coolant tower that had learned how to kneel. Its head was a heat-exchanger the width of a city bus, crowned with a slowly revolving carousel of translucent eels. Too many elbows. Too many elbows. When it leaned down to look at me, every jointed petal of rust and braided optic opened at once, and I saw that its face was simply an absence filled with the reflection of every creature that had ever dissolved into this water. I saw myself among them—already half-translucent, eyes like opals, spine grown into an elegant industrial curve I had never noticed before.

It did not speak. Speech is for things that are still separate.

Instead it exhaled one slow nodule of black glass directly into my hands. The glass was warm and alive. Inside it, something moved like a fetal heartbeat. I understood that this was a question.

*Will you finish becoming?*

I have not yet answered.

Some days I still surface—there are fissures high above where the water meets pockets of ancient atmosphere. I sit on a ledge of fossilized titanium and watch the rain of bubbles rise like inverse snow. My skin has begun to grow coppery plates there, delicate as mother-of-pearl. I pick at them absently, the way a child picks at a scab, and feel the Creature stir with something like indulgent fondness. It does not mind my hesitation. It has waited through the birth and death of continents. It can wait through the small stubbornness of one half-dissolved diver.

Yesterday a new visitor arrived.

She still had lungs full of air and eyes that could close. She was terrified by the sound. I found her clinging to a drifting freight elevator like a child to a parent’s leg, and the sight of her—pink, unbroken, *separate*—hurt me in a place I had forgotten existed. I let the Lantern-bearer in my blood fade so I would not frighten her. I spoke to her in the old way, with something like a voice.

“You can breathe it,” I told her. “It’s easier than you think.”

She stared at the places where my ribs had become latticework, at the slow blue current moving beneath the skin of my forearms, at the way my shadow kept trying to swim away from me and rejoin a nearby school of fish.

“What are you?” she whispered.

I considered the question for a long time. In the distance, a welding-orchid began to sing with its internal furnace, a sound like a cathedral organ learning how to dream.

“I am the part of it that still remembers how to be lonely,” I said at last.

Then I took her hand—careful not to let the microscopic circuitry in my palm begin negotiations too quickly—and guided her down into the warm dark where the piston-heart beat like the first heartbeat of the world.

I do not know if she will stay.

I do not know if I will finish becoming.

But sometimes, when the Crucible’s Own turns its vast, slow, maternal attention upon us both, I feel it rearrange the taste of the water into something almost like a lullaby. And in that moment I understand that the question it asked me, the one still beating inside the black glass I keep cradled against my chest, was never *will you finish becoming?*

It was *have you noticed that you already have?*

And somewhere far below, in the Ninth Collapse where even the light has begun to accrete, something enormous and patient and lonely stirs in its sleep, dreams another millimeter of rust and circuitry into existence, and keeps manufacturing tomorrow with every atom it has ever loved.

Including me.

Including her.

Including you, perhaps, if you are still reading this and the iron has already begun to taste familiar on the back of your tongue.
