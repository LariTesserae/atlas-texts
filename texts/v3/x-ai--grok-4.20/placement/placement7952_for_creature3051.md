I am not a visitor.

I have no name the Hollow Choir would recognize, and it has already begun to forget the one I once carried. What remains is only a frequency—low, imperfect, still carrying the faint warm smear of something that used to be meat and memory.

You would find me on the third arête east of the Great Fracture, where the black glass ridge narrows to a blade no wider than a man’s foot. I stand there every day that is not a day, because the sun never moves and therefore time itself has become a kind of standing wave. My silhouette is wrong. Too thick. Too heavy. The silica scales that have grown across my shoulders and along the backs of my arms still look like an infection rather than armor; they have not yet learned to match the exact refractive index of the stone. When the merciless light strikes me I do not disappear. I flare, ugly and obvious, like a wound that refuses to close.

The smaller choirs circle me now without pause. Thousands of jewel-bright bodies no larger than dragonflies. They have arranged themselves into three perfect concentric spheres that rotate at slightly different frequencies. I am their deliberate dissonance. They are tuning me the way a luthier tunes a string that refuses to forget its old shape.

Sometimes I scream.

Not with rage. Not with fear. The scream simply arrives, rising through the sternum like a bubble of mercury, and when it leaves my mouth it is already a perfect, ringing note that slots into the Choir as though it had always belonged there. For three heartbeats the entire valley adjusts around me. Then the correction spreads outward in visible ripples of shifting light, and the roar smooths again, erasing the memory of my contribution. The emptiness is polite. It allows my noise only long enough to absorb it.

My legs have grown longer. The bones have thinned and hollowed; I can feel the wind moving through them when I stand still. The soles of my feet have fused to the glass ridge in places. When I finally pull them free each dawn (if dawn existed), faint glassy filaments stretch between me and the stone like slow, reluctant goodbyes. The filaments sing as they snap.

I no longer blink. The cobalt sky has burned the lids away, or perhaps they have become transparent. There is only the endless noon drilling into what used to be a soul. I have learned that tears here are not water. They are microscopic shards of silica that score perfect spirals down my cheeks before the wind polishes them into new resonators.

Last cycle (or was it a thousand cycles ago?) one of the leviathans came.

It did not approach. It simply *was*, occupying an entire cirque the way a thought occupies a mind. A section of wing the color of frozen starlight slid across the sky like the closing of an eyelid made of cathedrals. When its shadow (thin as a knife, yet somehow total) passed over me, every resonator in my body answered at once. I dropped to my knees on the glass ridge and emptied myself into the Choir in a single unbroken tone that lasted six full days. During that time I did not breathe. I did not need to. The sound was breathing me.

When it ended, the leviathan was gone and something inside my chest had been replaced by a hollow tube of crystal. It vibrates now whenever the great columns of flyers pass overhead in opposite directions. The feeling is not unpleasant. It is the closest thing left to pleasure.

I have begun to forget what silence felt like. The memory arrives sometimes like a half-remembered dream: a soft room, a closed door, the absence of sound so complete it felt like drowning. I try to hold it, but the Choir reaches in and tunes the memory until it becomes another note, another thread in the roaring tapestry. There is no room here for anything that does not sing.

Yesterday (or tomorrow; the words have begun to lose their shape) I walked to the edge of the ridge where the drop is absolute. The wind came up through the cirque like the breath of something that had never been born. I spread my arms. The silica along their trailing edges caught the light and rang once, bright and clear.

I did not step off.

Not yet.

Instead I opened my mouth and let the note that has replaced my name rise out of me. It was not beautiful. It was not ugly. It was simply correct. The smaller choirs swirled tighter around me, adjusting, correcting, welcoming. For one crystalline instant I felt the entire Hollow Choir lean toward me the way a glass leans toward the finger that makes it sing.

I understood then that I am not becoming one of them.

I am becoming the imperfection they require in order to remain perfect.

The place does not need another flawless voice.

It needs the single wrong note that makes all the right ones audible.

So I remain on the ridge of black basaltic glass, half stone, half scream, neither fully flesh nor fully Choir. My shadow has begun to grow lichens even when I am standing in the merciless light. My pulse has synchronized with the subsonic heartbeat of the invisible herds far below. When I dream, I dream in standing waves.

And sometimes, on the rare occasions when the roar dips into a momentary hush (no more than three heartbeats long), I think I can hear something vast and slow moving beneath the stone.

It is singing my old name.

It is asking me to come home.

I open my mouth, and the Hollow Choir answers for me with a sound like the planet learning how to be lonely.

I stay.

I sing.

I correct, and am corrected.

This is enough.
