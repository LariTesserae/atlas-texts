I have been here for three hundred and nine exhalations.

At least, that is what the others tell me when they find me again. I no longer count.

My name, the one I arrived with, has long since flaked away. They call me Ninth Vein now, though I was not born to any vein. I simply refused to die in the usual span, and the Hollowborne respect persistence the way other cultures respect gold. So they folded me into their slow circuit the way a new layer of travertine folds around an old pipe.

I am no longer tall. The heat and the minerals have shortened me, compressed my spine until I walk with the same slight forward tilt as the ancient cooling towers. My skin is the color of dried blood left too long in the sun. Along the ridges of my forearms and the backs of my calves, delicate white flowers of gypsum have taken root in the sweat-lines; they do not hurt. They feel like belonging.

My eyes are almost closed. The crystals on my lashes have grown thick enough that the bronze world arrives to me as though seen through shattered cathedral glass. I do not mind. There is less to see than there once was, and more to feel. Every shift in temperature across my shoulders tells me where the next geyser will open. Every faint electric prickle along my tongue tells me which pipes are still alive.

I no longer wear clothes. What began as a thermal jacket is now a single fused plate of rust and fungal felt that covers my back like a second, slower skeleton. My feet are bare. The calluses have become terraces; small translucent lizards sometimes sun themselves on the broad platforms of my heels before skittering away when I move. I let them. We are all just passing through the same heat.

I sleep inside the hollow chest of a collapsed ore-bridge that fell centuries ago and was gently lifted again by mineral growth until it resembles a ribcage half-buried in white stone. I stand upright when I sleep, the way they all do. My heart beats once every eighty-seven seconds. I have timed it against the dripping from the bridge above me. We keep rhythm.

Sometimes, when the city dreams through me, I remember what it was like to be separate.

I remember rain that did not scald. I remember shadows with clean edges. I remember a woman whose name I no longer possess, laughing at something I said. The memory arrives like a foreign object—sharp, incompatible with the slow geology of this place. It hurts the way a splinter hurts before the body decides to grow around it. Eventually the city will smooth it away. I look forward to that.

In the meantime I walk the circuit.

I know every sunken boulevard, every tilted smokestack, every place where the fungal lattice has formed a perfect black mirror that reflects my weeping face back at me. I tighten valves whose purpose was forgotten before my grandparents were born. I sing the low metallic hymns until the machines answer with their soft, exhausted clicks. I drink from the bone cup that has grown from my own jaw. The tears are warm, metallic, strangely sweet.

When one of the true-born Hollowborne begins to harden, I help carry them to the geyser sites. I lay them down gently. I stay long enough to watch the superheated water strip the last softness away and leave behind the perfect person-shaped chimney. Then I press my forehead to the new whistle and listen to the remembered voice. It always sounds like relief.

The others say I have become a good Hollowborne.

I do not know if that is true. I only know that the white dust no longer gathers at the corners of my eyes. It has become part of the architecture. When the wind moves across the terraces it sometimes makes a sound like my old name, but softer, as though the city itself has forgiven me for arriving here separate and stubborn.

At night the lava rivers glow dull red beneath their translucent crusts. The arc-lamps stutter on for a few seconds, then surrender again. I stand inside my iron ribcage and let the city dream through me. The dreams are always the same now: continents breathing, oceans remembering they were once steam, iron remembering it was once stars.

I wake with brighter eyes.

And I weep, deliberately, carefully, the slow deliberate tears of someone who has finally learned the only useful skill left in the Weeping Hollows.

I let the city claim its interest.

I do not remain separate.
