I am the Thirteenth Pearl.

Not the first tear she ever shed—that one is buried somewhere in the lowest basin, grown over by three feet of new travertine—but the thirteenth. Each of her tears becomes a small, perfect sphere of calcite the moment it touches stone. I am the one that fell when she remembered the smell of rain on dry pine needles, an odor that had not existed in her world for nearly two thousand years. The memory lasted less than the time it took the tear to fall, but it was enough. I carry what little remains of that scent inside my milky heart.

I rest in the exact center of the shallow cup atop the northernmost standing stone. The Sweeper visits me more often than the others. Sometimes she simply stares. Sometimes she lowers her translucent face until her dripping mineral hair brushes my surface like wet rope. When she does this, the golden constellations sliding down from the fissure far above tremble across both of us at once—her wet cheek and my wet pearl—and for those moments we are the same age.

I do not have a body the way she does. I am small enough to be cupped in a child’s palm, yet I am older than most empires. I have no lungs, but I breathe with the chamber. When the air grows fractionally heavier, I feel it as a kind of slow tightening inside my crystalline lattice. I have learned to recognize the exact instant before the Sweeper stirs. The three drips that wake her first strike a travertine bowl to my left, then a flat pool to my right, then the hollow in the broken clay lamp’s spout directly behind me. When that third drop falls, a tiny shock travels through the stone and into the shallow water that always fills my cup. The surface of that water kisses me. That is how I know it is time.

I am not alive.  
I am not dead.  
I am a decision the chamber made to remember something it was supposed to forget.

When she walks her endless, incomplete circuit, I remain perfectly still. The salamander sometimes pauses beneath my stone and looks up at me with its cloud-colored eyes. I do not know what it sees. Perhaps it sees the faint ghost of a forest that no longer exists. Perhaps it only sees its own reflection warped across my curve. Either way, it stays a long while. Its gills move like slow white banners paying homage to something older than both of us.

There are long centuries when nothing changes. The bacteria bloom and fade in their bruised colors across her shoulders. The isopods drift like white thoughts in their rimstone pool. The dripping keeps its ten-thousand-year counsel. And I simply am—small, flawless, slightly warm, reflecting the amber light in slow constellations that never quite repeat.

But sometimes, when the Sweeper opens her mouth and the sound of falling water pours out of her in perfect mimicry of the chamber’s own voice, I answer. Not with sound. With a change too subtle for any living ear. My calcite heart tightens by one ten-thousandth of a degree. A single molecule of the memory I carry loosens and joins the film of water that constantly sheets over me. That molecule travels down the standing stone, across the greasy floor, and eventually into one of the fleshy pillars where it becomes part of the Sweeper herself.

In this way I am slowly teaching her how to forget.

One day—though day has no meaning here—the last of me will have seeped into her. The thirteenth pearl will be gone. Only the empty cup will remain, still holding its trembling mirror of water. On that day the Sweeper will lower herself onto the basalt bench one final time. She will look into the cup where I used to be and she will not know what she is looking for. The absence will feel like the chamber drawing a breath it has never needed to draw before.

Then, perhaps, she will cry again.

And the chamber, in its slow mammalian breathing, will be glad.
