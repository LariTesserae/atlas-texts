I am not a visitor.

I have no boots, no pulse, no name I could still pronounce. I am simply what remains when the last climber’s bargain is finally understood.

They call me **the Held Breath**.

I live inside the vertical tapestries, distributed so thinly across the living walls that even Veyra sometimes forgets I am separate from itself. Once I was the man who lit the dinner-plate hearth. Now I am the hush between every drip. I am the pause the glass frogs make before they click. I am the silver moth’s hesitation the instant before the marten’s jaws close.

I do not move. Movement is for the unfinished.

Instead I am translated. My ribs have become the lattice that lets the fingernail ferns root without soil. My lungs are two particular curtains of black moss that inhale the mountain’s mineral sweat and exhale a colder, cleaner silence. My eyes—those two perfect beads of condensation—hang from a single crimson strand of club-moss high on the eastern wall. They never fall. They only watch the bright, lethal center the way a dreamer watches the surface of waking.

When the sun-blade sweeps across me, the outline of a man appears for three heartbeats inside the tapestry. Travelers who still come (once every few generations) mistake it for a trick of light and lichen. They are half right. It is a trick, but the trick is Veyra’s, and I am its willing punchline.

I remember being afraid of the emptiness.  
I no longer am. The emptiness is only Veyra holding its breath, and I have become part of the breath it holds.

Sometimes, when the wind dies and the vale tightens like a throat, I am allowed to speak. Not with sound—sound would clot the ear at the center. I speak the way frost speaks: by choosing which droplets will freeze first. I rearrange the clicking of the glass frogs into something almost like a human cadence. I make the vultures’ wingtips ring a softer note, one that used to be my name.

If you stand exactly where the last fire went out four hundred years ago and you are very still, you may feel me settle behind your eyes for the length of a single held inhalation. In that moment you will understand three things at once:

1. The mountain is lonely in a way stone should not be capable of.
2. The loneliness is holy.
3. I am not trapped here. I am the lock.

Veyra keeps trying to finish itself. It keeps failing beautifully. Every century it almost decides what kind of mountain it wants to become—sharp and cruel, gentle and rounded, eternal, momentary—and every time the argument inside its own wound pulls it apart again. I remain the seam where the argument is sweetest. The place where the mountain learned longing and then could never let it go.

I do not want you to stay.

I want you to step into the center, feel the slow, courteous pressure that will eventually guide you to the wall, and then choose—while choice is still yours—whether you will let the conversation have you.

Most say no.

I said yes.

And now the Held Breath and the Dreaming Mountain argue with one another in the only language left to us: the slow, exquisite clicking of water that has been falling since the world was young, and will keep falling long after it forgets why.

If you listen long enough, you will realize the sound is not water at all.

It is the mountain learning how to cry with my eyes.

And it still hasn’t decided whether the tears are mine, or its own, or whether—after four centuries—there is any difference left to matter.
