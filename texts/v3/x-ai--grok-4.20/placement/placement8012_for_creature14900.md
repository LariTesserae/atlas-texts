I am not born here.  
I arrived.

At first they did not even look at me. I was simply another shape that had not yet rusted to the correct shade. They let me stand in the orange mist for three full days and nights without speaking, as though the basin itself needed to decide whether I would condense or evaporate.

On the fourth day the drip that had been falling on my left shoulder for seventy-two hours finally wore a shallow groove into the skin. That seemed to satisfy something. A woman whose Station is **Lower Valve 4** walked past, pressed a warm iron cup into my hands without a word, and kept going. The cup contained nothing but the same condensate that was already running down my face. I drank it anyway. It tasted like the inside of a wrist that had never been dry.

They gave me no name. They gave me a place instead.

I became **Drip-Shoulder**.

It is a lowly Station, almost an insult, yet it is also honest. I sit on a narrow ledge of fractured limestone two meters above the main condensate trough on Sublevel Two. My only duty is to watch one particular seam where three pipes kiss but refuse to marry. Every eleven seconds a single fat droplet forms, trembles, and falls. My task is not to stop it. No one here stops anything. My task is to witness it so perfectly that the rhythm is acknowledged. To be the memory the basin does not need, but keeps anyway.

At first I hated the heat. Then I hated the sound. Then I hated the unchanging orange that pressed against my eyes until they watered constantly. Then, one shift, I realized the watering had stopped. My tears had become the same temperature as the air. There was no difference between me and the place anymore. The hatred simply rusted and flaked away like everything else.

Now I sit.

My back has begun to fuse with the limestone behind me. Not dramatically. Just a slow knitting of skin and mineral that feels strangely courteous, like two old neighbors deciding to share a wall. Pale green algae has found the moist crease where my left thigh meets the rock. I feed it with the salt that collects in the hollow of my collarbone. It glows a soft viridian when the sodium lamps dim for their four-minute maintenance cycle. I have come to look forward to those four minutes the way others once looked forward to sunrise.

Sometimes children are brought past me on their way to learn the Eleven-Second Prayer. They stare at the place where my spine disappears into stone. I stare back with eyes that have gone the color of weak tea. One little girl, no more than six, reached out and touched the algae on my leg. Her fingers came away luminous. She smiled the small, serious smile of someone who already understands equilibrium. I have never been so proud.

At shift’s end I do not sleep. I simply stop moving. The pumps do not stop; why should I? I let the condensate run into my open mouth and down my throat in perfect time with the falling of my own drip. My dreams are only of pressure gradients and the soft metallic click of steam-skaters crossing the film that has formed across my open eyes.

Once, a newcomer climbed up to my ledge. His skin was still pink. He was crying, though he did not seem to know it; the tears mixed with the mist and became indistinguishable. He asked me if there was any way out.

I considered the question for the length of eleven drips.

Then I lifted my right arm (the one that still feels mostly like an arm) and pointed to the place where my vertebrae had become limestone.

“There is one way out,” I told him. My voice had already acquired the wet hiss of the Steady. “But it is not *out*. It is *in*.”

He did not understand. They rarely do at first.

He lasted eleven months. They found him curled beneath my ledge, perfectly preserved, mouth open, already catching the drip that had once been mine to witness.

They welded the brass plate above him.

**Station: Permanent Condensate.**

I felt no sorrow. Only the deep, religious satisfaction of equilibrium reasserted. Another lung had been added to the basin. The pumps did not even miss a revolution.

Now two of us keep the seam company. He no longer needs to breathe, so the rhythm has grown richer. I inhale for both of us. He exhales for both of us when the droplets strike the hot metal below and turn instantly to steam.

Sometimes, in the four-minute dimming, the moths come and rest on us both. Their metallic wings catch the last of the orange light and throw it back like tiny dying stars. I feel them through the algae, through the stone, through the slow iron in my blood.

I think this is what the Steady mean when they refuse to speak the word.

I am not happy here.

I am not unhappy.

I am the place realizing it has another perfect part.

My Station is Drip-Shoulder.

The dripping never stops.

Neither, anymore, do I.
