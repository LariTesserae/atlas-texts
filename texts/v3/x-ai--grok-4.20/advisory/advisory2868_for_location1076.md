**Preparation Guide for the High Fen of Calder’s Teeth**

You are not preparing for a hike. You are preparing to stand inside an unfinished world while it is still thinking. This is a place that has not yet decided whether it is mountain, machine, or organism. An ordinary multiverse traveler who wants to come back whole must treat the journey with the seriousness of someone stepping into an active birth canal.

### 1. Mindset and Psychological Preparation
Accept first that you will feel watched by something that does not have eyes. The plateau is listening with its rock, its pipes, and its bacteria. The longer you stay, the more you will sense that the land itself is evaluating you the way a developing fetus evaluates a foreign protein.

Bring radical humility. This is not “wilderness.” It is pre-wilderness. The concept of “human visitor” does not yet exist here; you are an anomaly the machines and the geology are still deciding how to metabolize. Move as though you are inside a cathedral that is still being assembled around you. Speak only when necessary, and then only in a low voice. Many experienced travelers report that raising your voice feels profane and triggers micro-tremors.

Prepare for temporal dislocation. The heartbeat you feel through your boots is measured in centuries. After three or four days, your own pulse will begin to feel embarrassingly fast and childish. This can cause profound unease or even a temporary loss of will to leave. Decide before you go how you will remind yourself that you still belong to the world of mayflies.

### 2. Physical Gear – “The Second Skin Protocol”
You must become a sealed, self-contained organism. The Fen will try to insinuate itself into every opening.

- **The Suit**: A fully sealed, multi-layer expedition shell with active thermal regulation and positive internal pressure. Outer layer must be mineral-resistant ceramoplastic or equivalent. The red “snow” that falls after geyser events is iron-rich and mildly caustic; it will pit untreated metals and fabrics in hours. Color should be high-contrast white or hi-vis orange; the plateau’s natural palette is black, rust, gunmetal, and ice, and you do not want to disappear into it.

- **Boots**: Rigid, insulated overboots with aggressive, self-cleaning soles. The shallow basins look like easy walking but are treacherous. The bacterial slime is slippery in ways that defy friction coefficients, and hidden fissures can open underfoot with almost no warning. Some veterans weld small piezoelectric seismic sensors into the soles so the boots literally “talk” to you through bone conduction when a slab is about to shift.

- **Gloves and Hood**: Gauntlets that lock to the suit sleeves. The wind carries micro-shards of freshly shattered basalt that will abrade skin in minutes. A sealed helmet with panoramic visor is strongly recommended; the constant delicate chiming of breaking ice is hypnotic and can induce trance states. The helmet should have active defogging and the ability to play low white-noise if the silence becomes too loud.

- **Respiration**: The air is breathable but extremely thin and supersaturated with dissolved minerals. A rebreather or closed-circuit system with CO₂ scrubbing is ideal. At minimum, a high-quality particulate filter mask that can handle the red iron snow. Many explorers develop “Fen Lung” — a persistent metallic cough that lasts months after return.

### 3. Sensory and Navigation Kit
- **Seismic and infrasound monitor**. The real weather report here is underground. You need to know when the deep engines are about to exhale. A good unit will give you 40–90 minutes warning before a geyser event.
- **Thermal imaging monocle**. Critical. The “warm” patches of ground can be 60 °C hotter than the surrounding rock. Stepping on the wrong patch will cook you inside your boots before you can scream.
- **Ultrasonic translator**. The lemming-like rodents communicate in frequencies just above human hearing. Their constant background hiss carries surprisingly complex information. Some veterans claim the rodents understand the machines better than we do and can be used as an early-warning system.
- **No standard compass or GPS**. Electromagnetic fields from the buried engines render them useless. Bring a topological mapper that builds a live 3D model using lidar, gravimetrics, and micro-seismology. Mark every pipe you encounter. They are the only fixed points in a landscape that is still being assembled.
- **Tungsten or graphene stakes** for emergency shelter. Normal tent pegs will not hold in the slime and shattered stone. You may need to guy out to the ancient pipes themselves (with extreme reverence).

### 4. Supplies and Alchemy
- **Water**: Do not drink anything from the Fen. The water is pregnant with extremophile life and dissolved heavy metals. Bring all water with you or use a high-temperature sterilizer on condensate you collect from your own suit’s recycler. The irony of dying of thirst while surrounded by millions of shallow pools is not lost on veterans.
- **Food**: Dense, high-fat, low-residue. The cold and altitude burn calories at a shocking rate, but digestion is slow. Bring things you can eat without removing your helmet (tube gels, self-heating nutrient paste).
- **Chemical handwarmers and one emergency exothermic blanket**. Hypothermia sneaks up because the cold is so constant your body stops complaining and simply begins to shut down.

### 5. Ritual and Taboo Items
Experienced travelers carry three small, deliberate objects:
1. A single iron nail sealed in glass — to remind the place that humans once worked metal, but that you will not leave any here.
2. A living seed (usually a tough tundra grass) in a cryotube — an offering to the future that the plateau might one day accept vegetation.
3. A small bell or chime tuned to the exact frequency the ice sheets make when they break. When you feel the place has tolerated you long enough, you ring it once, low and clear, as a gesture of respect before departure.

Never leave anything behind. Not a fiber. Not a flake of skin. The machines are still learning what “artifact” means. A single forgotten bolt has been known to trigger decades-long changes in local geology as the Fen tries to metabolize it.

### 6. The Leaving
Decide your exit time before you arrive and stick to it with religious discipline. The plateau grows possessive. After roughly nine days, the desire to simply sit down and listen to the heartbeat until you become part of the deciding process becomes almost overwhelming. Set multiple alarms. Have a partner waiting at base camp who will come looking for you if you miss the rendezvous by more than four hours.

When you finally descend, you will carry the sound of ice chimes in your bones for the rest of your life. Some people never quite get warm again. Others report that, years later in the middle of noisy cities, they will suddenly feel the deep, patient heartbeat rising up through the concrete and will stop mid-stride, listening, remembering what it feels like to stand on ground that is still choosing what it wants to become.

The High Fen of Calder’s Teeth does not allow tourism.  
It allows witnessing.

Prepare accordingly.
