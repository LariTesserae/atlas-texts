**Preparation Guide for the Underwet**

If you are an ordinary multiverse traveler (baseline human physiology, no innate environmental resistances), treat the Underwet as you would a long-duration voyage into a tropical deep-mine combined with a poorly ventilated greenhouse that has been running nonstop for decades. This is not a place you “visit.” It is a place you *endure* while it slowly decides whether to incorporate you into its gestation.

### 1. Physical and Medical Preparation (do this before descent)
- **Hydration and electrolyte management**: The air is so saturated that your sweat cannot evaporate. You will lose fluid through respiration and dripping skin faster than you expect. Pack 5–6 liters of electrolyte solution per 24-hour period, plus salt tablets. Your body will feel constantly clammy and feverish; this is normal.
- **Heat acclimatization**: Spend at least two weeks training in 38–42 °C (100–108 °F) environments with 95–100 % humidity before you go. Your core temperature will sit at the edge of heat exhaustion the entire time you are down there.
- **Lung and sinus protection**: The air is thick with fine limestone dust, mold spores, and mineral aerosols. A full-face respirator with P3/HEPA filters and an anti-fog coating is mandatory for the first 48 hours until your body adjusts. After that many people switch to a half-mask, but never remove all filtration.
- **Skin**: Every inch of exposed skin will stay wet. Apply medical-grade antifungal powder and zinc-oxide barrier cream *before* you descend. Reapply every four hours. Trench-foot and various maceration infections set in fast if you are careless.
- **Immunization & microbiome**: Get the strongest broad-spectrum fungal and bacterial prophylaxis your universe offers. The Underwet’s biosphere is gentle but extremely foreign; the translucent millipedes and milk eels carry their own commensal organisms.

### 2. Clothing and Personal Kit
- **Base layer**: Merino wool or synthetic underwear designed for constant wetness (think high-end caving or jungle warfare gear). Nothing cotton—ever.
- **Outer layer**: Lightweight, quick-dry synthetic coveralls in dark rust or ochre colors so the inevitable staining doesn’t drive you insane. Seal every cuff and collar with waterproof tape. Add a thin waterproof over-suit for the first few days if you bruise easily; the constant dripping from the ceiling feels like being gently beaten with warm wet fingers.
- **Footwear**: High-ankle rubber boots with steel toes and thick drainage holes. The water is only ankle-to-waist deep, but the bottom is a mixture of fine silt, abandoned bolts, and sleeping eels. You will be wading constantly.
- **Head protection**: A hard hat with a mounted lamp (sodium or warm-white LED, never cool white—it changes the whole emotional temperature of the place). Add a wide-brimmed waterproof hat over it to deflect ceiling drip.
- **Eyes**: Anti-fog swimming goggles or sealed safety glasses at all times. The mist and insect scales will coat unprotected eyes within minutes.

### 3. Gear You Must Carry
- **Moisture management**: Three changes of socks sealed in vacuum bags, a small ultraviolet dryer pod, and desiccant packs that you will never actually use—the Underwet defeats them all. Bring them anyway for psychological comfort.
- **Lighting**: Primary headlamp + two redundant waterproof torches. The sodium lamps are sparse and devotional; you will need your own light to read the faded safety markings on pipes and to avoid stepping on the bioluminescent eels (they startle beautifully when touched).
- **Sound management**: High-quality earplugs with a low-pass filter. The constant sub-sonic heartbeat and dripping will burrow into your mind after 30 hours. You need the ability to carve out silence.
- **Insect interaction kit**: The pale moths and millipedes are not hostile, but they *will* land on you, crawl into every crevice, and sleep on your shoulders if you remain still. Bring a soft silicone brush and a lightweight mesh head net for sleeping. Most travelers eventually stop caring and simply let the insects share their body heat.
- **Navigation and emergency**: The walkways disappear into the lake without warning. Carry a waterproof topographic slate, a powerful waterproof radio beacon, and a 300 m length of floating polypropylene line. If you go into the deeper sumps or the submerged tram tunnels, clip the line to every railing you pass.
- **Psychological kit**: A small, tightly sealed journal and pencil. The Underwet’s “gestational” atmosphere has a documented effect on long-term visitors—many begin to feel they are *inside* something that is thinking. Writing helps keep your sense of self separate from the breathing of the basin.

### 4. Behavioral Rules and Mindset
- Move slowly. The entire place operates on geological time. Rushing raises your body temperature and annoys the pumps.
- Never stand directly under a drip line for more than a minute. The ceiling has been known to release sudden warm deluges when pressure shifts.
- Do not try to “dry out” any part of the environment. You cannot. The machines keep the water warm; the water keeps the machines alive. Fighting this symbiosis only exhausts you.
- If you stand perfectly still in the lukewarm lake for ten minutes, the basin will inhale. You will feel the pressure change in your sinuses and the air grow momentarily thicker. Breathe with it. Fighting the rhythm is what causes headaches.
- The insects are part of the place. The milk eels will thread between your legs like curious cats. Accept them. The moment you show fear or disgust, the gentle indifference of the Underwet can shift toward something heavier.

### 5. Exit Strategy
Plan for no less than 72 hours and no more than 10 days. After roughly two weeks the boundary between your skin and the humidity begins to blur. People who stay longer start speaking in the slow, sighing cadence of the valves and sometimes refuse to leave the warm water.

Bring a powerful extraction winch, a dry change of clothes waiting in the descent shaft, and a warm blanket. The first breath of cooler, drier air after leaving the Underwet feels like being born a second time—raw, shocking, and strangely sad.

The Underwet is not hostile.  
It is patient, humid, rust-colored, and ancient in a way that has nothing to do with age.  
It is waiting to see what *you* will become while you are inside it.

Pack accordingly.  
Breathe accordingly.  
And when the basin inhales, inhale with it.
