**How to Prepare for Visiting the Underbasin**

You are not preparing for a cave, a ruin, or even a straightforward machine. You are preparing to step inside a single, ancient, self-sustaining organ that has forgotten it was ever built. Think of it as boarding the warm, dripping interior of a cathedral-sized creature that breathes through pipes and dreams in mineral. The following is what your body, mind, and kit actually need.

### Mental and Emotional Preparation
- Accept that the place is *intimate*. There is almost no open space; everything is touching, dripping on, or crawling over something else. Claustrophobes will feel it in their sternum within ten minutes.
- The sound is constant and layered. After two hours the metallic dripping and the pipe-crickets’ grinding chorus can push some people into a strange, trance-like irritation or even auditory hallucination. Decide in advance whether you will treat the noise as music or as an immune system trying to expel you.
- Time loses meaning. The light never changes. The surges come every few hours but there is no dawn or dusk. Bring a watch or timer you can glance at, or you may stay far longer than is healthy.

### Physiological Preparation
The environment is 31–34 °C with near-100 % humidity and zero airflow. Your body will treat the Underbasin as a fever it cannot break.

- **Heat acclimatization**: Spend at least a week training in a sauna or steam room wearing rain gear. You must be able to function while drenched in sweat that never evaporates.
- **Electrolytes and hydration**: You will lose water faster than you can comfortably drink it. Pack 4–5 liters of water with dissolving electrolyte tabs (high sodium and potassium). Drink constantly even when not thirsty.
- **Skin and mucous membranes**: The air is thick with spores, algae, and fermented organic mist. Bring medical-grade antifungal powder, barrier cream for feet and groin, and saline nasal spray. Without them you will develop macerated skin and sinus infections within a day.
- **Lungs**: The air is warm but not oxygen-poor; however the constant aerosol of minerals and mold can trigger bronchial irritation. A light cloth mask or bandana soaked in menthol helps.

### Clothing and Gear (minimalist but specific)
- **Base layer**: Synthetic or merino underwear that dries fast and fights fungus. Nothing cotton.
- **Outer layer**: A thin, long-sleeved “jungle” shirt and pants in a pale color (to match the ceramic and make the isopods easier to see). The fabric must be quick-dry and treated with permethrin (even though there are no mosquitoes, the chemical repels some of the more aggressive isopods).
- **Footwear**: Ankle-high neoprene booties or “canyoneering” boots with excellent drainage and aggressive grip. The floors are slick with travertine, algae, and cricket feces. Bring two pairs and rotate them; one will always be drying.
- **Hands**: Thin nitrile-dipped work gloves. The pipe-crickets bite when startled and their mandibles can draw blood.
- **Light**: Do *not* bring a bright flashlight unless you want to trigger panic across the entire food web. Instead bring two headlamps with a warm 2700 K setting and a red-light mode. The bioluminescent bacteria are sensitive; strong white light makes them dim for hours afterward.
- **Sound management**: High-quality earplugs with a “music” setting (they cut the painful frequencies of the crickets but still let you hear dripping and voices). Some travelers actually use noise-canceling earbuds playing soft pink noise to stay sane.

### Kit That Actually Matters
- A compact tarp or large silicone-coated groundsheet. There is almost nowhere dry to sit. You will want to create your own small “island.”
- Telescoping walking stick with a soft rubber foot. The three-dimensional labyrinth of troughs and bridges is treacherous; many of the walkways are only a hand wide and slick.
- Small collection of zip-lock bags, silica gel packets, and a tiny battery-powered dehumidifier puck. Anything paper or fabric you bring in will mildew in hours.
- A small spray bottle of 70 % isopropyl alcohol and a microfiber cloth — for cleaning the constant condensation off lenses, watch faces, and your own skin when it starts to feel greasy.
- Protein bars and salty snacks. The air itself smells like sweet-rot fermentation; after a while it suppresses appetite. You must force calories.
- A compact emergency blanket (reflective side out) — not for cold, but to create a small micro-climate when you need to cool your core temperature after hours of sweating.

### Biological Awareness
- The pale isopods are mostly harmless but will investigate any open wound.
- The pipe-crickets are territorial. If one begins singing directly above you, move. Their metallic stridulation at close range can cause temporary tinnitus.
- The fat blind salamanders are surprisingly fast in water. Do not put bare hands into any basin deeper than a few centimeters; they have been known to bite fingers hard enough to draw blood when they mistake them for prey.
- The pink mold is beautiful but appears to be mildly psychotropic if inhaled in high concentration for many hours. Keep sessions under six hours on the first few visits.

### Ritual Preparation
Many experienced multiverse travelers treat the Underbasin like a temple that demands respect:

1. Before entry, stand quietly for five minutes and match your breathing to the rhythm of the drips.
2. Carry a small ceramic tile (hand-cast, dinner-plate size if possible) as an offering. Leave it in one of the empty troughs. The locals say the machine remembers every tile that is returned to it.
3. Never kill anything. The ecology is ferocious but perfectly balanced; removing even a few isopods can cause a visible cascade within hours.
4. When you leave, strip completely in an antechamber, wash with fresh water and mild antifungal soap, and dry thoroughly before returning to any drier climate. The Underbasin likes to colonize travelers.

### Final Truth
You are not visiting a place.  
You are choosing, for a little while, to live inside the slow, warm, dripping bloodstream of an ancient machine that has become an ecosystem. It does not care about you. It will not kill you quickly. But if you are not meticulous, it will slowly, patiently, deliciously digest you—first your clothes, then your skin, then your sense of linear time—until you feel like just another warm, wet component that has always belonged there.

Pack light.  
Stay moist.  
Listen harder than you look.  
And never forget that the Underbasin is still, in its deepest ceramic bones, a machine performing the task it was built for, even if it no longer knows what that task once was.

It continues.  
So must you.
