**How to Prepare for the Hollow Choir**

You are not preparing for a mountain, a wilderness, or even a planet.  
You are preparing to step into a place that has already reached its final chord and has no interest in you. That knowledge must sit in your bones before you arrive.

### 1. Mental & Spiritual Preparation (Most Important)
- Spend at least two weeks before departure practicing **radical acceptance of irrelevance**. The Choir does not care whether you live, die, understand, or are moved by it. Many travelers who arrive with romantic or spiritual expectations break inside their first six hours. Meditate daily on the phrase: “I am a temporary vibration in a completed throat.”
- Train yourself to endure unbroken, overwhelming sound for long periods. Use noise generators that simulate permanent 95–110 dB layered cacophony (metallic shrieks over subsonic drumming over rhythmic stone impacts). Start at thirty minutes and work up to four hours without ear protection. Your nervous system must learn not to flinch.
- Decide in advance what “enough” means. The Choir has no peaks to summit, no temple at the center, no narrative reward. You must be able to leave while still feeling unfinished, because the place itself is finished.

### 2. Physical & Medical Preparation
- **Acclimatization protocol**: Begin a 21-day staged altitude program ending at no less than 6,000 m (19,700 ft) equivalent. The basaltic ridge sits higher than that in thin, cold air. Pulmonary and cerebral edema are real risks because the cold masks the hypoxia.
- Cardiovascular training must be brutal. You will be moving across knife-edge arêtes with a pack while breathing half the oxygen you’re used to. Train with a 25 kg pack on a treadmill set at 12–15 % incline inside a cold chamber.
- Get a full ENT workup. The constant sonic pressure can cause barotrauma, tinnitus that never leaves, or even spontaneous rupture of the eardrum if you are unlucky. Some travelers choose to have custom-molded bone-conduction comms implanted so they can still hear themselves think.
- Your eyes will be under assault from unfiltered UV and constant specular reflection off black glass and silica wings. Have photokeratitis (“snow blindness”) treatment ready and carry multiple pairs of high-end glacier goggles with side shields and interchangeable lenses (deep amber for general use, almost black for the brightest hours).

### 3. Gear – Ruthlessly Minimalist & Sonic-Aware
- **Clothing**: Three-layer modular system in matte black, charcoal, and rust red only. Any bright color becomes obscene against the completed stone. Everything must be wind-proof to 140 km/h gusts. Use fabrics that do not whistle or flap; even the smallest vibration adds to the Choir and feels like sacrilege.
- **Boots**: Custom triple-density shank with replaceable microspikes and a silent tread compound. You will walk on glass-polished slopes where a single scrape of metal on stone feels like screaming in a cathedral.
- **Hearing**: You have two choices—embrace the roar or try to dampen it. Most veterans use tunable active noise-canceling earpieces that can selectively reduce specific frequency bands (so you can still hear the subsonic herd pulses that warn of rockfall while softening the glass-bird shrieks). Never go fully deaf; the sudden silence when you remove protection is psychologically worse than the noise.
- **Water**: Carry a collapsible titanium still and a wide-spectrum UV/chemical purifier. Surface water is rare and the condensation that beads on every surface is your main source. Each drop already sings as it falls; you will learn to time your sips to the rhythm of the drip.
- **Shelter**: A modular carbon-fiber and aerogel bivouac pod that can be wedged into fissures. It must be perfectly silent when assembled. Any creak or slap of fabric will feel like an act of violence against the equilibrium. Most experienced travelers eventually stop using tents altogether and simply sleep in stone alcoves wrapped in insulated cloaks, letting the Choir sing them unconscious.
- **Navigation**: Visual triangulation only. Compasses are useless (the magnetic signature of the basaltic glass is chaotic). Satellite or orbital positioning may not exist depending on which universe layer you arrive from. Learn to read the fixed sun’s merciless angle and the layered flight strata of the silica-wing flocks as living contour lines.
- **Light discipline**: No artificial lights after your first night. The eye quickly adapts to the over-exposed cobalt world. Using a headlamp feels like stabbing the landscape.

### 4. Psychological Kit
- A single small object of profound personal meaning (a pebble from your childhood home, a letter from someone dead, etc.). The Choir will try to hollow you out. You will need one hard, private note that cannot be harmonized with its roar.
- A journal with waterproof, tear-proof pages. Many travelers stop writing words and begin drawing the negative shapes between the flying constellations. Some never write again after leaving.

### 5. Ritual & Etiquette
- Do not speak for the first 24 hours after arrival. Your voice will feel like vomit in a perfect mouth.
- Never throw anything. Not a crumb, not a pebble, not waste. The landscape is finished; any addition is violence.
- When you decide to leave, do not look back from the ridge. The urge to return is legendary and almost always fatal. The Choir does not release its listeners easily.

### What It Will Feel Like
After three days the cold moves from your skin into your thoughts.  
After seven days you stop narrating your own experience and simply become another vibrating surface.  
After fourteen days some people report that the sound is no longer external. It feels as though your skeleton has always been a tuning fork and the planet is only now striking it correctly.

Most people leave changed, quieter, and slightly harder, as though a thin layer of black glass has grown over some inner surface.

A few never leave.  
Not because they die—though some do—but because they realize they have always been one of the unseen herds moving along invisible ledges far below, and the ridge was only a brief interruption in a song that had already chosen them.

Pack light.  
Stay respectful.  
And remember: you are not here to sing.  
You are here to be sung.
