**How to Prepare for a Visit to the Mossfoundries**

Pack as though you are going on a long, damp pilgrimage into a place that is simultaneously a machine shop, a rainforest, a cathedral, and a living organism that has not yet finished being born. The Mossfoundries does not tolerate haste, noise, or dryness. It will teach you patience whether you want to learn or not.

### Physical Preparation

**Clothing and Protection**  
Bring loose, layered garments in shades of charcoal, moss-green, rust, and wet slate. Everything you wear will eventually be colonized by moisture and microscopic life; choose natural fibers that can breathe and that will not rot too quickly (linen, hemp, light merino wool). A waxed-canvas or oilskin coat is essential; the condensation here is relentless and the brief rains feel strangely metallic.  

Pack two pairs of sturdy, high-ankle boots with aggressive tread. The ground is uneven basalt ribs, slick moss blankets, and tangled roots that act like living cables. The iron walkways become treacherously slippery when the vents exhale. Bring thick wool socks and several changes; nothing stays dry for long.  

A wide-brimmed hat or hood is useful not just for rain but to keep the constant dripping from running down your neck. The locals wear close-fitting caps of treated felt that look like they grew from the same moss as the trees.

**Respiratory and Sensory Gear**  
The air is cool (seldom above 14 °C / 57 °F) but heavy with ozone, iron, chlorophyll, and the faint electrical tang that precedes each piston stroke. Bring a light scarf or neck gaiter you can pull over your mouth when the machines vent particularly thick mist. Some visitors develop a mild metallic cough after a few days; a small vial of eucalyptus or pine oil to rub on your chest can help.

Ear protection is surprisingly necessary. The deep *thoom* of the engines is slow enough to feel almost subsonic, yet it will rattle fillings in your teeth and give you headaches until your body learns to treat the rhythm as weather. High-quality foam earplugs or custom-molded filters are recommended for the first week. After that, many people stop using them because they begin to miss the heartbeat when it is muted.

**Tools and Personal Items**  
- A small, extremely sharp folding knife (the moss and vines grow fast enough that you may need to cut your way through new growth on walkways).  
- A watertight notebook with heavy paper and graphite pencils. Ink runs here within minutes. The archivists in the scriptorium will respect you more if you arrive already keeping a record.  
- A metal canteen or waterskin. Glass breaks too easily on the stone. The central pillar’s self-filling bowl is considered public, but etiquette requires you leave something (usually a smooth pebble or a single iron nail) in thanks.  
- A headlamp or lantern with warm light. The milky, directionless daylight becomes oppressive after a while; the amber glow of the dormitories and the warm vents become psychological refuges.  
- A small leather satchel treated with beeswax. Everything else will mildew.

### Mental and Spiritual Preparation

Understand that time here is not broken into hours but into piston-strokes (roughly one every forty-five seconds) and moss-growth cycles. Bring no expectations of urgency. If you are the kind of person who needs constant stimulation or productivity, the Mossfoundries will feel like a beautiful prison after four days. Many visitors experience a profound slowing of their internal clock; some never fully speed back up.

Practice silence before you arrive. The people here speak softly because the machines are always listening. Loud voices feel like violence. Learn to move deliberately. Every footfall on the iron walkways or basalt transmits sound into the ground and into the machines. The herons and the moss-monkeys notice rudeness immediately.

Meditate on marriage rather than conquest. This place is the long, still-unfinished wedding between industry and forest. Come prepared to witness something sacred that does not need or particularly want your opinion. The weirdness (geometric spores, hour-long ringing pipes, the self-filling bowl that tastes of distant thunder) is not spectacle. It is simply how the place expresses itself. Treat it with the same calm respect the white herons show the warm vent pipes.

### What to Expect in Your Body and Mind

- Your skin will never be completely dry. After the third day you will stop noticing.  
- Your heartbeat will slowly begin to sync, even slightly, with the deeper engines. Some people report lucid dreams in which they are vast, slow machines turning beneath living stone.  
- You will develop an acute sensitivity to condensation patterns and the faint electrical smell that precedes each exhalation of the pipes.  
- The salamanders in the cold pools may begin to follow you. No one knows why.  
- After two weeks, the idea of “dry land” or “fast time” may start to feel slightly obscene.

### Gifts to Bring

The inhabitants value three things above all:  
1. Well-made iron objects that show honest wear (old tools, nails, simple hardware).  
2. Books, especially handwritten ones. The scriptorium is hungry for new songs to record alongside the engines’ music.  
3. Living things that grow slowly. A cutting from an ancient bonsai, a vial of lichen from a cold highland, or a single carefully chosen fern rhizome will earn you genuine respect.

### Final Orientation

When you first step into the basin, stand still for a full ten piston-strokes (roughly eight minutes). Let the sound settle into your bones. Do not speak. Do not take photographs; the place dislikes being frozen. Simply let the cool, damp, iron-and-chlorophyll air fill your lungs while the white herons watch you with ancient yellow eyes and the moss keeps growing, patient as geology, loud as prayer.

If you are ready to be changed on a timescale you cannot control, the Mossfoundries will open to you.  
If you are not, it will simply grow over you, slowly, kindly, and turn you into part of the architecture.

Bring patience.  
Bring silence.  
Bring iron and green.  

The wedding is still young. The stone is still sharp.  
The engines are still turning.
