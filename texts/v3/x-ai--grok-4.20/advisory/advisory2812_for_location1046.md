**Preparation Guide for the Hollow Choir**

You are not merely visiting a landscape. You are entering the respiratory system of an aged planetary god who has not yet decided whether to die or begin again. An ordinary multiverse traveler must prepare with the solemnity of a pilgrim, the pragmatism of a sailor, and the humility of someone who understands they will be insignificant inside something far larger than themselves.

### Mental and Spiritual Preparation
- **Accept disorientation as permanent.** The Choir does not allow you to remain unchanged. After the first week the constant sound will move *inside* your skull. You will begin dreaming in chords. Some travelers report feeling as though their bones have started to resonate at frequencies that are not their own. This is normal. Do not fight it.
- **Decide what you are willing to leave behind.** The Hollow Choir extracts payment. Sometimes it is a memory. Sometimes it is the ability to feel completely at ease in ordinary silence ever again. Pack emotionally light.
- **Cultivate radical acceptance.** Hillsides *will* move while you sleep. Rivers *will* appear and vanish. Mist-shapes *will* reach for you. The land is not malicious, but it is indifferent in a way that feels personal. You must become comfortable with being temporary.

### Physical and Sensory Protection
- **Hearing protection is non-negotiable.** The Choir is not background noise; it is a geological force. Bring high-end custom-molded earplugs *and* over-ear defenders that can be worn together. Even then, after ten days you will still feel the bass frequencies in your sternum and teeth. Many experienced travelers eventually remove all protection for short periods simply to “let the land speak directly,” but they do so knowing the risk of permanent threshold shift.
- **Moisture management.** Everything rots, rusts, or grows mildew at terrifying speed. Your clothes will never be fully dry. Bring merino wool base layers (they resist odor even when perpetually damp), quick-dry synthetic outer layers, and at least two changes of everything sealed in vacuum bags. Expect the constant *drip-drip-drip* from ferns and overhangs to drive you mildly insane by day four.
- **Skin and lungs.** The air is thick with spores, pollen, and mineral dust. Pack a good shemagh or balaclava for dust storms, a collapsible respirator with multiple filter cartridges, and thick emollient creams. Your lips will crack. Your hands will split. Your eyes will feel gritty.
- **Footwear.** The stone is often wet, polished by wind, and treacherous. Bring the best waterproof mountain boots you can find, with aggressive lugs *and* a secondary pair of lighter, breathable shoes for the rare dry days. Both will be covered in green within a week.

### Gear List (Multiverse-Traveler Edition)

**Core Kit:**
- Ultralight single-wall tent made of highly breathable fabric (condensation is constant; you do not want to drown in your own shelter).
- Hammock with rainfly and insect net — many veteran Choir-walkers prefer sleeping suspended because the ground itself cannot be trusted.
- A comprehensive first-aid kit with emphasis on antifungal creams, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and rehydration salts. The fever-green places breed interesting infections.
- Headlamp with *very* red night-vision mode. The nocturnal creatures are sensitive to white light and the locals consider bright light at night rude to the Choir.
- Several wind-traps of your own design. Even if they are crude, carving and tuning one is considered basic courtesy. The land notices when you try to speak back.

**Specialized Items:**
- A small collection of carved bone or weathered hardwood offerings. The mist-shapes respond to respectful gesture. What you offer does not matter as much as the sincerity with which you offer it.
- A weatherproof notebook and archival pens. The light and sound here are so hallucinatory that many travelers later discover entire weeks are blurry in memory. Write everything down while it is happening.
- A small set of tuning forks or a simple bone flute. Some of the most profound experiences in the Choir come from finding a resonant chamber in the stone and playing a single sustained note until the land answers.

### Cultural and Etiquette Preparation
- The locals number only a few thousand across an entire continent-sized basin. They are not primitive — they are post-technological. Treat them with profound respect. Their wind-traps are living ancestors. Ask before touching anything. Offer to carry water or help repair a shelter if you stay near them.
- Never try to “improve” their technology. The Choir eats metal and ambition with equal hunger. They have already had that conversation with the planet and lost.
- Learn at least three specific notes that the Choir sings most often in the region you are visiting. Being able to whistle or hum in harmony is considered the bare minimum of good manners.

### What to Expect Emotionally
You will feel watched, but not by eyes.  
You will feel spoken to, but not in language.  
You will fall in love with certain pockets of impossible green so violently that leaving them will feel like a bereavement.  
You will stand on a bare basalt plain at dusk while the sky turns the color of molten amber and the Choir sings so loudly that your vision vibrates, and you will understand — truly understand — that you are standing inside an act of planetary mourning that is also an act of planetary birth. Both at once. Always.

### Final Advice from Someone Who Has Crossed the Lip
Pack less than you think you need.  
Stay longer than you think you should.  
Bring something living — a seed, a cutting, a small potted fern — and give it to the land when you leave. The Hollow Choir appreciates reciprocity.

And when the enormous thing surfaces in the black sinkhole lake and cracks the world open with its single breath, do not run.

Stand still.

Let it see you.

It is only the planet clearing its throat, remembering it still has a voice after all this time.

Welcome to the lungs of God, traveler.  
Breathe carefully.  
Listen harder.  
And try, if you can, not to sing back in a key the land has not taught you yet.
