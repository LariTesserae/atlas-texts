**Preparation for the High Ruin**

You are not preparing for a place.  
You are preparing to become compatible with something that has already decided what “you” means, and it is in no particular hurry to tell you.

### 1. Mental and Existential Calibration (most important)
- Accept that your identity will loosen. The High Ruin does not erode stone faster than it erodes narrative. After the first month you may no longer be certain whether you arrived, or whether you are something the plateau has been slowly remembering into existence. Pack no assumptions about linear time, causality, or the direction shadows are allowed to fall.
- Cultivate radical epistemic humility. The inhabitants do not argue with the breakdown; they amend their maps daily. Train yourself to feel curiosity without clinging. Curiosity is currency here. Certainty is a form of violence against the local grammar of reality.
- Prepare for the specific loneliness of being ignored by a mind vastly older than your species. The plateau is not hostile. It is simply thinking at a scale that renders you acoustically irrelevant. Learn to find that beautiful rather than terrifying, or the silence will hollow you out.

### 2. Physical Kit (ruthlessly minimalist)
The High Ruin punishes anything that believes it is permanent.

- Clothing: Three layers of merino wool and densely felted moss-fiber outer garments (obtain these on-site if possible; the locals’ felt is literally part of the ecology and therefore less likely to be rejected by the landscape). Nothing synthetic. Synthetics become brittle here within weeks, as if the air itself is offended by petroleum memory.
- Footwear: Soft, wide, ankle-high boots of lichen-cured leather with minimal tread. You will be walking on living moss that sometimes forgets it is a surface. Stiff soles or aggressive lugs are dangerous when gravity or direction become negotiable.
- Eye and skin protection: Polarized goggles with side shields (the pewter twilight and starlight proofs can cause retinal after-images that last days). A heavy scarf or balaclava that can be pulled over the mouth; every breath is a small betrayal of warmth.
- Water: You will not carry much. The plateau drinks, but it also gives. Learn the taste of fissure-water. It is cold enough to make your teeth sing and carries a faint metallic sweetness, like blood drawn from granite.
- Nutrition: High-fat, zero-fiber pemmican, nut butters, and aged cheeses. The local vegetation is technically edible but metabolically argumentative. Most travelers lose 15–20 % body mass in the first year regardless; the plateau is sculpting you.
- Tools: 
  - A single piece of high-carbon steel (small hand-axe or knife). It will slowly turn into red velvet, but the process takes years and the velvet cuts better than the steel ever did.
  - A leather-bound notebook of real paper and graphite pencils. Digital devices forget how to be devices here. Ink evaporates. Lead remembers.
  - A small silver bell. Not for signaling. The sound of clean metal is one of the few things that still behaves predictably and can anchor you when an afternoon decides to last thirty years.
  - A length of braided lichen rope (again, best acquired locally). It stretches when geometry forgets Euclidean rules and contracts when it remembers.

No tent. The half-finished city provides more shelter than you can imagine, and sleeping in the open is how the place gets to know you.

### 3. Temporal and Psychological Supplies
- Expect to stay between six months and never. Pack as though you will leave in three years, but emotionally prepare to die of old age among the moss.
- Bring one small, meaningless object that matters only to you (a childhood marble, a lover’s lock of hair, a lucky coin). When the breakdown begins to feel merciful, you will need something that stubbornly refuses to become symbolic.
- Develop a personal ritual that takes exactly seven minutes. The number is arbitrary but the precision is not. When time pools, ritual is the only reliable ruler.

### 4. Social and Linguistic Preparation
- Learn to speak very little and listen to silence extremely well. The inhabitants communicate partly through adjusted architecture. A new bridge that wasn’t there yesterday is a sentence. A tower that has decided to lie down is an elegy.
- Practice “tide-speaking”: the art of saying what the current geometry seems to want said. This is not mysticism. It is pattern recognition at the edge of ontology.
- Do not ask the locals their names. They change them when the streets do.

### 5. What the Place Will Do to You (be warned)
- Your sense of scale will invert. After a while, the continental plateau will feel intimate and your own body will feel like an embarrassing statistical anomaly.
- You will begin to dream in cartography. You will wake with the absolute conviction that your childhood home has been incorrectly placed on the map and that you must go correct it immediately. This passes after the third year.
- At some point the soft rain will stop feeling like weather and start feeling like the plateau’s attention. This is neither metaphor nor hallucination. It is the closest the place comes to looking at you.
- You may become part of an unfinished wall. Many travelers do. The locals will simply route around you and leave small offerings of carved bone at your feet. This is considered a form of respect.

### Final Instruction
Bring a question you are willing to let die.

The High Ruin is not a place that answers questions. It is a place that waits for the questions to finish asking themselves, after which it may, in its own time, finish building something that no longer needs the question.

Travel light.  
Travel slow.  
Travel ready to become damp, patient, and half-finished.

The plateau has already waited longer than suns live.

It can wait for you to be ready to stop being so loudly temporary.
