**How to Prepare for the Nascent Cathedral of the Weeping Engines**

Pack as though you are simultaneously visiting a tropical greenhouse, an active steel foundry, a bio-reactor, and a living organism that has not yet decided whether you are symbiont, irritant, or raw material.

### Physical Preparation

**Clothing and skin:**
- Wear loose, breathable, quick-drying synthetics or treated natural fibers that can handle constant 28 °C heat + 95–100 % humidity without turning into a personal swamp. Merino wool base layers work surprisingly well; they resist the metallic smell better than most synthetics.
- Bring at least two full changes of clothes sealed in waterproof bags. Everything you wear will become saturated with condensation, spores, and microscopic iron-oxide dust within two hours.
- Waterproof hiking boots with aggressive tread (the lattice floor is slick and occasionally shifts). Consider adding thin gaiters; the capillary channels sometimes overflow and create shallow, warm rivulets.
- A wide-brimmed hat or hood is essential. The constant drizzle from above is warm but relentless; droplets land in your hair and eyes and carry dissolved minerals that sting after a while.
- Thin nitrile or latex gloves if you intend to touch anything. The “living rust” and engineered mosses secrete mild acids and enzymes. They won’t kill you, but they will stain and slowly pit skin.

**Respiratory and sensory protection:**
- A good half-face respirator or at minimum a high-quality cloth mask treated with antimicrobial coating. The air is thick with spores, microscopic metal particulates, ozone, and the faint alkaline dust of precipitating limestone. After six hours your lungs will feel like they’ve been lightly sandblasted.
- Sealed goggles or wraparound glasses. The pearl-grey light is gentle but omnipresent; after a day your eyes will feel raw from never being able to fully relax.
- Earplugs that still allow low-frequency hearing. The sub-bass throb of the pumps and the occasional tectonic *groan* of a new buttress locking into place can become hypnotic or nauseating.

**Hydration and biology:**
- You will lose fluid faster than you expect. Bring 4–5 liters of clean water in collapsible containers. The local condensation is potable but tastes like warm blood and wet pennies.
- Electrolyte tablets. The combination of altitude giddiness and tropical humidity creates a strange, dreamy dehydration that sneaks up on you.
- Broad-spectrum antifungal powder and antibacterial salve. The environment is aggressively biotic. Minor cuts or abrasions turn into jewel-toned infections within hours if untreated.
- A small bottle of personal pH-neutralizing spray. If the hidden valves decide your current location is “too complete,” you may get a sudden misting of warm acidic fog.

### Mental and Temporal Preparation

This place is not a location. It is a slow-motion event wearing architecture as skin. Prepare yourself for the following psychological realities:

- **Temporal disorientation.** Because nothing is allowed to finish, your sense of progress vanishes. Hours feel like days. Standing still for twenty minutes lets you literally feel the floor rising beneath you. Bring a reliable watch with both local and “home” time, and set repeating micro-alarms so you do not simply drift into a spore-trance.
- **The urge to help or “fix.”** The instinct to wipe condensation, straighten a crooked pipe, or remove a dying beetle is almost overwhelming. Do not. The Cathedral is in a state of deliberate, sacred incompletion. Interfering can trigger defensive responses (increased acid mist, sudden rerouting of nutrient channels toward you, or the brass beetles deciding you are a useful substrate).
- **Sensory overload and seductive peace.** The combination of warm wetness, rhythmic dripping, gold-spore smoke, and the deep bass heartbeat is mildly narcotic. Many travelers report an overwhelming desire to simply lie down in the moss and become part of the next layer. Set strict time limits and travel with at least one other person who has permission to drag you out.

### Gear and Tools

- A lightweight, highly breathable tent or hammock with a fully sealed groundsheet. Sleeping directly on the lattice is possible but the slow accretion of new metal beneath you can trap you by morning.
- Chemical light sticks and a headlamp with red mode. The sourceless pearl light never changes, which destroys circadian rhythm. Red light helps.
- A compact infrared thermometer and hygrometer. You will want to track the slow breathing cycles of temperature and humidity; they are the closest thing the Cathedral has to a pulse.
- Small collection jars if you are a scientist or artist. The Cathedral tolerates respectful sampling but becomes agitated by large-scale removal.
- A notebook with waterproof pages. Writing here feels strangely like the Cathedral is reading over your shoulder. Many people report that their notes slowly acquire new sentences in a metallic, looping handwriting that is not theirs.

### Ritual and Etiquette

- Move slowly and speak quietly. Sudden motion or loud noise is interpreted as damage, and the structure may respond by accelerating growth in your vicinity (a disconcerting experience).
- Do not attempt to reach “the center” or “the highest point.” There is no such thing. The Cathedral is topologically anxious about completion; trying to find an ending frustrates it.
- If you feel the floor beneath you suddenly become eager or warm, or if the brass beetles begin to follow you in large numbers, it is time to leave. The Cathedral has decided you are either very interesting or very useful. Neither is comfortable.

Bring humility, patience, and the willingness to be slightly digested for a while. The Nascent Cathedral does not want visitors.  
It wants co-participants in its endless, weeping, half-finished becoming.

Come ready to feel your own pulse become just one more soft rhythm in the warm, metallic dark.
