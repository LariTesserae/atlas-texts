**How to Prepare for Visiting the Bloomworks**

You are not simply visiting a place. You are choosing to step inside an enormous, living, half-digested machine that has been thinking in slow green for centuries. An ordinary human multiverse traveler should treat this with the same gravity one would give to deep-sea diving, entering an active volcano, or walking into a cathedral that has become conscious.

### Mental and Psychological Preparation
- **Accept that you will be watched.** The vegetation is not background. It is a distributed intelligence that registers your presence as both irritation and nutrient opportunity. The longer you stay, the more it will study you. Some travelers report a distinct feeling of being *remembered*.
- **Decide how long you can afford to lose.** Time inside moves at sap-speed. Five hours can feel like twenty minutes, or the reverse. Bring a reliable external timepiece (mechanical, not electronic) and set multiple alarms. The longer you remain, the stronger the urge becomes to simply sit down and let the roots decide what to do with you.
- **Understand the philosophical hazard.** The Bloomworks blurs the boundary between organism and machine so thoroughly that many people experience a temporary but profound existential bleed. You may leave questioning whether your own veins are copper or xylem. This usually fades after a week, but not always.

### Physical Gear (Minimalist but Specific)
- **Clothing:** Full coverage, breathable, quick-drying synthetic or tightly woven natural fiber. Nothing cotton — it will never dry again. Long sleeves, high collar, lightweight gloves. The moisture is relentless; every exposed inch of skin will be constantly dripping. Consider a thin, hooded rain shell treated with antifungal spray.
- **Footwear:** Ankle-high boots with aggressive tread and a solid ankle seal. The ground is a living mat; it will try to claim your boots if they sink too deeply. Some veterans wear rubber over-boots or seal their laces with wax.
- **Respiratory management:** The air is so saturated it feels like breathing warm soup. A lightweight half-face respirator with moisture-trapping filters is advisable for anyone prone to respiratory issues. At minimum, bring a shemagh or scarf you can breathe through when the humidity peaks.
- **Light:** Normal sunlight is heavily filtered to a greenish, cathedral dimness. Bring a powerful headlamp with a red mode (the plants react less to red). Also pack two chemical glow sticks rated for 12+ hours; the blue bioluminescence that appears after true dark is beautiful but disorienting.
- **Cutting tools:** A sharp pruning saw and a strong pair of secateurs. Not for “fighting” the plants — that is pointless and insulting — but for gently clearing a path when curtains of creeper close behind you. Many experienced travelers also carry a small bottle of diluted vinegar or citric acid solution; a few sprays can temporarily discourage over-eager tendrils without causing lasting harm.
- **Containment and collection:** Waterproof pouches and specimen jars with silica gel packets. Anything organic you take out will try to keep growing. Anything metallic you bring in will begin to oxidize and be slowly incorporated within days.

### Biological and Medical Preparation
- **Antifungals and antibacterials:** The air is thick with spores. Pre-treat your skin and clothes with a broad-spectrum antifungal powder. Bring oral antifungals and a strong antibiotic course; some of the microorganisms in the Bloomworks have never been catalogued.
- **Hydration and electrolytes:** You will lose water through your skin faster than you can drink it. Carry at least four liters in collapsible bladders and strong electrolyte tablets. Never drink the condensate that collects in the old turbine housings, no matter how tempting it looks.
- **Skin protection:** A thick barrier cream containing zinc oxide and tea-tree oil. The constant moisture combined with plant enzymes can cause “green maceration” — skin that begins to soften and take on a mossy tint.
- **Vaccinations:** Ensure your tetanus is up to date. The rusted iron is everywhere, and the roots have ways of driving metal splinters deep.

### Behavioral Rules Inside the Bloomworks
1. **Move slowly and announce yourself.** Many travelers speak quietly to the vegetation as they walk. It is not superstition. Sudden movement or loud noise can trigger a coordinated “flinch response” across an entire wall of leaves.
2. **Do not remove living tissue unless it offers itself.** The place has a memory. Taking something that is still fully integrated can cause the surrounding structure to slowly rearrange itself around you in ways that feel like retaliation.
3. **Never spend the night.** The metabolic rate drops after sunset and the pressure to become part of the cycle increases dramatically. The millipedes and moths become more active. The metallic groaning sounds closer.
4. **Leave something behind.** Tradition among repeat visitors is to bring a small, non-living offering — a brass button, a length of copper wire, a glass marble. The Bloomworks seems to appreciate the gesture of voluntary incorporation. It makes your exit smoother.

### Exit Strategy
The boundary is sharp. Twenty paces beyond the outermost curtain of hanging roots, the air changes and ordinary physics reasserts itself. Have a trusted companion waiting outside with dry clothes, a thermos of strong coffee, and a blanket. The first ten minutes after leaving feel like being born: cold, bright, and violently dry.

Many people only visit once.  
The ones who return do so with the quiet understanding that they are not entirely sure which part of them is still purely human, and which part has begun, very slowly, to bloom.
