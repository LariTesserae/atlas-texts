**How to Prepare for Visiting the Hollowed Lantern**

Pack as though you are about to step into a cathedral made of living bone and wet velvet that has been holding its breath for ten thousand years. The Hollowed Lantern does not welcome tourists; it tolerates equilibrium-maintainers. You must arrive already in that state, or the place itself will correct you in ways you will feel for the rest of your life.

### Physical Preparation
- **Clothing**: Bring only fabrics that have already accepted moisture as their natural state — heavy, loosely woven linen, merino, or silk that has been pre-wetted and allowed to dry naturally several times so it no longer fights the damp. Dark charcoal, deep moss greens, and the color of wet bone are safest. Avoid anything that rustles, crinkles, or retains heat. Every layer must be capable of breathing because *you* will not be allowed to. Expect your clothes to never dry again. They will simply reach the exact saturation level of the Lantern and stay there, like everything else.
- **Footwear**: Soft, ankle-high boots of oiled leather or felted wool with soles that make no sound. Hobnails or hard rubber are unforgivable. The stone and roots are polished by centuries of deliberate, slow footsteps; you will feel disrespectful the moment your heel makes a noise louder than a drop of water.
- **Temperature**: The chill is not dramatic but it is absolute and unending. Bring thin merino base layers and one very heavy, voluminous wool cloak or robe that can be drawn close and still allow slow movement. You will wear it like a second skin. The cold settles into marrow and stays. Painkillers or anti-inflammatories for joints are wise; the damp will find every old injury.
- **Skin and hair**: Carry a small tin of lanolin or tallow-based balm. The air will pull every drop of oil from you within hours. Lips crack, cuticles split, hair becomes brittle. You must oil yourself like the pale orchids are oiled by the constant mist.

### Mental and Spiritual Preparation
This is the most important part.

Spend at least three full days before arrival practicing radical deceleration. Walk, speak, chew, even blink at half your normal speed. Train yourself to measure every breath. The Lantern will notice the difference between someone who is *trying* to be slow and someone who has *become* slow. The gap is what it punishes.

Meditate on the concept of “enough.” Sit with a single glass of water for an hour and do nothing but watch the light move across its surface. The Lantern is a place that has achieved perfection through refusal. If you arrive with any hunger for novelty, spectacle, or personal revelation, it will feel like a scream inside a monastery. You must genuinely want to become part of the ornamentation, even if only for a short time.

Prepare yourself to be seen by two hundred pairs of eyes that have measured every deviation for centuries. Their politeness is absolute. Their judgment is geological.

### What to Bring (and What the Lantern Permits)
- A single journal made of pre-aged, heavily pressed paper and a soft graphite stylus. Nothing that can be torn out. Nothing that can be removed. Write only what you are willing to leave behind forever if necessary.
- A small vial of your own scent (something earthy, fungal, mossy) so the moths and pale creatures can decide you belong to the pattern.
- One tiny, perfectly smooth stone from a place you love. You will be tempted to leave it at the base of the Wick. Most travelers do. The locals will pretend not to notice.
- No lights, no torches, no glowing devices. The phosphorescence is total. Any additional light is an act of violence.
- No food that crunches, sizzles, or has strong odor. The Lantern eats silence. You should arrive prepared to live on the thin, sweet porridge they make from the tiny translucent shrimp and pale orchid nectar. It tastes like cold moonlight and regret.

### Psychological Inventory (be brutally honest)
Ask yourself:
- Can I remain silent for days at a time without becoming frantic?
- Can I watch the same moth trace the same circle for six hours and feel wonder instead of boredom?
- Am I capable of leaving a place exactly as I found it, down to the position of individual water droplets?
- If the Lantern decided I belonged there forever, would part of me be relieved?

If the answer to any of these is no, reconsider.

### Ritual Approach
Arrive wet. Walk the last mile in silence through the petrified root-mass with water already running down your face and neck. Do not wipe it away. Let the chamber see that you have already begun to match its saturation.

When you finally step into the vast ribcage chamber, do not look up first. Look down at your own feet. Make sure they are moving at the exact rhythm of the falling droplets. Only then lift your gaze to the impossible green-and-violet density, the cold phosphorescent weight of light, and the single black needle of the Wick rising like the axis the world refused to spin around anymore.

The people will not greet you with words. They will simply make a space for you the way moss makes space for a new droplet — slowly, without celebration, as though you had always been missing and are now, at last, in the correct place.

If you are ready, the Hollowed Lantern will not disappoint you.  
It will also never let you go unchanged.

You will dream of falling water for years afterward, and every dream will be exactly the same length.
