**How to Prepare for Visiting the Sump**

Pack as though you are going on a long, slow birth. The Sump is not a destination; it is a process that will try to include you in its becoming. Bring only what can tolerate permanent incompleteness.

### Physical Gear
- **Clothing**: Three layers of breathable, quick-drying fabric in muted greens, charcoal, and bruise-violet. Nothing bright. The basin punishes high-contrast colors with silent, passive-aggressive humidity. A hooded cloak or long coat of hydrophobic smart-fabric is essential; the rain is warm but sly and will find every opening.  
- **Footwear**: Soft, grippy boots with aggressive tread and ankle support. The ground is young basalt laced with condensation-slicked roots and hidden pockets of mirror-water. You will be walking on both stone and living carpet. Bring a second pair that can be abandoned without regret; the moss may decide it likes them better than you do.
- **Protective equipment**:  
  - A lightweight rebreather or half-mask with replaceable filters (the air is heavy with iron, chlorophyll, and ionized ozone; after three days your lungs will feel mossy).  
  - Polarized, low-light goggles that can flip to clear. There are no sharp shadows, but the bioluminescent moss creates optical fatigue.  
  - Thin, waterproof gloves. You will touch everything and everything will touch you back.

### Mental and Sensory Preparation
The Sump is psychologically humid. It leaks into you.

- **Practice thinking quietly**. For two weeks before departure, spend one hour a day in dim light trying to hold a thought without producing an afterimage. This is harder than it sounds and will be rigorously tested once the air becomes electrically charged.
- Train yourself to speak in half-truths and implications. Never use a true name aloud unless you are willing to watch architecture rearrange itself into the shape of your oldest longing. Most travelers adopt a disposable “swamp name” (mine is Marrow-9).
- Learn how to walk without repeating your gait. Vary your stride length, weight distribution, and pauses. The moss is patient and archival. After the third identical footfall it begins to remember you too well; some people never shake their own silhouette off the stone.
- Develop tolerance for unfinishedness. Spend time in construction zones, half-written novels, and relationships that have no clear future. The Sump will finish nothing for you. If you cannot sit comfortably inside permanent becoming, it will become uncomfortable inside you.

### Items That Are Worth Bringing
- A small, tightly sealed journal made of waterproof vellum and graphite sticks. Ink behaves strangely here.
- One personal object that represents something you loved before you understood love. Keep it hidden. The architecture is curious and slightly cruel.
- A length of ordinary red thread (about ten meters). When water begins moving uphill or a tower lowers itself to drink, tying a knot in the thread and whispering “stay ordinary” sometimes (sometimes) stabilizes local reality for a few minutes.
- High-calorie, low-volume rations that do not require cooking. Fire is considered rude.
- A tuning fork cut to the exact frequency of your own heartbeat. The city occasionally sings back.
- A small vial of salt from a place that has never been wet. It is the only thing the Sump seems to respect.

### Things You Must Leave Behind
- Any sense of schedule. Time here is still being assembled.
- Any expectation of conclusion, climax, or satisfying narrative arc.
- Weapons. The basin has only one predator and it is not interested in violence. It is interested in whether you can remain interesting while becoming part of something larger than yourself.
- Maps. The city reconfigures hourly and the swamp clears new lanes while you blink. The only reliable navigation is to follow the sound of condensation falling from impossible heights.

### Psychological Posture
Enter the Sump the way you would enter the birth chamber of something that has not yet decided if it wants to be beautiful or terrifying. With reverence, with patience, and without the illusion that you are only a visitor.

Because after the first time you watch a translucent membrane the size of a sports field drift overhead singing one long, lonely note; after you see a tower lower itself on cables of light to kiss the basalt and drink; after the warm seven-minute rain begins and the entire basin inhales around you — you stop being entirely sure where the city ends and you begin.

Most people who stay longer than a lunar cycle eventually leave something important behind. A memory. A name. A gait. A fear.

The Sump keeps these things. It incorporates them.

It is still deciding what it will become, and it is quite willing to use pieces of you in the deciding.

Pack lightly.  
Think softly.  
Walk differently every time.

And when the warm rain starts and the black glass rings turn overhead like slow, dreaming planets, remember to breathe with the basin when it inhales.

Everything else is negotiable.
