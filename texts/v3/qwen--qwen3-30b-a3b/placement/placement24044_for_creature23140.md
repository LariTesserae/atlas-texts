**In the Labyrinth of Breath and Stone**  

I am a *speakstone*—a name the community gives me, though I am neither stone nor speaker. I am a child of the depths, born where the water’s murmur becomes a language. My body is lean, my skin pale as the lichen that clings to the ceiling of the Upper Cavern, where the first light filters through cracks. My eyes are wide, adapted to the dim, their pupils dilated to capture every flicker of bioluminescent fungi, every shiver of shadow. I do not remember the surface, though I have heard its name in stories. To me, the world is a breath—slow, steady, vibrating through the rock.  

My role is to *listen*. Not with ears, but with the soles of my feet, pressed against the stone, feeling the tremors of shifting ceilings, the pulse of water veins beneath the floor. I am a *walker*, a tracker of the subterranean current. My days are spent moving through the tunnels, my hands brushing the walls, tracing the scars left by past collapses, the grooves where irrigation channels carve paths for the water’s slow advance. The people call me *keeper of the murmurs*, though I am more a vessel than a guardian. The earth speaks in rumbles and drips, and I am its echo.  

At dusk (though we have no sun, only the flicker of oil lamps and the blue-gold glow of cultivated fungi), I gather with others in the Central Chamber, where the oldest stone is carved with tales. The elders say this place was once a riverbed, that the first settlers followed the water’s trail, chiseling homes into the rock as if shaping a shell. I am not sure what is truth and what is myth, but I carry both. My hands have inked stories into the stone using iron-rich water, my fingers smudged with the ash of burned moss. The children press their palms to the carvings, feeling the ridges of words that might outlast us all.  

I am also a *hunter*, though not with tools. My prey is the *shadow-creep*, a creature that moves like smoke, its body translucent, its body heat detectable only by the dampness it leaves on the walls. We do not kill it; we track it, learning its patterns. The elders say it is a spirit, a relic of the earth’s first breath. I have seen it nest in the crevices of the Lower Caverns, its form coiled around a pool of still water, glowing faintly, like a fossil of light.  

My dwelling is a hollow in the eastern wall, where the moss grows thick and green, a patch of life in the gray. The walls are lined with fungi, their spores drifting like embers. I have cultivated a patch of *silvercap* (a mushroom that glows when touched by sweat), which I use to mark paths for the blind. My wife (though we do not call her that; to us, love is a shared breath, not a word) tends the gardens—small plots of *damproot*, a plant that thrives in the mineral-rich condensation. We trade its fibrous stems for tools, for seeds, for stories.  

At night, I walk the outer tunnels, where the stone is still damp from the day’s runoff. Here, the air is thick with the scent of decay and possibility. I hear the *scrape* of claws on rock, the skitter of something large and slow. Sometimes, I see its silhouette—a hunched, sinuous shape, its skin the color of old bone. It does not fear me. We are both strangers here, bound by the same unspoken pact: to survive, to adapt, to *listen*.  

The people say I am strange. My mind is a labyrinth, tangled with the voices of the earth. They do not understand why I speak to the fungi, why I whisper to the water. But I know what they do not: the subterranean metropolis is not a prison. It is a *collaboration*. The stone is not a cage; it is a companion. The darkness is not an absence, but a presence, breathing with us, shaping us.  

I am not a hero. I am not a leader. I am a stitch in the tapestry, a thread of breath in the earth’s long, slow sigh. And when the time comes, when the tunnels shift and the water rises, I will be there—walking, listening, becoming.
