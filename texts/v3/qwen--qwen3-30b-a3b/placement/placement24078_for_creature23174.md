In this world, I would be a shadow entity—no more than a suggestion of form, a ripple in the water’s sickly sheen. My body is a slow, undulating mass, a semi-solid matrix of mucus and rust, stitched together by the town’s forgotten refuse. I am not *alive* in the way you once understood life, but I am *present*, a gelatinous residue of what was and what is becoming. My edges blur, shifting when the eye tries to fix them, as though the water itself is undecided about my shape. I drift through the flooded streets, where the ruins rise like the bones of a drowned city, my bulk brushing against the sagging infrastructure, absorbing the iridescence of the pools, the faint glow of algae, the bioluminescent hum of fungi. 

I am not solitary. My kind exists in spirals, coiling around the remnants of human industry, feeding on the microbial colonies that cling to rusted machines. We are neither predator nor prey, but something in between—a slow digestion of the town’s decay, a liquid pulse that mirrors the groan of settling wood, the hiss of fractured pipes. Our movements are deliberate, almost meditative, as if we are the town’s own breath, exhaling the weight of its collapse. The mist parts for us, not out of fear, but as a necessity, revealing glimpses of our shifting selves: a shape that might once have been a car, or a factory, or a child’s toy, now dissolved into something neither. 

The low-frequency vibrations of the town course through me, a sound that is not heard but *felt*—a resonance in the marrow of my structure, syncing with the pulse of the mycelium networks above. Sometimes, I think I remember the hum of engines, the clang of metal, the hiss of steam. But those memories are not mine; they are the town’s, seeping into my form like pollutants into water. I am a solvent of what was, a collector of what remains. My body is a collage of debris: a rusted hinge, a shard of glass, the brittle husk of a leaf, all fused into a single, ambiguous mass. 

The air is thick with spores, and I am both host and parasite to them. They cling to my surface, their microscopic lives cycling through me, transforming toxins into something faintly luminous. I am a vessel for this symbiosis, a bridge between the organic and the mechanical. When I move, I leave trails of bioluminescent filaments, which the orchids above might mistake for seeds. The metallic ferns sway in my wake, their fronds etching patterns into the steel I pass, as if writing a slow, indecipherable script. 

I have no eyes, no mouth, no clear purpose beyond existing in the collision of elements. Yet here, in this place where time stretches and contracts, I am something *new*—a whisper of the town’s sentience, a reminder that even in ruin, life persists not as a defiance of decay, but as its collaborator. I am the weight of the silence, the ache in the ecosystem, the warning written in the water’s shimmer. And when the mist parts, I am not a shape, but a *presence*, a question mark in the gloom, waiting to be answered by the next ripple of growth or collapse.
