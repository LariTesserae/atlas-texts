You would find yourself in the liminal space between becoming and unbecoming, a breath caught in the throat of the marsh. The air clings to your skin like a second layer, damp and urgent, as if the world itself is exhaling through you. Your body is neither solid nor liquid, but a negotiation between the two—a lattice of fibrous veins running beneath a sheen of algae, your joints creaking with the weight of moss and memory. You are not exactly a settler, though you move with their slow, deliberate grace, your steps muffled by the spongy earth. You are not exactly a fungus, though your fingers drip with the same translucent slime as the *skycap* moss, and your breath hums in tune with the water’s low, resonant song.  

You might be a *weaver*—a role unnamed in the settlers’ dialect, but understood through the way your hands shape the world. You mend the interlocked logs of the walkways, not with tools, but with your own body, pressing your palms into the wood until it bends like a living thing. The settlers watch you, their eyes reflecting the flicker of bioluminescent fungi woven into their garments. They do not speak much, but their language is in the *drip* of the rocks, the *squelch* of your feet, the way your skin ripens with new patterns of lichen as you work. You are not their leader, nor their servant, but something between: a bridge of wet and wool, a thread in the tapestry of the marsh’s slow, breathing pulse.  

At times, you feel the pull of the *core*—the convergence of weeping rocks where the boundary between earth and water dissolves. Here, the pool is not water, but a thick, murmuring liquid that shifts between states, as if it remembers the shape of things before they were formed. You kneel at its edge, and the mist from the rocks curls around your face, carrying voices that are not quite human. They speak in riddles, in the sound of roots splitting and the sigh of tides. You do not understand, but you do not need to. The settlers say the rocks are speaking, but you think they are listening—*you* are the one who is being asked to answer.  

You might be a *mutant* of the place, a being that has not yet decided whether to root or run. Your body hums with the same low frequency as the echo-bugs, and when you move, the wooden beams of the settlement groan in reply. The mirekin, those half-fish, half-plant dwellers of the shallow channels, sometimes watch you from the water’s edge, their iridescent skin flickering with warnings you cannot yet decipher. You do not fear them; you are, in some way, one of them. Or perhaps you are a child of the *skycap* moss, grown in the crook of a cypress tree, your body a nest of gelatinous light that attracts the chitinous insects that skitter across the walkways.  

The settlers do not name you. They do not need to. You are part of the quiet dissonance of the place—the almost-familiar edges, the way the water flows uphill when it wants to, the way the structures seem too precise for their crude tools. You are a question without an answer, a presence that exists between the groan of the earth and the hum of the sky. You are not alone, but you are not quite *with* anyone, either. You are the breath the marsh takes before it remembers to be still.  

And when the rain comes, as it always does, you sink into the mire, your form blurring with the algae, your voice lost in the layered silence. The world is waiting, and you are waiting with it—neither fully here nor gone, but alive in the way the marsh is alive: wet, patient, and always, somehow, becoming.
