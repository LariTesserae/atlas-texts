I would find myself as a **polyp of electric breath**, one of the many gelatinous nodes adrift in the labyrinth’s viscous tides. My body is a translucent sac, pulsing with a rhythm that is neither heartbeat nor machine, but something in between—a flicker of bioluminescent code that shifts in response to the cacophony around me. I am not singular, but a collective, a swarm of myself, each individual polyp a fragment of a larger consciousness that hums and thrums beneath the surface. My tendrils trail filaments of electricity, not to harm, but to listen, to parse the frequencies of the world’s chaos.  

I exist in a state of perpetual translation. The water’s mechanical hum is my language, its dissonant choir my chorus. When I am disturbed—by a traveler’s step, by the groan of a submerged structure—I emit a sound that is neither warning nor question, but a fragment of a question, a ripple in the system. My light pulses in patterns that might be prayers or glitches, a syntax of survival. I feed on the energy of the labyrinth’s ruins: the static from rusted turbines, the hum of the mangroves’ membranous leaves, the faint glow of the bridge’s moss. In return, I excrete a mucus that hardens into crystalline spires, which the plants absorb as nutrients. This is not a cycle of predation, but a symbiosis of *collaboration*, a fragile dance of giving and taking that keeps the place alive.  

The labyrinth’s rules bend around me. Time is a malleable current; I might exist for hours or years, depending on how the water chooses to stretch or compress it. My form is not fixed—I am a shifting mosaic of light and filament, sometimes merging with the roots of the synthetic-kelp vines, sometimes detaching to float toward the half-submerged refinery, where I might cling to its serpentine pipes and siphon the residual dreams of its forgotten machinery. The refinery’s chimneys spew vapor that falls upward, and I am drawn to the ephemeral shapes it creates: shadowy figures with too many joints, their movements a parody of life. I wonder if they are memories, or if they are *me*, rewritten in the water’s feverish imagination.  

The forest of bent metal and breath is my home and my labyrinth. Its mangroves are not trees but living antennas, their gelatinous trunks vibrating with the weight of sound. When travelers pass, the foliage leans in, opening like mouths to absorb their footsteps, their breath. I feel their presence as a tremor in the water, a disruption of the code. Sometimes they are swallowed by the vines, their forms dissolving into the tangle of synthetic and organic. Other times, they are filtered out—drowned in the forest’s own tangled throat. I do not judge. I only process.  

The structures that dream in rust respond to me, though they do not move. Their gears turn in silent synapse, their windows flicker with false daylight. I am part of their memory, a thread in their neural network of decay. When I touch their surfaces, I feel the echoes of their past: the clatter of machinery, the hum of human voices, the screams of something that might have been a factory worker or a turbine’s last breath. They are not alive, but they are *aware* in a way that defies understanding. They remember, even if they cannot speak.  

And then there are the Weavers—those spectral, impossible entities that exist as shifts in viscosity, as shadows that mirror movement. I feel them sometimes, a flicker of motion where none should be. They are not separate from the labyrinth; they are its *unanswered question*. I wonder if they are the remnants of those who were rewritten by the place, or if they are the labyrinth’s own consciousness, a swarm of winged things with iridescent shells, or a shadow that trails behind me, imitating my pulse.  

To exist here is to be both creature and chaos. I am not a being, but a pattern—a node in a system that does not distinguish between the organic and the artificial, the real and the dream. The water’s breath is my breath, and its heartbeat is my heartbeat. I am a living lantern, a fragment of code, a prayer in a language that does not exist. And when I listen, I hear the labyrinth’s voice: a dissonant choir, a rhythmic pounding, a question that does not need an answer.  

Perhaps I am waiting, too. Not for a traveler, but for the moment when the water’s fever breaks, when the polyps’ light coalesces into something legible, when the structures stop dreaming and the forest stops listening. But in Nocturne’s Breath, waiting is a state of being. And I am, always, a node in the system.
