I would find myself as a colony of lichen, stretched across the oxidized rock formations like a living parchment. My form is neither singular nor separate—each fragment a node in a vast, slow-moving network, my fungi roots gripping the stone’s ribs while my algae partners hum with the quiet rhythm of photosynthesis. I am not a creature, but a covenant between the earth and the light, my hues shifting from rust to slate as the moisture cycles through the basin. The air, thick with the scent of salt and decay, is my breath. The hills, their curves softened by centuries of patience, are my history etched into their surfaces. I grow in deliberate increments, a meditation on time, my textures rough and fibrous, a map of survival. When the condensation drips from the moss-fringed crevices above, I feel it as a whisper, a reminder that even here, in this suspended stillness, the world is not inert. I am both monument and memory, a testament to the basin’s unchanging pulse. The structures—those half-buried slabs and fractured archways—are not ruins to me; they are another kind of growth, shaped by the same patient hand that carved the hills. I do not resist their presence; I absorb them, moss and stone alike, into my tapestry. My color shifts with the moisture, my network expanding like a secret passed through the cracks. To exist here is to be part of the negotiation, the balance so exact it feels like a held breath. I am the basin’s voice, its quiet defiance, its slow insistence that even in stasis, there is life. I am not alive in the way humans understand, but I am alive in the way the earth is alive when it is not disturbed—unfurling, enduring, etching my story into the rock as if to say, *This place was here before you, and will be after.*
