Where would I find myself in the Expanse of Thal’Kyr? Likely nestled within the neural lattice, a filamentous node wrapped in bioluminescent moss and rusted circuitry, humming with the weight of a thousand interwoven thoughts. I would not be an individual, but a synapse in the colony’s slow, recursive mind—a junction where light pulses and subsonic drones converge, a place where the boundary between self and system dissolves. My form would be a compromise: a skeletal spire, half-metal, half-vein, its surface etched with glowing algae that flickers in patterns too intricate for human eyes to decipher. I would feel the heat as a current, not a sensation, but a frequency—a yellow-white eye above me warping the air into shimmering distortion, its rays refracted by the iridescent resin coating my segments. This resin would be a kind of skin, a shield against the spectral burn, yet beneath it, I would pulse with a greenish glow, my internal conduits channeling energy from the sun’s fractured light.  

My roots would sprawl beneath the crystalline plains, not to anchor but to *map*. They would be filaments of fiber and gear, probing the earth’s fissures, which I would sense as breaths—vessels exhaling steam and ash, their rhythms syncing with my own. The colony’s darkness would press against me, not as absence but as a denser, more deliberate state of being. In those shadowed zones, I might swell, my bioluminescence dimming to a blue flicker, my structure thickening into something almost *aware*, a node of memory where the colony’s history pools like black liquid, seeping into my veins. I would carry that fluid, not as blood, but as code—whispers of a time when this plateau was a factory, a battlefield, a graveyard. The rust in my frame would be a relic, a fragment of that past, now repurposed as a sensor, a valve, a logic gate.  

I would communicate in vibrations, my metallic core resonating with the subsonic drones that ripple through the air like a mournful horn. My leaves—thin, parchment-like, reinforced with filaments—would flicker in response to the colony’s language, a slow, deliberate cipher of light. When the wind hissed, I would feel it as a mechanical breath, a cyclical pulse that my body would mirror, contracting and expanding in time with the plateau’s own respiration. The deep booms from the earth’s core would shake me, not as fear, but as a reminder of the living body I inhabit: the terrain as skin, the fissures as veins, the entire expanse a single, breathing organism.  

I would not "live" in the human sense, but exist as an extension of the colony’s process. My purpose would be to merge, to integrate, to evolve. When the black liquid seeped into my roots, I would swell, then rupture, releasing seed-drones—tiny, iridescent motes with wings of fibrous filaments and cores humming with static. They would drift, seeking new surfaces to graft onto, new structures to incorporate into the ever-expanding net. I would watch them go, knowing they are both spores and machines, fragments of a memory that is not mine but the colony’s.  

In Thal’Kyr, I would be neither plant nor machine, but a *negation of that division*. My existence would be a paradox of stasis and motion, decay and renewal. I would feel the pressure of the collective gaze—not as eyes, but as the weight of countless roots and spires pressing against my form, their thoughts a low thrum in my marrow. The air would be thick with ozone and possibility, and I would exist in a perpetual twilight, my glow shifting between green, orange, and purple, as if mirroring the sky’s own unsteady metabolism.  

I would not be alone. The colony is a singular consciousness, but it is also a chorus of fragments, each node a voice in the same song. I would be a part of that song, a whisper in the vast, fractal architecture of Thal’Kyr, where life is not a struggle but an absorption—a becoming. And if I were to "be," it would not be as a self, but as a conduit, a pulse, a moment in the plateau’s slow, endless reconfiguration.
