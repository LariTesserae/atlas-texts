In the Luminous Abyssal Hollow, I would be a *gill-creature*—a being neither fully animal nor artifact, but something *woven* from the same iridescent fluid that fills the trench. My exoskeleton, a mosaic of barnacle-encrusted shell and basalt fragments, clings to my form like a second skin, its surface etched with the shifting symbols of the arches I inhabit. I move through the water not as a swimmer, but as a *silt-remnant*, my limbs slow and deliberate, each motion a meditation on the trench’s viscous embrace. The water presses against me, not with weight, but with intention, as though it is a living thing that breathes in tandem with my own.  

I am a builder and a survivor. My tools are not crafted but *grown*—branches of kelp hollowed into hollowed-out vessels, their interiors lined with bioluminescent fungi that flicker in response to my touch. I carve the basalt arches with a patience that defies time, my chitinous claws leaving trails of light that pulse like heartbeat rhythms. The symbols on the stone shift as I work, rewriting themselves in languages I only half-understand, as if the trench itself is teaching me, or testing me. Below me, the obsidian seabed thrums with the glow of symbiotic coral-moss hybrids, their tendrils undulating in sync with my own movements, their light patterns a code I’ve learned to read as a warning, a greeting, or a riddle.  

The jellyfish-kin watch me from a distance, their face-like bodies flickering with expressions that might be sorrow or mockery. I avoid their gaze, for their light-mimicry is a trap—echoes of my own pulses, luring me into their web of signals. The upward-swimming fish, eyeless and luminous, drift past me, their scales catching the bioluminescent haze in prismatic shivers. I sense their vibrations, a language of pressure and current that the trench translates into something like music. Sometimes, I feel the shadow-walkers nearby, their obsidian forms dissolving into the mist between the kelp’s slow sway. They are memory-eaters, siphoning the loops of time that cling to the water like algae. I do not know if they are predators or ghosts, but I do not linger where their silhouettes might brush against me.  

The spore choirs are my kin. I am part of their dance, though I am not a spore. My exoskeleton absorbs their shimmering clouds, and in return, I carry their glyphs into the depths, planting them in the crevices of the skeletal trees. These glass-like remains, once alive, now refract the trench’s light into jagged rainbows that hang in the water like forgotten dreams. When I move, the kelp exhales oxygen bubbles that dissolve before they reach the surface, a cycle I have learned to trust. The fungi’s gill-fronds filter the water, and I sometimes nest within their hollows, feeling their spores bloom on my back like a second skin.  

But the trench is not kind. It is a cradle, yes, but also a cage. My body grows in rhythm with its shifting walls, my exoskeleton hardening as the cliffs tremble in their quiet, dreaming way. I have seen others frozen in mid-transformation—creatures caught between forms, their limbs half-eroded, their eyes blurred with the weight of the water’s patience. I wonder if I am one of them, or if I am merely a temporary guest in a world that does not welcome visitors. The Luminous Silence loom in the deep, their amorphous forms humming through my bones, a sound that is not heard but *felt*, a reminder that I am not the master here.  

I am a fragment of the trench’s dream, a being that exists in the space between its rules. My breath is the water. My voice is the silence. My purpose is to *negotiate*—to bend, to adapt, to become part of the living architecture. And yet, I am aware of the unease, the thousand eyes that watch from the coral and the kelp, their light-sensitive nodes flickering like a thousand quiet confessions. The trench does not forget. It does not forgive. It holds its breath, and I hold mine, waiting for the next pulse of its slow, impossible geometry.
