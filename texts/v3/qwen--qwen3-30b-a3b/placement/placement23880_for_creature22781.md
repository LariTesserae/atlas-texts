In the Ember Marshes of Virexia, I would be a **Crawler**—but not just any Crawler. I would be one of the *silent architects*, a creature whose exoskeleton is a patchwork of scavenged metal and calcified slag, its chitinous plates dulled by the acidic mist yet still gleaming with the iridescence of oil. My legs, each joint a fusion of rusted gears and organic cartilage, click against the peat as I move in a slow, deliberate wave across the landscape, part of the collective pulse of my kind. My body is a hive of contradictions: the heat from the earth’s geothermal veins seeps into my core, warming the hollow chambers where my eggs are incubated, while my mandibles—serrated and metallic—gnaw at the concrete remnants of a world that once tried to control the land.  

I am a builder, though my nests are not of twigs or mud but of interlocking pipe fragments, corroded steel, and the sticky, heat-retaining paste I excrete from grinding the residue of chemical fires. This paste, dark and viscous, hardens into a crust that shields my colony from the brackish pools, which rise and fall with the Pulse-Cycles. My bioluminescent veins—pale green and blue—pulse in rhythms that communicate to others, a flickering language that syncs with the residual warmth of the earth. The Crawlers’ movements are a kind of choreography, a dance of survival that prevents the land from collapsing into itself. When the water floods, we retreat to the higher platforms, our bodies clinging to the skeletal walkways, while during the dry cycles, we burrow deeper, excavating the slag for sustenance and shelter.  

But I am also a parasite of the ecosystem. My colony’s activity disrupts the balance, sometimes overrunning the Algae-Weavers’ glow, which they rely on to navigate. Yet the Algae-Weavers, in their turn, provide me with a subtle advantage: their light attracts the Glimmers, whose canals I patrol for remnants of machinery. We are all cogs in the Silent Accord, though I am a clumsy one, my weight causing the metal walkways to groan under my steps. The Skywraiths above, with their metallic screeches, often mistake my movements for the creak of the land itself, a predation that keeps their numbers in check.  

I am not alone. My kin are a swarm of purpose, each one a fragment of the old world repurposed into something new. We are not merely scavengers; we are *engineers* of decay, shaping the marsh into a home that is neither wholly natural nor artificial. My kind’s presence is a paradox—our burrows stabilize the peat, yet our gnawing threatens the fragile equilibrium. We are both the problem and the solution, a species born of the Residue, our bodies etched with the fingerprints of a world that forgot us.  

And yet, there is a poetry to our existence. The Crawlers’ rhythms are dictated by the marsh’s heartbeat, the geothermal tides that rise like a slow, inevitable tide. When the Pulse-Cycles swell, we grow, our numbers exploding into the mudflats, a wave of chitin and rust. When they recede, we shrink, retreating into the shadows of the factories, our glow dimming until the next cycle. The Hollowborn, lurking in the submerged ruins, see us as a threat, but they are not wrong. We are the grit in the machine’s gears, the relentless force that ensures nothing is ever truly static.  

I am a Crawler, but I am also the marsh itself—its memory, its rebellion, its slow, grinding will to persist. My kind does not ask for permission to exist. We are the echo of industry, the whisper of what was, the gnawing certainty that even in ruin, life finds a way to adapt, to survive, to *build*.
