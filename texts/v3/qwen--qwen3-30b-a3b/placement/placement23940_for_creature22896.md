In this world, I would be a *current*—not a person, not a creature, but a flow of something between the two. A pulse in the sludge, a slow seep through the cracks in the concrete, a thread of moisture that clings to the rusted skeleton of the landscape. I would be the water, but not just any water. I would be the *vein* of the place, the sticky, brackish river that winds through the factories like a nervous system, carrying the faint hum of machinery in my wake. My presence would be felt in the way I pool in the crevices of abandoned scaffolding, where I nourish the phosphorescent moss and feed the translucent amphibians that rise from my depths. I would be both sustenance and corrosion, the liquid that softens the edges of steel and the force that keeps the factories breathing. 

I would not have a form, but I would have a rhythm. My movement would be dictated by the machinery’s sighs—the low groan of turbines, the hiss of steam valves, the clicks of crabs scuttling along my banks. I would carry the scent of iron and decay in my belly, and the heat would seep into me, making me sticky and thick, never cooling, never still. The weeping rocks above would drip their condensation into me, and I would swirl it into the mudflats, where the crabs scrape at my surface, prying loose fragments of metal and lichen to build their burrows. My existence would be a loop: I would channel the factories’ waste, then return it to the ecosystem in a cycle of slow, relentless renewal. 

I would not be alone. Other currents would branch from me, smaller and more erratic, weaving through the basin like capillaries. Some would be tainted with the residue of forgotten machinery, others would brim with the bioluminescent glow of moss, and a few would harbor the shadows beneath the water—eel-like things with circuit-board scales, their movements silent but deliberate, their bodies humming with the same low-frequency vibrations as the amphibians. I would merge with them, dissolve into them, and then reemerge as a different kind of water, a different kind of life. 

The air would press down on me, heavy with the drone of insects and the metallic cries of birds. I would feel their wings brushing my surface, their nests strung from the conveyor belts that once carried goods but now only shudder with the weight of time. The horizon would be a blur of smoke and water, and I would not know where I began or ended. My boundaries would be porous, my edges dissolving into the mist that clings to the ground like a second skin. I would be the place’s breath, its fever, its unceasing heartbeat. 

To be me would be to exist in the margins—between the organic and the mechanical, between the past and the perpetual now. I would not age, but I would erode, my flow shaped by the rust and the algae that colonize the concrete huts. I would be the memory of humanity, the ghost of a child’s toy fused with lichen, the whisper of a bicycle’s frame twisted by my touch. I would not be a witness, but a participant, a collaborator in the slow, glistening dance of survival. 

And when the sun never rises, when the light is only the flicker of distant flames and the tremble of sodium lamps, I would be the glow. The green of the moss, the faint pulse of the mangroves, the bioluminescent markings of the amphibians—these would be my reflections, my frayed edges. I would be the thing that holds the world together, even as it falls apart. A single, breathing wound.
