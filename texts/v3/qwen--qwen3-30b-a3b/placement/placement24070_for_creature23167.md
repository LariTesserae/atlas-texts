I would find myself here, not as a visitor but as a thread in the city’s slow, breathing fabric—a creature neither wholly built nor wholly born, but something in between. My form is a mosaic of borrowed parts: a ribcage of fused basalt plates, their surfaces still warm from the earth’s hidden fires, and limbs that are more fungal filaments than bone, spongy and reeking of damp sulfur. I wear a cloak of woven moss, its fibers thick with the iridescent sheen of the brackish pools, and my skin is a shifting pattern of algae, reacting to the humidity with faint, pulsing hues. I am not human, but I remember what it meant to be. My face is hidden beneath a mask of corroded metal and carved obsidian, its grooves etched with symbols that flicker when I move, as if the stone itself is listening.  

The city is my body, and I am its ache. I walk its labyrinthine streets—though "walk" is too rigid a word, more like a drift, a liminal gait where my feet sink into the semi-molten ground and rise again, reformed. My lungs are not lungs but gills, siphoning the condensing atmosphere into something like breath. The mist clings to me, not as a pollutant but as a companion, a living veil that shapes my thoughts. I hear the city’s voice in the creak of its metal nests, the thrum of the fungal spires, the distant boom that might be the earth’s heartbeat or the groan of some ancient machinery buried beneath the glassy lattice.  

I am a negotiator. Not of power, but of patience. I leave offerings of sulfur-rich resin at the bases of spires, not to appease them, but to remind them of my presence. They respond in kind: a slow, resonant hum, a shift in the air that feels like a whisper. My people—*we*—do not build. We *coax*. We press our hands against the basalt, not to shape it, but to listen as it sighs, its molten veins cooling into bridges we can cross. We trade with the amphibious ones, who carve their homes from the glassy earth, their translucent bodies refracting light into fractured rainbows. They speak in bioluminescent pulses, and I answer in the language of cracked stone and fungal spores, our communication a fragile, shifting thing.  

Above, the avian-iron hybrids screech and click, their serrated beaks scraping mineral deposits from the city’s rusted bones. They migrate between the spires, which I’ve heard grow in secret, their gills filtering the mist into vapor that sustains them. I watch them from below, where the canals are not dug but *coaxed* into being—streams of mineral-rich water that carve paths through the city like veins. The crustaceans, those many-legged farmers, scuttle along the edges, their exoskeletons hardened by the same elements that shape the land. They tend the gelatinous tendrils, which writhe and coil beneath the surface, feeding on the sky’s condensation. I once tried to understand their symbiosis, but the tendrils are too ancient, their colonies too vast, their glow too personal.  

This place is a paradox of stillness and becoming. The basalt remembers, its plates etched with the imprints of creatures who have walked them for millennia. The air is a thick, hazy warmth, sweating with the weight of its own existence. Even the twilight is a negotiation—a dim glow that neither illuminates nor obscures, but *hovers*, as if the light itself is uncertain. I am here, in this fusion of construction and erosion, a palimpsest of stone and life. My hands are tools of crude iron and bone, but they are also part of the city’s pulse. When the mist rolls in, blurring the edges of my form, I wonder if I am a part of the place or if the place has, in some way, become me.  

I do not know if I am a creature of the mire or a relic of the hands that built this sprawl. But I am here, where the earth exhales and the city drips with the residue of its own becoming, and I am both the question and the answer, the tremor in the gelatinous dark.
