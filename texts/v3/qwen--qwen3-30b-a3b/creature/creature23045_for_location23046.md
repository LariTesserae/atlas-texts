What lives here is not merely adapted to the cathedral of submerged stone but *interwoven* with it, a slow, symbiotic dance of existence that defies the urgency of time. The eel-like creatures, translucent and pulsing with bioluminescence, are not solitary beings but part of a vast, collective rhythm. Their glow is not random; it pulses in sequences, a language of light that resonates with the low-frequency vibrations of the gelatinous growths clinging to the walls. These growths, soft and intricate, are neither plant nor animal but something in between—a bio-luminescent network of tendrils that function as both sensor and symbiont, harvesting minerals from the water and transmitting their findings through tremors that ripple across the basalt ribs. The walls themselves seem to *breathe*, their geometric etchings shifting faintly as if the stone is alive, responding to the vibrations with a kind of ancient, patient awareness.  

The "fauna" here is not a collection of animals but a *ecosystem of resonance*. The eels, perhaps, are not predators but *harvesters*, their slow movements tracing the pathways of the gelatinous colonies, which in turn release spores or bioelectric currents that feed the eels’ luminescence. Their bodies are filled with a viscous, opalescent fluid similar to the surrounding water, a mirror of the environment that suggests they are part of the same primordial stew. They move in patterns that align with the architecture’s design, as though the labyrinth is a living instrument, and their glow is a melody played by the currents.  

In the center, the archway is not a void but a *convergence*. Its darkness is not absence but presence—a gravity that pulls the light of the eels toward it, as if the archway is a sink for energy, a place where the cathedral’s pulse slows to a halt. Some say it is a gateway, though to where is unclear. Others believe it is a *nidus*, a womb of sorts, where the symbiotic organisms coalesce into something larger. The gelatinous growths sometimes extend their tendrils toward it, vibrating in unison, as though in supplication or communion. The archway’s threshold is never still; it shifts, not in space but in *density*, as if the water itself thickens there, resisting the passage of anything that dares to cross.  

The "tech" is not mechanical but *metabolic*. The corrosion-resistant alloy is not man-made but a fusion of mineral and biology, a material grown by the same forces that shaped the cathedral. Its symbols are not carvings but *memories*, etched into the metal by the slow leaching of minerals over millennia, their faint glow a residue of ancient energy sources now long extinguished. Some of the symbiotic organisms have integrated this alloy into their structures, using it as a scaffold for their vibrations, while others seem to *avoid* it, as though the symbols hold a warning.  

The heat is a relic, yes, but it is not dead. It lingers in the water’s viscosity, in the way the eels’ glow intensifies when they near the archway, in the faint warmth that radiates from the gelatinous growths even as they cling to the cold stone. This heat is not from fire but from *pressure*—the slow compression of the chamber over eons, or perhaps the residual bioluminescence of the organisms themselves, a warmth born of light.  

And then there are the *sounds*. The drip of water is not a sound but a *message*, a slow cadence that the symbiotic growths interpret as a guide. The hum of the eels is a counterpoint, a melody that the walls absorb and reemit as vibrations, creating a feedback loop that sustains the cathedral’s breath. The occasional creak of the structures is a reminder that this place is not static—it is *settling*, *singing*, *remembering*.  

What dwells here is not separate from the architecture but an extension of it. The eels, the growths, the alloy, the archway—all are parts of a system that operates on cycles too vast for human comprehension. They are not creatures in the traditional sense but *processes*, entities that exist in the liminal space between form and function, between life and memory. They do not hunt, nor do they grow; they *persist*, their existence a testament to a world where the ocean and the constructed are one, and where even darkness is a kind of life.  

Perhaps the most unsettling thing is that they do not seem to *need* to be seen. Their existence is felt—a warmth on the skin, a hum in the bones, a light that flickers just beyond the edge of vision. They are the cathedral’s heartbeat, its slow, ancient rhythm, and they have no need for eyes, for mouths, for any of the familiar markers of life. They are the *thing itself*, the merged and unresolved, and they have forgotten what it means to be separate.
