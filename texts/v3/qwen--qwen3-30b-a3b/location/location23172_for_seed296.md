The landscape is a labyrinth of jagged, wind-sculpted peaks that rise like the broken teeth of some ancient, slumbering leviathan, their slopes draped in a thick, emerald shroud of moss and ferns that cling stubbornly to the rock. The air here is thin and cool, a persistent chill that seeps through layers of wool and metal, carrying the sharp tang of oxidized machinery and the damp musk of decomposing foliage. Rivers carve through the valleys in silvery threads, their surfaces rippling with the faintest breath of wind, while condensation forms into mist that clings to the cliffs, pooling in crevices and feeding underground springs that gurgle like the lifeblood of the earth. The terrain is both cruel and complacent—extreme elevation has pressed the land into a fragile equilibrium, where the mountains seem to have settled into their final, brittle form, their ancient erosion etched into every fissure and canyon. The rock is a patchwork of slate and quartz, fractured by time and reinforced by the clumsy hands of human industry: rusted girders, twisted pipes, and the skeletal remains of factories half-swallowed by the earth. 

The scale is vast, stretching beyond the horizon into a realm where the sky bleeds into the peaks, and the city—what remains of it—spreads across the ridgelines in a patchwork of crumbling infrastructure and overgrown tenements. Buildings here are not built *on* the land but *into* it, their foundations buried beneath layers of sediment and ivy, their facades pockmarked by the slow decay of time. The density of the place is suffocating: the city’s tangled networks of walkways and scaffolding are crammed with so many layers of construction that they seem to collapse into one another, while the vegetation presses in from all sides, roots fracturing concrete, vines threading through rusted machinery, and ferns sprouting from the lips of forgotten vents. The air hums with the low, persistent drone of engines that never fully died, their vibrations felt in the bones rather than heard, as if the mountain itself is exhaling through the cracks of human ambition. 

Light is fractured and diffuse, filtered through a ceiling of ash-gray clouds that hang low, casting the world in a perpetual twilight. The dimness amplifies the strange, the way the moss glows faintly with bioluminescent fungi, or how the rivers reflect not the sky but a distorted, shimmering tapestry of rust and stone. The fauna is sparse, but not absent—shadows flit between the ruins, their shapes too fluid to be entirely natural. There are birds with too many wings, their cries echoing in hollows where the wind has carved out resonant chambers, and insects that hum in frequencies just beyond hearing, their exoskeletons glinting with metallic sheen. The ecosystem is a paradox: active in its quiet way, with streams carving new paths through the ancient rock, and the city’s decaying structures slowly merging with the land, as if the earth is reclaiming them. 

Weirdness lingers in the details—the way the condensation on the buildings forms into liquid that tastes of salt and ozone, the way the vegetation seems to pulse faintly in the dark, or the rumor that the rivers flow backward during the third moon cycle. The place feels suspended between epochs: a city that once thrived on the edge of the world, now a relic swallowed by its own excess, where the line between nature and construction has blurred into something neither fully alive nor entirely dead. It is a place of quiet tension, where the hum of industry and the whisper of wind compete for dominance, and where the cool, damp air carries the scent of both decay and possibility.
