## The Inhabitants of Keth-Vossan

---

They are not the kind of people who arrive all at once. They come in the way the moss comes — a few threads at first, then a mat, then a surface you can't remember being bare. Most of them came because the work was here, or because the work somewhere else ran out, or because someone they knew was here and the basin's cool, steady air seemed preferable to whatever they were breathing before. They do not talk about why they stay. They do not need to. The answer is in the way a person's boots know exactly where to step on a path they have walked a hundred times, the way a hand finds a railing in the dark without hesitation. You stay because the ground, once it has accepted you, does not ask you to leave. It simply asks you to pay attention.

---

**The miners** are the oldest continuous population. They are a narrow-shouldered people, not from malnutrition but from habit — the habit of squeezing into passages that were generous last month and are now barely adequate, the instinct to press their shoulders against the wall when the stone groans, which it does, regularly, like an old house remembering its joints. They have thick hands, not calloused exactly but *dense*, the way the rock itself is dense, and they hold their tools with an intimacy that looks almost conversational. They do not use the word *mine* in the possessive. They say *the face* when they mean the wall of exposed stone, as though they are referring to a person. They are not wrong to do so. The miners know that what they are extracting is still on its way up — that the ore they haul out today was deeper last year and will be shallower next year, a slow migration they do not claim to fully understand. They describe it the way you might describe a tide: *It's coming up on the north vein. Give it a season.* Their patience is not philosophical. It is practical. They have simply learned that arguing with the geology of the basin is less productive than listening to it.

At night — the real night, when the day-shift sleeps and the electric lights are dimmed to save the generators — the miners who pull the night shifts sit in the pumphouse with tin cups of something hot and watch the gauges. They do not talk much. The hum is louder at night. They know what the biologists say about the fauna responding to it. They nod, the way people nod when they have known something longer than the theory explaining it. One of them — an older man with a crooked back and a name that gets shortened differently depending on who's speaking — once said that the hum sounds like a voice trying to remember a word. He did not say this poetically. He said it the way a person reports a symptom. The others considered it. Nobody disagreed.

---

**The engineers** arrived later, or at least their influence did, visible in the second and third layers of construction — the metal-framed dormitories with proper drainage, the overhead conduit runs organized into something approaching a grid, the reinforced foundations that allowed buildings to go up without worrying whether the ground would accept them. The engineers are more recent arrivals and more restless. They carry notebooks. They measure things. They argue about cooling rates and load tolerances and the exact depth at which the basin's substrate transitions from sediment to something harder and stranger — something that does not behave like standard geology and that no one has found an agreed-upon name for. They are responsible for keeping the generators running, the water flowing, the crane operable. They are, in a sense, the town's fight against entropy, though they would phrase it differently. They would say they are giving the ground a chance to stabilize.

The engineers and the miners do not always agree. The miners want to follow the ore deeper. The engineers want to reinforce first, reinforce always, because the tunnels change — they *move*, is the only word for it — and a passage that was four feet wide in spring may be eighteen inches by autumn, not through collapse but through a kind of slow, silent contraction, as though the rock is digesting the space. The miners resent this caution. The engineers resent the recklessness. Neither side is wrong. Both sides work in the morning. Between them, the town holds.

---

**The biologists** are the most recent arrivals and the least settled. There are only a few — two, maybe three at any given time — and they are identifiable by their tendency to stand still for long periods in the rain-slicked open lots, staring at things that are not there yet. They study the swifts, the insect populations, the creatures in the pools. They are the ones who first documented the synchronized surfacing — the way every pool-dwelling thing rises to the waterline at exactly the same moment when the night-hum reaches a certain frequency, as though responding to a signal with no visible source. They have theories. The swifts alter their spiraling patterns on nights when the hum deepens; their flight paths become tighter, more compressed, as though the air itself is thinner. The insect clouds go still for those twelve exact seconds — not scattering, not landing, simply *pausing* — and then resume with no visible change in behavior. The rodent-things, whatever they are (they have dark, flat teeth and translucent ears and they breed faster than any mammal of their size should be able to), show no response to the hum at all, which the biologists find more disturbing than any response could be. *The fact that they don't react suggests either that they can't perceive it or that they've already adapted to it completely,* one of the biologists wrote in a letter that was shared widely among the others. *Neither possibility is comforting.*

The biologists do not stay long. The basin's pace does not suit the rhythm of peer review or publication. They come, they observe, they leave with notebooks full of data that does not resolve into clean conclusions, and they are replaced by other biologists who arrive with the same mixture of fascination and unease. They are tolerated by the miners and respected by the engineers, who appreciate their rigor even if they distrust their conclusions. The biologists, for their part, are fascinated by the practical knowledge the miners possess — the folk taxonomy, the read-and-respond intuition that has no peer-reviewed equivalent. They write it down. The miners are indifferent to being written down.

---

**Then there are the others.**

The cook who runs the fire behind the dormitory — a wide woman with a voice like a dropped pan, who feeds everyone regardless of shift affiliation and who somehow always has enough, though nothing is ever stored for longer than a day. She does not talk about where the food comes from. Nobody asks. The answer is somewhere between *the usual channels* and *the basin provides*, and either one is unsatisfying and both of them are true.

The children. There are a dozen, maybe fifteen — the count changes, because some of them are residents and some of them are visitors, and because no one has ever successfully imposed a census on Keth-Vossan. They play in the open lots around the idle machinery with a recklessness that horrifies visiting engineers and does not impress the miners at all. They know the paths. They know which metal surfaces are warm and which are slick. They can identify the swifts by the sound of their wings. They do not fear the hum. They put their hands flat on the rock at night, the way you might press your ear to a door, and they listen with an attention that adults have spent years training themselves out of.

The drifter-types, who come up the eastern path with packs and stay for a week or a month or a season, sleeping in the half-collapsed processing plant or the crane's shadow, performing small tasks in exchange for meals and the basin's peculiar stillness. They leave changed, or at least they say they do. They come back, sometimes. The basin has a way of pulling at a person's sense of permanence — not threatening it, exactly, but... *softening* it. The idea that the ground beneath you might become something different, slowly, without malice or intent, is either liberating or terrifying depending on the person. The drifters tend toward the former. The engineers tend toward the latter. The miners do not think about it. They have already made their peace with a process that has no endpoint.

---

**And beneath all of them — beneath the people, beneath the foundations, beneath the tunnels that rearrange themselves with geological patience — the pool-things breathe.**

They are not fish. They are not amphibians. The biologists have catalogued their morphology: elongated, silver-flanked, gilled but surfacing regularly, responding to vibrations in the water column with a speed and unanimity that suggests something closer to a shared nervous system than individual instinct. They school in the small dark pools like thoughts assembling into a sentence. They vanish into silt that forms and dissolves on cycles no one has matched to any known process. The miners do not think of them at all, the way a person living near the ocean does not think about the deep water. The engineers consider them a nuisance — a variable in the basin's water chemistry that complicates readings. The biologists think of them constantly. They are the basin's most visible secret, and they are alive, and no one can say what they are becoming, or whether the basin knows, or whether the difference between the two matters at all.

---

Keth-Vossan does not have a population so much as it has a *constellation* — a set of presences that hold their positions relative to one another and to the ground beneath them, shifting slowly, occasionally flaring or dimming, but never leaving the pattern entirely. The people here are not settlers in any traditional sense. They are participants. They build, and the ground resettles. They extract, and the rock regenerates. They listen to the hum, and the hum does not answer but does not refuse them either. To live in the caldera is to agree, tacitly and without ceremony, to be part of a process whose conclusion — if conclusion is even the right word for it — will not arrive in any span of time that a human being can meaningfully inhabit.

And yet the cook makes her fire. The children chase each other around the crane. The swifts tear out of the cooling towers at dawn like something being ripped free. The rock hums beneath everything, patient and low, and the basin holds its silver light over all of it with the indifference of something that has not yet decided what it is — and may never, and may not need to.

The life here is not a settlement. It is a negotiation. And both sides, for now, are still at the table.
