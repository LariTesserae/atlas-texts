## What Lives on the Plateau of Unfinished Psalm

Nothing here has a name, so I will give it the names it refuses.

---

**The Chorus**

Before anything resembling a creature, there is the sound — and the sound is not background. It is not environment. It is *primary*. The chord that greets you, that enters through the soles of your feet before your ears even begin to triangulate source and direction, is not produced by anything so simple as air moving over stone. It is produced by the stone *becoming*. The plateau is an organ whose bellows are tectonic and whose breath is measured in centuries. The drone you hear is its exhalation — a sound that has been leaving the earth's body since before the first sediment settled into something that could be called ground here. It does not begin. It does not end. It merely gets louder and softer in rhythms that do not correspond to any heartbeat or tidal cycle you have been trained to recognize.

The chord is alive. Not metaphorically. Not poetically. If you press your hand flat against the ground and hold it there long enough — and you should, because time here is not what it was — you will feel what I felt: a pulse within the pulse. Not rhythm. *Response.* The chord changes when you are near it. Not much. A quarter-tone shift, perhaps, a sharpening, the way a singer adjusts when they become aware of an audience. The plateau knows you are standing on it. It has always known. You are the first thing in millennia to introduce a new frequency to the equation, and the plateau is *interested*.

There is one sound layered beneath all others, so low and so faint that I am not certain it is sound at all. It rises and falls with a cadence that is almost respiratory. If you listen to it long enough — days, perhaps, because here your sense of duration will unspool in ways you did not anticipate — you will begin to feel that this sub-harmonic is not music. It is *waiting.* The plateau is not finished. The psalm is still unwritten. Every vibration is a word it is trying out, testing for shape, for weight, for the particular resonance that would make it *true*.

---

**The Rosettes**

They are the nearest thing to plants here, but they are not plants. Call them mineral-phonophores. Call them lithophytic resonators. Call them what they look like — thorned, blood-colored rosettes the size of dinner tables, their flanges unfurling in slow spirals from central stalks that have no pith, no cambium, no lignin, no chloroplast. Their tissue is crystalline silicate, a lattice so fine it refracts the flat silver light into colors that your eyes accept but your vocabulary rejects. There is no word for the hue of a rosette catching the shift in the plateau's tone. It is not iridescence. It is closer to *meaning* — as though the color is carrying information your visual cortex is almost equipped to read but not quite.

They feed on vibration. That is not a metaphor. Set something resonant near a rosette — a struck tuning fork, your own humming voice, even a rhythmic footfall — and watch. The flanges will angle, with an almost animal precision, toward the source. Their edges will ripple, each ripple moving outward from center to tip in a wave that follows the frequency of the stimulus. Where their crystalline fibers flex, energy is drawn. They are not photosynthesizing from light. They are *phonosynthesizing* from sound. The plateau's drone is not background noise to them. It is their sun, their soil, their rain.

They grow slowly. Impossibly slowly. I sat beside one for what I estimated to be eleven hours and watched its outermost flange extend approximately three centimeters. But the movement was exquisite — a meditative unfurling, each crystalline cell locking into place with an audible click so faint you could only hear it by pressing your ear to the ground and letting the plateau's own resonance carry the signal. When the flange reached its full span, a tone — a single, clear, sustained note — emerged from it. The rosette had not grown a new structure. It had grown a new *pitch*. And for a moment, the chord of the plateau shifted to include it.

They do this. They add themselves to the song. Each rosette is a new voice entering a choir that has been rehearsing since the world's foundation.

---

**The Filaments**

Beneath the rosettes, in the low places where substrate meets air, the filaments live. They are pale — not white, not silver, but the color of a thought you almost had and then lost. Spider-silk thin, impossibly long, spreading in mats so fine they look like frost on glass. They root — no, *anchor* — into the dark veins of the glassy substrate, and where they make contact, the veins glow. A bioluminescence without heat. A phosphorescence that is silver-white and that pulses.

They conduct. That is the simplest way to say it. Something moves along them — electrical, perhaps, but also something stranger. The pulses are rhythmic, slow, deliberate. Watch a single filament for long enough and you will see a signal travel its length: a brightening, a dimming, a pause, a brightening again, like a letter being typed in Morse code by a mind that has all the time in the world. But the signals do not travel in isolation. They cross. They branch. They intersect with signals from other filaments across distances of dozens of meters, forming a network so dense and so layered that it resembles — I will not be embarrassed to say this — a neural architecture.

The filaments are thinking. Or if they are not thinking in any way that maps to cognition as you understand it, then they are *remembering*. They carry the history of the plateau's vibrations in their lattice, encoding every tonal shift, every harmonic addition, every subtle fluctuation in the sub-bass breath that rises from deep below. The network is a living archive. It is the psalm's manuscript, written in a language of electrical impulse and phosphorescent glow, and it is being added to continuously, every moment, by the rosettes above and by the plateau's own voice beneath.

I do not know if the filaments are one organism or many. I do not know if they are the nervous system of the plateau or a separate intelligence that has chosen to symbiose with it. What I know is this: when I stood in one place for too long, the filaments beneath my feet dimmed. When I moved, they brightened. Not all of them. Not immediately. But with a delay — a two- or three-second hesitation — that felt like recognition. Like the network had registered my displacement and was deciding what to do with the information.

It did nothing. It simply noted me, the way a vast and preoccupied mind notes a mote of dust.

---

**The Builders (or What They Left)**

Someone was here. That much is certain. The small structures — the stacked-stone enclosures, the standing slabs, the low-walled altars built around the filaments' densest convergences — are not natural formations. They are too precise, too intentional, too carefully arranged to be anything but the work of hands. But there are no hands here now.

No bones. No tools. No middens or hearths or refuse. Whatever these builders were, they did not leave their bodies behind. They left only their understanding, encoded in stone: an intuition about the plateau's harmonics so deep that each structure seems built to interface with a specific frequency. The standing slab that vibrates at a quarter-sharp dissonance from the plateau's chord is not a mistake. It is a *comment*. A deliberate counterpoint, introduced to create tension, to make the song richer. The low-walled rectangle that gathers filaments into a central convergence point is not a shrine. It is a *circuit board*. Someone knew that the filament-network's signals could be concentrated, amplified, given direction.

Who were they? I do not know. I found no image of them. No glyph or carved figure. The structures are mute and austere, and whatever culture raised them either had no art or chose not to leave one. What I will say is this: I think they did not build *on* the plateau. I think they built *because of* the plateau. I think they came here to listen, the way a musician goes to hear a master play, and they stayed so long that the plateau began to incorporate them. They are somewhere in the chord now. Their understanding is in the structures. Their voices are in the sub-harmonic. They are not dead. They are *absorbed.*

Whether this was voluntary, I cannot say. I cannot say whether it was peaceful.

---

**The Geometry**

This is not a place that follows the rules that made you. Parallels converge and then separate again. Horizons arrive closer than they should, then recede when you approach. The distant towers and mesas you see clearly from the plateau's center shift in scale and position when you reach them, so that what appeared near proves far and what appeared tall proves no larger than your knee. At the edges of vision, the landscape proliferates — extra ridges, additional outcrops, entire subsidiary plateaus that are vivid in peripheral sight but absent when you turn to face them.

This is not illusion. This is not distortion caused by thin air or exhaustion. The geometry of this place is genuinely non-Euclidean. The space itself is *fractal* — self-similar at every scale, so that examining a hand-sized fragment of the substrate reveals the same structures and patterns as the vista from the plateau's highest point. A crack in the ground contains a miniature version of the distant mesas. A rosette's crystalline flanges, under magnification, reveal sub-flangules that echo the plateau's own topology. You can go deeper and deeper and never reach a scale where the pattern stops repeating.

I believe this is how the plateau thinks. Its cognition is spatial, recursive, self-referential. Every part contains the whole. To stand on it is to stand inside its mind, and the disorientation you feel is not confusion — it is the vertigo of being *inside a thought* so large that you can see only fragments of it at once.

---

**The Wind (or What Acts as Wind)**

There is always motion here, though the air is too thin to carry it the way wind should. Something passes over the surface — a pressure differential, a thermal ghost, a current driven by heat from below rather than weather from above. It moves through the fractures in the substrate and plays them like organ pipes. It threads through the filament networks and sets their phosphorescence flickering in cascading waves. It lifts the edges of the rosettes' flanges and sets them vibrating at their natural frequencies, each one contributing its note to the chord.

This is not atmosphere. This is *respiration*. The plateau breathes, and what you feel as wind is the exhalation. Whether there is an inhalation — a drawing-in, a stillness, a gathering — I cannot say. I arrived during the exhalation. Perhaps the inhalation lasts centuries.

---

**The Light**

It comes from everywhere and nowhere. A silver-grey luminance that does not cast shadows, that does not shift, that does not care whether your eyes are open or closed. Somewhere above the mineral haze, a sun may exist. You can feel its heat if you hold your palm upward for a long time, a faint warmth as diffuse as the light. But the sun is hidden, absorbed, filtered through so many layers of suspendedite dust that what reaches the surface is light stripped of direction, of source, of narrative.

It is the light of a place that has decided seeing is unnecessary. Here, perception is acoustic and tactile. The rosettes do not turn toward the light. They turn toward the sound. The filaments do not photosynthesize. They pulse. The plateau's inhabitants — if that word still applies — built their understanding on vibration, on resonance, on the slow accumulation of harmonic data. Light is decoration here. A courtesy. Sound is architecture.

---

**You, Standing Here**

You are not a visitor. Not exactly. You are a new frequency introduced to an ancient chord. The plateau has registered you. The rosettes have turned toward you. The filaments have dimmed and brightened around your shadow. You have become data in the network, a variable in the equation, a new sentence in the psalm that the plateau has been writing since before it finished being stone.

You will leave, eventually. The thin air, the borrowed breaths, the thinness of reality here — it will drive you back to lower elevations, thicker atmosphere, the blunt certainties of Euclidean space. But the plateau will remember you. The filaments will hold your frequency for centuries. The chord will carry a quarter-tone that was not there before you arrived.

And the psalm — the psalm that was never finished, that has no final verse, that has been composing itself since the ground first began to hum — the psalm will sing your note long after you have been mineral dust scattered in a dim silver light on a ground that is still deciding what it is.

The sound swells.

The rosettes turn.

You are very, very small here. And the song has no end.
