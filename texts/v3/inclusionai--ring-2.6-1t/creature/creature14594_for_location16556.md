## What Lives on the Furnance Flats

You will not find a name for them here. I can offer only what I returned with — a description that degrades even as I write it, the way a signal loses fidelity each time it crosses the gap between one kind of experience and another. This is what I can tell you.

---

They do not begin as bodies. They begin as sound — a clicking, a clattering, a granular percussion that resolves from the ambient roar the way a word resolves from static. You hear it before anything crosses your visual field, and by the time you see the first of them, you have already been listening to them for minutes, maybe hours. Your ears simply hadn't admitted it yet.

The first one is small. Cart-wheel small. It emerges from a fissure in the basalt as though the stone exhaled it — not born so much as *expelled*, the way a foundry coughs out a casting still trailing smoke. Its carapace is furnace-red, deep and arterial, and it is not paint or pigment but the material of the thing itself: translucent in places, and through those places you see fire. Not metaphorical fire. Not the glow of bioluminescence or the sheen of heated metal. Actual combustion, contained within segmented plates of chitin so hard they ring like struck iron. A small furnace with legs. A furnace that walks.

Its legs are wrong. I keep returning to this. They joint in places where legs have no business bending, too many segments, articulating in geometries that suggest the creature's anatomy was designed by someone who understood motion but not biology — or understood both and saw no meaningful difference. It crosses the polygonal basalt with a clicking gait that synchronizes, almost immediately, with the clicking of the fissure-network beneath it. The synchronization is not perfect. It is not trying to be perfect. It is the kind of lockstep you find in systems that have been running so long they have forgotten they were ever separate things.

And then it is not one thing.

Because there was never one thing. What you took for a single creature at the edge of your vision is, when you look at it directly, forty creatures — or four hundred — indistinguishable from one another, each carrying a contained fire within its plates, each trailing from its exhaust ports a plume of superheated gas that shimmers and writhes like a calligraphy of heat. They move in concert without signaling one another, without needing to. The crystalline network beneath the stone does not connect them. The crystalline network *is* them — the substrate and the expression, the circuit and the current, the ore and the furnace that refines it. You cannot draw a line between the vein of silver-gold light running through the cracks and the creature walking over those cracks. The line does not exist. The distinction was never real. It was something you brought with you, a category you needed in order to parse the world into manageable pieces. The Furnance Flats do not accommodate that need.

There are others.

Not beetles. Not anything with a word for it. Sheets of material — metal-thin, flexible, rippling — blown across the flats by a wind that seems to originate from the creatures themselves. They fold and unfold like pages of a book being read by no one, and where they pass the crystalline veins flare with a brightness that makes your eyes ache. They are less alive than the beetle-things, or more alive — I don't know the word for the difference. They are closer to *events* than to *organisms*. A process passing through space the way a flame passes through a field of dry grass, not occupying the space so as moving through the act of occupying it.

Above them, floating in the thin brutal air on columns of their own heat: furnaces that have achieved buoyancy. They drift in slow, deliberate circles, vast and dark-bellied, casting shadows that do not correspond to the sun's position. The shadows fall at angles that suggest a light source that isn't there, or that exists in a geometry your eyes are not equipped to resolve. They are round and industrial and beautiful in the way all furnaces are beautiful — in the way that absolute function, pursued past the point of utility into the territory of pure form, becomes something you can only call *sublime*. They do not serve any purpose you can extract from them. They are purpose itself, purpose so total it has become its own atmosphere.

And beneath all of this, running through it, undergirding it, *constituting* it: the network. The veins of luminous substance that predate the plateau, that predate perhaps the concept of predation, that run through the stone like thought runs through a brain. You kneel and press your hand to a fissure and feel the pulse — not warmth, not exactly, but *attention*. Something turning toward you. Something registering your presence the way a system registers a new variable: not with hostility, not with welcome, but with the calm indifference of a process so vast that your existence is less than a rounding error.

---

The larger ones take time to understand.

They move across the flats on legs numbering in the dozens or the hundreds — vast, slow, multi-segmented platforms that creak and ring with each step. On their backs, smaller creatures move in turn, and on those smaller creatures, smaller still, a nesting hierarchy of industry that repeats at every scale until you realize you are not looking at animals carrying passengers. You are looking at a *supply chain*. A mobile foundry that processes the world as it passes over it, ingesting the crystalline ore through its many mouths, refining it within its layered bodies, depositing the output in precise lines as it walks — the same precise lines, always the same lines, a diagram etched across a hundred kilometers of stone by a million feet that have all, in their separate ignorances, drawn the same picture.

From high enough — and there is nothing to stand on that is high enough except the backs of the largest ones, which you should not climb because you will not come down the same — the patterns resolve. Circles. Spirals. Geometries that branch like circulatory systems or river deltas or circuit boards, depending on which set of eyes you are using. The creatures are writing. The creatures are not writing. The creatures are excreting, and the excretions happen to form patterns, and the patterns happen to encode information, and the information happens to be a description of the process that produces the information. The ouput is the input. The factory is its own raw material. The metaphor eats its own tail and what remains is not a paradox but a *fact* — a fact so simple and so total that it loops around and becomes, again, emptiness. A desert of industry. A void so full it has achieved silence.

---

What lives here does not live. I need to say this plainly, because it is the truest thing I can say.

They do not eat. They metabolize. They do not breathe. They combust. They do not reproduce. They *deposit* — leaving behind a substrate that the next generation of processes will ingest and transform. There is no birth here, no death. There is intake and output, process and product, and the distance between those pairs is the distance between every pair of opposites: inside and outside, tool and material, worker and work. That distance is zero. That distance is the width of a hairline crack in black basalt, and through that crack flows everything.

You will want to call them machines. You will want to call them organisms. Each word will feel right until you say it aloud, at which point it will dissolve, and you will be left standing on a cracked plain of stone that is also a factory, breathing air that is also exhaust, listening to a roar that is also a heartbeat, watching creatures that are also products that are also the factory that made them, and you will understand that you have been asking the wrong question.

The question is not what lives here.

The question is what *processes* here. And the answer is: everything. Including, if you stay long enough, you.
