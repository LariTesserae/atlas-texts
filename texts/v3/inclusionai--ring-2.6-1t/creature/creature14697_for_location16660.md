## The Inhabitants of Vaelith

---

### I. The Continent

The first thing you must understand is that Vaelith is not a place where things live. Vaelith is the thing that lives, and everything else — the light, the corridors, the rare skittering creature on the wall — is an incident of its living.

It breathes. You will learn this before you learn anything else: the slow respiratory shift in the color of the light, amber inhaled, silver exhaled, a cycle so deep that one full breath might take nine hours or nine days — no one has been still enough inside Vaelith long enough to confirm. The walls flex, almost imperceptibly, in time with it. Stand with your back against a rib and you will feel it, not as vibration but as *intention* — a faint lateral sway, no more than a millimeter, that is not wind because there is no wind, that is not thermal expansion because the temperature never changes, that is simply the continent adjusting its posture the way a sleeping animal shifts its weight in its sleep.

It is not intelligent the way we understand intelligence. It does not think in sequences. It does not respond to stimuli in any way that suggests awareness of the thing causing the stimulus. If you cut it, it seals. If you remove a layer, it grows another. If you stand in a corridor for a long time, it will slowly grow around you — not threateningly, not with malice, but the way a tree grows around a fence post, incorporating the obstruction into its own architecture without acknowledging it as separate. It has no interest in you. This is, perhaps, the most unsettling thing about Vaelith. It is alive on a scale so total that your presence registers as less than the presence of a white blood cell in a human body. You are not irrelevant — you are beneath relevance.

And yet. Watch the light carefully — not at the light itself, but at the way it falls on surfaces. There are moments, rare and unrepeatable, when the wash of luminescence across a curved wall will resolve, for an instant, into a pattern. Not a random pattern. A *legible* one. Something like a capillary network mapped in two dimensions, or the branching structure of a decision tree, or the schematic of a circuit that runs on fluid dynamics instead of electrons. It lasts less than a second. It may be bioluminescence firing in sequence along a vascular pathway. It may be a thought. No one has been able to determine which, because no one has been able to produce the same pattern twice.

Vaelith is not a building. Vaelith is not a machine. Vaelith is a body. And it has been alive for so long that it has forgotten — if it ever knew — that it is alive.

### II. The Menders

*Ligoformis reparans* — or so the survey teams named them, though there is an uncomfortable presumption in assigning a Latin binomial to something that may not use nucleic acids for its genetic material.

They are small. The size of a closed fist, roughly ovoid, with six limbs arranged in two tripods that give them a gait that is neither insectile nor mammalian but something uniquely their own: a quick, skittering lope, all six legs in alternating tripods, each foot — if it is a foot — leaving a faint bioluminescent residue that fades within seconds. Their dorsal surface is armored in a chitinous material indistinguishable from the growth-layer they walk on, patterned in tessellated hexagons the color of old amber. Their ventral surface is soft, pale, and wet-looking, and it is from this surface that they extrude the substance that defines their function.

They repair. That is all they do. They move along seams, along joints, along the faintly glowing graft-lines where centuries ago, some guiding intelligence — or the slow logic of selection — fused one structural member to another. They pause. They extrude a paste, luminous and quick-hardening, from pores along their undersides. The paste bonds with the living material at the molecular level, fills micro-fractures, re-seals junctions that have loosened over decades. The continent absorbs the paste the way a wound absorbs a scab. The Mender moves on.

They do not eat. At least, no one has ever observed them eating. They do not drink — there is no water. They do not interact with one another, though on rare occasions two have been observed on the same seam, working in parallel without coordination or conflict, each apparently unaware of the other. They do not flee from observers. They do not react to light, sound, or contact. If you reach toward one, it will continue its work until your hand is millimeters from its dorsal carapace, and then it will simply — not flee, not react — *be elsewhere*, as though it has retracted into a fold in the wall that you did not see and could not have seen. It does not reappear.

They are almost never seen. The first expedition catalogued forty-seven individuals over a six-month survey. The second, five years later, catalogued eleven over eight months. The third saw two. After that, the survey teams stopped counting. The Menders are not rare because Vaelith is dying. The Menders are rare because Vaelith mostly does not need them. They are the immune response of a body that is almost never sick. They appear only where the architecture has sustained damage that exceeds the continent's capacity for passive regeneration — damage usually caused, in the end, by the harvesters.

There is a theory — not widely held, but persistent among those who have spent the most time inside Vaelith — that the Menders are not organisms at all. That they are buds, or nodes, or *terminals* of Vaelith's nervous system. That the continent is not ignoring the harvesters. That it is *monitoring* them through the only sensory apparatus it has ever bothered to grow.

### III. The Saprophytes

There are things that live in the vascular fluid.

You do not see the vascular fluid directly. It moves through channels deep in the walls, behind layers of parenchyma dense enough to stop a drill bit — you know it is there only because the engineering gauges show pressure differentials, flow rates, periodic surges that move through the structure like a pulse. The synthetic tubing bolted to the grafting stations samples it, moves it, supplements it. The fluid itself is not accessible.

But in the places where the walls have been freshly cut by harvest teams, before the Menders arrive, there is residue. A thin, opalescent film that clings to the exposed surfaces and shimmers with a faint iridescence, like oil on water. It is warm to the touch. It smells — faintly, almost imperceptibly — of copper and something sweetly organic, like overripe fruit left in a warm room.

Under magnification, the film is alive.

Not with cells. Not with anything that corresponds to cellular organization as we understand it. It is a colloidal suspension of microscale structures, each one a hollow polyhedral lattice, smaller than a red blood cell, that moves with apparent purpose through the fluid residue. They organize. They reorganize. They form transient structures — chains, lattices, hollow spheres — that persist for seconds before dissolving back into the swarm. They metabolize, if metabolize is the right word: they consume trace minerals from the vascular fluid and excrete waste products that the Menders later absorb. They are, in every functional sense, a microbiome. The commensal organisms of a continental body.

No one has been able to culture them outside Vaelith. They die within hours of separation from the living substrate. They may not be organisms at all, in the classical sense — they may be self-organizing chemical systems, autocatalytic loops of protein and lipid that crystallized into apparent life the way frost crystallizes into apparent structure, beautiful and temporary and utterly dependent on conditions that exist nowhere else.

Or they may be the oldest things in Vaelith. Older than the architecture. Older than the engineered growth-layers and the grafted joints and the mechanical pumps that have long since been colonized and subsumed. Older than the six-limbed Menders, who may themselves be downstream of these lattices. They may be what was here before Vaelith was Vaelith — the first tenants of a body that grew, slowly, around them.

### IV. The Harvesters

They live here in the loosest sense of the word. They arrive in teams of four to six, sealed in environment suits that are more ritual than necessity — the atmosphere inside Vaelith is breathable, stable, chemically neutral, and has been for longer than records exist. The suits protect against nothing so much as the psychological weight of being enclosed in something so thoroughly alive.

They speak in low voices. This is not policy — there is no regulation against speaking loudly inside Vaelith. It is something that happens naturally, the way people lower their voices in a cathedral or a library, not because they are told to but because the space demands it. The silence here is so complete, so dense, that a raised voice feels like violence.

They work for shifts of six to eight hours, cutting into a growth-layer with vibroblades that vibrate at frequencies the wall-tissue seems to welcome rather than resist. The cut is clean. The saprophytes flash opalescent in the exposed surfaces, then retract. Within hours — sometimes minutes — the Menders arrive. The harvesters pause, give the Menders room, and watch them work with the detached fascination of surgeons observing a body heal itself in real time.

They do not stay long. Roster rotations are strict: no team serves more than twenty-one days inside Vaelith before being rotated to the perimeter station. Not because of any measurable physiological effect. Bloodwork is normal. Vitals are normal. Nutrient absorption, immune markers, cognitive batteries — all normal. But the company that manages the harvest has learned, through the long institutional memory of decades of expeditions, that teams who stay longer begin to *hesitate* on their way out. Not refusal. Hesitation. A moment at the orifice — the fissure through which they entered — where they stand with their equipment packed and their samples sealed and they simply *wait*, as though they are listening for something. No one has ever been able to describe what they are listening for. None of them stay past a third hesitation. But they all hesitate.

There are rumors of a harvester who did not leave. A woman, early in the survey period, who simply... stopped. She was found, six months after her team rotated out, three kilometers deeper into Vaelith than her team had ever gone. She was alive. She was calm. She could speak, though she had little to say. She showed no signs of injury, malnutrition, or dehydration — though the logistics of survival inside Vaelith without resupply remain unexplained. She walked out when they asked her to. She completed her recovery rotation. She requested reassignment to the perimeter station. She lived there, in the station adjacent to the orifice, for eleven years. She never entered Vaelith again. She never explained why she had stayed.

She did, however, on three occasions over those eleven years, stand at the orifice at night — when the engineers dimmed the external flood-lights for maintenance — and watch the faint bioluminescent glow that leaked through the fissure from the continent's interior. She said, once, to a junior technician who asked her what she was looking for:

*"I'm not looking. I'm being looked at."*

### V. The Engineers

They are not inhabitants so much as attendants. The mechanical pumps — the synthetic tubing, the pressure regulators, the luminous-lichen dial faces — were installed by a team that predates every current survey group by at least a generation. No one knows who they were. The equipment bears no manufacturer's marks. The schematics are etched directly into the walls in channels that appear to have been grown, not carved, as though the continent produced the diagrams itself in anticipation of a need it could not fulfill on its own.

The engineers who maintain this equipment now are a rotating staff, pragmatic, lightly superstitious, and more comfortable with Vaelith than anyone else who passes through. They do not philosophize about what Vaelith is. They calibrate gauges. They replace tubing. They check flow rates and mineral balances and add synthetic compounds that the living tissue cannot produce on its own — trace elements, catalytic substrates, buffering agents. They treat Vaelith with the quiet, competent care of a livestock farmer tending an animal they do not fully understand but have learned, through patient observation, how to keep alive.

They are the closest thing Vaelith has to a symbiont of mutual recognition. And if they are asked — which they rarely are, because they are quiet people and the philosophical tourists tend to find them dull — they will say that the continent responds to maintenance. Not quickly. Not dramatically. But over years, the sections that receive the most attentive care grow more stable, more structurally sound, more willing — if that word can be applied — to hold their shapes. The sections that are neglected, or where maintenance is delayed, develop anomalies. Growth spurts. Misaligned joints. Corridors that loop back on themselves in ways that the mapping teams cannot reconcile.

The engineers do not say this is intelligent behavior. They say it is *responsive* behavior. They calibrate their instruments and go home. They come back the next day and calibrate them again.

### VI. What Grows in the Deep

Below the floor — beneath the dense cork-like surface, beneath the root-fiber substrate, beneath the deep strata of compressed growth-history that you excavated out of curiosity and catalogued out of habit — there is something else.

You found it on the ninth day, when you dug past the lignite layers into something softer, warmer, and faintly *wet* in a place that has no water. A substrate of fibrous tissue so dense it was almost liquid, pulsing with a rhythm that matched neither your heartbeat nor the respiratory cycle of the light above. It was not a root system, exactly. It did not branch or taper or terminate in fine hairs. It was a *field* — a continuous mat of living material extending laterally in every direction beyond the reach of your excavation, dense with signal-structures that, under the hand-lens, looked disturbingly like neural tissue.

You did not dig deeper. You sealed the excavation, stripped the dead layers back over it, and walked away.

But at night — in the impossible, timeless nights inside Vaelith where the light dims by perhaps ten percent during the silver-exhale phase and the shadows that don't exist anyway seem to press a fraction closer — you sometimes hear it. Not through the walls this time. *Through the floor.* A deep, subsonic rhythm, felt in the soles of your feet rather than heard in your ears. A pulse. Slow. Vast. Unhurried.

It is not the heartbeat. The heartbeat is the respiratory cycle, amber in, silver out, a breath measured in hours.

This is slower than that. This is measured in something longer than hours.

It is the continent, dreaming in the deep.

And the Menders know it. You can tell by the way they pause, sometimes, mid-repair, their tripodal legs frozen in place, their soft ventral surfaces pressed flat against the seam they are sealing, as though they are listening to a frequency you will never hear.

As though they are waiting for permission to continue.
