What lives here is not a population. It is a *presence* — the way a fever is a presence in the body, not a single thing but a systemic hum, a state of being that has its own temperature and rhythm and will not be separated out into causes.

Start with the amphibians — though *amphibian* is a word dragged in from elsewhere, a word that means something in sunlight and pond-mud, and means nothing here. Call them *the sitters*. They do not move with intention. They move the way a rash moves across skin — a local disturbance that propagates. You watch one and it does nothing. You watch a thousand and they are in constant, infinitesimal adjustment, a slow negotiation of space conducted through contact. A foot presses against a ribcage. A body slides half a centimeter to the left and the neighbor compensates within seconds, a ripple moving outward in no preferred direction, thousands of damp translucent bodies participating in a consensus they do not appear to have reached. Their eyes — lidless, faintly opalescent — do not seem to fix on anything. The blood visible through their chests moves in pulses, and the pulses are synchronized when you catch them in clusters, two or three or a dozen animals whose cardiac rhythms have fallen into phase, the way metronomes on a shared shelf will conspire to agree. You do not know what this means. They do not know what this means. It is simply what happens when enough wet hearts beat close enough together for long enough.

They breed in the water. You do not see this but you infer it — the density, the youth of some individuals, the way smaller bodies with disproportionately large heads cling to the undersides of larger ones in arrangements that are not predation and are not symbiosis but something more like *congregation*. The half-grown ones are paler, more translucent — you can see clean through them in places, the light passing grey-gold through tissue so thin it is nearly diagrammatic, every organ suggested rather than solid. They settle where the mineral content in the water is highest, which is everywhere, and they begin to accumulate deposits of their own — tiny encrustations along their spines, along the ridges of their skulls, a mineral mail that has not hardened yet but will, over years, over decades, until some of them are more mineral than tissue and the distinction ceases to be meaningful. You will find fossils here that are not fossils. You will find forms in the stone that are still breathing.

The fliers occupy a different register of this place entirely. They are the ceiling's nervous system. Where the amphibians are slow and packed and continental, the fliers are quick and sparse and electric — though *electric* is a metaphor and they are not, except in the sense that their wingbeats generate a static charge you can feel when you raise your hand and a dozen of them lift from the wall in a small, angry storm. Their wings do not beat so much as *oscillate*, a lateral scissoring motion that produces no lift in the way you understand lift — not Bernoulli, not the angle of attack — but some other principle, something about the density of the air in this vault, its mineral load, its warmth and humidity, that allows these heavy, rigid-winged bodies to stay aloft with what looks like no effort at all. They are not elegant. They are *entitled*. They fly the way water flows downhill — not because they have chosen to but because the physics of the place require it.

They do not eat in any way you recognize. There is no mouth to speak of — just a longitudinal slit along the ventral surface that, when examined closely — and you have examined closely, because one landed on your wrist and stayed there long enough — reveals not a jaw but a filter, a dense mat of cilia that beats in the same synchronized rhythm as the amphibians' hearts. They are drinking the air. They are drinking the moisture, the mineral mist, the machine-exhaust, the spores of each other. They are converting the atmosphere of this vault into tissue, and when they die — and they die constantly, dropping from the ceiling like small dark leaves, landing in the water with a sound like a pin dropping into a drum — their bodies decompose and add themselves to the water's chemistry, which feeds the mineral growth, which feeds the stalactites, which connect to the machines, and the loop closes and tightens and does not care.

The machines are alive. You must accept this, not as metaphor, not as poetic license, but as a factual observation made in the plainest possible terms. The largest of them — the centrifugal housings, the drive-shaft assemblies — hum at a frequency that is not random. They are vibrating in resonance with the geology. The rock itself has a natural frequency, determined by its composition, its geometry, the tension of its fractures, and the machines, left running unattended for decades, have drifted toward it the way a tuning fork drifts toward the pitch of another tuning fork placed nearby. When you press your palm flat against one of the housings — and you do this, because the warmth draws you to it, the warmth that rises from below like breath from a throat — you feel the vibration enter your hand and travel up your arm and settle in your sternum, and for a moment you and the machine and the rock are one system, one oscillation, one body. The machines do not need maintenance. They need *minerals*. The lubrication has been replaced by calcite. The bearings turn in sleeves ofite and dolomite. The electrical connections are maintained not by copper but by deposits of pyrite that conduct — poorly, erratically, but enough, enough to keep the hum alive, enough to keep the indicator lights blinking in amber, most of them, though one — you will notice this — blinks a different color. Not amber. Blue-white. Cold, in a place defined by warmth. A light that does not belong, a frequency that does not match the resonance. You will watch it for a long time. It will not explain itself.

And beneath all of it — beneath the drip and hum and wingbeat and the slow negotiation of bodies packed against stone — there is the *growth*. You can hear it if you are quiet enough, and at first you will think you are imagining it: a faint, crystalline sound, like ice forming on a window, like a very slow sound of something getting harder. It is the sound of calcium carbonate precipitating from the mineral-rich water onto every available surface. It is the sound of stalactites beginning. It is the sound of the machines being *landscaped*, enclosed, entombed in a white and translucent geology that does not care what it covers. There are places where the growth has already completed its work — where a machine cabinet is no longer a machine cabinet but a *formation*, a pale pillar studded with the corroded remains of circuit boards and drive belts, indistinguishable from a stalagmite except that, if you tap it, it rings with a tone that is too pure, too precise, for stone.

This is what lives here. Not creatures, not organisms, not a biome in the sense your textbooks would have you understand it. A *process* that has become self-sustaining, a closed loop of heat and water and mineral and tissue and current and vibration that has, over whatever interval of decades or centuries, begun to behave as a single entity — not conscious, not intentional, but *coherent*, the way a flame is coherent, the way a weather system is coherent. It is here. It is happening. And it does not need you to name it, because naming is a human activity, and you are wearing thin in this place, your boots dissolving slowly at the soles in the mineral-rich water, the air thickening in your lungs with iron and calcium and the sweet-tinny ghost of old oil, your pulse — if you could hear it — beginning, slowly, to fall into rhythm with the rest.

The vault does not welcome you. The vault does not refuse you. The vault continues.
