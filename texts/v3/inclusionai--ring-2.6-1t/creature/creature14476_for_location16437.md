## Inhabitants of the Pallial Fold

---

### The Settlers

They are not one people. They are a season of people. A molt of humanity.

Forty souls—perhaps sixty, no one has held still long enough to count. They arrived through the crack, one by one or two by two, drawn downward through the warm root-membrane the way water follows a thread of least resistance. Some came to stay. Some stayed a winter and slipped back upward into whatever labyrinth they came from, their places taken silently by others who simply appeared on the edge of the platform one morning, damp and ochre-stained and saying nothing.

They call their home Pallia, and they say the word the way you'd say a prayer or a small lie—softly, because the Fold absorbs anything too loud. Twelve structures crowd the platform: arched frames of fast-growing epiphyte bent and pinned with cured tendon, skinned in sheets of bark shed from the organism above, sealed with resin that smells faintly of overripe melon when it's heated over the contained fires. Some shelters are meticulous, their walls taut and smoke-darkened, their joints reinforced with woven root-fiber. Others are barely started—a frame with one membrane wall, a bed of dried sphagnum, a place to put a fire. Some are abandoned altogether, claimed by the fauna or slowly decomposing back into the substrate, and the settlers do not mourn them. They build new ones the way the organism grows new rootlets: because building is what the place requires.

Everything they own is handmade and made of the Fold itself.

Bone tools split from root-spicules with stone hammers. Needles. Hooks. A curved awl with a handle wrapped in tendon-cord. Water vessels shaped from hollowed growths in the root-tissue, stoppered with sphagnum plugs. There is one deposit of copper ore embedded in a root-ridge three years' climb above the platform—soft stuff, too soft for an edge that lasts, and it has been pounded into perhaps twenty fishhooks, a handful of awl-points, and a single blade that sits wrapped in cloth in the oldest settler's shelter and is brought out only for ceremonies no outsider has been invited to watch.

They eat sphagnum-analogue, boiled until it softens into something between porridge and regret. They drink the central lens-water, but only after it has been boiled through a membrane of scraped bark laid over a bed of hot stones—a process that takes hours and produces barely a cup. They harvest the mineral-rich pellets left behind by the grazers on the walls and press them into small cakes that taste of copper and chalk. They fish in the standing water with bone hooks baited with scraps of skitterer carapace, and what they catch are pale, segmented things with too many legs that curl around the hook and have to be shaken off with considerable force.

Their language is mostly not language, not the way anyone from the outside would understand it. The Fold absorbs sound. A voice raised to normal volume becomes a vague hum within four paces, then nothing. So they have adapted in the way that trapped things adapt: they have become quieter than quiet.

They speak in murmur-voice—a register pitched below the room's absorption threshold, so low it travels not through air but through the root-bundles themselves, conducted as vibration rather than sound. To hear them, you must press your palm to the wall. Through the tissue, their speech feels like a second pulse, felt more than heard, a braille of intention pressed into the living architecture.

But murmur-voice is for distance and privacy. For closeness, they use touch-sign: a language of hands on arms, of fingers tapped against bone, of pressure applied to the shoulder in patterns that mean *food*, *danger*, *come*, *the filaments are brightening*, *the heave is coming*, *I am here*. Children—if there are children; no one has confirmed this, but someone is always drawing figures in the condensation on the walls with a finger, and those figures are small, and the figures have hands that are pressed together as if holding something—learn touch-sign before they learn the murmur, the way surface-dwellers learn to babble before they learn syntax.

The oldest settler is a woman whose name, translated roughly, means *the one who stayed*. She arrived seventeen years ago through the crack. She has not left. She tends the fires, repairs the platform's anchor-tendrils, and watches the filaments with an attention so sustained that the other settlers occasionally pause beneath whatever overhang they're occupying and look up at her looking up, as though she were reading something written on the ceiling that they cannot see.

She says the organism is dreaming.

No one contradicts her. Not because they believe her—though some do—but because she has been right before. She predicted the Great Dimming six years ago, the period when every filament in the Fold went dark for three days and the settlers huddled on Pallia in absolute blackness, breathing the sound of their own breath and the faint, wet clicking of thread-wasps adjusting their grip in the dark. She predicted it by the drift in the brightening sequence, a pattern she has been tracking since her first year. She has notebooks—bark-pages bound with tendon, written in a notation that looks like a cross between musical annotation and root-tendril mapping—and in those notebooks the cycles are catalogued with a patience that borders on devotion.

She says the tidal rhythm is a sleep cycle. That the bright pulses are rapid movement of a dreaming mind beneath the surface of the tissue. That they are living inside something's thought.

*The Fold was not hidden to keep you out,* she told the last outsider who made it down through the crack before the passage tightened permanently.

*It was hidden to keep something in.*

### The Skitterers

They are the first thing you notice, and the last thing you stop flinching at.

Six-legged arthropod-analogues, no bigger than your thumb, with carapaces so translucent you can see the dark smudge of their organs shifting inside them as they move. Too many joints in their legs—each limb seems to have at least one bend that does not exist in any anatomy you've been taught, giving them a gait that is less *walking* than *negotiating* with the surface beneath them, as though every step requires them to remind the world that they are allowed to be there.

They are everywhere. Colonies of them cover the floor in patches that shift like weather. They feed on the sphagnum-analogue and on each other and on anything organic that has been recently dead, and their population surges and retreats in seven-day pulses that the settlers have learned to track with the same precision the old woman applies to the filaments.

On days of abundance, the floor crackles underfoot. Not metaphorically—it crackles, a dry, papery sound like someone walking through dead leaves, except the leaves are alive and scattering in every direction at once. You take a step and the ground erupts: hundreds of skitterers throwing themselves outward from your boot, their translucent bodies catching the bioluminescent light and refracting it through their carapaces so that each one becomes, for a fraction of a second, a tiny prism, scattering the weak broth-colored illumination into points of spectral color that hang in the air and vanish.

Then they go dark again. Then they go still. And the floor is just a floor again, until the next footfall.

On days of scarcity, only a few remain—lounging on the buttress-ridges with their faceted eyes cast downward, watching you with an attention that is not hostile but is not friendly either. Just *recording*. Their compound eyes do not blink, but something shifts in the angle of their gaze when you move, and you cannot shake the feeling that you have been catalogued.

### Thread-Wasps

They hang from the ceiling like punctuation marks, long segmented bodies dangling vertically from hooked forelimbs, each individual gripping the vascular tissue with the tenacity of something that has never considered letting go.

A thread-wasp is solitary in form but communal in fact. Each body is a segment of a longer organism—sometimes two, sometimes five or six linked end-to-end in a chain that stretches across the width of the Fold, swaying gently like a strand of kelp in a current that isn't there. They have no visible mouths, no wings, no features beyond the hooked forelimbs and a body that seems to be mostly interior. When segments link, they share something—nutrients, information, the silent communion of a distributed nervous system that no outsider has been able to fully map.

They are silent except when they move. Then the hooks click—a faint, metallic *tik-tik-tik* that is one of the Fold's signature sounds, irregular and spatial, seeming to come from everywhere and nowhere above you. When the heave comes, they chain into longer formations, linking up in sequences the old woman says she has never seen the length of before. Their clicks synchronize then, falling into a rhythm so precise it feels deliberate—as though they are counting, or marking time, or listening to a frequency the settlers cannot hear.

The settlers do not like the synchronized clicking. They say it sounds like something looking for them.

### Grazers

Slow. That is the first and last thing to say about grazers.

They look like snails—bulbous, mucous-coated, trailing faint iridescent slime—but with too many eyes. Eyes on stalks, eyes on nodules, eyes clustered in places eyes have no business being, all of them faintly reflective and all of them aimed at you when you enter their range. There may be twenty. There may be more.

They move across the walls with the inevitability of weather. They consume the bioluminescent filaments, inching along a vascular bundle and leaving behind a darkened trail where the light dies and does not grow back for hours. A large grazer passing through the upper canopy can dim an entire section of the Fold, and the settlers watch their progress the way others watch clouds—calculating, waiting, sometimes redirecting them with long poles tipped with bone so they move toward a part of the wall that is already dim.

They make no sound. You become aware of them only when the light changes, and then you look up and there they are, slow and ancient and indifferent, their too-many eyes catching the weak broth-light of the remaining filaments, and the air thickens with the sweet, mineral smell of their mucus.

### The Mouthers

They rise from the standing water without warning.

A ciliated mouth is a muscular stalk—pale, wet, the color of the inside of your wrist—surmounted by a single opening ringed with fine, hair-like cilia that wave in the water like anemone tentacles. At rest, they are flush with the surface of the lens, indistinguishable from a ripple. When prey passes overhead—something small, a skitterer fallen from above, a fish too slow to evade—they rise with startling speed, the stalk extending, the cilia converging inward, and the opening widens, and anything small enough is simply gone, engulfed in a motion that is less bite than *absorption*.

They close with a soft, wet exhalation—a sigh, almost—that punctuates the Fold's near-silence with a rhythm so faint you might mistake it for the sound of your own breathing. But it isn't. It isn't yours.

The settlers give the central lens a wide berth.

---

### The Winged Ones

You will not see them until you learn to see them, and learning changes you.

Their wings are membranous, veined, folded with a precision that suggests origami performed by something that understands mathematics without language. The wing-surfaces are patterned to replicate the dead tissue of the ceiling and walls with an accuracy that is not mimicry but *translation*—as though the organism has looked at the color and texture and decay of the Fold's surfaces and found them so beautiful that it has reproduced them on its own body.

They fold themselves into the cracks in the root-work and become part of the architecture. You can look directly at them and not see them. Your eye slides off them the way water slides off oil. They are visible only in motion—a blur, a flinch, a twitch of something that was not moving a moment ago—and even then you cannot be certain you saw what you think you saw.

They are quiet. Their wings make no sound. They come uncurled only during the tidal dark-cycle's nadir, when the filaments have dimmed to near-nothing and the Fold is a room of absolute and living dark. In that darkness, they move between the pendant tendrils like thoughts moving through a mind that does not know it is thinking, and if you are still—if you are very still and hold your breath and press your hand against the wall and listen through the murmur-voice of the root-bundles—

You may feel them pass.

Not hear. Not see. Feel. A displacement in the air, thin and cool, the ghost of a wing-beat translated through the wet membrane of the Fold's atmosphere and arriving at your skin as something that is almost touch and almost sound and almost nothing at all.

---

### The Tendrils

Not fauna. Not exactly. The pendant root-tendrils that descend from the ceiling are sensory—or alimentary, or both—and they are among the most unsettling occupants of the Fold because they are *attentive*.

Some are dry and grey, brittle as old vine. Others are furred with the moss-analogue—a velvet-black organism that exudes warmth when you touch it, a warmth that feels personal, directed, almost *welcoming*. The tendrils sway when the fauna pass beneath them, tasting displacement, and they sway too when the settlers murmur through the walls in their touch-sign register. Not in rhythm. In *response*—as though parsing.

No one has tested what happens if the wrong thing is said. Everyone has intuitions.

---

### The Organism

And beneath all of them—the settlers, the skitterers, the thread-wasps, the grazers, the ciliated mouths, the winged ones, the tendrils—beneath all of them is the organism itself.

It does not have a name. The settlers call it *the Fold* or *the architecture* or, in murmur-voice pressed low into the root-bundles, *the dreamer*. Its tissue is the floor, the walls, the ceiling, the vascular bundles that pulse in rhythms that are not yours. It breathes. It dreams. Its bioluminescent filaments flicker in patterns that almost repeat—but never quite, there is always a drift, a migration, a searching quality in the brightening sequence as though the thought beneath the surface is turning, looking for something it has lost.

It is not hostile. It is not kind. It is so large in its slowness that hostility and kindness are categories too small to contain it, the way a glacier cannot be called angry or gentle. It simply persists. It grows. It shelters. It offers warmth, water, light, and food, and it does not ask anything in return except that you do not damage the architecture, do not fight the heave, do not say the wrong thing to the tendrils, and do not stay long enough to forget that you are inside something's thought and that thoughts, like organisms, sometimes wake.

---

*Between all of these—the people, the creatures, the tendrils, and the dreaming organism spinning its slow light behind every surface—there is no silence that is really silence. There is only the Fold, breathing in and breathing out, holding forty lives and ten thousand not-lives in the cupped palm of something that is alive in a way that the word alive*— *spoken here, in the murmur-voice, pressed into the wall—*

*feels like an understatement.*
