**What lives in the gorge – a portrait of the unseen tenants**

---

### 1. The first colonists: mosses and crustose lichens  

The first thing you notice on the damp stone is a thin, almost velvet‑black film that clings to every crack and ledge. These are **rock‑dwelling mosses**—species of *Grimmia* and *Schistidium* that can survive with almost no soil, drawing moisture from the mist that condenses on the walls. Their leaves are no bigger than a pinhead, but together they form a living sponge that holds water for weeks, softening the rock and releasing a faint, sweet‑green scent when the sun finally grazes the gorge.

Intertwined with the mosses are **crustose lichens**—a partnership of a fungus and a photosynthetic alga or cyanobacterium. Their thalli are only a few micrometres thick, yet they secrete acids that etch the stone, creating tiny pits where later, larger plants can take root. In the dim light they appear as a patchwork of ochre, grey‑green, and black, like a living map of the gorge’s chemistry.

---

### 2. The fast‑growers: ferns and herbaceous perennials  

Where a thin layer of organic debris accumulates—often in the hollows left by a recent rockfall—**ferns** unfurl their fronds with astonishing speed. *Asplenium* and *Polystichum* species send up tightly curled fiddleheads that open within days, their fronds a deep, almost black green that seems to glow in the low light. Their rhizomes creep horizontally, anchoring themselves to the stone with tiny, hair‑like holdfasts that secrete a mild adhesive.

Among the ferns, **low‑growing herbaceous perennials**—tiny *Saxifraga* and *Cystopteris*—sprout from crevices, their leaves thick and waxy to retain moisture. They flower only briefly, producing minute white or pale pink blossoms that are pollinated by the wind rather than insects, their pollen drifting like fine dust through the mist.

---

### 3. The hidden engineers: fungi and mycorrhizal networks  

Beneath the visible greenery lies a **mycelial web** that stitches the whole community together. White, thread‑like hyphae of *Trichoderma* and *Mycena* species penetrate the moss, the fern roots, and even the thin soil that forms in pockets of decaying plant matter. These fungi act as both decomposers and nutrient brokers: they break down fallen leaves and dead wood, releasing nitrogen and phosphorus that the plants desperately need, and in return they receive sugars from the photosynthetic partners.

Some fungi form **ectomycorrhizal associations** with the young birch saplings that have somehow taken hold on the north‑facing ledges. The fungal sheath around the roots expands the tree’s absorptive surface, allowing it to tap moisture and minerals from the constantly shifting substrate. In exchange, the birch supplies the fungus with carbohydrates, a silent barter that keeps the whole system alive.

---

### 4. Microscopic tenants: bacteria, algae, and cyanobacteria  

Where water seeps through cracks, thin films of **biofilm** develop—communities of bacteria, green algae, and cyanobacteria that coat the stone in a slick, iridescent sheen. *Cyanobacteria* such as *Nostoc* fix atmospheric nitrogen, turning it into a form usable by the plants above. Their colonies appear as gelatinous, blue‑green blobs that swell after a rain and shrink as the water evaporates, pulsing like tiny, living reservoirs.

In the standing pools that form at the base of temporary waterfalls, **diatoms** and **green algae** thrive, their cell walls siliceous and delicate, turning the water a faint, milky green. These microscopic plants are the first link in a food chain that, for now, ends with the decomposers—fungi and bacteria that recycle the algae back into nutrients for the mosses and ferns.

---

### 5. Epiphytes and the vertical garden  

On the higher, more exposed ledges where soil is virtually absent, **epiphytic plants** have taken to the moss itself. Tiny *Tillandsia*‑like bromeliads (though not true bromeliads, they are convergent) cling to the mossy surface, their leaves forming small cups that trap rainwater. Inside those cups, a miniature ecosystem develops: a few millimetres of water hosts rotifers, tardigrades, and bacterial colonies that feed on detritus, while the plant absorbs the dissolved nutrients through its leaves.

Even the **young birch and alder saplings** that sprout from rock crevices become hosts to a thin layer of **bryophytes**—tiny liverworts and hornworts—that carpet their bark, adding another layer of moisture retention and surface area for fungal hyphae.

---

### 6. The rhythm of life and death  

Everything here moves on a **fast, relentless clock**. A fern frond may unfurl in a single afternoon, only to be buried by a fresh slide of scree an hour later. Mosses that have taken months to establish can be scraped away by a sudden rush of water, yet within days new spores, carried on the wind, settle into the same crevice and begin again. Fungi spread their mycelium at a rate of a few centimetres per day, constantly seeking new sources of organic matter, while bacterial colonies double in number within hours when moisture is abundant.

Death is as immediate as growth. Fallen leaves are colonised by saprophytic fungi within minutes of hitting the damp floor, breaking down into a dark, crumbly humus that is quickly incorporated into the thin soil. Even the stones themselves are being digested—acidic lichen secretions and bacterial weathering slowly pit the rock, preparing it for the next generation of roots.

---

### 7. The sound of living stone  

Though no animal ears hear it, the gorge hums with the **acoustic signatures of life**. The constant drip of water striking moss‑covered rock creates a soft, percussive patter, like a thousand tiny drums. Wind moving through the narrow throat vibrates the thin, flexible stems of the ferns, producing a low, almost sub‑sonic hum that blends with the deeper resonance of falling water. When a rockslide occurs, the sudden release of tension in the stone sends a low‑frequency boom that travels through the mycelial network, causing a brief, collective shiver in the root systems of nearby plants—a silent, subterranean ripple of response.

---

In this newly‑born canyon, life is not a gentle, pastoral tableau. It is a **turbulent, self‑organising engine**—mosses and lichens paving the way, ferns and saplings racing to fill every gap, fungi stitching the pieces together, and microscopic organisms cycling nutrients at a breathless pace. Together they form a living scaffold that clings to the raw, still‑shifting stone, turning a wound in the mountain into a churning, green, ever‑changing organism of its own.
