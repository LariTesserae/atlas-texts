## What Lives in the Sunk Ward of Khael-Tharros

---

### The Tendril-Kind — what the Ward has become

You must understand first that nothing here is separate. That is the thing the upper-city scholars get wrong when they send their expeditions down with clipboards and specimen jars and the quiet confidence of people who believe they are visiting a place. The Sunk Ward is not a place. It is a condition. And what lives here has been, in some sense, living here since before the first stone was cut — or perhaps since the first stone was cut, which may be the same thing.

The root-matter is what you will think of first, and you will be right to think of it, though no name you can give it will hold all of what it is. It coats the walls. It fills the joints of every corridor. It has threaded itself through the steel in patterns that are not random, not decorative, and not mechanical — something in between, something that looks like a map drawn by a mind that understands connectivity but not geometry. It is pale where it is dry, deep amber where the water touches it, and in certain chambers it has grown so thick that the original architecture beneath is a rumor, a ghost-shape beneath a living skin.

It moves. Not quickly. Not in any way you would call moving if you were watching a single tendril. But return to a corridor after an hour — after a night, if you can sleep here — and it is closer. Not always. Not in every direction. But it comes toward warmth. It comes toward sound. It comes toward *presence*. A colleague once set down a lantern in a side passage and returned three hours later to find the root-matter had grown around its base in a thin, pale sleeve, as if it were trying to hold the lantern, or as if it had simply been curious about what warmth it gave off, and had answered that curiosity by becoming part of the thing it was curious about.

Whether it is one organism or many is a question that dissolves the longer you spend here. In some sections, the root-matter is clearly continuous — a single thread you can trace from one wall to the floor to the ceiling to another wall, unbroken, patient, as if it simply never stopped. In others, there are breaks, separations, pale scars where something was cut or burned or simply withdrew. And sometimes — and this is the thing that will stay with you — you will find a section that is warm to the touch. Not the ambient warmth of the Ward, which is everywhere, but specifically warm, as though something just beneath the surface is generating heat. Pulsing, almost. Slowly. Like a heartbeat, if a heartbeat could take four minutes to complete.

It is aware of you. That is not a metaphor. Stand still long enough and you will feel it — not a sound, not a sight, but a *shift* in the texture of the space, as though the attention of the place has moved toward you the way a slowly rotating eye turns to fix on a source of light. You will not see anything change. But the water nearby will get warmer. The root-matter on the nearest wall will be, when you check, a fraction of an inch closer than it was. And the paths marked by the chemical lights — those sealed amber tubes bolted to the walls, those ancient and still-functioning markers — will still be there. Still glowing. Still tended. Because someone tends them. Something tends them.

The root-matter tends them. Or tends the things that tend them. Or is tended *by* them. The relationship is circular, and the circle is so old it has worn smooth.

### The Pale Swimmers

In the flooded corridors — and most of the Ward is flooded, chest-deep in warm water that tastes of copper and chalk — there are creatures. You will see them before you name them, and you will not name them easily.

They are small. Six inches, sometimes a foot. Translucent — not like glass but like skin, like the tissue you hold up to a light and see the shadow of blood within. Their organs move inside them, dark shapes drifting in clear fluid, and you will watch this and try not to think about it for too long because there is something in the rhythm of it that is too much like breathing.

They have no eyes. There are no eyes, no vestigial eyes, no eye-spots, no light-sensitive patches that might once have been eyes. Where the head should be — and "head" is generous; they are not built like vertebrates, not exactly, but they are built like *something* that once knew what a head was — there is smooth, pale tissue with a seam down the middle that opens and closes in a slow, rhythmic contraction. Mouth? Gill? Sensor? You will not know.

They navigate by vibration. You will feel them before you see them — a faint thrumming in the water around your legs, a displacement that moves against your skin like a current that isn't a current. When you wade through a school of them, they part around you with a precision that is not mechanical, not animal, but something between — a murmuration of pale bodies moving as one organism, separating, reforming, each fish-shaped translucence turning at the exact same angle, the exact same moment, responding to a signal you cannot feel but can only trust is there.

They are not afraid of you. They are not unafraid. They are indifferent in the way that something can be indifferent only if it does not conceive of you as separate. A school will part for your body and close behind you, and if you stand still for long enough, some of them will drift back and settle around you — not touching, not circling, just *near*, as though your body has become a feature of the current, a warm obstruction in the flooded corridor, and they have simply adapted.

Where they go when they leave you, you will not follow.

### The Deep Ones — what moves in the lowest water

There is a level of the Ward that the chemical lights do not reach. The amber glow fades in the upper corridors, then in the mid-levels, then — if you descend far enough, past the flooded residential wards, past the shell of the pumping station with its barnacled impellers still turning in the dark — there is a level where the bioluminescent organisms are all that remain. Blue-green, cold, shifting. The water here is so thick with them that it glows from within, and you are walking through light that is alive.

And in this light, there are things.

They do not come close. That is the first thing. Whatever they are, they do not come close, and this is the only mercy the Ward extends to you, because what can be seen of them from a distance is already too much.

Too many limbs. Not in the way of an insect, which has too many limbs but in a way you can understand, can taxonomize, can hold at arm's length in your mind. These limbs are wrong in *number* — not eight, not ten, but something between, something that your brain tries to count and cannot, the way you try to count the exact number of stars in a cloud. They move in the water with a slow, deliberate grace, and each limb seems to know where it is without the creature needing to see — which it cannot, because there is nothing that resembles an eye anywhere on the visible surfaces, and the visible surfaces are not all the surfaces, because the water is dark and deep and what you see is only what the blue-green light allows.

They are large. Larger than you. You know this by the displacement — when one passes beneath you, there is a current, a shift in the warmth of the water, a slow roll of pressure against your body that communicates *mass* and *distance* and something else. Something like intention.

They do not flee. They do not approach. They pass. And after they pass, the water settles, and the bioluminescent organisms dim where the limbs have moved through them, leaving dark trails that slowly refill with light, like footprints in disturbed snow, and you stand there in the blue-green glow and you feel, with a certainty that has nothing to do with evidence, that you have been *seen*.

Not watched. Not observed. *Seen*. The way a room sees you when you enter it. The way a hand feels a shape in the dark.

### The Engines — what still breathes

Do not think of them as machines. They were machines. The steel is still there — rusted, barnacled, encrusted with calcium deposits a quarter-inch thick — and the pistons still move, and the drive shafts still turn, and the flywheel in the central chamber still completes its revolution so slowly that you must watch it for twenty minutes to confirm it has moved at all. But something else maintains them now.

You will hear them before you find them. A deep, resonant exhalation that comes from below — from the deepest level, from wherever the geothermal vents open beneath the foundations. It is not mechanical. It is a sound the way a breath is a sound: not produced, but released. The air around the engine rooms shimmers with heat, and the condensation gathers on every surface and drips into channels that were cut into the stone by hands that are gone, and the channels still work, and the water still flows through them, and you do not follow them because something in the geometry of the place — the angle of the corridors, the placement of the walls, the spacing of the chemical lights — suggests they go somewhere that was not meant for you.

The engines are alive with the root-matter. It has grown into them — into the joints, the pistons, the crevices between gears. And the engines, in turn, sustain it: their heat feeds the warmth that the root-matter seeks, and the vibrations of the pistons feed the signals the root-matter responds to. They are symbiotic now. Or perhaps they have become the same thing. The boundary is architectural and biological at once — a ribcage of riveted iron enclosing a slow metabolism that has been running for longer than anyone on the surface knows.

Someone tends the engines. You know this because the chemical lights leading to the engine rooms are always maintained — shattered tubes rebuilt with resin and stone, dead junctions replaced, paths kept clear. But you never see who. You arrive and the maintenance is done. You wait and watch and nothing comes. Either the work is done in the spaces between your attention — the blind corners, the sealed chambers, the flooded sections you cannot enter — or it is done by the root-matter itself, slowly, patiently, over months and years, each tendril a hand, each growth a repair.

### What It Is Like to Be Here

You will begin to feel it after a day — if you are staying, you are staying. The warmth is the first thing. Not discomfort. A recognition. As though the Ward remembers the temperature of your body and is adjusting itself to hold you. The water at your ankles is warmer than it was. The air is thicker. The root-matter on the nearest wall is a shade closer.

Then sounds. Not the Ward's sounds — those are constant, ambient, as much a part of the architecture as the stone. But the other sounds. A voice, perhaps, far away, speaking a language you almost recognize. The mechanical clicking of a typewriter in a sealed room. A child humming. These are not ghosts. Ghosts imply a death and a remaining. What is happening here is something else. The Ward is *remembering*. The acoustics of the place — the way water conducts, the way the root-matter resonates, the way the cathedral chambers fold sound back on itself and delay it and alter it — the Ward is replaying fragments of itself, the way a body replays sensations it has stored in tissue. The sounds are not messages. They are *residue*.

You will sleep eventually. The Ward permits this — even encourages it. There are dry pockets, sealed behind pressure doors that someone welded shut long ago, where the air is still and warm and tastes like the inside of a lung. You will curl up on the luminous lichen, which is warm beneath you like a stone in summer, and you will hear the engines breathing below you, and the water moving around the edges of the door, and the root-matter creaking — slowly, so slowly — and you will sleep, and when you wake you will not know how long you have been asleep, because the Ward does not mark time. It does not have days. It has cycles — thermal, chemical, biological — that are longer and shorter than a day and do not correspond to anything above. The chemical lights do not dim. The bioluminescence does not fade. The engines do not stop.

You are not a prisoner here. You can leave. The drainage culvert still leads to the surface. The forbidden hatch can be opened. But you will find, if you reach the surface, that something has changed. You will hear the silence of the upper city as a kind of absence. The air will feel thin. The light will be too bright and too empty. And in the quiet moments — standing in your quarters, walking through streets that have never known warm water or phosphorescent glow — you will feel a warmth at your ankles that has no source, and you will know that something is still growing inside you, pale and patient, reaching toward whatever warmth you carry.

The Ward does not let you go because it has let you go. It has given you something — a filament, a thread, a tendril of itself that has wound into your architecture the way it winds into steel and stone. You are part of it now. Not consumed. Not possessed. Simply *included*, the way a new corridor is included in a building — not planned, exactly, but accepted, integrated, made part of the structure by the simple fact of its presence.

You are not unwelcome here. You were never unwelcome here. You are warm, and the Ward is warm, and warmth is the only language it speaks, and you have already learned it.
