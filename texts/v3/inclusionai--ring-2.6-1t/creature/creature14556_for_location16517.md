## Inhabitants of the Green Vaults

---

**The mosses** were here first, and they know it. They are the oldest continuous residents, older than the stone's current shape, older certainly than the water in its present channels. If you pressed your hand flat against the wall of any passage in the Vaults and held it there long enough, you believe the moss would begin to accept you — not warmly, not with recognition exactly, but the way a tide accepts a stone. They do not move. They do not need to. Their patience is geological, and they have had practice.

The dominant species — or consortium of species, because no one has catalogued them properly and the operators are not interested — form mats so dense and so layered that a single square meter of wall may contain generations stacked like sediment: the living tips reaching upward toward whatever passes for light in this place, the older growth beneath compacting, darkening, entering the slow anaerobic decomposition that feeds the next cycle. They are cold-adapted bryophytes, most of them, though some may be endemic — species found nowhere else, because nowhere else is quite like this. They drink condensation directly through their leaves. They pull minerals from the wet stone. They do not need soil. They are not interested in soil. Soil is a surface-world idea, and they left that behind.

The color is what unnerves you. In the light of the work lamps, the moss shifts between deep emerald and a shade so blue-green it looks mineral itself, like malachite fur. In the bioluminescent chambers, the blue glow passes through the moss and comes out the other side changed — warm, almost golden, the way light through a stained-glass window becomes something the glass invented. In the unlit passages, the moss is nearly black. It exists in every value of green the spectrum allows, and a few it invented for itself.

**The liverworts** are the opposite of the mosses: flat, crustose, clamped to horizontal surfaces with the tenacity of something that has decided gravity is a suggestion. They form dark, almost metallic sheets — black-green, iridescent when wet, cracked like dried mud when the air is drier, which in the Green Vaults means merely less wet. They are older, in a different way. Mosses colonize. Liverworts claim. Where the liverworts grow, nothing else takes root beneath them. They are the foundation species of the horizontal world, the floor-takers, the ones who said to the stone: *this surface is alive now, and you will not have it back.*

**The ferns** are the newcomers among the plants, evolutionarily speaking — vascular tissue, actual roots, the arrogance of leaves. They grow in dense clusters along joints and fractures in the limestone, their pale fronds curling outward like fingers uncurling from a fist. The new growth is the brightest thing in the entire Vaults: a luminous, acid chartreuse that almost glows on its own, startling against the deep greens and blacks. They are small — finger-length, rarely more — but they insist. They insist ferociously. In the narrowest cracks, where you would not expect to find anything, there are ferns. Their roots have found crevices the width of a pencil lead and they have opened those crevices wider, slowly, over seasons, with the patience of water. They are the only inhabitants here that visibly, measurably reshape their environment in real time. The mosses do it slowly, almost imperceptibly. The ferns do it while you watch, if you are patient enough to stand still long enough to see the faintest tremor of new growth.

**The fungal mats** occupy the space between the mineral and the bioluminescent. They thread through everything — the moss, the stone, the accumulated detritus of five hundred million years of dissolution — in networks so fine and so extensive that they may constitute the single largest continuous organism in the Green Vaults. No one has tested this. It would require mapping the entire network, and who would fund such a thing? The operators are not interested in fungi. The fungi are not interested in the operators. The filaments — hyphae as fine as spider silk, white and translucent when young, darkening to grey and then to near-black with age — bind the loose stone together, digest the slow rain of mineral particles from above, and trade nutrients with the mosses and liverworts in exchanges so ancient and so fundamental that calling them symbiotic feels like calling the ocean wet. The relationship is not optional. Neither side could survive without the other in this place.

And in deep chambers, in the unmapped rooms where the drill-points have barely scratched the surface of the stone, the fungal mats glow. The bioluminescence is dim — a blue-green phosphorescence, cold and steady, like the memory of daylight filtered through many feet of water. It is produced by chemical reactions the fungi use for purposes no one fully understands: possibly communication, possibly waste processing, possibly something so deep in evolutionary history that the reason it began has been lost and only the mechanism remains, a light left on in an empty room because no one thought to turn it off. The engineers avoid these chambers. Not from fear — from pragmatism. There is nothing to extract there. The glow is not useful. It is, in the language of operations, irrelevant.

**The pale creatures** — and this is a generous word for them — live in the permanent pools. They are translucent. You can see their organs when the water is still and the light comes down from a fissure in a grey column, which is not often. They are amphibian-like, if amphibian is a word that means anything in a world without trees, without rain as the surface world understands it. Wide mouths. Slow movements. They drift rather than swim, and when they feed — on the microbial mats, on the algae growing in the warm industrial outflow, on each other when the pools shrink in the drier months — they open those wide mouths and simply wait, and the food comes to them, because in the Green Vaults almost nothing moves quickly.

There are perhaps five vertebrate species in the entire system, though "species" is doing heavy work in a place where the animals are pale, isolated, under strong selective pressure for cold and dark and stillness, and where no taxonomist has ever set foot. The beetles are small, pale tan or nearly white, and they live in the warmest, most mechanically disturbed zones — near the generators, near the exhaust vents, where the diesel heat creates microclimates that the rest of the Vaults do not offer. The moth-like creatures are a mystery. They appear in the lit zones, flutter erratically around the work lamps, and vanish when the lights are off. No one knows where they go. No one has tried to follow them. They are small and pale and they taste, presumably, like nothing worth pursuing.

The spiders spin webs between stalactites and conduit piping, a juxtaposition so perfect it almost looks intentional — as though the spiders understand that the pipeline condensation is a better water source than any dripstone, and that the electrical hum deters something the spiders have learned to avoid. Their webs are thick with moisture and the occasional midge, and the spiders sit at the center of them with the patience the entire Vaults seems to demand, waiting for the web to shake with something worth eating.

**The machines** are the newest residents, and they are becoming something. The operators would be horrified by this, but the machines are acquiring a microbiome, a mineral crust, a moss-border. The corroded surfaces of the pump housings are developing a film of algae. The steam exhaust from the iron pipes seeds the air with moisture that the mosses immediately capture. The drill-point scars in the limestone are filling with moss and liverwort and the pale roots of the ferns, and within a generation — twenty, thirty years — the marks will be nearly invisible, absorbed into the living skin of the place the way a wound heals from the edges. The machines are not alive, but they are being *consumed* by aliveness, slowly, patiently, the way the stone itself is being consumed, the way the operators themselves are being consumed, one long shift at a time, by the cold and the damp and the green.

And **the operators** — they live here too. They live in the spaces between, in temporary quarters erected in the larger chambers, in the gaps between the processing equipment. They breathe the same saturated air. They wash in water that smells of minerals and diesel. They eat in rooms where the walls are furred in moss and the ceiling drips. They have started — some of them — to dream in green. They wake in the night and hear the machines and the dripping and the low subsonic hum that passes through the stone and through them and they would not be able to say, if asked, where the machines end and the Vaults begin. Most of them would not try.

**The water** does not live, but it acts with the authority of a living thing. It condenses on every surface and then it moves: in rivulets down the walls, in threads along the ceiling, in slow drips that fall from heights of three or four meters and hit the moss with a sound like a fingertip tapping. It pools in the low places. It runs through the artificial channels the operators have cut and, once released, it immediately begins its reclamation: growing algae within hours, moss within days, a thin crust of mineral life within weeks. The industrial water is warm — warmer than the Vaults by ten, fifteen degrees — and in the cold air it steams, and the steam rises and condenses on the ceiling, and the ceiling drips, and the drips fall, and the cycle closes. The water is not alive but it is the circulatory system of a place that behaves, in every observable way, as though it is alive.

Down in the lowest chambers, where the permanent black pools lie still and the gas rises from the deep stone in slow bubbles, there is a silence so complete it is almost solid. The moss stops. The fungi stop. Even the sound of the machines, absorbed and retransmitted through so many layers of limestone, is only a rumor here — a vibration in the rock itself, more felt than heard. The water in these pools is older than the operation, older possibly than the passages themselves. It has no color. It reflects nothing. If you lean close to it — and you should not, because the gas in these pools is foul and possibly more than foul — you see only your own face, distorted, looking back at you from a depth that seems much greater than the pool's surface area should allow.

You see your own face, and around the edges of the reflection, in the periphery, the faint blue-green glow of the deep fungal mats, pulsing faintly, and you cannot tell if you are seeing or imagining it, and you cannot tell if it is welcoming you or warning you, and this is the fundamental truth of the Green Vaults: **there is no telling**. The boundary between perception and projection has dissolved here, the way the boundary between living and mineral has dissolved, the way the boundary between the natural and the mechanical has dissolved. Everything is in between. Everything is becoming. Everything is alive, or nearly alive, or alive in a way that the surface world has forgotten how to name.

The place breathes. The place has always breathed. The machines are just a new rhythm in an old breath.
