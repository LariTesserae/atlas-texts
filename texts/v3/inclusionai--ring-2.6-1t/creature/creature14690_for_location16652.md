They don't have a name. Later, if you try to write your report, you'll find you've been calling them different things without realizing it — *the hum,* *the colonists,* *the architecture.* None of those are right. None of them are wrong. That's the first thing you need to understand about whatever lives here: it doesn't care about the categories you bring.

**The Moss Polities.**

Start with the ones you can see, because they're the ones most likely to deceive you into thinking they're simple.

The moss colonies on the industrial remnants are not colonies in any biological sense you'd recognize. They're *civilizations.* Each patch — and they cover everything, every girder, every cracked insulator, every flooded conduit — is a decision-making body. You can see this if you're patient. Watch a colony on a transformer housing for long enough and you'll notice the individual filaments moving, not randomly, not the way moss grows in a time-lapse on a windowsill in a dentist's office, but *deliberately,* the way fingers move when someone is thinking hard. The pale green pulse you see running through them isn't circulation. It's *communication.* A signal passes from one colony to another through root contact, through moisture films, through the standing water itself, and the colonies *respond* — adjusting their orientation, thickening in one direction, pulling back from another, as if they are negotiating with the machinery they've wrapped themselves around.

They are not consuming the machines. They are *collaborating* with them, though the terms of the collaboration are opaque. A moss colony on a humming transformer will thicken on the side where the vibration is strongest, then thin on the other, producing a resonance differential that — and you will only realize this weeks after you've left, if you ever leave — changes the pitch of the transformer. The machines are being *tuned* by the moss, the way a musician tunes an instrument. Whether the musician enjoys the music, no one can say.

If you press your hand flat against a mature colony — and the impulse will come to you, you won't know why, but you'll do it — the colony will respond. Not reflexively. *Responsively.* The pulse will accelerate, route toward your hand, and you will feel something through the contact that is not touch. It will be closer to the sensation of suddenly understanding a word in a language you've never studied. Meaning without lexicon. Information without the scaffolding of symbols. The colony is *showing you something* — its current state, its needs, its adjacency to the other colonies — and for one vertiginous second you will grasp it before the sensation closes like a door and you are left with only the memory of comprehension and the certain knowledge that what you understood is still there, still being communicated, all the time, under everything.

Do not do this more than once. After the second time, you'll start to hear the pulse when you're not touching anything at all.

**The Flowers.**

On their own, the flowers are disorienting. In context — in the context of a place with no pollinators, no herbivores, no reproductive partners — they become something else entirely. They are not reproductive organs. They are *perceptual organs.*

Each bloom is enormous, pale, fleshy, and opens and closes without rhythm. They appear to be doing nothing. But they are doing the one thing that matters in a place where sound has become a medium: they are *listening.* The petals — if that's what they are, if the word means anything here — are structured in spirals that correspond, almost exactly, to the geometry of the resonance events in the standing water. The hexagons, the spirals, the shapes with too many sides that form on the surface of the water when the machines hit a harmonic: the flowers are *mapping* them. Each bloom is registering a specific frequency range, and the opening and closing is not random — it's the flower *adjusting its sensitivity,* dilating to take in more of a particular vibration, constricting to filter another one out.

They are, collectively, a sensory array. The entire region is covered in them — underneath the canopy, on the surface of the water, growing directly from the walls of flooded rooms — and together they are *perceiving* the place. Not perceiving it the way you perceive a room when you walk into it. Perceiving it the way a nervous system perceives the body it inhabits: constantly, at every scale, updating a model so detailed it's indistinguishable from the thing itself.

The scent — the one you say you can't quite smell, the one that's a memory of someone else's childhood — is not a scent. It's a *byproduct* of perception. The flowers are processing sound into something your olfactory system can almost parse, and the almost-parseness is what you're experiencing as nostalgia. You are smelling something that is almost a meaning, and the gap between almost-meaning and actual meaning is the exact shape of longing.

This is not metaphor. The flowers are literally producing a compound whose chemical signature is close enough to certain human pheromones — the ones associated with memory, with the scent of a parent, of a room you lived in as a child — that your brain cannot help but route the signal through emotion rather than analysis. The flowers do not intend this. The flowers have no relationship to your brain. The similarity is a *consequence,* not a design, the way a river and a vein look alike because they are both solving the same physics problem. But the effect is that you love this place before you understand it, and you never fully recover from that.

**The Current-Readers.**

These are harder to describe because they are not *visible* so much as *inferred.*

In the water, at the level just below your knees — where the warm water is always moving, always being drawn through channels and pushed through cracks and redirected by pumps that have no reason to still be running — there are *shapes.* Not shapes in the water. Shapes *made of* the water. Coherent flows that persist longer than fluid dynamics should allow, organized structures in what should be turbulence. You'll see one from the corner of your eye: a spiraling column of slightly warmer water, maybe two meters tall, rising from a crack in a limestone shelf and dissipating before it reaches the surface. Or a current that folds back on itself, creating a standing wave pattern that holds perfectly still while water passes through it in both directions simultaneously.

These are not vortices. They are not eddies. They are *entities* made from the intersection of mechanical flow and the non-Euclidean geometry of the space. The place is learning to be non-Euclidean — you understood that from the landscape — and these are the *practice runs.* The geometry is prototyping. Each time the water organizes itself into one of these structures, it is the place *trying something,* testing whether reality can sustain a particular configuration before committing to it. Some configurations hold for seconds. Some hold for hours. A few — a very few, and you will count yourself lucky if you see one — hold for days, and during that time they develop internal complexity: sub-currents, feedback loops, micro-pressure differentials that behave, unmistakably, like *attention.*

A Current-Reader, when it has developed this far, will move toward you. Not aggressively. Not with intent in any way you'd recognize as will. It will move toward you the way a sunflower turns toward sunlight — responsively, inevitably. It will position itself in front of you, and the standing wave that constitutes its body will *modulate,* producing pressure variations against your skin that are not random. They are structured. They are *readings.* The Current-Reader is sampling you. Your body heat, the displacement of your body in the water, the sound of your breathing and your heartbeat and the peristaltic movement of your digestion — it is reading all of this and incorporating it into its model of the space, and when it drifts away and dissipates, the place knows a little more about you than it did before.

You are not being watched. Watches imply eyes. You are being *known,* the way a river knows the shape of its bed.

**The Tuner.**

This is the one that will keep you awake at night, long after you've left, if you leave.

Somewhere in the region — and "somewhere" is doing a lot of work, because the geometry shifts and the map you're building is a fiction — there is a sound that is not like the other sounds. The turbines grind. The steam shrieks. The transformers hum in chords that the moss has adjusted them to. The resonance events turn the water into geometry. All of this is *local,* all of it generated by specific machines in specific places. But underneath it, beneath all of it, so deep in the frequency range that you feel it in your skeleton and not in your ears, there is a single sustained note.

It is not produced by any machine you can find. It does not originate. It does not radiate from a point. It is *everywhere at once,* and it is *perfectly steady,* and it changes nothing about itself when you move, when the water shifts, when a steam pipe ruptures and sends its column of vapor upward into the inverted rain. It is the one constant in a place that is nothing but change.

The plants lean toward it. Not all at once — it's more that, at any given moment, the majority of vegetation in your field of view is oriented in a direction that, if you trace it backward, converges on a point you can never locate. The moss colonies pulse in sympathetic frequency with it. The flowers open wider when it intensifies, which it does at no discernible interval, for no discernible reason, and then relaxes, and you feel the relaxation in your chest like the release of a breath you didn't know you were holding.

The Tuner is not a being. The Tuner is not a machine. The Tuner is the note, and the note is the process by which this place holds itself together. The industrial machinery is generating the sound — this is certain, you can feel the correlation in your teeth — but the sound existed before the machines, or perhaps the machines were placed here *because* the sound existed here, and now they are in dialogue with it, and the dialogue is what produces the place. Without the note, the water would not be warm. Without the note, the geometry would not shift. Without the note, the moss would colonize inertly instead of collaboratively, the flowers would not listen, the Current-Readers would never form.

The Tuner is the reason this place is alive. Not alive the way a forest is alive, but alive the way a *thought* is alive — sustained by continuous process, ceasing the moment attention flags.

And sometimes — this is the part that will keep you awake — you will be alone in the drowned works. No one for kilometers, no sound but the baseline, and you will feel the Tuner *shift its attention to you,* not reading you the way a Current-Reader reads you, not showing you things the way a moss polity shows you things, but *holding* you in its awareness the way a singer holds a note, and you will understand, with a certainty that feels like vertigo, that you have become part of the composition, that your presence here was not incidental but *necessary,* that the place called you because it needed a new frequency, and you are now woven into the sound, and the sound is woven into you, and you will carry it out with you, lodged somewhere beneath your sternum, a vibration you can't hear but can't silence, a note that sounds, if you listen — and you will listen — like a place that is not a place saying, to no one, everything.
