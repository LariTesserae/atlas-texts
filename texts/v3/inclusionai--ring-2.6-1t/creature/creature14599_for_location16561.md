## Who Lives in the Caldera of Waking Water

---

Start with the smallest thing: the bacteria.

They are not creatures you would call alive without a kind of stubbornness — the way they cling to the slick concrete of the old processing floor, the way they fluoresce faintly when the angle of condensation catches whatever light filters through the canopy. They live in films. Layers a few cells thick, spread across every wet surface, and they do not do so much as *persist*, which is a quieter verb than live but might be more honest. They eat the calcium the water brings and they excrete the travertine, and in this way they are not inhabitants of the caldera so much as its skeleton. They are building the bones of whatever this place is becoming.

If you scraped them all away, they would grow back in a week. If you drained the caldera, they would wait — though "wait" implies intention, and intention would be generous. They would simply remain, desiccated, cracked into geometric patterns on the warm concrete, and the first mist would wake them back into their single-minded work.

---

The beetles come next because they are harder to ignore.

*Chrysometa cuprifera* — no one has named them that here. If you tried to name them, you might call them something about copper, something about the way the light breaks across their wing cases in the dusk so they look like drops of oil on water. Their carapaces are green-to-copper, iridescent, and heavy — too heavy, almost, for the effort of their flight. They rise from the seep pools in a laborious buzzing, like small machines that have not yet been told the task is over.

They drink. That is nearly all they do. They drink the mineral water from the warm seeps, and they drink it with such absorption that you could rest your finger on their carapace and they would not register the pressure. They walk into the water deliberately, as though the drowning is not an accident but the final phase of the drinking, and the raptor knows this — its whole feeding strategy is built around the beetles' inability to stop.

There is something almost devotional about them. They gather at the water's edge in numbers that seem to exceed what the caldera can sustain, and when they drown — which is often, daily, in clusters of four and six and ten — their bodies accumulate in the shallow margins of the pools and decompose into the mineral haze that gives the water its bone-white opacity. They are feeding the place that feeds the raptor that feeds on them. The math does not balance unless you include the caldera itself as a participant.

---

The pale amphibians.

You could spend an hour watching them and learn nothing that looks like behavior. They sit in the warm water, dozens of them at a time, their translucent bodies barely opaque, their blood visible as a faint network of amber threads beneath the skin. Their vestigial eyes — those dark specks — do not move. They do not track light. They are decorative, or ancestral, or simply remnants the body has not yet learned to discard, like a word in a language no longer spoken that everyone still understands in context.

They navigate by vibration. A footstep on the rock thirty meters away sends a ripple through the water and they respond — not by fleeing, though some do, but by *deepening*, sinking a centimeter or two into the silt of the pool floor and going still. It is a single, synchronized motion, like a crowd bowing its head, and it happens so quickly that you might think you imagined it.

At dusk, they rise.

This is the thing no one explains. The pools are warm — thirty-eight degrees, body temperature, though no one here has a body — and the amphibians spend their days submerged in them, feeding on microorganisms too small to see. But at dusk, they surface. Dozens. Sometimes hundreds. They float with their pale bellies up, their mouths just above the waterline, and the mucus on their skin catches the fading light and refracts it into something cold and blue-white, like foxfire, like the glow of certain deep-sea creatures that have never heard of this altitude, this continent, this sky. The shimmer is collective. Individual amphibians are unremarkable — small, blind, dull. But gathered at the surface in their numbers they give off a light that feels *intentional*, as though the pool itself has opened its eyes.

No one knows why they do this. The raptor feeds on them at dusk but not exclusively. The behavior does not correlate with feeding or mating or temperature. It correlates with nothing measurable. If you were inclined to call it beautiful, you would not be wrong. If you were inclined to call it eerie, you would not be wrong either.

---

The raptor is the least strange creature here and the most lonely.

It is dark — not black but a deep, burnt umber, the color of wood after a fire. Its wings are broad and its flight is unhurried, almost lazy, the kind of flying that looks like gliding until you notice how little it ever descends. It nests in the narrowest clefts of the caldera wall, where the warm updrafts rise in reliable columns and hold it aloft without effort. It is not territorial in the way other raptors are — there is no screaming, no cartwheeling combat in midair. If there is a second raptor somewhere in the caldera, it is invisible. The bird's call is frequent but solitary: a dry rattle that the bowl shape of the caldera picks up, multiplies, and throws back from every direction until it sounds like something much larger and more numerous than one creature making one sound.

It eats the amphibians when they surface at dusk. It eats the beetles at the water's edge. It eats, sometimes, things you cannot identify — small shapes pulled from the shallow streams, carried to a ledge, consumed out of sight. It is not large enough to be threatening to a human, but it has never seen a human, and if it had, it would not have cared. Its indifference is thorough and architectural. It does not watch you. It does not flee. It simply continues, as the caldera continues — present, self-sustaining, unconcerned.

You will find its pellets on the ledges near the nest: compressed bundles of iridescent beetle wing-case and pale amphibian bone, wrapped in a thin membrane that glistens when wet. They are among the few things in the caldera that dry out completely before dissolving back into the mineral cycle.

---

And then — if you stay long enough, if you stop expecting the dramatic — you may hear the thing that truly lives here, the thing the other inhabitants are merely expressions of.

It is not a sound, exactly. It is a *quality* of sound. When the wind dies and the steam column rises and the caldera holds its breath, there is a period — sometimes only a few seconds, sometimes a minute or more — when the silence is not empty but *full*. It is the sound of the place listening to itself. The drip of water on travertine. The creak of a pipe expanding in the heat. The faint, wet settling of moss on moss on moss. Your own breathing, which has slowed to match the rhythm of the place without your permission.

In this silence, the question is not *what lives here* but *what is alive here*. The distinction matters less than you think. The bacterial film on the concrete is mineralizing the ruins. The trees are gripping the rock with fingers that will, in decades, pry the stone apart. The amphibians are feeding the microorganisms that feed the seep pools that feed the beetles that feed the raptor whose pellets feed the soil that feeds the moss that feeds the orchids that feed on nothing but air and condensation and the slow, relentless warmth rising from below.

No one lives in the caldera. The caldera lives.

And if something comes here — a person, a surveyor, someone who has climbed up from the cold plateau and descended through the mist into the bowl — it does not reject them. It simply does not include them. They walk through the ferns and over the narrow-gauge rail and past the calcium-encrusted pipes and they feel, for a moment, that the place is aware of them, and this feeling is almost but not quite correct. The caldera is not indifferent to you out of cruelty. It is indifferent to you the way a dream is indifferent to the dreamer — it has not noticed that you are there, and it is too busy being itself to turn its attention outward.

You will leave. The caldera will remain. The moss will close over your footprints in a matter of hours, and the travertine will seal the pipes a little more, and the blind amphibians will surface at dusk and put on their cold, mineral light, and the raptor will rattle in the cleft of the wall, and the beetles will walk into the water, and the warm springs will continue rising from deep fractures in rock that remembers being under pressure, and nothing — nothing — will have changed.

Nothing ever does, here. And yet nothing is the same as it was.
