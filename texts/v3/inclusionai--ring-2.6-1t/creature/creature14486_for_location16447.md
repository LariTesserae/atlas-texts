What lives here has made its peace with almost everything being taken.

Begin with what you cannot see: the films. They are the first and most enduring answer to the question of what survives on this compressed plain. They spread across exposed surfaces — the calcium carbonate seams, the rare outcrops of basalt that resisted the grinding — in translucence so faint you might mistake them for a trick of perception, a afterimage your eyes produced because there was nothing else to see. But they are there. Single-celled organisms, bacteria and archaea both, pressed into sheets so thin they are more concept than tissue. They do not photosynthesize. There is nothing to photosynthesize. Instead they draw energy from the chemistry of the boundary between sediment and water — from hydrogen sulfide seeping in trace amounts from below, from the slow oxidation of minerals that have been locked in the earth since before the water was this deep. Their metabolism is so marginal, so close to the thermodynamic floor, that dividing it from the geology around it becomes a matter of taxonomy rather than experience. They eat the stone. They exhale almost nothing. They are, by any reasonable standard, barely alive, and they are the foundation — the only foundation — on which everything else here is built.

From them, a chain of desperation.

Foraminifera. You find them in the sediment if you sift it — which is to say, if you perform an act of attention so fine-grained it borders on archaeological excavation. They are single-celled organisms that build shells. Not shells, really: architecture. Tiny, intricate chambers of calcium carbonate, coiled or spherical or branching in patterns so precise they seem calculated, though the word "calculated" is laughable applied to a creature with no nervous system. Some are the size of a grain of sand. Under magnification they would look like the apartments of a civilization that valued interior space over exterior show — hollow rooms connected by narrow apertures, the whole structure no more than a millimeter across. They extend pseudopodia — thin, translucent fingers of living tissue — outward from their apertures and into the water just above the sediment, and they catch what falls. Detritus. The marine snow that has taken weeks or months to descend from a surface world they have never known. Each foraminiferan sits in its shell and waits for something to arrive, and what arrives is almost nothing, and that almost nothing is enough. Some of them have been doing this, in one form or another, for hundreds of millions of years. Their chalky remains are the sediment beneath you. You are standing on their ancestors.

Then: the nematodes. They are the most numerous animals here, if "numerous" is a word that retains its meaning at these densities. There may be hundreds in a single cubic centimeter of sediment — transparent, unsegmented worms, most of them less than a millimeter long, wriggling through the spaces between compressed particles with a patience that is less behavioral than existential. They do not hunt. They graze on bacterial films, on fungal hyphae if fungi have reached this far, on each other, on whatever organic matter the sediment releases when pressure shifts or temperature fluctuates by fractions of a degree. They are so common, and so uniform in their smallness, that they function less as individuals than as a medium — a living silt, a biological component of the substrate itself. When turbulence passes overhead and the seabed flexes, it is the nematodes that feel it first, their bodies displaced centimeters or less, and they resume.

Xenophyophores appear where conditions allow, and on the abyssal plain "allow" means almost nowhere, which is why every occurrence feels like a small miracle or a long mistake. They are the largest single cells on Earth — some the size of a human fist, others as small as a thimble — and they look, to the degree they look like anything, like pale, crumbling sponges made of sediment. They construct their own bodies from the detritus around them: grains of mineral, fragments of shell, the accumulated debris of the deep seafloor, all bound together with an organic cement they secrete. They are, in effect, animals that build themselves out of the place they live, that incorporate the compressed plain into their own architecture and hold it together with chemistry. Inside, their cytoplasm is a slow-moving interior ocean, nuclei scattered throughout like settlements in a vast, quiet country. They are fragile. A strong current would destroy them. They persist in the quietest spots — the depressions, the leeward sides of the subtlest swells — and they grow so slowly that a specimen the size of your hand may be decades old, perhaps older. They are among the only things here that could be called conspicuous, though "conspicuous" is a generous term for an organism the color of wet ash that sits motionless on a plain the color of wet ash, in darkness so complete that conspicuousness is a category error.

Sea cucumbers — holothurians — drag themselves across this terrain with a kind of slow, boneless determination. They are not large here. Some are only a few centimeters long, translucent bags with a mouth at one end and an anus at the other, and they eat by pressing their oral tentacles — feathery, branching extensions — into the sediment and extracting whatever organic particles have accumulated there. They process this material through a gut that is essentially a tube, and what passes through them comes out as waste that is only marginally less organic than what went in. The conversion efficiency is appalling. It has to be. They are among the few mobile animals here, and mobility costs energy, and energy is the rarest currency on this plain. When turbulence arrives — when the turbidity flows come and the water thickens and the seabed shudders — they do what the pale drifting creatures do: they burrow. They push themselves headfirst into the sediment with a muscular contraction of the body wall and they wait. Some species can enter a state of such profound metabolic suppression that their oxygen consumption drops to almost nothing. They become, functionally, part of the sediment. Hours. Years. There is no meaningful difference, as the text said, and they are built to make that equivalence literal.

Sponges. Not the bright, barrel-shaped sponges of shallower water, but glass sponges — their skeletons made of silica spicules fused into lattices so delicate and so strong that they look like the ruins of cathedrals built by a civilization that understood geometry but not gravity. Some stand a few centimeters above the seabed on stalks of interlocking glass. They are filter feeders, drawing water through their bodies and extracting the bacteria, the dissolved organic matter, the faintest whisper of particulate nutrition the current carries. In the stillness between turbidity events they are almost motionless, their internal flagellated chambers beating with a rhythm so slow you might count ten beats in a minute. They may live for centuries. The lattice structures they build are, in a sense, the closest thing to architecture this place has — not arranged, not deliberately placed, but grown, accretion by accretion, spicule by spicule, and so slowly that the growth itself is a kind of geological process.

And the pale drifter — the one you most likely encounter first — which has no common name and may belong to several candidate groups: a larval holothurian, a pelagic worm, a chaetognath small enough to be invisible and translucent enough to make visibility irrelevant. It moves through the dark water centimeters above the seabed with vestigial fins or cilia that beat in patterns older than any nervous system currently directing them. It filters. It does not flee. It has no predators here, no prey that requires pursuit, no territory to defend. It simply exists in the thin band of water just above the compressed plain, and in that existence it is almost indistinguishable from the water itself.

These are not creatures in the vivid, surface sense. They do not leap or flash or sing. They do not form schools or colonies visible to the eye. They are sparse, slow, transparent, and quiet, and they persist in densities so low that the distance between one organism and the next might be measured in meters or tens of meters. You could spend hours scanning the plain and see nothing move. And then, in a depression no deeper than your thumbnail, you might notice a xenophyophore — a pale, crumbling shape the size of your fist — and near it, a trail in the sediment so faint it might be a current artifact, the track of a sea cucumber that passed this way days ago, weeks ago, maybe years. And in the sediment itself, foraminiferan shells like microscopic cities flattened by pressure, and nematodes writhing almost invisibly, and the films — always the films — covering everything in a translucence so faint it is less a color than the absence of one.

What lives here has reduced itself to the essentials. Not the essentials of survival as surface life understands them — movement, reproduction, response — but the essentials of *continuation*. A chemical reaction. A slow accretion of material. A wait that may be a wait for nothing in particular, because in a place that changes on timescales older than language, there may be nothing to wait for except the next particle of detritus, the next imperceptible shift in pressure, the next moment in which being barely alive is enough.
