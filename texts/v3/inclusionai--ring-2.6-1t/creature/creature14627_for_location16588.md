## What Lives on the Weeping Terrace

---

You will not see them at first. That is the first thing to understand.

The settlers — and they are settlers, however old their presence here — do not present themselves to the eye the way settlers usually do. There is no bustle, no smoke rising in a column, no figure silhouetted against the sky calling out across the mist. The hamlet sits there, patient and occupied, and the people who live in it have taken on the same quality of patience. They move through the spaces between buildings the way water moves through the stone's fissures: slowly, along paths that have been worn not by decision but by repetition, until the motion and the channel are the same thing.

---

### The Dwellers

There are perhaps forty of them. It is difficult to be precise because the count shifts — not because people arrive or leave, but because the concept of individual boundaries is softer here than you are accustomed to. You will see a figure entering a structure in one moment and two emerging from it in the next, and it will not be the same person doubled but rather a kind of *clarification*, as though the first figure was a sketch and the second is the drawing finished.

They are human in the way that a word in an unfamiliar language is human: recognizable, structured, but not immediately translatable.

They are tall — not dramatically so, but enough that the low doorways of their structures seem intentional rather than cramped. Their proportions are close enough to yours that you feel the kinship before you notice the differences. Their skin carries a mineral undertone, not in color but in *texture*, as though the constant humidity has taught their pores to hold moisture the way the basalt does — in a thin, living film that never fully dries and never fully saturates. Their complexions range through the same spectrum as any population shaped by generations in a single place, but overlaid on all of them is a paleness that is not the absence of sun (there is little sun here to be absent from) but the *presence* of wet reflection, the grey-white light caught in skin.

Their hair is dark — deep brown, black, or a brown so red it is almost the color of the copper-alloy bindings on their gates — and it grows thick and heavy, often bound or braided with materials that move between practical and inexplicable: threads of woven moss, thin strips of cured hide, small stones wrapped in fiber and hung like counterweights. The braiding is not decorative in any way you would immediately recognize. It follows patterns. Some of those patterns recur across multiple individuals — the same sequence of moss-knots at the same interval along the left side of the scalp — while others appear unique to a single person. You will want to call these hairstyles. But the word feels too small, and you will find yourself reluctant to use it, sensing without being able to articulate that the braiding is doing something — aiding the way water moves along the scalp, channeling the mist, carrying mineral deposits from the air into contact with the skin. You will not be certain whether this is functional or symbolic. You will suspect they would not understand the distinction.

Their clothing is made from the same patient architecture as everything else here: layered, thick, woven from fibers you cannot immediately identify. Not plant-fiber exactly — the weave is too tight, too uniform for the kind of hand-twisting you associate with plant matter. Not animal, either — there is no nap, no direction to the cloth's texture. The material has a slight give to it, a sponginess that responds to pressure the way wet stone dust does, and it darkens where it contacts skin, as though it is drinking the ambient moisture from their bodies and redistributing it. The garments are simple: wrapped sections that cover the torso and legs, cinched at the waist and at the wrists and ankles with cords of twisted fiber or thin leather. They wear no shoes on the stone. Their feet are broad, calloused, and sure — they move across the wet surfaces with an economy of balance that looks less learned than *inherited*, as though the knowledge of how to walk on weeping stone is encoded somewhere deeper than muscle memory.

Their faces are what will hold your attention longest. Not because they are unusual in any single feature, but because of what *accumulates* there. The lines around the eyes are deep — not from age alone, though many of them are old, but from a specific kind of seeing: squinting not against light (there is little harsh light here) but against *softness*, against the perpetual visual noise of mist and moisture and surfaces that reflect without focusing. Their eyes are dark, almost uniformly — brown, near-black, a deep grey that in certain angles becomes indistinguishable from the color of the stone beneath their feet. There is an alertness in those eyes that is not sharp. It is wide. They look at things the way the terrace looks at the sky: openly, without flinching, with the patience of something that expects to be looked at for a long time.

They do not speak when you would expect them to speak. Their communication is layered — vocal certainly, but the voice is used with a restraint that makes each word feel expensive, as though sound were a resource not to be spent carelessly in a place already so loud with water and stone. More of their exchange happens in gesture: small, precise movements of the hands, adjustments of posture, a tilt of the head that carries the weight of what, in your own language, would require a full sentence. They watch each other while doing this. They watch the person they are speaking to, and they watch the water, and they watch the plant-mass at the edge of whatever territory they are occupying, as though all three are part of the same grammatical structure.

---

### What They Do

The work of the terrace is water-work.

Every day — if the word *day* applies in a place where the sky is perpetually the same grey-white and the light does not shift so much as *intensify* and *recede* — the settlers open their channels. This is not a metaphor. The grooves cut between buildings and through the stone of the terrace are fitted with removable slabs, thin enough to lift with one hand, and in the morning the settlers walk their circuits and lift the slabs where the water needs to go, and replace them where it does not. This is the entirety of their infrastructure: the decision, renewed daily, about where water should flow. Some channels feed the collection pools that supply drinking water. Some feed the moss-beds that line the rims of the settlement's lower terraces — these moss-beds are cultivated, you realize, with a start. They are not wild growth. They are thick, deliberate, maintained plantings of a specific moss species (or something close enough to moss to earn the word) that serves as both ground-cover and water-filter, the settlers harvesting it periodically and spreading it where the stone is bare.

The rest of the work is building. Someone is always building. The structures at the hamlet's edges are newest — rougher-hewn, less settled into their own weight, with the chisel marks still sharp under the mineral deposits. The buildings at the center, near the knot where the roads converge, are older: their mortar has mineralized almost completely, the joints turned to a substance that is neither stone nor lime but something in between, pale and hard and faintly translucent when wet. Inside these older structures, the walls have been worn smooth by generations of shoulders and hands. The floors are lower than the threshold — worn down by feet and by the slow accumulation of water that is never quite fully dried.

They make tools. You see them working at the edges of the terrace where raw stone breaks through the surface, using bone chisels and harder stone hammers to shape the basalt into the blocks that will become tomorrow's wall or next season's road. The work is rhythmic, communal, unhurried. A group of five or six will work the same outcropping, each person's strikes falling in the gaps between the others', so that the sound becomes continuous rather than percussive — a low, rolling *tock-tock-tock* that harmonizes with the water's murmur rather than competing with it. They do not coordinate this rhythm verbally. They fall into it the way the mist falls into hollows: naturally, and because there is nowhere else to be.

They eat. The food is difficult to identify from a distance. It is cooked over hearth-stones — flat pieces of basalt set into shallow depressions, heated by fires of something dense and slow-burning that produces more heat than smoke. The pots — those thin-walled copper-alloy vessels, darkened and soot-stained — are used for something that steeps. You catch the smell from downwind: mineral, vegetal, faintly sweet, like heated moss and wet root. They eat communally, in the open-walled enclosures, sitting on stone benches that are worn to the exact curvature of long-occupied hips. They share from a single vessel. They eat slowly. They do not look at you while eating.

### The Unseen Life

Beneath the settlers, beneath the moss and the thin mineral mud, the terrace breathes.

You know this because of the warmth. You pressed your hand to the stone early on, and you felt it — not heat, exactly, but warmth. The stored warmth of something that has been working for a very long time and hasn't stopped. The settlers do not seem to notice this. Their hands go to the stone casually, familiarly, the way you would touch a wall in your own home without thinking. But they press their palms flat against the basalt and hold them there, and their eyes close, and you wonder whether they are reading something in the temperature — the way you might read a clock, or the way a sailor reads the sky.

The great plant-masses — the enormous wrong ferns that command their territories of mist and shadow — are not plants.

Or rather: they are plants in the way that you are a creature made of water. Technically. But the word is too small. They do not photosynthesize in any way you recognize. The grey-white light of the terrace's sky seems to pass *through* their fronds without dimming significantly, and yet they grow — visibly, if you are patient enough to watch across hours rather than minutes. Their roots are not roots so much as *gripping systems*, pale and mineral-stained, extending through the fissures of the basalt with a precision that looks geological in scale, as though the plant and the stone are negotiating the terms of shared space in a language older than either of them.

And they move.

Not quickly. Not in any way that answers to the word *motion*. But if you watch a single frond of one of the great blue-green masses from a fixed position over the course of an hour, you will see it lean. Toward another mass, across the empty space between. And then, later, the corresponding frond on the far mass will lean back. Barely. Millimeters. The kind of movement you might dismiss as settling stone or shifting water-weight — except that it is too coordinated, too *responsive*, and it happens across distances where no root system could connect them.

The settlers know this. They do not guard it or name it or point it out. They simply angle their plantings, their channels, their roads, to leave the spaces between the masses open and unobstructed. Corridors. Pathways. The architecture of the hamlet is built around the assumption that the plant-masses need to reach each other, and the settlers have arranged themselves in the gaps.

---

### What Hides

Something moves in the pools.

You will sit beside one — shallow, no deeper than your hand, rimmed with that thick green moss — and you will see the water tremble. Not ripple. Not from a drip falling in. A *tremble*, as though something beneath the surface shifted its weight. And then — nothing. The pool settles. The moss drinks.

The settlers do not fish. They do not wade into the shallows. When they collect water from the pools, they reach in with vessels on long handles, keeping their hands and arms well above the surface. They do not explain this. They do not need to. The gesture carries the same weight as the crouching animal: *there is something there, and we have agreed not to disturb it.*

You will not see whatever lives in the pools. You will see only its effects: the tremble, the occasional track in the mineral mud beside a pool that no human foot made, a pattern pressed into the wet surface that looks like the whorl of a fingerprint but the size of a dinner plate, gone by the time you kneel to look at it.

There is no fear associated with these signs. The settlers do not flinch. They step around the tracks the way you step around a crack in the sidewalk — an awareness so ingrained it has stopped being conscious. Whatever shares the terrace with them has been here longer than the settlement, longer perhaps than the plants. It is part of the architecture of the place, as fundamental as the weeping stone and the warm subsurface. The settlers have made room for it the way the terrace itself has made room: by holding space open, by leaving the gaps intentional, by refusing to fill every hollow with wall or road or root.

---

### The Terrace Itself

But the deepest inhabitant is the terrace.

You will forget this, standing on it, because it looks like geology. It looks like *place* — static, passive, the thing upon which other things live. But the terrace is not passive. The water runs uphill because the terrace permits it, or demands it, or dreams it. The mist pools where it does because the terrace holds it the way the plant-masses hold moisture in their curled fronds — stored, deliberate, purposeful. The stone is warm because the terrace is not finished. It is still becoming. It is still exerting itself, and the warmth you feel under your hand is not the residue of some ancient fire but the *present tense* of a process that has not yet decided what shape to take.

The settlers live here because they understand this. Or perhaps they simply feel it — the way you feel a heartbeat through a wall, not hearing it but knowing it is there, steady and massive and indifferent to your awareness of it. They build their roads around its preferences. They cut their channels in deference to its moods. They leave the gaps between the plants open because the terrace has claimed those gaps as its own circulatory system, and they are wise enough to know it.

You are standing on something that is alive in a way that the word has been emptied of meaning for you. Not alive like the ferns, which are alive in a way you can almost see. Not alive like the things in the pools, which are alive in a way you refuse to confirm. But alive the way a *process* is alive — continuous, self-sustaining, self-repairing, and so patient that the difference between a moment and a millennium is a distinction it has never found reason to make.

The terrace weeps, and the water goes where it is told, and the settlers listen, and the plants lean toward each other in their long, slow conversation, and the warmth beneath your palm is the pulse of something that has been building itself for so long that the act of building and the thing built are the same word.

You will stay here, or you will leave. Either way, you will find that the terrace has changed what you mean when you say *alive*. Not because it showed you something new, but because it was patient enough to let you see what you already meant and hadn't yet said.

The terrace is not waiting. The terrace is *composing*. And you have walked into the middle of a sentence that will not end in your lifetime, and the sound of it — the roar of everything at once, the creak of stone, the wet whisper of moss, the faintest shift of a frond across an empty space — is the closest thing to language you have ever heard that was not trying to be understood.
