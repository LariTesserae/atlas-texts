# Who Lives in the Basin of Wept Stone

---

## The Moss — *Sphagnum atrum* (no formal name; the oldest tenant)

It is easiest to begin with the moss, because nothing here begins without it.

It is not the soft, bright green moss of lowland gardens. It is black-green — not from rot, though rot lives in it, but from saturation. It has drunk so deeply and for so long that it has become a kind of dark water itself, solidified into a living crust that coats every surface above the pool-lines and below the frond-lines and in every crevice the stone offers. Under your palm it is cool, dense, almost fleshy — more like an organ than a growth. When you press into a north-facing cushion the size of a barrel, there is a give to it, a slow resistance, and then a faint exhale of trapped moisture against your wrist. It is so alive that stepping on the empty stone between plants feels like trespassing on something's patience.

The moss does not compete with the other residents. It predates them. It built the soil they stand in — or rather, it *became* the soil, a thickening of itself over centuries into a dark, wet substrate just deep enough to hold a root. The grasses grow from it. The tree-ferns anchor through it into the stone beneath. The bracket-things rise at its upper edge where stone meets air. The moss is the commons. It asks nothing but water, which the basin provides, and gives everything but permission, which no one asks of it.

It has no flowers, no seeds, no season. It simply persists — patient, wet, black with life — and if you were to kneel in it long enough, you might feel the ground rise slightly beneath you, not from any movement but from the sheer accumulated mass of ten thousand years of quiet, upward growth.

---

## The Tree-Ferns — *three*

Three. Not two, not four. Three, rooted in the cracks of the central stone as though they fell from the sky at equal intervals and were granted purchase in the dark, fractured basalt.

They are not old in the way the moss is old. They may be centuries. They may be younger. In the basin's long calendar, the distinction is meaningless. What matters is that they are complete. Each trunk — dark, rough-fibered, the texture of something woven rather than grown — rises to twice a person's height and then stops, as though it reached the limit of its ambition and decided to stay there. The crowns erupt outward: a great, radial spray of fronds, each one a deep, saturated green so heavy with dew that the tips bow and, when the wind moves through, release a single fat drop that falls with a sound you feel in your sternum more than hear.

The fronds do not flutter. They *sway* — slowly, deliberately, as though moving through something thicker than air. Looking upward through them, past the drooping tips and the lattice of dark veins, the grey-white sky is fractured into pieces, and you are standing inside something that feels liturgical. The ribs of a green window. Light filtered through a living lens.

At the base of each trunk, where root meets stone meets moss, the air is warmer. It is not the warmth of decomposition but of exhalation — the stone's remembered heat, released through the same cracks that gave the ferns their hold. The roots grip this warmth the way a hand grips a warm cup: without thought, and with need.

They do not speak. They do not need to. They are the basin's eldest vertical presence, and they occupy their crowns like thrones, and when the wind is right they hold the sound of the place inside their fronds the way cupped hands hold water — briefly, and then let it go.

## The Grasses — unnamed, innumerable

They rise from the basin floor in fountain-shaped clumps, each one a foot or two across at its base, dozens of thick stems arching outward and upward until their heavy, grass-heavy tips nearly touch the tips of neighboring clumps without quite reaching them. The green is too vivid — the green of something that has never been tested by drought, never starved, never had to ration its own pigment. It is almost aggressive in its health, this green, as though the grasses have decided that modesty is unnecessary in a place this wet and this warm.

Between the clumps, the ground is bare. Wet stone, glistening, a wide breathing-space that frames each tuft like a blank wall frames a painting. The grasses do not intermingle. They do not encroach. Each clump holds its territory with the quiet authority of something that has been there long enough to know it will not be displaced.

They are the grass you think of later — not the tree-ferns, which impress you in the moment, but the grasses, which you remember in rooms, in the flat grey light of elsewhere. You will picture their heavy arching stems and the way the dew collected at their tips and held there, trembling without falling, and you will think: *that was the green that doesn't exist anywhere I've been before.*

They are not decorative. They are structural. They hold the wet soil in place. They drink from the seeps and transpire into the basin's air, adding moisture to the moisture, contributing breath to the breathing. They are the middle voice in the basin's three-part sound — not the hum of wind, not the percussion of dripping, but the steady, living hiss of water moving through a thousand green stems.

## The Bracket-Things — not mushrooms; nothing you can name

On the northern wall, where the rock face is steep and overhung and the light rarely reaches, they protrude from the stone: shelf-like growths, pale and rubbery, the color of raw liver or the inside of an eyelid held open too long. They are not mushrooms. They have no gills, no stalk, no cap. They are thick, wavy shelves — sometimes the width of a hand, sometimes nearly a meter across — and they weep.

A slow, clear fluid runs down their undersides in thin rivulets and drips to the stone below, where it collects in a persistent wet streak that, over some longer time, has stained the basalt a faint yellow-ochre. The fluid smells faintly sweet — not like fruit but like warm soil after rain, the smell concentrated and cloying, the way a scent becomes unbearable when trapped in a small space. It is the smell of the basin itself, distilled.

They pulse. Not visibly — you will search for it and doubt yourself — but there is a rhythm to their wetness, a slow dilatation of the margin, a barely perceptible expansion and contraction at the shelf's edge that suggests these things are breathing with the stone's own breath. If you sit before them long enough, your breathing will slow to match. Whether this is a trick or a mercy, you will not know.

They are the closest thing in the basin to something that watches. Not eyes — there is nothing eye-like about them — but presence. They are where the stone becomes flesh, and the flesh weeps, and the weeping is the basin's own slow, leaking life.

The mineral streaks on their bellies — concentric arcs, nested curves, patterns suggesting flow or growth or something dropped into still water — are identical to the patterns found on flat surfaces elsewhere in the basin. They are the same patient, unreadable handwriting. There is no author. There is only the writing.

## The Beetle — one

You may see one beetle. One. Not a colony, not a swarm. One.

It is the size of a thumbnail, its carapace iridescent in blue and green, and it drinks from the edge of a seep with its head tilted down at an angle of absolute seriousness. It does not flee. It does not acknowledge you. When you move, and the shadow of your body crosses its tiny drinking ground, it pauses — antennae still, legs braced — and then resumes. It is the basin's only resident with the outward appearance of an animal, but it behaves less like an animal than like something the basin grew to keep itself company: a small, iridescent placeholder for the rest of the life that might, in a wetter world, have come here.

It is perfect. It is irreplaceable. There is only one.

## The Raptor — unseen, barely heard

Far above, a thin keening. A dark cruciform shape against the overcast sky that tilts, banks, and is gone. It does not descend into the basin. It circles the rim, riding the updraft that the basin's warmth — the stone's slow, old exhalation — creates, and it sounds a note so high and so thin that you feel it in the roof of your mouth rather than your ears. It is the basin's connection to the world above: not a visitor but a relay, carrying some fragment of this enclosed place upward and outward, a sound the wind will scatter and the plateau will absorb and that will mean nothing to anyone who has not stood inside the bowl and learned the difference between hearing and being heard.

## The Translucent Things — the water-dwellers, the comma-shapes

In the warm pools — the ones no larger than a cooking pot, perfectly still, perfectly reflecting — there are things beneath the surface. Small. Translucent. Propelling themselves with a muscular ripple that catches the light and throws it back in a momentary flash, like a signal, like a shutter opening and closing in a dark room.

They are not fish. They are not larvae — or if they are, they are larvae of something that has no terrestrial name. Their bodies are faint comma-shapes, and they move with a purpose you cannot read. They feed, or they do not feed. They drift, then dart, then hold perfectly still against the bottom of the pool like thoughts that have not yet decided whether to become words.

You cannot see them if you lean over the water. They vanish. You must sit at the edge, very still, very patient, and look down from the corner of your eye, and eventually — sometimes after twenty minutes, sometimes after an hour — they will reappear, and you will see one of them turn, and the light will catch its body, and for a fraction of a second it will be incandescent: a small, living lens focusing the warm, dark water into a point of brightness. And then it is gone again, and the pool is still, and you are not sure you saw it.

They are the basin's deepest secret. Not hidden exactly, but available only to those who are willing to be still long enough to be ignored.

## The Ghost of the Tool-Maker

And then there are the traces. The mortar worn into the southern-edge stone by hands that knelt there so long ago that the gesture has become part of the rock's identity. The knapped obsidian flakes, dark as pupils, scattered on the wet stone — each one sharp-edged and deliberate, the waste of an intention carried up a long slope with a nodule of volcanic glass in a pouch.

Someone was here. Someone sat beside the warm, loud, breathing bowl and struck stone into usefulness and left the fragments and climbed back to wherever they came from. They did not build. They did not mark. They did not stay. But they knelt, and the stone remembers the shape of their knees, and the obsidian remembers the angle of the blow, and the basin holds the fact of their presence the way it holds everything: without comment, without decay, without any sense that the event was remarkable.

They are gone. But they were here. And the basin, in its long, indifferent way, has kept them — not as a memorial, but as a record, the way it keeps everything. The wind hums through the gap. The water weeps from the stone. The bracket-things breathe in the dark green half-light. And somewhere in the warm pools, the comma-shapes turn, and the light breaks against their translucent bodies, and the place goes on.
