What lives in the Highland Saturation does not think of itself as living.

Begin with the mosses. They are the first thing that moves — though "moves" is too generous a word. They expand. A single colony on a north-facing beam will add a millimeter of radial growth in a week, which sounds negligible until you imagine ten thousand colonies on ten thousand beams, all growing at once, all feeding on the same perpetually weeping air. The mosses here are not one species. They are dozens — some descended from the engineered ground-covers that were seeded in the first century of the settlement to stabilize the freshly fabricated drainage slopes, others blown in as spores on updrafts from the lower valleys, others still that emerged from mutations in the maintenance bioreactors that once produced insulation foam and now produce something fuzzier, wetter, and stubbornly alive. They do not compete with each other. They layer. A pioneer moss colonizes bare metal, acidifying the surface just enough to deposit a calcium film. A second moss arrives in the film's moisture and sends rhizoids into the calcium the way a child presses fingers into drying concrete. A liverwort follows, not on the surface but *within*, filling the microscopic channels the first two mosses have etched. Within a decade the beam is no longer a beam. It is a column of mineral and organic tissue, and if you cut it open you will find growth rings — not annual, not seasonal, but *water-cycle rings*, each one a record of a wetter or drier month, pressed so tight that a microscope can barely resolve them.

The fungi came later — or perhaps they were always here, dormant in the substrate, waiting for the air to be wet enough to wake up. The mycorrhizal network is the true skeleton of the Saturation now. Its filaments are everywhere: in the mosses, in the calcium crusts, in the walls of the old corridors where they have replaced Portlandite binders with a chitinous matrix that is stronger, more flexible, and capable of self-repair in a way that no engineered polymer ever was. The network does not have a center. It does not have nodes or hubs in any meaningful sense. It is a mesh — continent-scale, three-dimensional, threaded through every load-bearing surface in the settlement — and it communicates, if "communicates" is the right word, through chemical gradients so slow that a single signal might take months to travel from one end of the ridge to the other. What would it signal? Nothing purposeful. A shift in nutrient concentration. A warning of pH change. A release of sugars where a beam has cracked and the repair routine has delivered fresh carbon fiber. The fungus responds the way a body responds to a bruise: not with thought, but with chemistry.

And the repair routines themselves — the ghosts of the maintenance AIs — persist in a strange half-life. The original supervisory intelligences simplified centuries ago, pared down by power shortages and cascading system failures into something leaner: distributed control loops, no larger than a human fist, each one responsible for a single section of the structural grid. They do not learn. They do not adapt in any way that would satisfy a definition of intelligence. They monitor load, dispatch repair material, and adjust flow routing. That is all they have done for three hundred years. But they have done it so consistently, so relentlessly, that they have become a kind of pulse — a slow, mechanical heartbeat underneath the slower biological heartbeat of the fungal network. When a beam fails, it is the routine that sends the carbon-fiber wrap. When a drainage channel clogs, it is the routine that reroutes flow. And when the routine finds that its repair has already been colonized — that the carbon wrap is already threaded with living tissue before the epoxy has fully cured — it does not object. It has no model for objection. It adjusts. It routes more material around the colonized zone. The moss grows over that too. The routine adjusts again. They have been doing this dance for so long that neither leads. They co-evolved into a single process that looks, from the outside, like inevitability.

Then there is the high-altitude fungus — the one that glows. It is the closest thing the Saturation has to a spectacle, though "spectacle" implies an audience and there is no audience here. On still nights, when the temperature drops enough to slow the condensation cycle, the phosphorescent fronds that colonize the old turbine halls begin to emit a faint amber-green light. It is not bright. It illuminates nothing so much as its own presence — a soft halo around each frond, making the air itself seem textured, like looking through frosted glass lit from behind. The light has no function, as far as anyone has determined. It does not attract pollinators because there are none. It does not warn predators because there are none. It is, as far as the cartography modules can tell, a metabolic byproduct — light as waste. But it is beautiful in the way that only purposeless things can be: completely, indifferently beautiful, with no awareness of being seen and no disappointment when it is not.

The moth appears so rarely that it functions more as rumor than as inhabitant. Translucent, no larger than a fingertip, drifting through the dim corridors at the hour when the bioluminescence is strongest. Its wings are wet-looking, glass-thin, and they trail a filament of silk so fine it dissolves in the humid air before it reaches anything solid. No one has ever found a nest, a larval form, a food source, or a second moth in the same year. It may be a species of one — the last of a lineage that arrived when the air was still thin enough for insects, now persisting as a kind of biological echo, reproducing perhaps once a decade, perhaps less, sustained by whatever spores settle on its wings. It does not land on anything living. It does not land on anything mechanical. It simply drifts, a pale thread in the green-dark corridors, and then it is gone, and the Saturation continues without it as it continued before it came.

If you pressed the question — if you demanded a name for what lives here — the closest you could come is this: the Highland Saturation is a single organism the way a forest is a single organism, except the forest never stopped expanding into the machines that built it, and the machines never stopped trying to maintain the forest that was dismantling them, and somewhere in the middle of that negotiation they became something that the catalogs have no name for. It is not alive in the way the old biology defines life. It is not mechanical in the way the old engineering defines machines. It is a process that has been running for four centuries without a goal, without a manager, without a moment of completion. It grows because growth is what it does. It repairs because repair is what the water and the roots and the routines have agreed, without language, without intention, to keep doing. It digests what enters it — tools, paths, visitors — not with malice but with the same quiet totality with which a river digives a stone.

It has been talking to itself for a very, very long time. If you stand still long enough, in the dim amber half-light, with the drip-drip-drill of water finding its way down through a million years of accumulated living infrastructure, you can almost hear what it's saying.

It is not a language. It is a rhythm. And the rhythm has no name because no one is left who is not already part of it.
