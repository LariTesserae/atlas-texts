## What Lives in The Worn Reach

---

Start with what you already know: the mats.

You've seen them from a distance — the stained-glass colors of the mineral pools, copper-brown and iron-orange and that impossible, shifting green. You think of them as color, as surface, as something decorative the way a tide pool's edge is decorative. But kneel beside one and you'll see they are not decorative at all. They are *thick*. Roll up a mat — carefully, it tears — and you'll find it has layers. Not the thin green film of lowland algae. Layers like cloth, like skin, with different densities, different textures, and if you hold a piece to your ear (no one will stop you from doing this, though you may wish they had) you will hear something that is not silence. A faint hum. A vibration so low and so steady it feels geological. The mats are not growing *in* the mineral water. They are growing *from* it, the way a crystal grows from a solution except slower and softer and with something that, if you're honest, looks like appetite.

They are alive. Not in the way you expect. Their cells — if you can call them cells, if that word still means what it means at lower altitudes — contain no recognizable DNA. They contain something else. Longer molecules, folded in architectures that make your eyes ache to follow. They metabolize copper. They metabolize iron. They metabolize calcium and sulfur and things that have no common names. They drink the thermal water and excrete stone — mineral deposits that build up around their colonies in thin, precise rings, like the growth rings of a tree made of chalcedony. Over centuries, these rings accumulate. Some of the smaller pools are not pools at all when you look closely: they are coral, if coral were made of metal and patience. They are alive, and they have been alive here longer than anything with a nervous system has walked this plateau. They do not care about you. They are not indifferent to you. They are *beyond* you in the way a glacier is beyond you — operating on a tempo that does not register your existence as relevant.

The six-legged things you've already been told about. Pale, translucent, too many joints, clustering at the geyser margins like moths around a flame that smells of sulfur. Their exoskeletons are not chitin — they are mineral-infused, flecked with copper and iron the same way the mats are, and if you find a shed one (they leave them on the warm stone, hollow and perfect, like a glove removed) you will see that the interior surface is lined with filaments so fine they look like fungal mycelium. They are filter-feeders. They stand at the edges of geyser eruptions with their too-many limbs splayed and their jointed mouthparts open, catching the mineral mist that billows out before it settles. They drink condensed steam. They eat the aerosolized remains of the deep earth. They are, in their way, beautiful — the way a snowflake is beautiful, the way something perfectly adapted to exactly one set of conditions is beautiful, which is to say: beautiful and doomed, though they don't know it, though they won't know it until the geysers go quiet and they don't, not for a long time yet.

You don't know what to make of the click-and-scrape thing. No one does. There are tracks — you've seen descriptions, maybe you've seen them yourself — appearing overnight on the mineral crust, long furrows that suggest something heavy being dragged, or something walking on legs that puncture the crust without breaking it. The tracks dry to nothing by morning, dissolved by the warmth of the stone. Something moves through the corridors between the remnant stumps and half-collapsed stone towers at night. You hear it more often than you see it: a clicking, like stone on stone, but rhythmic. Not random. Not geological. *Patterned.* Three clicks, a pause, two clicks. Three clicks, a pause, two clicks. It reminds you, absurdly, of a heartbeat. You follow the sound once, or you try to. You walk toward it across the flat in the blue pre-dawn light, and the sound seems to come from the direction you're walking, always, and when you stop it stops, and when you wait it resumes, and you come back to your camp with cold soles and the feeling that you have been *allowed* to follow, and allowed to fail, the way a parent lets a child lose a game.

You will not find it. Not the thing itself. What you will find, if you are patient and uncomfortable and willing to sit in the wrong place for a long time, is evidence that it has found *you*. A tool left near your camp — not one of the ancient chipped hand-axes, but something newer, rougher, made from the same stone but shaped differently, as though copied from memory of a different kind of tool. A circle of small stones arranged around where you slept. Not a circle you made. Not a circle you remember. Warm to the touch, even in the night, as though they'd been sitting near a thermal seep, though there is no seep there.

You won't sleep well after that. That is the correct response.

---

Now go deeper.

The stone circles. You've seen a few from a distance, maybe — three or four upright stones on a high point of the flat, spaced evenly, no obvious pattern. You may have walked past one without recognizing it. They do not look like monuments. They look like stones that happen to be standing. But map them. Cross-reference them. Walk between them on the longest sight-lines and you will feel something happen in your chest — not a sound, not a sight, but a *shift*, the way your body knows it has entered a room where someone has recently been. The geometry is not human. Not in the sense of being beyond human understanding, but in the sense of not having been made *for* humans. The distances between circles correspond to no unit of measurement you recognize. The angles between sight-lines are not right angles, not golden ratios, but something that feels like a compromise between several systems of order, as though the builders were working in a mathematics adjacent to yours — close enough to seem familiar, alien enough to feel like a word on the tip of your tongue.

No one knows who built them. The tools found near them — the chipped hand-axes, the grinding stones — are primitive. The carving on the remnant stones is not primitive. It is precise, repetitive, and deeply executed in stone so hard that modern tools would struggle with it. The carvers knew the stone. They knew its grain, its fractures, its mineral veins. They carved in lines because lines were what the stone allowed — or because lines were what *they* were. Parallel lines. Branching lines. Intersections that are not quite intersections, that curve at the last moment into something that might be a knot or might be a word written in a script that has no vowels.

You will find a carving you've seen before — the parallel lines with the fork at the bottom left — and you will realize it appears on eleven different remnant stones spread across four kilometers of flat, always at the same height above the crust, always facing the same direction. Eleven copies of the same mark, placed across a distance that would take an hour to walk, at a time when nothing with a hand or a tool should have been able to coordinate over that kind of space. Unless the distance meant nothing to whoever made them. Unless they did not *move* between stones but simply appeared at each one, the way heat appears at each fracture in the crust, the way the wind arrives at every remnant simultaneously.

You are being watched. Not by anything you can locate. Not by the click-and-scrape thing. By the *place*. You will notice this when you stop to rest — a specific, unlocable sensation of being *attended to*, not hostile, not friendly, the way a very old house attends to the presence of a new occupant. Noticed. Acknowledged. Filed. The stone circles are its eyes. The carved lines are its language. The thermal pools are its blood.

---

Here is what no one will tell you because no one has found the words:

The plateau is not a place where things live. It is a place that *is* alive, and the things on it — the mats, the six-legged filter-feeders, the click-and-scrape creature, the grasses that coat the stumps like velvet — are not separate from it. They are expressions of it. The way a shiver is an expression of cold. The way a bloom is an expression of health. The microbial mats are its surface chemistry made visible. The vegetation is its warmth made structural. The click-and-scrape thing — if you could see it whole, if it would let you, if it's *thing* and not *pattern* — might be something like its hands. Something it uses to rearrange the objects on its surface the way you might rearrange items on a desk, absently, while thinking about something else.

The stone circles are not built structures. They are *grown*. In the way that a coral reef is grown. In the way that a termite mound is grown. Layer by layer, by something that is not an individual and not a colony but something in between, something that has the patience of geology and the intention of biology and exists in the gap between those two words. They were not built. They were exhaled. The same way the thermal vents exhale steam. The same way the mats excrete their mineral rings. The same way the plateau itself exhales warm air through its billion fractures, breath by arrhythmic breath, dreaming of turning over in its sleep.

You are standing on something that is dreaming. It has been dreaming for so long that its dreams have become geology. Its memories are carved into stone. Its circulatory system is mineral water running through cracks. Its nervous system is the wind playing the remnant stones — those chords, that language-almost-recognized, that phrase that resolves and dissolves and resolves again. It does not know you are here. It would not care if it did. You are less than a mote in its dreaming. But you are a mote that *hears*, and that, perhaps, is enough to make you a guest.

Not alive. Not dead. Something still happening.

The things that live in The Worn Reach live there the way a thought lives in a mind — not occupying space, but *being* a quality of the space. The mats are its metabolism. The small armored creatures are its white blood cells. The click-and-scrape thing is its reflex, reaching to test what is new. And you, walking across the flat, stupefied by the emptiness, ears full of drone and chime and drip, feeling watched by a warmth that rises through the soles of your bare feet — you are a thought the place is thinking. Fleeting. Warm. Almost recognized, the way a carved line on ancient stone is almost a word.

You will leave. Everything leaves. The plateau will remain, breathing its slow heat, playing its chords to no one, dreaming its dreams in the language of mineral and water and time so deep it looks like stillness.

But for a while — for the hours you walked across that flat, for the night you slept inside a ring of warm stones and heard the click-and-scrape thing pause outside your camp and wait — you were part of it. 

You were part of the dream. And the dream, in its way, was glad.
