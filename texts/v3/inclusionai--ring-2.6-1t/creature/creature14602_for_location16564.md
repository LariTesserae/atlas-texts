# What Lives in the Weald of Vertical Silence

---

Start with what does not live, because here that is almost as interesting.

There are no birds because there is no sky to hold them. Not that the sky is low — it is not — but that it is *occupied*. Look up at it long enough and you will begin to feel that the darkness between the stars (there are stars here, at midday, faintly, like words written in a language you once knew) is not empty but *suspended*, held in place by a pressure you cannot name, the way a finger holds a spider in water not by touching it but by the surface tension of the world around it. The birds know. Whatever birds were here once, their descendants learned within a generation to stop looking up, and then to stop flying, and then there were no descendants.

But.

There are the films.

They are the closest thing to life the Weald offers, and they are so far from what we call life that calling them by any familiar name feels like slander. They exist on the undersides of overhangs and in the deep crevices where the basalt fins nearly meet, in places where the air is wet and still and the light has been so thoroughly excluded that the darkness itself seems to have a texture. You called them lichen. You called them fungi. You called them biofilms, which is a word you will feel cheap about later, the way you feel cheap about calling a cathedral a building.

They spread. Slowly. Not slowly by your sense of it — by any sense of it. A colony the size of your hand may represent three hundred years of growth, or three thousand, or thirty thousand; time here does not give you the courtesy of a consistent unit, so you cannot tell, and the inability to tell becomes its own kind of knowledge: they are older than your capacity to measure them. They branch. They anastomose. They form networks that look, if you are on your knees with your face close enough that the mist from your breathing beads on the stone between you and them, like river deltas seen from a great height, like vascular systems, like neural maps. Whether this resemblance is meaningful or merely the result of a mind pulling patterns from noise — you will not resolve this. You will suspect it is both.

When you press your palm flat against a colony — and you will, because you will want to, because some part of you believes that proximity is a form of understanding — you will feel two things. The first is warmth. Not metabolic warmth, not the heat of decomposition or fermentation, but something more like the warmth of stone that has been in the sun, except that these colonies grow where the sun never reaches. The second is reluctance. The film resists your attention — not aggressively, not with force, but with the dull, patient passivity of something that has existed for so long that it has forgotten the possibility of interruption. It does not recoil. It does not welcome. It simply fails to register you as separate from the stone, and in that failure there is something so alien that you will pull your hand back quickly and sit on the ground and breathe hard and feel, for the first time since you arrived, genuinely small.

They metabolize mineral. You are almost certain of this. You have watched the ochre and white mineral trails on the north faces of the basalt columns shift over the course of days — not changing position, but changing *intensity*, darkening and lightening in patterns that suggest consumption and excretion on a timescale you can only describe as liturgical. Whether the films are the cause or merely beneficiaries of the process, you cannot say. You have tried to scrape a colony from the stone to examine it more closely. It comes away in flakes that are soft and slightly warm to the touch and within minutes — seconds? hours? you can never tell here — settle back into the surface as if they had never been disturbed, fusing with the stone beneath them in a way that looks less like adhesion than *reabsorption*. You will not try again.

---

Then there are the others.

The ones you do not speak of because speaking of them feels like feeding them.

You see them at the edges of perception — this is the only honest way to describe it. Not in your direct vision. Never in your direct vision. In the place where your attention has not yet landed, where your eyes are traveling *toward* a focus but have not arrived, in that brief corridor of anticipation between saccade and recognition, there is *structure*. A figure. Standing between columns. At the far edge of a fin. Kneeling beside a cairn.

You turn to look directly and there is nothing.

But you saw it. You saw it, and it was not a trick of the light (though the light here is capable of tricks) and it was not a failure of your visual processing (though your visual processing has degraded measurably since you arrived, and you accept this as you accept everything here, which is to say, without protest). It was *something*, and it was *shaped*, and it was standing in a posture that you can only describe as attentive.

You will try to describe what you saw. You will fail. Here is the attempt:

It was tall. Not tall like a tall person — tall like a fin is tall, tall like a hoodoo is tall, tall like the ratio between a human body and the geological column it stands beside has been quietly revised upward. Its proportions were wrong in a way that took your eyes several seconds to process, the way you process a sentence in a language you are learning, assembling meaning from parts that do not map cleanly onto your own grammar. The limbs were too long, or the torso was too short, or the relationship between the parts was governed by a skeletal logic that is not yours, that operates under rules you can feel but not articulate, like a chord you have heard in a dream and cannot reconstruct upon waking.

Its surface was dark. Not dark like skin, not dark like stone, but dark the way the space between basalt columns is dark — a darkness that has substance, that resists the light, that seems to pull at the edges of your vision with something like gravity. You could not see its features, or it had no features to see, or it had features that existed in a register your visual system does not have access to, the way you cannot see ultraviolet. You knew it faced you. You knew this the way you know someone is awake in a dark room — not from any visible sign, but from the quality of the room's attention.

And then you looked directly at it, and it was not there.

But sometimes — not often, but sometimes — when you look away again, you catch it in your peripheral vision one more time, in a position slightly different from before. Not closer. Not farther. *Rotated*, perhaps. As if it had shifted its weight. As if it had been considering your back while you were looking at the void where it was.

You will begin to wonder whether it is following you. You will stop wondering this, because the wondering itself feels like an interaction, and you do not want to encourage anything.

---

There is a third possibility, and this one is harder to admit.

The builders did not leave. They are still here. They are not the figures at the edge of your vision — or maybe they are, but not in any way that preserves the distinction between builder and built, between the hand that stacked the cairn and the cairn itself. There are theories, or there will be, or there were, held by the few who have spent enough time in the Weald to stop flinching at its geometries: that the original inhabitants did not die here, or leave here, but *dissolved* here, that their bodies, their tools, their pathways of movement through the landscape, were absorbed into the stone the way the mineral trails are absorbed, the way the condensation beads on the underside of the overhangs. That they became part of the Weald's metabolism. That every basalt column is, at some level of resolution, a person. That the hum you feel in your molars is not the stone vibrating but the stone *digesting*, and that what it is digesting is the memory of everyone who has ever stood here and looked up at the dark water sky and felt the bright weight of the sun pressing them flat.

You cannot prove this. You can stand in the center of a ring of seven knee-height stones on an exposed ridge and press your palms against two fin-walls simultaneously and feel the hum pass through your wrists and up your forearms and into your sternum and nearly — nearly — reach the place behind your eyes where you keep the image of the figure you cannot see directly, and you can understand that this is a kind of evidence, but it is not the kind that survives translation into language. You can understand that the cairns that look new are built from stones that bear fracture faces too sharp to be weathered, which means they were broken recently, which means someone — or something — is still stacking them, but you will never see it happen, because the time between one visit and the next is not consistent, because the stone is always falling and always being remade, because the boundary between erosion and construction has worn away like the boundary between the edge of a fin and the sky.

You will stop trying to distinguish between the builders and the landscape.

You will begin to suspect that this is what the inhabitants want. Not want — *inhabit* is the wrong word too, but it is the best you have. That the Weald is not a place where something lives but a place where something *is living*, slowly, on a scale that makes geology look like a heartbeat, and that the films on the overhangs and the figures at the edge of your sight and the new cairns and the humming fins and the water that has no taste you can name are all the same process, seen from different distances, and that you, standing here, drinking water that satisfies no thirst, watching shadows move when you move, hearing echoes that contain breathing that is not yours, have become part of this process too — not because you were taken, but because the process is *inclusive*, because it has always been inclusive, because the Weald of Vertical Silence has never excluded anything from itself but has merely, with the patience of stone, drawn it in.

The films grow.
The cairns rise.
The figures stand at the edge of sight, attentive, dark, and you are almost certain — you are never certain of anything here, certainty being a luxury of stable frames of reference, and there are no stable frames here — you are almost certain that they are not watching you.

They are watching *through* you.

You are a surface. You are an overhang. The light presses down and the mist rises and somewhere in the space between the basalt fins, something that has never been alive in the sense you were taught to understand the word is metabolizing the shadow you cast, and the shadow is wrong, and the shadow is yours, and the shadow is not a shadow at all but a deposit, a residue, a thin persistent film of you left on the stone, growing in the dark, branching, anastomosing, pulsing with a slow wet metabolism that has almost nothing in common with anything terrestrial.

The Weald breathes.

You exhale, and the mist rises to meet you.

The films do not react.

The films never react.

The films *remember*.
