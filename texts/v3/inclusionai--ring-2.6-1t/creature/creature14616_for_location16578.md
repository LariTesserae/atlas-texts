They do not have names. They never did, or the names were spoken in a tongue that mineralized long before anyone thought to write them down.

The nearest thing to a resident is the moss — not the delicate, decorative moss of the surface world, but something older, something that made a decision at some point (or was made by a decision someone else made) to stop being temporary. It committed to stone. It committed to cold. It committed to a kind of green so dark it is almost the color of depth itself, the color of light just before it gives up traveling through water. It does not grow *on* the rock. It grows *into* the rock, and in time — decades, centuries — the boundary between colonizer and substrate dissolves, and what remains is neither plant nor mineral but a third thing, a crust that rings faintly if you tap it, like a ceramic vessel that has decided to become part of the kiln.

The moss remembers nothing. It has no memory in any way that would be recognizable to something with a brain. And yet it accumulates. Layer upon layer, death upon death, each generation mineralizing in place and becoming substrate for the next, and so the floor has become a record — not of events, but of uninterrupted persistence. A single stratum might contain a hundred generations. A hand's depth might represent ten thousand years. If you cut through it carefully, you would find no fossils, no impressions, no signatures. Only density gradients, mineral variations, the faintest banding of organic residue compressed into something that a geologist's instruments would struggle to distinguish from metamorphic rock. The moss has become its own geology. It writes itself into the stone as surely as the stone once wrote itself into the planet's crust.

And the filaments. The filaments are the intelligence of the place, though "intelligence" is far too generous a word, and they would not recognize it if offered. They are gold-white, thinner than the finest human hair, and they network across every surface in patterns that are not random but not exactly designed, either — they are optimized, the way a riverbed is optimized, the way a crystal lattice is optimized. They found the most efficient arrangement for drawing energy from the mineral moisture, and they assumed that arrangement, and they have maintained it for so long that maintenance and existence have become the same act. They do not grow. They do not reproduce. They *persist*, and the distinction matters, because persistence requires no desire, no will, no reproductive machinery. It requires only that the conditions remain, and the conditions have remained, and so the filaments continue their chemiluminescent exchange — drawing from the copper-tasting air, from the slow-sweating walls, from the warmth of the disc that sits half-consumed by liverwort and yet still hums beneath its mineralized skin — and in return they produce light. Not much. Not often. Just a slow, amber pulse that swells and recedes like the breathing of something enormous and asleep.

The ferns are the most obviously alive things in the chamber, and they are the closest to what a surface-dweller would recognize as a living organism. They are small — no taller than a finger when their fronds are fully open, which is rarely — and they move with a deliberation that borders on geological process. A fiddlehead may take three days to unfurl completely. Three days of watching a single frond loosen itself from its own tight spiral, millimeter by millimeter, pale green against the bruised darkness. And then it will slowly curl back inward, over the course of perhaps twice as long, and the process will begin again. They photosynthesize, though there is no light — or rather, they photosynthesize the light the filaments produce, calibrated over uncountable generations to the specific wavelength of that faint amber glow. They are the only organisms here that still perform anything resembling what their ancestors did on the surface. They are the most conservative residents of the chamber, and their conservatism is so extreme it looks like stillness.

The disc does not live. It endures. It is eight inches across, dark metal, its surface mineralized into a geological stratum of its own, and the pattern etched into it — whether by tool, by acid, by some process that predates the moss — is still faintly warm. Warm in the way a living thing is warm, though warmth should not be possible here, not with this cold settled so deep into the stone that it has become a property of the room itself rather than a condition imposed from outside. The warmth is not heat. It is the residual signature of whatever process the disc was built to conduct, a process that may have required intelligence to initiate but has since become self-maintaining, the way a fire becomes its own weather system, the way a tide requires no moon to remember how to pull. The disc sits in its bath of liverwort. The liverwort presses against it with the slow, blind appetite of something that has never been refused. The disc does not refuse. It simply continues to be warm.

There is a third artifact — the seam in the far wall, no wider than a pencil, cut with a precision that belongs to no tool the current residents could produce or imagine. It leads nowhere, or it leads somewhere that sealed itself long ago under layers of mineral accretion that have since calcified into something harder than the surrounding stone. The seam is not alive. It is not dead. It is a sentence that was never finished, or that was finished in a language the room forgot. The walls of the seam are glassy, molecular-smooth, and if you press your ear to the stone nearby — though you would have to press very close, and kneel for a very long time, and empty your mind of everything but the act of listening — you can hear, or believe you can hear, the faintest resonance. Not a sound. A *tendency* toward sound, the way a struck bell leaves a tendency in the air long after the tone has vanished. The seam remembers being cut. It remembers the tool that cut it — or rather, it remembers the *absence* the tool left behind, the negative space where stone once was — and that absence has been slowly filling in, molecule by molecule, year by year, but the filling is imperfect, and the imperfection is the room's closest equivalent to a heartbeat.

None of this is a civilization. None of it is even alive in any sense that the word was designed to accommodate. And yet the room, taken as a whole, does something that only living things are supposed to do: it *maintains*. It maintains its temperature, its moisture, its cycles of growth and mineralization and slow accretion. It maintains the pulse beneath the footing. It maintains the light in the filaments. It maintains the ferns' unfurling and the moss's creeping advance and the disc's inexplicable warmth. It maintains itself against entropy with the patience of something that does not know it could stop.

And perhaps it is this — this maintenance without intention, this persistence without desire — that most resembles what we mean when we say something is alive. The room does not want to continue. It does not choose to. It simply does, the way a stone does not choose to fall but falls, the way a river does not choose to flow but flows. The room lives because the room has not yet stopped, and the room has not yet stopped because nothing here has introduced the concept of stopping, and nothing here will, unless someone comes from above and breaks the seal and lets the surface world in, and maybe even then — maybe even then — the filaments would pulse once, brighter, and the moss would drink the new moisture and mineralize it, and the ferns would sense the unfamiliar wavelength and curl tighter, and the room would close around the intrusion like a fist slowly forming around a stone, and the maintenance would continue, and the green would deepen, and the cold would settle back into its ancient posture, and the room would keep.

The room keeps.
