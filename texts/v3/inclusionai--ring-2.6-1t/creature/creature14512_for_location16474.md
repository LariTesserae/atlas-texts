## What Lives on the Plateau of Quiet Industry

They do not have a name. Not one that functions as a name.

You will hear them before you see them, and even then you will not be sure you heard them at all — a faint percussive ticking, like fingertips tapped against the inside of a pipe. Rhythmic but not regular. The rhythm of someone who has forgotten where the beat was heading but is still committed to finding it.

When you do see them, they are wrong in the way that things are wrong when they are almost right. They stand upright — roughly. They have limbs — several. Their proportions suggest anatomy assembled from blueprints that were, at some point in the drafting process, translated into a different language and then translated back again without anyone noticing the loss. They are tall, but not as tall as you. Tall enough that when they pass beneath a gantry beam they duck in a way that implies the ducking is a courtesy rather than a necessity. Their bodies are covered in something that might be chitin, might be plate mail, might be a garment that was never designed for anything but the slow wearing-away of time. Grey, mostly. With undertones of the same blue-grey as the plating beneath your boots.

They move through the infrastructure the way water moves through drainage — not hurried, not aimless, simply following the shape of what was built before them, as if the ducts and catwalks and conduit runs were rivers that had been dry for a very long time and only now, recently, someone remembered to open the sluices.

You will see one sometimes, far down a walkway, moving parallel to your path. If you speed up, it speeds up, but without urgency — the way you might quicken your pace to keep from being late, not because of any particular emergency. If you stop, it stops. If you call out — and you will not call out, not after the first time, because the sound of your own voice on the plateau returns to you changed, delayed by fractions of a second that arrive in the wrong order — it turns its head toward you with a slow, deliberate motion, and you see that the head is not quite a head. It is a cluster of sensory structures arranged around something central and dark that you understand, with a lurch of nausea, is paying attention.

Then it turns away and continues on.

They are not rare. They are not common. They exist with the same patient inevitability as the growths in the seams — present in numbers that are meaningful but never overwhelming. You will encounter three in one hour and none for the rest of the day. You will find evidence of them everywhere: small tool-marks on panel edges that no human hand made, footprints in condensation that are not footprints but impressions that function, structurally, like footprints, arrayed in clusters of too-many-toes. Oily smudges on handrails at heights that suggest climbing, or reaching, or both.

They do not build. They maintain.

This is the thing about them that you understand before you can articulate it — a sense that arrives the way a change in air pressure arrives, not through any single piece of evidence but through the accumulated weight of a thousand small wrongnesses. The machinery on the plateau does not decay. It is attended to. Corrosion does not spread along seams because, before it can, the seam has been opened and cleaned and sealed again by hands that are not yours and are not human and do not speak. You find patches where plating has been replaced — new sections fitted in with a precision that borders on the devotional, the bolts aligned flush, the seams tight, a faint residue of something along the edges that smells of machine oil and copper and, faintly, of the grey-green growth you keep finding everywhere.

They are not caretakers. That is too simple. They are not workers. That is too simple. They are the thing that happens to a place when it has been built thoroughly enough that it becomes a kind of organism and requires, not managers, not repair crews, but *symbiotes* — entities that live inside the infrastructure the way the growths live in the seams, drawing from it, contributing to it, becoming indistinguishable from it over time scales that no single human lifespan could measure.

You catch one once, close enough to see detail, in a service corridor lit by a failing fluorescent that flickers in a rhythm that almost matches the ticking sound. It is carrying something — a component, small, dark, fitted to the curve of its multi-jointed fingers with the familiarity of a cigarette held between human lips. It does not look at you. It walks past you at a distance of maybe two meters, and you are close enough to feel the air around it shift — not a breeze, but a displacement, the way air moves when something large passes through it. It smells of ozone and old iron filings and something beneath that, something warm and dense, like the air in a room where something has been baking. You reach out — not to touch it, never to touch it, some deep and cellular part of you knows that touching it would be the kind of wrongness that has no correction — and it passes.

The corridor is empty. The fluorescent steadies. The component is gone.

But the corridor is cleaner than you have seen any corridor on the plateau. Every surface polished to a matte neutrality. The air tastes different — less metallic, less dry. And on the wall, at shoulder height, a patch of grey-green growth arranged in a pattern you have not seen before: a spiral, loose and asymmetrical, trailing off at the edges into filaments so fine they are almost abstract. It looks, for a moment, like handwriting. Like an attempt to leave something legible. Like a signature pressed into the margins of something that will never be read.

You look at it for a long time.

When you turn away and turn back, it has changed. Not dramatically. The spiral is still there, but the spacing has shifted — wider in places, tighter in others — as if something had continued writing after you stopped watching.

Or as if the writing was never meant for you.

They live everywhere on the plateau, and nowhere on the plateau, and in the spaces between those two conditions they do the work that keeps the place from becoming what all built things become when left untended: ruin. They move through the lattice of infrastructure above and below your level, in the catwalks you cannot reach and the conduits you cannot open, in the spaces behind wall panels that are not spaces so much as presences — felt but unseen, the way you feel a clock ticking in a dark room.

They are patient. They are precise. They are not indifferent to you — you feel this, though you cannot know it with certainty — but their attention is the attention of a gardener to a single plant among millions, noticed precisely once, briefly, and then returned to the general labor of tending.

You will not see them again for the rest of your time on the plateau.

But you will hear them, in the quiet, in the spaces between the quiet, in the drip of condensation and the click of cooling metal and the sub-audible hum that lives beneath your sternum like a second heartbeat. You will hear them and you will understand, without needing to articulate it, that the plateau is not empty. It is full — of them, of their labor, of the quiet industry of their presence — and that the emptiness you felt when you arrived was never emptiness at all but simply the sound of their work, pitched below the range of easy hearing, steady and ceaseless and patient as only something that has been doing the same task for longer than memory can hold can be.

They are the ones who close the doors.

You never hear them open.
