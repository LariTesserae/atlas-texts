## The Inhabitants of the Quiet Plateau

---

They are not animals, not exactly. Not plants. Not minerals, though they borrow from the stone so thoroughly that you can be forgiven for assuming they grew there, the way a stalactite grows — patient, mineral, inevitable.

They are the first thing you understand about this place. Not the sky, not the light, not the strange geometry of a world without a proper sun. The creatures. Because you cannot take a single step without acknowledging them, and after a few hours you cannot remember what the ground looked like beneath them, as if they had always been there, as if the plateau were not a landscape at all but a living surface — a skin — stretched over the bones of the world.

There are so many.

You will try to count them once. You will kneel and try to estimate how many occupy a square metre of the valley floor. You will fail. They exceed the eye's capacity to individuate. They exist the way sand exists: you can grasp a handful, but the dune refuses to be numbered. In the places where they are densest — the broad valley floors, the sheltered basins where ancient water left its mineral deposits — you cannot see the ground at all. Only them. A field of small, hard shapes packed together with the intimacy of pearls in an oyster shell, and just as indifferent to your presence.

---

### The Shells

This is what you will call them at first, for lack of anything better. *Shells*. They are the size of a closed fist at their largest, though most are smaller — the size of a walnut, a tangerine, a cupped hand. Ovoid, mostly, though some are flattened along one axis, worn into shapes that feel hydrodynamic, as if water had once shaped them, back when water moved on the surface with more confidence. Others are nearly spherical. Some approach radial symmetry, built outward from a central point in concentric chambers sealed shut, each one a tiny fortress with rooms inside that nothing will ever enter again.

Their surfaces are mineral-dark. Iron-grey to near-black. But hold one in your hand — if you can pry it loose from the grip of its neighbours, which requires more force than you'd expect, a strange suction, a tenacity that has nothing to do with muscle — and turn it under the pewter light, and you will see fractures in the surface that catch the dimness and throw it back as colour. Not the colours you know. Not red or blue or green in any way your language has tidy words for. Iridescent, yes, but the word is inadequate. It is the colour of oil on dark water. The colour of a soap film held up to a light that doesn't fully exist. It shifts as you rotate the shell, and for a moment you feel as though you are looking at something interior — something that was meant to be seen from inside, by a creature whose eyes you cannot find.

Because there are no eyes. No mouth you can discern. No opening of any kind that your first examination would reveal. The shell is seamless, or nearly so — there are seams, fine as hair, circling the body in tight whorls, but they do not open. They are sutures. The lines where growth met itself and pressed together and said *here, and here, and here*. They are like the lines on a skull, if a skull could grow new chambers every year and seal them shut behind it.

Underneath — the ventral surface, the part that faces the stone — is smooth and flat. Flush against the ground. They sit on the plateau the way a sleeping face sits on a pillow: wholly surrendered, wholly present, with an intimacy that feels almost involuntary. You look at a field of them and you think of a congregation. You think of a burial ground. You think of something you once read about ancient seabeds, about the way the shells of dead organisms accumulated over millennia until they became the stone itself — and you wonder if these are alive, or if they are in the process of becoming something else, something geological, something that will be quarried and cut and named in a language that does not yet exist.

But they move.

This is the thing. This is what you will not forgive them for, or what you will find most unsettling — not their stillness, but the *kind* of stillness. It is not the stillness of sleep. It is not the stillness of death. It is the stillness of *waiting*, which is a different thing entirely. A creature that is asleep is not aware that it is still. A creature that is dead has forgotten movement. But a creature that is *waiting* — that has folded its intent into a patience so profound it no longer resembles intent at all — that creature is awake in a way you cannot quite see, and it is watching you with organs you cannot find.

You will mark a spot on your second day. You will arrange a small cairn — not to disturb the shells, but to define your reference point. You will photograph the arrangement from three angles. You will walk away for four hours. When you return, the shells have rearranged themselves. Not dramatically. Not in any way your first glance would catch. But one near your left boot-print has rotated fifteen degrees clockwise. The cluster you knelt beside has closed ranks where your weight forced them apart — and now there is no gap, no evidence you were ever there. One shell has shifted two of its own body-lengths, which is nothing, which is less than nothing, which is *centimetres in four hours* — and you will feel something loosen in your understanding of what speed means, what patience means, what it means to be alive on a timescale that your body can feel but your mind cannot hold.

Over days, they migrate centimetres. Over seasons, they rearrange the texture of the ground. The fine regolith on the valley floors is held in place not by geology but by their sheer accumulated weight — a living sediment, a crust of organisms pressing themselves into the earth with a persistence that water and wind cannot overcome. They eat the dark crusts that grow on the rock, or they eat what the crusts leave behind when they contract, or they eat the mineral films that appear on exposed surfaces during the bright phase of the light-cycle. No one watching them has ever been certain. They do not seem to breathe. They do not seem to metabolize in any way you can measure. They simply *persist*, with the persistence of stone itself, and you begin to understand that the boundary between organism and geology is not a line here — it is a gradient, a slow transition, a conversation between the living and the ancient that has been going on for so long that neither side remembers it began.

### The Crusts

You will look at them and think: *this is the simplest thing here*. You will be wrong.

The crusts are colonies. Communities. Neither plant nor bacterial mat nor lichen, though they share characteristics with all of these. They spread across the valley floors and lower slopes in thin, dark sheets — black-green when dry, nearly black when wet, and everywhere in between, so that the ground itself seems to shift colour depending on where you are in the brightness cycle. From a distance, they look like stains. Like water damage on an ancient manuscript. Like something that happened to the rock a long time ago and never went away.

But up close, a single square metre of crust is a universe.

Filaments. Tiny, thread-like structures that extend from the surface of the mat in tight, organized arrays — not randomly, not in the loose way that fungal hyphae spread, but with a precision that suggests architecture. Pod-like structures, barely visible to the naked eye, arranged in clusters along the filament junctions, each one a closed vessel whose function is unknown. Surfaces that bead moisture into perfect spheres — water placed on the crust by condensation or by the thin films that flow across the rock during the warming phase does not spread or soak in. Instead, it pulls itself into spheres, each one the size of a pinhead, and rolls across the colony like mercury across glass, gathering nothing, leaving nothing, moving with a purpose that belongs to surface tension and nothing else.

During the bright phase, the crusts extend their filaments into the air — millimetres, no more, but enough to change the texture of the ground from something inert to something that looks, if you are willing to stretch the word, *alive*. The valley floors seem to ripple. The dark sheets soften. For a few hours, the plateau looks almost gentle, almost soft, and you might think — if you did not know better — that you were looking at a grassland from a great distance, or a meadow seen through fog.

Then the brightness dims. The filaments retract. The mats contract into the stone, pulling themselves flat, sealing themselves against the cold, and you are left looking at rock again — *rock*, nothing but rock — as if the living world had never touched it at all.

They are older than the shell-creatures. You can tell from the way the shells eat them, the way the shell-creatures orient themselves on the valley floors where the crusts are thickest, as though they are diners gathered around a table. The crusts were here before the industry. They were here, perhaps, before the atmosphere had the breath for anything this complex. They do not care about the shafts. They do not care about you. They tighten their filaments and they wait for the light, and the light comes, and the light goes, and they open, and they close, and they are the oldest patient thing on the plateau.

### The Others

Between the shells and the crusts, there are others.

In the narrow spaces between rocks — the crevices, the fractures, the tight seams in the escarpments — there are thin-bodied things. Flattened to an extraordinary degree, as if they had been pressed by a force that understood exactly how much a living thing could be compressed without ceasing to function. They ripple when they sense vibration. Not when they are touched — they have no touch, at least not in the way you would recognize. They sense the displacement of air, the faintest tremor in the stone, the pressure wave of your footsteps transmitted through the rock at a distance you cannot calculate. When they ripple, their bodies move in coordinated waves, like a flag in a wind that has no source, and for a moment the crack in the rock looks like it is breathing.

On the cliff faces, anchored to the stone with holdfasts that have dissolved the surrounding rock into a socket shaped exactly to their bodies, the thread-things extend upward during the bright phase and retract during the dim. They are long — three, four metres — and thin as sewing thread, pale grey or translucent, and they hang in the air like filaments of a web that has no centre. They filter. The air on the plateau is not empty: it carries particulate matter, mineral dust, ice crystals, the faintest moisture. The thread-things catch this material in structures so fine you need magnification to see them, and they draw it slowly inward, metre by metre, pulling sustenance from the atmosphere the way the crusts pull it from the rock. They are silent. They are patient. They look like nothing so much as the thoughts of the plateau, made visible and thin and swaying in a wind that does not blow.

And in the valleys, where subsurface water occasionally pools — in the low places where the faint seepages emerge and the cold cannot quite prevent liquid from existing — there are larger things. Sluggish. Many-limbed. Half-submerged, with their bodies half in the water and half in the mud, and their limbs splayed out across the basin floor like the roots of a tree that has fallen into a shallow pond. They are rare. Solitary. You will see perhaps one in a day, if the conditions are right, if the basin is full enough, if the bright phase has lasted long enough to thin the ice at the edges. They do not flee when you approach. They do not flee when you kneel beside them and watch the slow, deliberate motion of their limbs pulling themselves a few centimetres through the muck. They leave tracks — overlapping, crisscrossing, filling the basin floors with a palimpsest of trails that look like cuneiform, like a writing system designed for a language that has only one word, repeated ten thousand times.

They are not the same species, probably. The valley things are too different from the cliff things and the gap-things and the ground-shells. They are not even the same *kind* of life, if "kind" means anything on a plateau where the boundary between organism and environment has been eroded to nothing. But they are all part of the same density, the same coverage, the same strange insistence on *being here* — on pressing themselves into every crack, every surface, every space that the geology has made available. They are the reef. They are the forest floor. They are the thing that happens when life has had time — not millions of years, not hundreds of millions, but *billions* — to fill every silence with a body.

---

### The Hum

And beneath all of them, running through them, connecting them in a way that is not neural and not chemical and not mechanical but something older than any of those — the hum.

Press your hand flat against the ground where the fauna are thickest. Hold it there. Do not move. And you will feel it: a vibration so low that it exists at the border between tactile and auditory, between feeling and hearing, between your body and the stone. It is not a pulse. It is not a rhythm. It has no tempo, no period, no rise and fall. It is continuous. It is subsonic. It sits below the frequency of speech, below the frequency of music, in a register so deep that your chest becomes the resonating chamber — your sternum, your ribs, the spaces between your organs, all of them vibrating in sympathy with a sound you cannot quite hear but cannot mistake.

It may be the movement of air through the interstices of a billion hard bodies — the shells packed so tightly that the gaps between them form channels, and the wind (what wind there is) moves through those channels and sets them humming the way a finger on a wine glass sets the glass singing. It may be thermal — differential heating of the shell-creatures' surfaces, expansion and contraction at scales too small to see, a trillion tiny adjustments that sum to a single, sustained note. It may be communication. It may be incidental. No one has settled this.

What matters is that it is there. That it rearranges something in you when you feel it. That it makes you understand, in a way your conscious mind cannot fully articulate, that the plateau is not a place where things live but a place that *is* living — slowly, diffusely, on a scale that makes a forest look like a spark, a reef look like a word spoken too quickly to catch.

The fauna are part of this. The crusts are part of this. The thread-things and the valley-things and the gap-ripple-creatures are part of this. They are not organized. They are not coordinated. They do not act as one. But they are *dense*, and their density generates something that none of them could generate alone — a subsonic chorus, a geological murmur, a sound that is the voice of ten billion small and patient lives, each one pressing its body against the stone and saying, in a language older than hearing: *here. Here. Here.*

And the plateau listens. And the plateau hums back. And the plateau, at the boundary between living and not-living, between organism and world, continues its slow conversation with itself — indifferent, and complete, and older than anything that has ever stood on its surface and called it home.
