## The Inhabitants of the Lattice Ward

They are not a people you would recognize immediately as remarkable.

The first ones you see are usually the conduit-scrapers — small figures in waxed canvas smocks, faces darkened by hand-oil, moving along the root columns with the practiced ease of children climbing a familiar tree. They carry coil-hooks and wire-brushes and small pots of conductive paste the color of dried blood. Their job is to keep the first-generation wiring clear of bark-growth, and they do it the way their parents did, and their parents before them: by touch, mostly, because the conductivity of the living tissue changes with moisture and you can feel the difference through the copper. They hum while they work, not from radio or habit but because the Ward is quiet during outflow cycles and silence here feels like pressure against the ears. They leave their humming behind on the surfaces and the next shift can sometimes tell, by the faintest residual vibration in the copper, who was working last.

The scrapers are not a guild. There is no formal induction. You learn by standing next to someone until they stop flinching at the movement of your hands. Most of them are long-limbed — a practical adaptation, the joke goes, though the bone-density studies the University cartographers once proposed were politely declined — and they have an unusual comfort with height, working forty feet up on swaying root arches as casually as others would walk a hallway. Their hands are callused in specific patterns: the fingertips rough, the palms smooth, because they grip with fingers and brace with the heel of the hand against bark. They can tell the age of a bracket by the taste of the corrosion. They are not sentimental about the old ironwork, but neither do they rush. The Ward does not reward rushing.

Below them, in the avenues where the mist collects, you find the cistern-tenders. They are a different kind of quiet. Their work is water — measuring the levels in the translucent root-cisterns, testing for mineral load, adjusting the sheet-metal channels that carry rainfall to the storage points. They are the ones who know the rain schedules best, not from instruments but from the smell of the air and the particular way the bioluminescent threads in the columns brighten hours before precipitation, a chemiluminescent precursor flare visible only if you know where to look. The tenders walk slowly. Their children, if they watch long enough, learn to walk slowly too, and visitors often remark on it — the pace of the Lattice Ward is the pace of water finding a channel, not the pace of people going somewhere.

There are others. The transformer-minders, who sit in the cradle-welds among the oil-cooled units and monitor the deep hum, adjusting tap-changers with long insulated rods. They are the closest thing the Ward has to priests, though they would not use the word. Some of them report what they call *listening dreams* — periods of deep sleep during which the harmonic signature of the transformers seems to continue inside the skull, not as sound but as structure. They wake with a certainty about the state of the grid that they can verify instrumentally and almost always find to be correct. They do not theorize about this. They consider it a skill, like echolocation, and are mildly irritated when the University people call it anomalous cognition.

The children grow up understanding the three layers before they understand the concept of *outside*. They play games of *phase-tag* — running through the avenues, freezing during inflow when sound carries and anyone could hear you, moving during outflow when the silence grants you cover. They climb the secondary root-growth, which the scrapers prune back from the cable runs and leave in piles at the base of the columns, perfect for building into the latticed structures that give the Ward its name. The children build rooms and corridors and little kingdoms of trimmed wood and salvaged conduit, and they disappear into them for hours. No one checks on them because the piles are always near the adults' work and the Ward is, by nature, transparent — the light is always even, the mist rarely thick enough to obscure more than a few paces.

The old ones sit where the avenues widen into small plazas — spots where the root-columns flare and the ground is firmer, packed down by generations of footfall. They watch. That is not a metaphor; it is the primary activity of the old in the Lattice Ward. They sit on root-knobs and smoothed brackets and they watch the work with an attention that is not idle and not supervisory but something else — the attention of a system monitoring itself. They speak rarely and in a register so low that visitors sometimes think they are not speaking at all but are instead demonstrating, for the benefit of the undertrained ear, the sub-vocal frequencies the Ward's bioluminescence responds to. This is almost certainly not true. But the children who watch the old ones sometimes notice, in the outflow phase, that the green-gold threads in the root walls pulse slightly faster when the old ones are talking.

The pale beetles are tolerated. They colonize the junction boxes and short-circuit occasionally, and the technicians brush them out with electrostatic wands, gently, the way you might brush crumbs from a shared table. No one has been able to breed them in captivity. They appear to migrate between boxes along the electromagnetic gradients of the grid itself, following load patterns with a fidelity that exceeds any sensor the Ward possesses. The conduit-scrapers call them *grid-mice* and consider them good luck — not for any superstitious reason but because a thriving beetle population indicates clean electromagnetic conditions, which means the first-generation wiring is holding.

The wading birds nobody owns. They arrive at dawn in ones and twos from whatever lies beyond the eastern root wall — a place the residents call *outside* with the same neutral, pro-forma tone they use for weather. The birds pick through the detritus mat, eat what they find, and leave by midday. There have been attempts to mark them, track them, understand where they go. The markers always come back on different birds, weeks later, perched in the Ward like they had never left. The ornithologists from the University eventually concluded that the species avoids open sky — that the birds travel entirely through enclosed root-systems, a network no one has fully mapped. The Ward residents were not surprised.

And then there is the thrumming deeper in — the arrhythmic pulse that vibrates the puddles and is catalogued under *ambient vibration source, unclassified.* The residents do not speak of it, not out of fear or taboo but out of a kind of commercial courtesy, the way one does not discuss the inner workings of a business partner's machinery. Occasionally, a new arrival asks what it is, and the answer is always the same: *It depends on the phase. During inflow it's slower. During outflow it's deeper. It's been here longer than the transformers.* This is true. This is also the kind of answer that provides no information whatsoever, and the new arrivals who stay long enough stop asking.

The people of the Lattice Ward do not think of themselves as living inside something. They think of themselves as living *with* something, the way one lives with weather or with a longstanding neighbor whose habits are never fully understood but whose presence is the texture of the day. The organism breathes, and they breathe with it. It regrows, and they tend the regrowth. The transformers hum, and they listen. The lattice corrects, and they correct with it — a wire reseated, a root-pruned, a corroded bracket replaced with a salvaged part that will corrode in its turn. No one here is building for the future. No one here is preserving the past. They are maintaining the present, which in the Lattice Ward is a living thing — patient, slow, and older than anyone's memory, including its own.

There is an expression the children learn before they learn to read, a phrase threaded through the Ward like the bioluminescence threads through the root walls. It has no clear translation. The closest rendering is *the hum is home* — but that implies the hum is separate, and the children, who have not yet learned to make that distinction, would not understand why anyone would say it that way. They simply stand still, and they feel the pulse, and they know.
