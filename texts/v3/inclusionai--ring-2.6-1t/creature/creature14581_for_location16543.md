**The Inhabitants of the Rooted Borough**

What you see from the rim is a city of green, but the true citizens are invisible to the naked eye.  They move, breathe, and build in the wet, dim corridors that the larger plants have woven together.  Below the canopy‑mats, in the spongy cushion‑moss floor, in the crevices of the columnar stems, and in the dripping curtains of epiphytic root‑fibers, a teeming, microscopic metropolis thrives.

---

### The Foundation Layer – Microbial Pioneers

The first settlers are the **bacteria** and **archaea** that colonize every wet surface.  In the thin film of condensation that beads on the mosses, rod‑shaped *Pseudomonas* and *Bacillus* glide along water films, scavenging dissolved organic carbon leached from the living plants.  Among them, **nitrogen‑fixing cyanobacteria**—tiny filaments of *Nostoc* and *Anabaena*—form gelatinous colonies that cling to the undersides of cushion‑plant leaves, turning atmospheric N₂ into ammonia that feeds the whole community.

Their waste, in turn, is a feast for **fungi**.  Mycelial networks of **ectomycorrhizal basidiomycetes**—pale, branching hyphae that glisten like wet silk—wrap around the roots of the dwarf heath‑relatives and the columnar sedges.  These fungi trade soil minerals (phosphorus, potassium, trace metals) for the sugars the plants photosynthesize, stitching the “buildings” together underground.  In the soft, decomposing bases of older columns, **saprotrophic ascomycetes** (e.g., *Cladosporium*, *Penicillium*) break down cellulose and lignin, releasing a slow, steady pulse of nutrients that the mosses eagerly absorb.

---

### The Tiny Grazers – Micro‑Arthropods and Nematodes

Where the fungal hyphae thicken, a hidden herd of **micro‑arthropods** appears.  **Springtails** (Collembola) hop from cushion to cushion, their bodies barely a millimeter long, feeding on fungal spores and decaying plant fragments.  Their silvery, waxy cuticles glisten in the dim light, and when disturbed they launch themselves into the mist, vanishing like tiny, living raindrops.

**Mites**—oribatid and prostigmatid—crawl over the same surfaces, their many legs feeling for the thin biofilm of algae that coats the moss.  They are the borough’s janitors, shredding dead tissue and mixing it into the substrate, ensuring that nothing is wasted.

In the water films that run down the column walls, **nematodes**—most only a few hundred micrometers long—wiggle through the slime, grazing on bacteria and fungal hyphae.  Some are predators, others are fungal feeders, and a few are parasites of the tiny arthropods, creating a delicate, three‑dimensional food web that pulses in the perpetual drizzle.

---

### The Epiphytic Canopy – A Second‑Story Ecosystem

Higher up, where the columnar canopies nearly touch, the **massive epiphytic orchids** (their jointed, pale stems resembling the ribs of a ship) host a distinct community.  Their aerial roots are wrapped in a thick mat of **mycorrhizal fungi** that siphon water and minerals from the dripping air.  Tiny **bryophytes**—minute liverworts and hornworts—cling to the root surfaces, photosynthesizing in the filtered light and adding a thin, green veneer to the orchid’s skeleton.

Within the orchid’s root crevices, **rotifers** and **tardigrades** (the “water bears”) find refuge in the thin water films.  When the moisture thins, they enter a dormant tun, only to revive when the next condensation event beads the surface.  Their presence is a reminder that even the most minute water pocket can be a world unto itself.

---

### The Unseen Engineers – Fungal Connectors

Perhaps the most remarkable residents are the **mycorrhizal networks** that link column to column, bridge to bridge.  Hyphae thread through the cushion‑moss floor, climb the heath‑wood stems, and weave through the epiphytic lattice, forming a living conduit that transports nutrients, water, and even chemical signals between plants.  When a column begins to soften, the network swiftly redistributes its stored carbon, allowing neighboring plants to shore up the weakening structure.  In this way the borough repairs itself, a city that never truly sleeps, its repairs carried out by an army of fungal filaments.

---

### The Rhythm of Decay and Renewal

All of these organisms work in concert with the slow, patient pulse of growth and decay.  As a column ages, its base softens, fungi infiltrate, and the once‑rigid heath‑wood becomes a spongy nursery for new mosses and seedlings.  The microbial community accelerates this transformation, breaking down lignin, releasing nitrogen, and creating a fertile patch where a new column may eventually rise.  The cycle repeats endlessly: birth, expansion, senescence, decomposition, and rebirth—all happening at the scale of millimeters and minutes, yet shaping the macroscopic architecture that you see from the rim.

---

### In Summary

The Rooted Borough is not a silent ruin; it is a bustling, invisible city.  Its citizens are bacteria, cyanobacteria, mycorrhizal fungi, saprotrophic molds, springtails, mites, nematodes, rotifers, tardigrades, and a host of microscopic algae.  They live in the water films, within the living walls of the columnar plants, and on the aerial roots of the epiphytic orchids.  Together they drive the borough’s metabolism, turning light, moisture, and stone into a continuously rebuilding, breathing structure—a place where the unseen is as vital as the visible, and where life persists in the quiet, perpetual drizzle of a high‑altitude cloud forest.
