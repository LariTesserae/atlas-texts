# The Inhabitants of the Vault

---

Nothing here was placed on purpose. Everything arrived — was born, was carried down, was blown in through a fracture, was shipped in a crate because someone thought it would be useful — and then the Vault decided what it would become. The inhabitants are not residents in any comfortable sense. They are participants in a system that is still writing its own rules, and they know it, and they stay anyway. Some because they can't leave. Most because they've started to want things the Vault offers that the surface no longer can.

---

**The Foundry Workers**

They arrive for shifts in freight elevators that rattle so badly the copper taste in the air sharpens to a bite. They wear standard-issue thermal regulation suits, but half of them have stopped bothering with the helmets — the air is breathable, if you don't mind the mineral tang and the faint static charge that makes the hair on your forearms stand up near the conduit walls. They are young, mostly. The kind of young that comes from off-world indenture contracts, from surface cities where the economy has cratered, from family traditions of subsurface labor that go back three or four generations now. They have calloused hands and a specific way of walking — not cautious, exactly, but *negotiating*, the way you move through a space where the geometry might shift between visits. They know which corridors have grown narrower since last month. They know which floor-plates flex underfoot because roots have lifted them from below.

They do not romanticize the work. The foundry is hot, loud in the way that sits in your chest, and the light is always wrong — blue-green from the canopy above, orange from the pour basins, white from the inspection lamps on their foreheads — so that every face looks like something excavated, something pulled from silt. But they have learned the rhythms. They know that a certain smell — sharp, almost citric — means a resin vein has ruptured nearby and they should reroute. They know that when the Ventilation Collective sends a pulse of warm air through the eastern shafts, the canopy contracts slightly and light dims for a few minutes, and this is not malfunction but cycle, and they should wait. They have superstitions. They name sections of the Vault after dead coworkers. They leave small offerings of polished ore at the base of structures they believe to be old-growth — though no one can agree on what *old-growth* means for something that has no seasons and no rings.

They are not a community, exactly. They are a population. But in the hab-blocks, where the growth has come through the walls in thick, pulsing columns and the residents have carved rooms around them rather than through them, something like community has emerged — not warmth, not intimacy, but a shared fluency in the language of negotiation with a living system that does not care about them but is, grudgingly, responsive. A woman in the third hab-module has learned to trigger growth surges in a specific corridor by adjusting the photoelectric cycle on her streetlamp. A man in the fifth module has trained a colony of resin-pickers to clean a wound on his arm by leaving his sleeve rolled up near their nesting site. These are not friendships. They are arrangements. But they are the closest thing to intimacy the Vault permits, and the workers have stopped distinguishing between the two.

**The Resin-Pickers**

The small ones. You notice them first as sound — a dry, papery scritching, like someone shuffling a deck of cards very slowly in a very large room. They are fist-sized, six-legged, lightly chitinous, the color of wet sandstone, and they move with the quiet coordination of creatures that have never known open sky and therefore have never needed to fear anything larger than themselves.

They cluster. Not in hives — they have no queens, no comb structure, no communal nest. They cluster the way condensation clusters on glass: distributed, simultaneous, responding to conditions rather than commands. When a stem is cut or a wound opens in the living tissue of the Vault, the resin begins to well — luminous, clear, briefly beautiful before it oxidizes to dull gold — and within minutes the pickers arrive. They do not rush. They arrive with the unhurried certainty of rain. They feed on the fresh resin, drinking it through proboscises that uncoil from beneath their segmented heads, and as they feed they excrete a fine, mineral-rich dust that settles into the cracks in the floorplate and, over weeks, becomes soilite — the substrate the plants need to anchor new growth. They are, in this way, both consumer and producer, destruction and creation happening simultaneously in bodies the size of your fist, and if that seems like a metaphor for something, the Vault doesn't care. Metaphor is a surface thing.

They tolerate human presence. They will crawl across a worker's boot without hesitation, will continue feeding on a fresh wound in the wall while someone passes within a meter. But they are not tame. They do not adapt to handling. A picker that is picked up will go rigid, then release a drop of resin from a gland near its thorax — a defensive measure, the biologists say, though it accomplishes nothing more than staining your fingers amber for a week. They have no eyes, or if they have eyes, the eyes are vestigial, suited only to distinguish light from dark, the blue-green wash of the canopy from the deeper black of uncolonized stone. They navigate by chemical gradient, by the taste of trace minerals in the air, by the faint electromagnetic fields the plant network generates — a language of the world without language.

The children in the hab-blocks watch them for hours. The children build mazes for them. The children do not know why, only that the pickers move through the mazes with a patience and certainty that the children, whose lives are cramped and scheduled and defined by the demands of a settlement that is always a little unfinished, find deeply, wordlessly compelling.

**The Drapes**

The predators. No one calls them by a single name — they are referred to variously as *shadows*, *folds*, *the hanging ones*, and several names in several languages that translate roughly to *the thing between seeing and not-seeing*. They are the apex inhabitants of the mid-canopy, and they are what the Vault dreams when it dreams violence.

They are large — wingspan of roughly two meters when unfolded, though folded they are almost invisible against the canopy wall, their membranous wings capable of flattening themselves against the living tissue with a smoothness that no engineered material can replicate. Their bodies are lean, long, articulated in ways that suggest an evolutionary history shaped by narrow spaces and the absolute necessity of silence. They do not fly so much as *translate* — a ripple, a shift, a glide so smooth it looks like the space between two moments has been cut away and the creature has stepped through the gap. They do not hunt in open space. They hunt in the architecture — between conduits, behind growth masses, in the negative spaces the Vault creates almost accidentally, the way a lung creates the space for air.

Their mouths are wide relative to their skulls, and they open without the visible motion of hinges — the jaw structure is so flexible that the entire front of the head can unzip, revealing a set of teeth that are not teeth but mineralized filaments, stiff and crystalline, capable of slicing through chitin and resin-hardened plant tissue with equal ease. They eat the pickers. They eat anything that fits between those filaments, including — on at least four documented occasions — small animals that wandered in from surface-level caves through fissures no one knew existed. They do not eat the plants, though they nest in them, their membranous wings chemically indistinguishable from living canopy tissue after a few months of attachment. The plants seem not to object. Whether this is symbiosis, parasitism, or simply the Vault's indifference being mistaken for biological accommodation is a question the botanists argue about on slow nights.

They call. This is what people remember. Once every few hours, sometimes less, one of them releases a sound — not a hunting sound, not a territorial sound, but something that sounds, impossibly, like longing. A low, resonant moan that passes through stone and tissue and resin and steel without distortion, as if the Vault itself has decided to speak through the predator's body. No one has been able to explain the mechanism. No one has been able to explain why it happens at irregular intervals. No one has been able to explain why, when the call sounds, the pickers freeze and the machinery seems to hush and even the growth — the slow, relentless, geological-scale creep of living tissue through dead rock — appears, for one moment, to pause.

When the call fades, the workers sometimes look up. Not at the canopy, which is too diffuse and too bright to resolve into anything specific, but at the quality of the sound, at the way it moved through the space, at the absence it leaves behind. They are not afraid of it. Fear requires a sense of self that the Vault, over the decades, has a way of eroding — not destroying, but redistributing, the way a river redistributes a shoreline. You are still you. You are just less sure where you end and the place begins.

**The Massive Root**

There is one structure — it is difficult to call it either biological or architectural, so call it what it is — that runs from the northern wall of the cavern to the southern edge of the foundry complex, a distance of nearly a kilometer. It is a root-analog, though it is six meters in diameter at its widest and its surface is textured with what look like growth rings, except the rings are not rings — they are nodes, and the nodes pulse with a slow, visible rhythm, a dim gold light that travels inward from the surface along spiral paths. It is the oldest living structure in the Vault. The first survey team found it already growing when they broke through the eastern shaft thirty-eight years ago. They had not expected to find anything. They had expected geology. Instead, they found this: a single root, thicker than any they had imagined, threaded through a fracture system that should have been inert, drawing minerals from three different geological strata simultaneously, its sap so luminescent that the survey team followed it by its glow through three kilometers of dark rock before they reached the cavern and understood, for the first time, the scale of what they had found.

No one has cut it. No one has tried. It provides structural support to the eastern hab-block cluster, feeds mineral-rich resin to at least four factory modules, and serves as what the engineers — only half-jokingly — call the *spine* of the Vault's living subsystem. Root tendrils branch from it into the surrounding rock at intervals too regular to be random, and some of the botanists have begun to suspect it is not a single organism but a networked colony so deeply integrated that individuality has become meaningless — that the question "is it one thing or many things" is as unanswerable as "is a lung one thing or many things," that the distinction is a surface-level concept that collapses under the weight of what is actually happening in the stone.

At night — though there is no night — when the photoelectric cycle dims and the canopied light shifts from blue-green to a deep, slow indigo, the spine pulses. Not brighter. *Deeper*. The gold light retreats from the surface and moves inward, and the whole structure seems to contract slightly, as if breathing out, and in that moment the Vault feels less like a place and more like a process — something that is not happening to the people inside it but is happening *through* them, *with* them, in the slow negotiation between stone and light and growth and the small, electric, temporary persistence of human will.

---

**What the Vault Wants**

It wants nothing. This is the terrifying and beautiful truth the inhabitants circle without ever quite stating: the Vault is not sentient, not in any way that maps to the word as it is used on the surface. It does not plan. It does not desire. It grows. But growing, at this scale, in this enclosed space, among these structures and these bodies, produces effects that *look* like intention without being intention — the way a river delta *looks* designed, the way a termite mound *looks* intentional, the way the human body *looks* purposeful at every scale until you look closely enough to see that it is only chemistry, only physics, only the blind and perfect mathematics of systems seeking equilibrium.

The inhabitants understand this. They understand it the way one understands mortality — abstractly, always, but not in the body. They live inside a system that is assembling itself around them, that does not hate them or love them or even notice them except in the same way a forest notices the mycorrhizal networks threading through its soil: as infrastructure. As substrate. As something to grow through, around, and over, with the patience of stone and the inevitability of chemistry.

And yet.

The woman in the third module, the one who learned to trigger growth surges, says she sometimes feels the Vault *responding*. Not consciously. Not with language. But the growth comes where she directs it now, almost, and the light shifts when she stands in certain corridors, and the resonance of the Drape-calls seems to vibrate at a frequency that sits exactly in the center of her chest, and she stands there in the blue-green haze and feels something she cannot name and will not try to name because naming it would make it smaller and she is not ready for it to be smaller.

This is what lives in the Vault.

Not just bodies. Not just systems.

A question.

The question of what happens when a living place and a living population reach the point where neither can say, with certainty, where one ends and the other begins — and neither wants to.
