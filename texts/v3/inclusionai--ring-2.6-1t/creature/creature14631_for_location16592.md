In the beginning you think no one lives here. That is the first wrong conclusion, and it is the one most visitors reach, because the Shelved — as they will later come to be called, by those who talk about them afterward, though never on the shelf itself — do not announce. They do not stand at doorways. They do not look up when you approach. The buildings sit, and the ground sits, and the cisterns sit, and the wiry grass sits in its cracks, and everything gives the impression of a place that finished its business long before you arrived and has nothing left to attend to.

But then you notice the concaves.

The polished hollows in the limestone between the buildings — the ones that hold the rain-mirrors in the mornings — they are not polished by weather. You can tell because the wear pattern is wrong for erosion. Weather rounds and softens. These concaves are cupped, deliberate, worn by the slow rotation of something pressing down and turning and pressing again over decades, over centuries. The depth is too uniform. The symmetry is too patient to be accidental. Someone shaped these basins, not with tools, but with presence. With the sustained, quiet weight of simply being there.

You will begin to see them on the second day, maybe the third. You will not see them all at once. You will see one, and then you will have to decide whether you saw it or whether the light shifted and created the impression. They are low — lower than the buildings, which is to say they do not enter the buildings at all. They live on the open limestone between the structures, in the bare concavities and on the worn flagstones, in the spaces the builders left deliberately empty.

From a distance, they look like stone. This is not metaphor. They are the blue-grey of the limestone, the same cool tint, and their surfaces have the same faint crystalline catch that the shelf rock has when the thin light strikes it at the angle the valleys' light never reaches. They are not large. Most of them, seated as they are in the concavities, with their limbs — if limbs is the word — folded under and around themselves, would not overflow a large cooking pot. Some are smaller. Some are larger. But none of them rise above knee-height on a standing person, and they do not stand.

What they do is sit. They sit in the polished hollows and they face outward, mostly, toward the gaps between buildings, toward the edge of the shelf, toward the gorge they cannot see. Their faces — you will search a long time for this word and settle on it reluctantly because it is not the right word, but there is no better one — their faces are smooth. Not featureless. Smooth the way a river stone is smooth: there is suggestion there, an almost-expression, the memory of features that might have formed if the pressure had continued a little longer. Two low ridges where brows might be. A slight hollow where the suggestion of eyes rests — not open, closed, lidless, but closed the way a mineral is closed, the way quartz closes around a thread of carbon and holds it for a million years without looking at it. A mouth-line, but it has never opened, or it opened once and very long ago and the stone grew back over it.

They are warm. This surprises you, because the shelf is cold, and the stone is cold, and the air has that pressure behind the eyes that speaks to altitude and exposure. But if you are close enough — and you will be, because they do not move when you approach, because their entire relationship to movement appears to be one of deep, geological reluctance — you will feel the warmth. It is not body-heat, not exactly. It is the warmth of something that has been holding the same position for so long that friction has generated a low, persistent warmth, like a hand pressed flat against a stone wall in winter, but reversed: the stone is the one that is warm, and the hand is the one that has always been warm, and the difference between the two has blurred.

There are — you begin to count, out of the same compulsion that makes you count the buildings — perhaps thirty of them visible from the central shelf. Some are alone. Some are in pairs, set close together in the same concavity, their edges touching with that same mortar-less closeness the buildings have, fitted by patience rather than intention. When two sit this way, and you look long enough, the boundary between them becomes difficult to trace. Not because they fuse, but because the stone-grey of one shades, almost imperceptibly, into the stone-grey of the next, and the low ridges of their faces angle toward each other in a way that might be intimacy or might be geology.

You will hear them before you are willing to say they make sound at all. It comes as a vibration first, felt through the soles of your boots rather than heard — a low, subsonic thrum at the edge of perception, like placing your ear against a hive, except deeper, slower, less urgent. Then, if you sit down near one — and the concavities are shaped, you realize, to receive a human body as well as theirs, and you understand that you are meant to sit — then, if you sit, and you are still, and the afternoon mist is doing its slow work of turning the ground to a broken constellation, you will hear it: a tone. A single tone. Not a note, because notes have beginnings and ends and relationships to other notes. This is one tone, sustained, with no vibrato, no waver, no breath behind it. It rises from the chest of the one nearest you — if chest is the word, if nearest is — and it holds, and it holds, and the concavity beneath you catches it and returns it, slightly changed, slightly lower, and then the next one repeats it, slightly lower still, and then the next, and what you have thought was silence reveals itself as a network of held tones, all nearly identical, all spaced just far enough apart in pitch that they do not harmonize and they do not clash, they simply coexist, the way the buildings coexist, the way the grass coexists with the mortar — present, tenacious, and asking for nothing.

They are not singing. Singing implies melody, implies narrative, implies a listener they are performing for. This is closer to what the stone does when the temperature shifts and its crystalline structure adjusts by fractions of a millimeter. It is what the cisterns do when they fill. It is functional vibration. It is what it sounds like when a thing that has completed its becoming simply continues, not out of stubbornness but out of a completeness that has no need to resolve or conclude.

You will try to speak to them. They will not respond — not because they cannot, but because speech is not in their repertoire, the way flight is not in the repertoire of stone. Something will shift, though, when you talk. The nearest one will tilt — not much, maybe two degrees — the suggestion of attention. The tone it was holding will shift by a quarter-tone, not in response to what you said, but in response to the fact of your speaking: the presence of a new frequency in the space, any frequency, acknowledged the way the cisterns acknowledge rain.

You will offer them food. They will not take it. You will offer them water. They will not take it. You will sit among them for hours and then days, and the warmth from the nearest one will begin to feel like companionship — not because it is intentional, not because there is recognition or welcome in it, but because warmth is warmth, and the thrum beneath you is steady, and the broken constellation on the wet ground is the most beautiful thing you have ever seen and it is not beautiful because someone made it beautiful but because someone — these ones, the Shelved — simply lived close enough together, and for long enough, that the rain found the shape of their living and polished it into concavities that hold the sky.

On the last morning, before the mist lifts and the light shifts back toward the amber of the low valleys, you will see — or believe you see — that one of them, the one nearest the eastern wall, the boundary wall that is not a barrier but a statement of edges, has changed. Not in posture. Not in position. But the blue-grey of its surface has, for the first time, a variation: a thin line, lighter, running from the ridge where a brow might be down to the smooth place where a mouth might open. It is fine as a hair. It is the color ofite freshly exposed. 

Cracked. Not crumbling. Cracked the way the mortar is cracked — structurally sound, holding its shape, but bearing a record of some internal shift that happened so slowly and so long ago that by the time it surfaced, it had already settled into identity.

Growth. If you were to name it, you would call it growth. But it is the growth of stone, which is to say it is imperceptible and irreversible and does not need a witness.

You do not cross the wall. You sit among them one final morning, and the tones shift around you like a conversation no one is having with anyone, and when you rise to leave, the Shelved do not watch you go. They are already attending to the light, to the mist leaving, to the shallow mirrors in the concavities brightening and then going still, one by one, as the sun clears the shelf's eastern edge.

They will be here when you are gone. They were here before you, and they will be here after, holding their tones, wearing their faces, polishing the ground beneath them with the slow, enormous patience of things that have agreed — once, and without wavering — to simply remain.
