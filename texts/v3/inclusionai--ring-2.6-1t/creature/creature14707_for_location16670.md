# The Inhabitants of the Calcinate Plateau

---

Begin with the mats.

Before anything here has a shape you could name as living, before anything moves or breathes or recoils from light, there are the mats — and they are already cities. Layered sheets of chemosynthetic consortia, bacteria and archaea bound into a single substrate of collective appetite. They are not green. Nothing here is green. They are rust and sulfur-yellow, deep arterial red, black. They spread across wet mineral surfaces with the patience of erosion itself, fraction of a fraction of a fraction per year, and yet they are the first thing you see if you know where to look: the margins of the brine pools, the seeps along the fault lines, the thread of warmth where water first breaks the crust.

They metabolize what the plateau offers without apology — sulfides, iron compounds, carbonates — and what they cannot use, they excrete as solid deposit. Calcium carbonate.ite. Iron oxide. They are, in this sense, not merely alive but architectural. They build the ground they stand on. They are laying down sediment that will, in time, become the terraces — those ochre and copper-green ledges stacked like the seats of an amphitheater no audience will ever fill. The mats do not know this. The mats do not need to. They have been doing it long before the word *knowing* meant anything, and they will continue long after the word has forgotten us.

From the mats, everything else descends.

---

The crawlers come first among the visible fauna: flat-bodied things, six-legged, armored in biomineral plates scraped from the terraces and reforged into exoskeletons with a mineral precision that looks almost industrial. They move across rock faces with a dry click-click-click — the only metronomic sound the plateau reliably produces — and they graze the mats in thin, deliberate paths, leaving pale scars across the red and black surfaces. They are small, no larger than a hand, and they are armored so thoroughly that when they die their remains persist for years, mineral-heavy shells settling into the crust like small tectonic events. A young crawler's shell is mostly organic, still flexible, still translucent at the joints. An old one isiteite composite, heavy, ornate ridged, and you would pick it up from the rubble and think it was a stone shaped by water.

They are not intelligent. They are not even close. But they are *thorough.* A crawler will spend a day crossing two meters of rock face, consuming a mat-layer so thin you could read through it, and when it reaches the edge of its territory it stops, sits, and waits — not out of patience but out of programming. When the thermal gradient shifts, when a new seep opens, when the brine pools rise after a condensation cycle and expose fresh wet surface, the crawlers are there. They are always there. They are the plateau's most reliable cartographers, mapping the slow migration of water by the paths of their grazing.

---

Higher. Further from the mats, where the chemical exchange is no longer direct but mediated through consumption, the plateau keeps its predators.

The largest of them is eyeless, forearm-length, low-slung, the color of bakedite dusted withite oxide that serves it as both armor and camouflage. It moves through rubble fields without sound — its limbs are padded, its joints fluid, its body held so close to the ground that it seems to flow rather than walk. Its head is raised slightly, angled forward, and at its tip is a bifurcated organ that reads the world in two spectra at once: chemical gradient on one side, infrared on the other. It does not see. It does not need to see. It tastes the warmth. It follows the heat signature of a vent hissing through a narrow fissure and finds, in the rubble around that vent, the crawlers and the smaller arthropod-things that have gathered for the warmth. It strikes without sound and carries its prey back into the rubble field, where it feeds in silence, and when it is done it excretes aite-rich waste that cements into the substrate within hours.

Nothing is wasted here. That is not philosophy. It is chemistry.

---

There are others — smaller, stranger things. Tube-dwellers anchored to rock faces, translucent and pale, with no apparent sensory organs, filtering thewater through calcareous bodies with the slow rhythm of someone breathing in their sleep. They retract when touched and do not emerge for days. No one on the plateau has given them a name. The scientists in Estravan's processing sheds have called them *vacui* in their notes — a half-joke, because they are so obviously present and so completely opaque to inquiry. The tubes they build from their own secretions are spiraled, precise, and when the organisms die inside them the tubes persist, mineral-enriched, adding to the crust one more layer of something that is not quite rock and not quite bone.

And then — the pool.

At the plateau's eastern edge there is a shallow basin, fed by a seep that runs warm but not hot, where the water is almost clear and the bottom is covered in a mat that does not belong to any known chemosynthetic consortium. It does not match genetic databases. It does not match morphological ones. It grows in concentric rings, slowly, expanding outward with a regularity that would be hypnotic if you could watch it for long enough without the vertigo that the altitude brings. At night it glows — blue-white, pulsing on a cycle of approximately forty seconds, though the pulse is not perfectly regular. Sometimes it skips. Sometimes it doubles. The scientists have argued about this. Some say it is a metabolic byproduct, light as waste. Others say it is signaling — to whom, for what purpose, no one can say.

The fauna that feed on this mat are found nowhere else. They are tubular, translucent, anchored, and they have no eyes, no antennae, no apparent means of detecting anything beyond the chemical signature of the mat beneath them. They do not flee from shadows. They do not respond to vibration. They filter, and they glow faintly with the reflected light of the mat, and when you reach toward them your hand passes through a body that is mostly water held in shape byite scaffolding.

They are not friendly. They are not hostile. They are simply *elsewhere* — so thoroughly of this one pool, this one mat, this one seep, that to remove them would be to destroy them, not relocate them. They are the pool's way of knowing itself.

---

And then there is what the plateau keeps in reserve — the things that do not quite behave.

In the oldest quarry, the walls curve. Not from stress, not from erosion — the deposits accumulate in still water, and still water should produce flat planes. But theite grows here in concave cups, millimeters deep, centimeters across, perfectly regular, deepening over years. Maintenance crews have scraped them. They return. No one has been able to produce a mechanical explanation. The water flows evenly. The temperature is uniform. And yet the mineral is cupped, as if something underneath is *receiving* it.

The pipes in the processing sheds bend. Installed straight, they develop gentle curves over decades, always toward the nearest thermal vent. Not sags — the pipes are supported, braced, constrained. The bends are smooth, continuous, and they appear faster in certain seasons, when the thermal gradient is steepest. The crews straighten them. They bend back within months. Someone in the settlement's technical office has started logging the curvature in a spreadsheet. The curve is accelerating.

At the southern edge, where the plateau drops into the lowland basin, the horizon ripples. Not the shimmer everyone sees — the heat-flicker above the rocks that even the oldest residents have stopped noticing. This is at the horizon line itself, a wavering, a flexing, as if the distance were a membrane being tested by pressure from the other side. Dawn and dusk are the worst, or the best, depending on whether you want to watch. The instruments record nothing. The horizon continues to ripple.

---

And the people.

The people of Estravan are, by necessity, a particular kind of person. Two thousand souls in a town built into a mineral loop, breathing mineral air, drinking mineral water filtered through forty layers of processing, walking on mineral crust, beneath a sky that is too near and too total and presses down like a hand on the back of the neck.

They are quiet. Not sullen, not taciturn — the silence here is learned, not imposed. You learn it the way you learn the altitude: slowly, and then you don't remember the learning. Conversations in Estravan are short, functional, carried out in low voices and with many pauses. The pauses are not uncomfortable. They are how people here leave room for the sound of the place — the click of crawlers on rock, the hiss of vents, the faint percussion of condensation forming on every surface an hour after sunset, the drip and trickle of water finding new paths through the crust.

The children grow up identifying mineral deposits before they learn to read. They are given hand lenses before they are given books. They learn the names of the mat species the way children elsewhere learn the names of birds: with a mixture of precision and tenderness, because the mats are beautiful in their way — arterial red at the margins of seeps, sulfur-yellow in the shallows, black in the deep basins where the brine is densest and the chemical exchange is most vigorous.

The adults work the evaporation ponds, maintain the pipeline network, repair the cooling towers, export the catalyticite and rare salts that justify the settlement's existence to whatever corporate entity currently holds the charter. They are competent, watchful, and faintly mineral themselves — their skin takes on a pale cast from the brine mist, their hands are rough fromite dust, their coughs in winter are dry and chalky. They do not romanticize the plateau. They respect it the way you respect something that will outlast you: completely, and without illusions.

And at night, in the condensation hours, when fog pools in the streets and the dew coats every surface and the settlement becomes a constellation of lamplight suspended in white vapor, some of the residents walk to the eastern edge to watch the pool glow.

They will not tell you why.

They will say, if pressed, that it is beautiful. They will say it has always been beautiful. And if you ask whether the pulse has changed — the skips, the doubles — they will look at you with an expression that is not suspicion but something older and more patient, as if they have been asked a question that the plateau is still deciding whether to answer.

---

The terminalite is this: nothing on the Calcinate Plateau is merely alive. Everything is *participating* — in the chemistry, in the deposition, in the slow negotiation between heat and water and mineral and time. The mats are participating. The crawlers are participating. The eyeless predators and the translucent tube-dwellers and the unnameable mat in the eastern pool are all participating. The people of Estravan are participating. The bending pipes and the cupping walls and the rippling horizon — they are all part of a process that was here long before anyone arrived, that does not require consciousness to continue, and that will not pause or hesitate when the settlement is gone and the last pipeline has corroded and the last evaporation basin has filled with sediment and salt.

The plateau is listening to itself. It has been listening for a very long time.

And it is beginning to answer.
