## What Lives in the Resonance Flats

---

What lives there is not a thing you can point to. It is a stack of things, each one layered on the last, each one feeding the one above and the one below, and the whole mass vibrating at a frequency that sits in your sternum like a second heartbeat. You do not encounter individual creatures in the Flats. You encounter populations — densities — as though the place has decided, over some vast and patient stretch of time, that the distinction between one organism and another is a luxury it can no longer afford.

Start small. Start where the ground meets the ankle.

The calciumite-and-chitin crust underfoot is not dead mineral. It is a living substrate, threaded through with mycelial networks so dense they function as a single distributed organism — or perhaps as many organisms that have lost the ability to function alone. The fungal mats are the first thing that lives here, and everything else is an elaboration of them. They are pale, semi-translucent, faintly amber where bioluminescence bleeds through in slow arrhythmic pulses. They do not grow so much as *assert*. A bare surface does not exist in the Flats for longer than a few hours before a mat claims it, binding mineral particles together with chitinous adhesives, redistributing moisture through capillary channels thinner than a human hair. If you cut a mat open — and it would resist the blade, not with force but with a tenacious flexibility, like cutting into wet leather reinforced with wire — you would find it organized into functional strata: a basal layer that drinks from the seeping water, a middle layer that processes and stores minerals, and a surface layer that photosynthesizes, or something analogous to it, using the faint light that filters down through the conduit canopy. The amber glow is a byproduct. Whether it serves a communicative function — whether the mats are signaling to each other, to the fauna, to the columns — is unclear. The Flats hum in a way that suggests intention, but intention is a word that belongs to minds with edges, and the mats have no edges.

On the mats graze the littlest lives. You see them first as movement — a skittering at the periphery, a faint clicking like someone running a fingernail along a comb. They are six-legged, arthropod-scale, translucent enough that you can watch their feeding assemblies work: mandible-combs raking across the mat surface, stripping off filaments, ingesting the mineral-chitin composite and excreting a finer, more uniform paste that the mat below somehow welcomes, the way soil welcomes compost. There are dozens of species, maybe hundreds. Some are pale, almost white. Others carry faint chromatophore shifts — not for camouflage, because there is nothing to hide against in a world where every surface is alive and the same color, but for signaling. Mating displays. Territorial flares. Warnings that ripple outward through a grazing cluster like a stone dropped into still water. In aggregate, they sound like rain on a tin roof — a constant, fine, high-frequency static that never stops, not even at what passes for night, when the mats dim to coal-red and the air thickens with condensation.

They are food for the beetle-shelled ones. Larger by only a margin — fingernail-sized, maybe thumbnail-sized — but armored initeiteite carapaces so hard they ring when struck. You find their shed shells everywhere: empty husks that the fungal mats dissolve and reabsorb within hours, recycling the calcium back into the crust. The creatures themselves are obsessive self-recyclers. They eat the mineral-fungal composite, extract what they need, extrude a fresh shell segment, and stitch it into place with a biological resin that hardens in minutes. They are always molting, always rebuilding. A colony of them sounds like a pot of popcorn — sharp, irregular, percussive pops as carapace segments separate and reform. They are skittish. When a larger creature passes nearby, the sound shifts — a sudden silence, then a frantic scatter-clicking — and then, moments later, a tentative return, as though they are listening for something to finish before they resume.

What finishes is what lives in the middle.

The bladder-creatures. You hear them before you see them — a deep, wet thrumming that rises and falls in slow chords, cathedral organ notes played through water. Three meters, four meters long. Bodies that are less bodies than inflatable structures: membranous, semi-rigid, covered in a mesh ofiteiteite fibers that give them shape without giving them rigidity. They drift through the lower corridors between the hexagonal columns, buoyant, maneuvering by expelling water from pore-lined flanks in rhythmic jets. The motion is graceful beyond anything it should be — these are large animals moving through a dense, cluttered environment with the unhurried precision of whales in open ocean. They feed on the medium-dark sludge that moves through the conduits with a sluggish deliberateness that suggests the sludge itself may be alive, or at least digestible. Filtration structures line their throats — baleen-like, but not keratin; something harder, more mineral, vibrating at frequencies that separate nutrient from waste with mechanical precision. The vibration is what you hear. A single bladder-creature sounds like a bowed pipe organ string. Five of them, passing in formation overhead, will resonate with the humming columns in ways that make your ribs ache and your vision blur at the edges. They are not singing. They are functioning. But the function is beautiful, and the beauty is unintentional, and the unintentionality is what makes the Flats unmake you a little each time you hear it.

Higher, in the canopy where conduits cross and fiber-optic curtains hang in dense, light-shifting veils, the flyers live. They are not flyers — nothing with true wings lives here — but they fall with purpose. Membrane-winged, lightweight, their bodies built for controlled descent along thermal gradients generated by the conduit network itself. They navigate by echolocation. Clicks. Not the soft, exploratory clicks of a bat but sharp, broadband pulses that hit your skin like tiny fingers snapping against your forearms and your neck and your cheekbones. You feel them before you hear them, and you hear them as a physical pressure, a wall of sound that does not pass through you so much as it passes *you* — as though you are the obstacle and the sound is choosing to go around. They hunt the smaller arthropods in shrieking bursts of motion, dozens of them at once, diving through conduit clusters with a precision that borders on choreography. The sound of a hunting flock is the loudest single event in the Flats — a broadband shriek that wipes thought clean, that leaves you standing in a sudden white silence inside your own skull, blinking, hands over ears, while translucent shapes streak past overhead in a storm of clicks and wing-membrane booms.

They do not attack you. Nothing in the Flats attacks you. You are not prey. You are not anything. You are a brief thermodynamic event — warm, damp, metabolizing — in a system that has been running so long it no longer distinguishes between disturbances and inputs.

And beneath all of it, connecting all of it, processing all of it: the neural-ganglia clusters. Fist-sized nodules at every conduit junction, pulsing faint blue-white, rerouting flows, adjusting pressure, modulating the frequencies that the columns hum. They are not brains. They are not even analogous to brains, not exactly. They are processors in the oldest and most literal sense — they process. Nutrient flow. Electrical signals. Pressure differentials. Information in the broadest thermodynamic sense. A pulse through a ganglion changes the pitch of the adjacent column, which changes the resonance of the corridor, which changes the flight path of the membrane-winged hunters, which changes where the arthropods scatter, which changes where the slurry deposits form, which changes what the bladder-creatures filter, which changes the harmonic output of the entire system. No one thing controls it. No one thing is in charge. The Flats are a ten-thousand-layered metabolism with no center, no architect, no intelligence in any way you would recognize, and yet it responds. It adapts. It incorporates.

A mat grows on your boot within an hour.

A conduit reroutes around your body within a day.

Within a week, the calcium in your bones has begun to resonate at a frequency the columns recognize, and the arthropods have stopped scattering when you step, and the rain that condenses on the conduit canopy weeps a little more on one side than the other, shaped by a body that is no longer entirely yours, and the hum in your sternum is no longer your heartbeat but something older, something that was here before the ridges cupping this basin formed, something that will be here after the basin itself has dissolved into the slow geological forgetting of the world above.

You learn quickly not to stand still.

The Flats fill in around you.

You become infrastructure.
