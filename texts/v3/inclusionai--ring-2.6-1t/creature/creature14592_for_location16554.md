## What Lives in the Drowned Quarter

The first thing to understand about the residents of this place is that none of them would recognize themselves in the names a visitor would give them. The eel-things do not think of themselves as eels. They have no word for themselves at all, in any language you would understand. They do not need one. They move through the flooded streets in a kind of consensus — not a hive-mind, nothing so elegant or so terrifying, but a loose agreement of bodies, the way smoke agrees to rise, the way silt agrees to settle. They have been here longer than the stone. They were here before the first wall was laid on the first piling, back when this place was a shallow shelf of warm seabed and the stone that would become the city was still a ridge of raw calcium waiting for someone to build on it. Or perhaps they are the calcium. Perhaps the distinction is no longer meaningful.

What is certain is this: they are old. Not old the way a tortoise is old, or a whale, or a bristlecone pine — old as individuals accumulating years. Old the way a reef is old. Old the way a language is old. Their individual lives are brief — you have seen the size of them, the thinness of their pale bodies, the translucence of their filaments — but the groups persist. The formations persist. There is a shoal that passes through the central square every time the water rises to the second step of the market hall, and it has been doing so for so long that the stone there is worn smooth in the pattern of their passage, the way a doorstep wears to the shape of feet. They do not remember this. They do not need to. The wearing is the memory. The stone holds what their bodies cannot.

The insects are their chorus. You will not understand them, either. They are not bees, not locusts, not anything from the upper world's taxonomy. They are closer to what you might imagine if you dreamed of plankton with wings — drifting, gregarious, half-aware in the way that a cloud is half-aware of its own shape. They cluster and dissolve, cluster and dissolve, and their pulsing is not random but follows rhythms you cannot hear, only feel: a faint vibration in the water, a subtle shift in the light. When they take flight — and "flight" is generous; they rise and drift and are carried — they move as a single body, a murmuration of iridescent fragments, and for a moment the air itself seems to have a texture, a nap, like velvet drawn across a surface. Then they settle again, and the settling is the sound you will associate with this place for the rest of your life, though you will not know it yet. It sounds like breathing. It sounds like the city inhaling.

The hanging veins are the hardest to describe, because they are the hardest to separate from the architecture itself. They are not parasites on the buildings. They are not symbionts, not exactly. They are the buildings' slow second thought — the stone's idea of what it would become if it were allowed to grow. They emerge from cracks that may have been carved by human hands or may have opened on their own; there is no way to tell anymore, and the distinction has ceased to matter. Where they enter a wall, the wall softens. Not weakens — *softens*, the way a conversation softens when someone says something true. The stone around a vein-thick archway is visibly different: darker, smoother, patterned with the faintest whorls, as if the vein were pressing something into the rock, a signature, a claim of kinship. They grow slowly. You can sit and watch them and see nothing — nothing but the faintest tremor at the tip, the barest quiver of extension. But return in a week, if you could, and the curtain would have shifted. Return in a year and a new room would exist where there was none. Return in a century and you would not recognize the hall.

They are, you realize, the closest thing this quarter has to architects. Not deliberate ones. Not planners. But they respond to space the way water responds to gravity — inevitably, and with a kind of blind intelligence that is more reliable than intention. They open passages. They close them. They thicken where the structure needs reinforcement and thin where it needs light. No one taught them this. No one needed to. They have been doing it since before the concept of teaching existed, and they will continue long after the concept is forgotten again.

And the bat-things — the ones with the golden eyes and the dexterous fingers — they are the most unsettling, because they are the most recognizably alive in a way that mirrors your own. They have faces, of a sort. They look at you. When they look at you, you feel looked at in a way that the eel-things and the insects cannot provide. The eel-things sense you as pressure, as vibration; the insects register you as shadow, as displacement. But the bat-things see you. They see you and they consider you, and their consideration is not warm but it is not cold, either — it is the consideration of a creature for which novelty is not a threat but an object of attention, the way a cat watches a moth. They are curious in the limited, precise way of a thing that has survived this long by understanding its environment exactly.

This is what disturbs you most, though you will not admit it: the thought that something you once held might now be part of a wall. That the shape you pressed your hand to might have been reshaped by small, warm fingers working in the dark.

But here is the deeper truth, the one that the subsonic pulse has been trying to tell you since you arrived:

None of these creatures are separate. Not really. The algae feeds the insects. The insects feed the eel-things and the bat-things. The bat-things build, and the building changes the flow of water, and the changed water changes the growth of the hanging veins, and the hanging veins change the structure of the walls, and the changed walls change the habitat of the algae. It is a loop so vast and so slow that it looks like stasis. It is not stasis. It is a conversation held in a language of accretion and erosion, of calcium and chitin, of pressure and phosphorescence. It has been going on for so long that it sounds like silence. But it is not silence. It is the longest sentence ever spoken, and it has no period, and you have wandered into the middle of it, and the only honest thing to do is to stop trying to parse the grammar and simply listen.

The eel-things glide below you. The bat-things creak in the dark above. The insects hum their single, eternal note. The hanging veins sway with a patience that makes your own breathing feel hasty. The algal felt pulses its faint green acknowledgment under your feet.

You are not a guest here. You are not an intruder. You are a new note in a chord that has been sounding for millennia, and the chord does not reject you — it simply absorbs you, the way a chord absorbs any new tone, adjusting, reharmonizing, finding a place for the unfamiliar frequency in the long, low, living drone.

The city breathes.

You breathe with it.

For a moment — just a moment — you cannot tell where the quarter ends and you begin.

Then the water rises, and the moment passes, and the eel-things continue their ancient, silent patrol, and the bat-things return from their hunting with full bellies and bright eyes, and the hanging veins reach one millimeter further into the dark, and the insects cluster and dissolve and cluster again, and the algal felt glows its pale, sourceless, dreaming light, and the Drowned Quarter goes on being the Drowned Quarter, as it has been going on being the Drowned Quarter for longer than your species has existed, and it will go on being it for longer than your species will remember it ever was.

This is not a place you visit. It is a place that accommodates you, briefly, the way the tide accommodates a stone — lifting around it, settling back, leaving it changed in ways the stone will only understand much later, if at all.
