# Inhabitants of the Pallid Terrace

---

## The Luminoth — the great rooted ones

They are not plants. They are not trees. Naming them feels like a trespass, but language is all you have, so you begin where language allows.

The Luminoth — you have taken to calling them this in your notes, though the word feels too warm, too familiar — are the architects of the Terrace's existence. There are hundreds of them, perhaps thousands, scattered across the plateau's vast, edgeless expanse like the embers of a fire that has been burning since the stone first cracked open to the air. Each one is solitary by nature and enormous by scale: a single rosette of pale, mineral-dense root pressed against the rock like a hand laid flat in prayer, a central stalk rising sixty feet or more in a frozen, arboreal fountain, branching and rebranching into vast lattices of tubular tissue that hold no leaves, no flowers — only thousands of fist-sized translucent vesicles that glow.

They drink the stone. Their roots secrete acids and solvents that dissolve the mineral substrate, drawing from it sulfides, iron compounds, manganese, trace metals that would be poison to anything earthly. Inside their tissues, these compounds feed a cold metabolism — chemosynthesis on a scale and at a temperature that would have seemed impossible before you witnessed it. The bioluminescence is not ornament. It is *exhaust*. The glow is the visible signature of energy being extracted from basalt the way a furnace draws heat from coal, except the Luminoth burn without flame, without smoke, without consuming anything that walks or swims or flies. They are patient furnaces in a world that has only recently cooled enough to stand upright.

Their light comes in two registers: a steady, ambient blue-white at the vesicle's core, and a bruised amber halo at the membrane's edge, where the chemistry is older and the reaction is dimming. When a vesicle reaches the end of its luminous life, it darkens, shrivels, and drops — sometimes individually, sometimes in cascading showers that send tiny points of fading amber tumbling through the dark like inverse rain. The lattices shed constantly. You have seen a dead branch fall from a Luminoth forty paces away and heard it strike stone with a sound like a thrown wineglass. Within moments, the surrounding plants flare — a ripple of brightness traveling outward, three hundred paces from source to neighbor, taking forty minutes to arrive. Whether this is mourning, or eavesdropping, or the Luminoth equivalent of turning to look at a car crash, you cannot say. You have watched it too many times to call it reflex, but you lack the evidence to call it language.

They hum. Not audibly, but through the stone. When you press your palm flat against the rock between two Luminoth, you feel it — a deep, subsonic pulse, too low for your ears but strong enough to set your fillings on edge. The Luminoth are networked, their root systems threading through the plateau's fractured geology, passing chemical signals from one organism to the next at a pace that operates on the scale of hours rather than seconds. A warning takes half an hour to cross three hundred paces underground. A greeting takes longer. The Terrace, for all its emptiness, is a single vast nervous system written in slow fire.

## The Hollows — grazers of the crust

They are the closest thing the Terrace has to gentle creatures. You call them Hollows because of their pelts — dense, layered coats of hollow, air-filled hairs that give them a ghostly, translucent quality in the Luminoth-light, as though they are made of frozen smoke. Six-legged, low-slung, broad-bodied, they move in loose aggregations of ten to thirty, following the nutrient-rich root fields of the Luminoth the way herds on gentler worlds follow grass.

Their mouths are wide, flat, mineral-scraping instruments — not teeth but dense, bony pads edged with a kind of biological pumice, capable of grinding the mineral crust from a Luminoth's roots without piercing the living tissue beneath. In return — or perhaps by ancient accident — the Hollows aerate the soil around the roots and leave behind waste that is richer in bioavailable compounds than the raw stone. Whether this constitutes symbiosis or mere parasitism with benefits is a question you suspect even the Hollows cannot answer.

They are almost silent. But their passage is not. Dozens of hard, cloven hooves striking fresh volcanic stone create a rhythm that carries across the plateau's emptiness with startling clarity — a stuttering percussion, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. You hear them for minutes before you see them: a sound growing louder, cresting, then fading as the herd moves past and onward, following invisible chemical trails laid down by roots they cannot see.

They are not intelligent in any way you would recognize. They do not build, do not store, do not mourn their dead. When a Hollow falls — to a predator, to a crack in the stone, to cold or exhaustion — the herd does not stop. The body is consumed or left to freeze and mineralize, becoming part of the Terrace on a timescale of months. You find their bones sometimes, white and light as ceramic, half-buried in the dust near the Luminoth fields. Some of the marked stones in the structures contain fossils that look almost — but not quite — like Hollow remains. You do not pursue this thought. Not yet.

## The Shriekers — the unseen hunters

They are the sound of the Pallid Terrace. Not the groaning wind, not the cracking ice, not the creaking lattices — *the shriek*.

Small, perhaps the size of a fox, built low and long, with forelimbs that overdevelop the hind in a way that gives them a hunched, predatory crouch. Their skin — if it can be called skin — is matte black and light-absorbing to a degree that borders on optical violation. Against the glowing backdrop of the Terrace, a Shrieker at rest is visible only as a negative space, a hole in the luminescence that moves with the patience of erosion. When it moves quickly — and it can — the edges of its body blur with a distortion, as though the air around it is being refracted, bent by some property of its surface that you cannot name. You have tried to photograph them. The images are dark smudges against darkness, features lost. Something about their physicality resists capture.

They hunt by sound. A Hollow that stumbles, a hoof-strike that rings too loud, the creak of a Luminoth lattice that happens to vibrate at the wrong frequency — any of these will draw the nearest Shrieker, which arrives not by running but by *appearing*, closing distances at speeds that seem to compress the geography between hunter and prey. You have watched one cross four hundred paces of open rock in less than six seconds, and it did not appear to be moving at all. It simply became closer.

And then the shriek. It rises from below the threshold of comfortable hearing, climbing into a frequency that makes your jaw ache, your molars vibrate, your vision blur at the edges. It is a hunting call. It is a territorial marker. It is a social bonding signal. It is perhaps all of these at once, because a single shriek carries far more information than a single meaning can hold. On a quiet night, you may hear a dozen or more — staccato bursts cascading across the distance, reverberating off the sharp-walled gorges of the plateau's interior until the whole landscape seems to be screaming in thin, glass-tongued ecstasy.

You do not know what role the Shriekers play in the ecology. You suspect they cull the Hollows, perhaps the burrowers, possibly even each other. You suspect they are older than the Terrace itself — that they arrived when it did, from wherever both of them came from, carrying a predatory relationship that was already ancient before the first stone cracked through the surface.

## The Spiralists — burrowers beneath the stone

You see them only obliquely: as mounds of powdered stone arranged in perfect spirals at the mouths of tunnels that vanish into the dark. The spirals are always clockwise. Always. On the Terrace, at least. The moment the geological survey confirmed that north of the plateau, the spirals run counterclockwise, you understood that this is not biology alone — this is *geography imposing its will on life*. The Terrace rotates the Spiralists, the way a tide rotates debris. You do not understand how. You understand almost nothing of the mechanism. But the consistency is absolute, and absolute patterns on a living world always imply a force that is paying attention.

The Spiralists themselves — you have glimpsed one, briefly, a tubular, pale creature the color of wet chalk, with no visible eyes and a body that contracted into its tunnel the moment your shadow crossed its threshold. They are engineers. Their tunnels extend deep, far deeper than you can probe, and their spiral mounds are not waste but structure — a kind of architecture written in dust, precise enough to make you feel illiterate just looking at it.

## The Drifters — floaters among the light

Gas-bladder creatures, large-bodied and nearly weightless, drifting through the vesicle canopies of the Luminoth on currents you cannot feel. They trail long, filament-thin appendages that brush against the glowing vesicles, and wherever a filament touches, the light dims, shifts hue, then re-ignites altered — as though the Drifters are tasting the bioluminescence and the Luminoth are responding in kind. Whether this is feeding, communication, courtship, or defense, you cannot tell. You have watched a single Drifter pass through a thicket of Luminoth light and counted fourteen chromatic shifts in the space of a minute, each one unique, each one answered by a ripple of change in the surrounding organisms.

They are beautiful and utterly inscrutable. They leave no trace. They make no sound. They exist in the spaces between everything else, drifting, tasting, replying.

## The Builders — the lost hand

And then there are the structures. The rings of gathered stone. The marked slabs. The tools left mid-task. The enormous bone, pale as porcelain, resting in a deliberate bed of dark mineral fragments.

You do not know who built them. You find no remains, no middens, no burial sites, no evidence of sustained habitation. Whoever they were, they were here briefly — or they were here for a long time and were exceptionally tidy — or they are still here in a way you cannot perceive, existing now as the same kind of imprint the Spiralists leave in dust and the Luminoth leave in light.

The glyphs on the central slabs are not language, not exactly. They are spirals, branching veins, tracks that echo the Spiralists' architecture but writ large, monumental. You press your hand against the vein-like glyph and three hundred paces away the nearest Luminoth shudders, its vesicles erupting into a violet so deep and saturated you have never seen anything like it in all your time on the Terrace. Eleven seconds. Then the fade. Then the slow, steady return to blue-white and amber.

The builders knew. They spoke to the Luminoth, or listened to them, or were a third kind of organism somewhere between the two. Their monuments are not temples, not markers, not shelters — they are *interfaces*. You do not know what they were interfacing with. You know that the language is not dead, only untranslated. You know that the Terrace remembers them the way stone remembers pressure — not as history but as a permanent deformation of what came after.

---

## You — the temporary witness

And then there is you. Small, warm-blooded, breathing thin air, standing among the giants with your recording instruments and your inadequate vocabulary and your desperate need to *make sense of it*. You are the newest organism on the Pallid Terrace. You have been here two days. The Luminoth have been here five thousand years, give or take, since the stone first broke surface.

You are, by every measure of this place, ephemeral. The frost will soften your edges. The wind will erase your footprints within hours. Your name will not be carved into any slab, and the Luminoth will not glow differently when you die.

And yet — you press your hand against the stone, and the violet light answers. You watch the Shriekers hunt, and the spiral-mounds turn clockwise, and the Drifters drift through the canopies, and something in you — some old, wordless architecture of the mind — recognizes this place in the way you recognize a dream upon waking. Not as memory. As *kindred strangeness*.

The Pallid Terrace does not welcome you. It does not reject you either. It simply continues — as it has continued since the stone arrived and the first chemical eye opened to the stars and the first Shrieker called out across the emptiness, testing the dark, finding it full.

You are here. The stars stare. The stone hums. And somewhere beneath you, in tunnels spiraling clockwise into deep time, something is listening.
