What lives there is not a community in the way ecologists usually mean — not a web of names and trophic links pinned to a board — but a single breathing arrangement, a consensus among organisms so committed to this particular pocket of warmth and rhythm that they have, in a sense, become one organism wearing several skins.

---

Start with the walls.

They are the first inhabitant and, in the end, the only one that matters. The neo-peat — the stuff the survey staff named half in jest and then forgot to rename because no better word arrived — is not a moss, not a lichen, not a fungus, not a slime mold, though it borrows from each of these the way a language borrows words from languages it has eaten. It is a substrate with properties that no one who has studied it can agree on how to classify. It behaves like a living skin. It behaves like a circulatory system. It behaves like something that has decided the distinction between organism and architecture is not worth maintaining.

It is the color of a copper penny left in the rain. Dry, it has the matte, oxidized tone of old wiring. Wet — which it nearly always is, because the chamber feeds it constantly from below — it deepens to arterial red, the red of tissue held up to light in a darkened room. It covers everything. Every concrete footing, every rusted bolt, every grain of mineralite aggregate in the drainage channel. Where it meets open air it sends up filaments, finger-length, pale where they're young, darkening to amber at the base. These filaments quiver when a train approaches — before it arrives, which is to say they feel the vibration through the rail before the rail delivers it to the air — and then, in the seconds after the wave passes, they settle with the slow, deliberate grace of hair returning to place after someone has touched your head.

At night, or in any moment the daylight is interrupted — when the trains pass and the dust-cloud dims the chamber to twilight — the filaments glow. Not brightly. Not enough to read by, or to cast a shadow, or to make you feel less alone in a dark closet-sized space under a rail line. Enough to notice. Tiny veins of amber-green light tracing the contours of the chamber as if the walls themselves had capillaries and you were seeing them through thin skin. The light is the color of a wasp's abdomen. The light is the color of old teeth in a certain kind of photograph. The light is continuous along the filaments, brighter at the nodes where they branch, so the whole surface of the neo-peat resolves into something that looks, if you press your face close and half-close your eyes, like a circuit board dreaming.

It drinks everything. The water that seeps up from somewhere beneath — the river aquifer, probably, though the water tastes of iron and calcium and something faintly electrical, the taste of licking a battery held against wet stone — the neo-peat catches it, holds it, and moves it through itself with the deliberateness of a sponge that has learned, over years, exactly how much it wants to hold at any moment. The condensation cycle runs without pause in this warm pocket (26 to 28 Celsius, constant, as if the chamber has a thermostat made of patience), and the ceiling weeps in slow threads that the neo-peat receives and redistributes. At the filament tips, droplets form — not randomly but in cascading chains, one after another after another, a slow applause that has been going on, apparently, without interruption, for as long as anyone has thought to listen.

It does not grow. It does not shrink. It maintains. It is the only organism in the chamber that has made no attempt to expand. It has, if you allow the metaphor, made peace with its edges.

---

Then the flies.

*Hesperophyga railensis.* Small, dark-bodied, with wings that catch the bioluminescent glow of the filaments and throw it back in faint prismatic flashes. No one has formally described the species yet. It may be years before the taxonomy catches up to what the flies themselves already know.

They roost in the filament canopy — thousands of them, pressed together in the copper-lit dark like commuters on a late train. They do not buzz. They do not wander. They grip the filaments with their fore-tarsi and wait, wings folded, antennae angled slightly forward, like listeners.

Every eleven minutes, the trains come. The pressure wave arrives before the sound does — you feel it first in your sternum, then in the chamber walls, then in the water film on the neo-peat surface. The flies respond as one. They release, all at once, from the filaments, and ride the pressure wave upward for two or three or, if the conditions are right, four glorious meters before the air steadies and they settle again. The flight lasts less than a second. It is, by any measure, unnecessary — there is nothing up there they need — and yet this is how they breed. Their mating displays are synchronized to the passage schedule. Males and females launch together in the wave, and the brief moment of weightlessness, of suspension in the hot mineral air, is when the signals pass between them — chemical, vibrational, something we do not have a word for because no doctoral student has yet stayed long enough to name it.

They know the schedule. They do not know the schedule. They simply *are* the schedule, the way a tide does not know the moon but answers to it.

---

The beetles are smaller than the flies and more secretive. They live pressed into the neo-peat surface, their elytra the same copper-dark as the dry substrate, and they come out to drum. The drumming is the loudest deliberate sound in the chamber between trains: a rapid, rhythmic percussion produced by the abdomen striking the wet neo-peat, and it goes on for minutes at a time — not random, not chaotic, but structured in the way that dripping water is structured, in the way that weather is structured, with patterns that repeat but never quite repeat identically.

The patterns shift with the season. They also, for reasons no one has been able to explain, shift with the tonal quality of each train's passage. Coal loads, which are heavier and produce a lower resonance, elicit a slower, deeper drumming. Empty ballast cars, lighter and sharper, produce a higher, more staccato response. The beetles know the difference. They have always known the difference. A doctoral student from Ketterval once set up an array of microphones and spectrograms and recorded six weeks of drumming matched against six weeks of train manifests. She published nothing from the data. She went home, according to the story, and sat very quietly for a long time.

---

In the water film — the thin, warm layer that coats the neo-peat like a membrane — lives the frog.

*Limnoderma trephos.* Pale. Eyeless. The size of a thumbnail, which means that if you held one in your palm you could cover it with a thumbprint. Its skin is transparent. Through it you can see the heart: small, dark, beating with a rhythm that has nothing to do with trains and everything to do with whatever internal clock a creature develops when it has never had light to divide its world into day and night. The heart beats. The frog waits.

It does not react to light because there is no light down here worth reacting to — only the bioluminescent glow of the filaments, and the penlight of a visitor, which it ignores entirely. It navigates by vibration, which is to say it navigates by the trains, by the beetles, by your breathing. Its world is a map of tremors. Press yourself flat against a table and imagine that the only information you have about reality is how it vibrates.

During the passage — every eleven minutes, the wall of low-frequency sound, the ripples spreading outward across the water film from a center point like a stone dropped in perfect stillness — the frog presses itself flat. Motionless. Pressed into the neo-peat as if ironed into place, heart visible through the skin, beating slowly. It could be sleeping. It could be terrified. There is no way to tell the difference in something that has never opened an eye.

Then the vibration fades. The ripples settle. And the frog resumes its patient, almost imperceptible hunting of nematodes and micro-worms in the water film, moving with the slow deliberation of a hand reaching across a dark table for something it knows is there.

There are many of them. Sometimes, kneeling at the moss-gap with a penlight, you see them scattered across the red surface like seeds in soil, motionless, waiting. They do not look at you. They are not aware of you. They are aware of the eleven minutes.

---

The spider is dark and no larger than a grain of rice.

Its webs are the most unsettling artifact in the chamber — not because of their size, but because of their precision. The geometry is hexagonal, and the hexagons are so regular, so uniform, that they look machined. You would swear they were stamped out by a tiny die and laid into position, but they are not. They are built, strand by strand, by a spider that moves through the filament canopy and between the rusted bolt-heads with a deliberateness that is genuinely hard to watch. It does not hurry. It does not hesitate. It places each thread with the calm authority of something that has never once doubted it had the right materials or the right space.

The webs are enormously strong for their weight. They do not break during the vibration events, though they flex — you can see them flex, the silk stretching and returning like the membrane of a tiny speaker playing a note below hearing. What the spider catches is not food, not exactly. It catches vibrations. It catches information. What it does with the information is its own business.

---

And beneath all of this, the nematodes and micro-worms — the base of whatever passes for a food chain here — threading through the water film, through the neo-peat, through the calcium lattice of the substrate itself, too small to see, too numerous to count, feeding the frog, feeding the spider in its indirect way, feeding the beetles indirectly, cycling nutrients in a system so tightly closed it seems to have no input or output except the trains from above and the seep from below, and both of those are mechanical, not biological — and yet the chamber runs, the chamber *works,* on its own terms, with no purpose and no output and no need for anyone to kneel at the moss-gap and peer down with a light.

But you do. And you hear the clicking — the water falling onto water onto water, the calcium-lattice contractions, the clock unwinding — and you feel the vibration in your molars, and you see the filaments glowing green-amber in the dust-cloud's shadow, and you understand, without being able to say how, that the place is not empty. It never was. It is so full of life that life here has stopped trying to look like anything but the walls themselves.
