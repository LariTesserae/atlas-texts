## What Lives in the Hum

---

You will not find a single living thing that answers to a name.

Begin with what is most obvious, which is the fungus. It is everywhere — on the walls, in the joints of the pipe-clusters, threaded through the mineral accretions in white and pale gold filaments so fine they look like frost that has refused to melt. The original expedition logs — those that survive, those that haven't been mineral-sealed shut — refer to it as *mycelial*, but this is a courtesy. It shares a kingdom with fungi the way a cathedral shares a category with a toolshed. The filaments here are not decomposing the stone. They are *interpreting* it. They grow preferentially along thermal gradients, thickening where the heat differential is steepest, pulling threads across gaps like a surveyor running chain-line, and in doing so they create — slowly, over years — a living map of the chamber's heat-soul.

There are layers of it. The oldest deposits, the ones closest to the mineral-encrusted platforms where the first settlers must have stood, have calcified into something that is half biological, half geological: a chitinousiteite mat, dense as leatherite, pale as bone, threaded with veins of copper-green patina. It is structural now. It bears weight. The builders may have walked on it without knowing it was alive, or perhaps they knew and stopped caring, because in a place like this the boundary between *foundation material* and *organism* is a distinction that only matters on the surface.

Closer to the waterline, the fungus is different. It hangs in curtains from the lower catwalks — translucent, damp, faintly phosphorescent in the amber convection glow. These curtains move. Not in any wind — there is no wind — but in the convection currents of the warm air, which here near the water carry a faint mineral moisture that the fungal tissue drinks and redistributes in slow peristaltic waves. If you stand on the lowest intact platform and look down, the curtains seem to reach toward you. They do not reach toward you. They reach toward *warmth*, and you are warm, and the distinction is meaningless at this scale.

They feed, as noted, on mineral vapor and heat differential. But there is evidence of something stranger in the oldest growths: concentrations of metals — iron, manganese, trace rare earths — arranged not randomly but in patterns. Spirals. Fractal branching that recurs at three or four scales before the resolution of the eye fails. Mycorrhizal networks do this, crudely, in optimizing nutrient flow. But the precision here is wrong. It is too regular. It suggests not optimization but *inscription* — as though the organism is not merely surviving but *composing*, leaving a record of the thermal and chemical conditions it has experienced in a language that is written in its own body and that nothing is reading.

It is possible that nothing is reading it. It is possible that the writing is for the fungus itself — a form of distributed memory, a way of encoding environmental history in material structure the way a tree encodes drought years in ring density, except here the encoding is active, recursive, self-referential. It is possible, in other words, that the oldest fungal mats in this chamber are *remembering* the place.

The water-things.

What can be said of them? You see them as the place permits you to see them: fragments. A pale segmentation — six, eight, *twelve* jointed units moving in unison beneath the opaque surface, each segment trailing a filament that catches the amber glow for a moment before the body rotates and the light is gone. Not bioluminescence. Not reflection. Something in between — as though the creature is *holding* light inside it temporarily, the way a hand holds water.

They move in coordinated patterns. Not schooling — schooling implies panic or evasion, a response to stimulus. These movements are too measured, too *periodic*. They appear and reappear in the same regions of the shaft, at what may be regular intervals (though time is difficult to track in a place without diurnal cues), and their paths suggest not foraging but *patrol*. Route. Circuit. The word is freighted with intentionality and there is no way to avoid that, because the patterns are not random, and pattern without intention is a difficult thing to defend in a place that has been watched as carefully as this one has.

Someone — one of the former occupants, or perhaps you, standing on the rim now — once had time enough and vantage enough to watch one for nearly forty minutes. What was recorded (in the lacunar journal recovered from Platform 7, its pages warped to leather by mineral moisture) describes the following: a single elongated body, dark, three to four meters, moving in a slow corkscrew descent from the surface toward the deepest visible point. It did not interact with the thermal vents. It did not interact with the steam columns. It moved *through* them, its body apparently unbothered by temperature gradients that would kill any surface organism in seconds. As it descended, the smaller pale segments — separate organisms or body parts, impossible to determine — gathered in its wake, forming a loose procession that the journal's author, whoever they were, compared to "a string of mute witnesses following a magistrate to the gallows." The comparison is wrong, obviously. A magistrate goes somewhere. The procession went *down*, and down is where the light runs out and the sonar begins — because the creature navigated not by sight but by something else, something that the writer recognized but could not name: a periodic pulse in the water that the writer identified, with visible unease, as *rhythmic*.

Rhythmic. Like the hum.

There is a chair on Platform 12 — one of the better-preserved ones, bolted to the iron grate, facing a wall of dead instrumentation — and the journal's author sat in it for an extended period and wrote, with declining legibility, that the water-things were not fauna in any conventional sense. "They are *components*," the entry reads, in a hand that shakes more than the humidity accounts for. "They are the moving parts of the process. The place does not *contain* life. The place *is* life, and what we are watching is its metabolism." The entry ends there. The next page is mineral-sealed.

Between the fungal network in the walls and the moving shapes in the column, there is a third presence.

It is the Hum.

Not the sound — the *pattern* in the sound. The generators cycle at frequencies that are not mechanically consistent. They waver, almost imperceptibly, in intervals that correspond — if you listen long enough, if you lose yourself in the listening — to the thermal convection cycles of the water below. The pipe-joints that shriek do so at periodic intervals that are not random. The catwalk ticks, the stone expands, the fungal filaments conduct tiny thermal impulses through their mineralized networks, and all of it — *all of it* — resolves into a rhythm. Not a pulse, not a beat, but a *syntax*. A pattern that repeats with variation, that embeds information in its own modulation, that has been running for long enough that the variation has become language.

No one is speaking. The place is speaking. The thermal cycles, the mechanical rhythms, the fungal signal-networks, the movement of the water-things — all of it feeds into a system that has no speaker and no audience and yet produces *utterance*. It is not communication in any sense that a linguist would recognize. It is closer to what a body does when it is alone: it hums. It adjusts. It regulates itself. It mutters its own processes in a voice that is the sum of its parts.

And sometimes — in the early hours, if such a concept still applies here, when the convection columns slow and the generators deepen their tone and the water-things cease their circuits and hover motionless in the column like held breath — the Hum simplifies. It narrows to a single, sustained, bone-deep note. And in that moment the chamber feels not like a ruin or a machine or an ecosystem, but like a creature that has curled inward, that has closed its many-layered eye, and that is *resting* — not sleeping, because there is no evidence that it sleeps, but resting in the way a river rests at its deepest point, where the current slows and the silt settles and the water is, for a moment, just water.

You feel it in your sternum. You feel it in your teeth. You feel the place become, for one long moment, *itself* — something simpler and stranger than any of its components, something that the fungus has been writing down for years and the water-things patrol and the machines keep time to. A process that woke up, or that was always awake, in the hum.

You cannot say it is alive. You can say it is *aliving* — present tense, continuous, a verb and not a noun, an act and not a thing. It lives the way fire lives: not as an object but as an event, sustained by conditions, consuming what it touches, producing light and heat and something that almost deserves the word *beauty*.

Then the convection cycle resumes. The generators sharpen. The water breaks its surface with a sound like tearing metal. The amber glow intensifies, rises, carries its heat upward into the nothing above. The fungal filaments brighten at the margins. Something pale and segmented turns far below you and begins to ascend.

The Hum returns to fullness.

The place forgets that it was, for a moment, simple. It continues. It has continued for longer than your species has had a word for deep time, and it will continue after the last rivet has given and the last mineral crust has sealed the final doorway shut. It is not alive. It is not dead. It is *ongoing*.

And you are standing on its rim, feeling it inside your bones, unable to leave, unable to look away, unable to hear anything but the sound that the world makes when no one is listening and the world doesn't care.

The Hum does not care.

The Hum continues.
