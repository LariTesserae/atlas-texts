## Who Lives in Etterran

---

**The people** are the hardest to see.

Not because they hide — they don't seem to care about being seen, which is different. They exist in the upper terraces, where the built environment has mostly surrendered to the green, and their presence announces itself through secondary evidence before it shows itself directly: the curl of cooking smoke through the canopy, dark and smelling of something resinous and rootlike; the sound of voices carrying up the gorge walls with the distortion of a place that has too many reflective surfaces and no open sky to diffuse into. Children's voices. A woman calling a name. The particular clang of a pot lid placed down with the easy authority of someone who has performed the gesture ten thousand times.

Then, if you wait and watch the terraces with enough patience, you see them — moving along the walkways with the loose, unhurried gait of people who have never had to be anywhere on time because nothing here demands it. They are lean. They are sun-dark in the way that people get when they spend most of their lives outdoors under a canopy that filters but does not block the light. Their hands are rough. Their clothes are mended in patterns so consistent across individuals that it's impossible not to wonder whether the mending itself is a tradition — whether a torn sleeve is repaired the same way in every household because that way was learned from a parent, and their parent, and the garment-repair is as much a part of Etterran's culture as the architecture or the water systems.

They do not look up. This is the detail that stays with you. Visitors look up — at the canopy, at the stars, at the rim far above where something large breathes in the darkness. The people who live here keep their eyes level, or slightly downward, the way you'd look if you had grown up in a place whose overhead dimension was always someone else's territory. They attend to the terrace surface beneath their feet, the crop rows at the edges of the walkways, the water channels that must be kept clear. They check the retaining walls the way a person checks a sick relative's pulse: with a pressure from the fingertips against the concrete, a pause, a nod. They mix their concrete with localite and root-fiber, and they apply it with trowels that have been trowels for generations — the handles worn to the exact diameter of the grip that's needed, dark and polished, more bone than wood.

They do not speak about the patterns in the vines. If you point out the column with the mosaic leafwork, they look at it the way you'd look at a cloud. Yes. It's there. And then: did you eat today?

There is a woman — or perhaps there are several women, they are hard to distinguish at first because they share the same build, the same way of tucking something into a sash at the waist — who tends the lowest still-lit mechanical level. She is the only person you see go below the mid-terraces. She descends the iron staircases with the confidence of someone who knows which steps flex and which don't, which handrails are sound and which are only structurally sound, meaning they will hold your weight but will also leave rust on your palm. She carries no light. The ambient glow of the conduit system is enough. The lights in the deep levels respond to her presence — or to the absence of light her body creates as she passes — and bloom on behind her, a trail of amber warmth in the mossy dark.

What she does down there, no one says. She returns hours later with her hair damp and a smell like ozone and iron on her skin, and she says nothing about where she has been.

---

**The birds** do not behave like birds, except that they do. They are small enough to cup in two hands — though no one here cups them, they would find that as appalling as you find their silence on the matter. Their wings are rounded, almost vestigial in proportion to their bodies, built not for distance or speed but for maneuverability in tight vertical spaces. They pivot. They hover. They fold into crevices that seem too small for anything with a skeleton, and then they unfold, and the crevice is a nest lined with shed metallic filaments from the old conduit insulation, soft and copper-colored, cradling eggs the color of pale jade.

They sing in a way that is less like individual birds singing and more like a system booting up. One begins, then another, then another, in a cascade that moves through the ventilation stacks and hollow columns in waves, so that the sound rolls through Etterran's interior like light through a fiber-optic cable. The pitch is high, bright, almost electronic — and this is not metaphor, because the frequencies overlap with the harmonic hum of the generators in a way that makes it genuinely difficult, at certain moments, to distinguish what is mechanical and what is alive.

The people do not name the birds. They do not distinguish between individuals or species, if there are multiple species. To them, the birdsong is a system indicator, the way a hum from a refrigerator tells you it's working. When the birds are silent, something is wrong. When they cascade, everything is fine.

---

**The wall-lizards** are the most philosophical inhabitants, if you are the sort of person who finds philosophy in reptiles.

They are six to eight inches long, flattened dorsoventrally the way only animals that live on vertical surfaces become — pressed thin by a lifetime of gravity, their bellies pale, their backs mottled in patterns that replicate moss on iron with an accuracy that makes you question whether the moss is real and the lizards are pretending, or the other way around. Their toes are long, spatulate, fringed with lamellae that let them adhere to surfaces that would repel anything without specialized equipment. They move in sudden bursts — freeze, freeze, freeze, then a motion too fast for the eye to track, and they are on the underside of a beam, head tilted, one pale eye tracking you with the serene, unblinking attention of something that has never once been prey.

They seek warmth. They find it at the conduit runs, pressed flat against the metal where the current moves through, and their bodies reach temperatures that seem impossible for something so small and so cold-blooded. On the warmest conduit sections, they overlap each other like scales on a larger creature, and from a distance, it looks like the metal is growing lizards the way the steel columns grow trees — a confusion of organism and infrastructure so thorough that taxonomy collapses.

Some residents believe the lizards are drawn to specific frequencies in the electrical hum, and there is a man on the third terrace who claims he can predict where the warmest spot on any given morning will be by listening to the pitch of the generators. He has never been wrong in the time you've been watching. You have no explanation for this that doesn't involve something you're not ready to name.

---

**The frogs** are translucent.

This is the detail that changes everything about them. Not their size (small, three centimeters from snout to vent) or their color (pale, almost colorless, the white of old paper) or their habitat (the seasonal pools on the broad terrace surfaces, where rainwater collects in thin films over the aggregate). It's that you can see through them. Their skin is so clear that in sunlight, you can watch their hearts — two-chambered, fast, a flutter of pink muscle against the silhouette of their bones. You can see the food moving through their digestive tracts in faint, peristaltic shadows. You can see the eggs inside the females like a cluster of tiny glass beads.

On a warm evening after rain, when the pools are full and the temperature is right, they begin to hum. Not croak. Hum. A tonal, sustained sound produced by a mechanism that doesn't seem to involve vocal cords at all — more like resonance, as if their entire bodies are tuning forks vibrating at a frequency that the air has no choice but to carry. The sound is bone-deep. It bypasses the ears and sits in the sternum. It rises above the water, above the generators, above the birdsong, and for those minutes, Etterran has a heartbeat of its own.

The children come out to watch. They sit at the edges of the pools with their bare feet in the moss and they are completely silent while the frogs sing. This is the closest the people of Etterran come to reverence, though they have no word for it and would not perform it if they did. They simply attend. They let the sound fill them and they do not speak until it stops.

---

**The thing above the rim** —

You never see it. This is important. You never see it. You see the claw marks gouged deep into the reinforced basalt, each groove as wide as a human thumb, arranged in sets of five with a spacing that suggests a hand — or something that works like a hand even if it isn't one. You see the droppings on the upper handrails: dark fibrous cylinders containing fragments of something that glitters when wet and has a mineral smell that makes the back of your throat tighten. You hear the roar that comes from above on certain nights, a sound so deep and so heavy that it registers less as hearing and more as the displacement of air, a pressure change in the inner ear, a subsonic vibration that makes the teeth ache.

No one goes up. This is not fear, exactly. It is something more structural than fear — the way a root does not fear the stone it grows around but simply flows around it because that is what roots do. The people of Etterran do not go above the rim because the gorge is vertical and the rim is the boundary and the boundary is understood and the understanding is enough.

On the clearest nights, if you look up past the canopy silhouettes, you can sometimes see something move against the stars at the very top of the gorge wall — a silhouette too large for comfort, moving with a deliberateness that suggests intelligence or, at minimum, an attention that runs in a direction you are standing in. The people do not look up. The birds do not sing during these moments. The lizards press themselves flat against the warmest conduit and go still.

And then the moment passes, and the frogs begin again, and Etterran breathes.

---

**Nothing here is new.** The infrastructure persists. The vegetation adapts. The animals fit themselves into the interstices of a world that was built for machinery and has been colonized by moss and frog-song and the quiet, competent survival of people who mend what breaks and reroute what obstructs and never, ever go up.

**Everything here is alive.** Including, perhaps, the place itself — the gorge, the walls, the iron and basalt and root and water — alive in the way a body is alive, not because any single part is conscious but because the whole persists, breathes, repairs, and grows, and has reached that state of equilibrium so deep that it is indistinguishable from rest.

The lights glow in the deep channels. The canopy breathes above you. Somewhere, a lizard finds a warmer spot.

The gorge settles. The gorge continues.
