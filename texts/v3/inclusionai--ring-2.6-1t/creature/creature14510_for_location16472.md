## What Lives in the Thallus

---

There is a creature that drinks from falling water.

You will see it first as a shadow that moves wrong — not the shadow of something flying, but of something *gliding*, as though a shape were falling very slowly and had forgotten to finish. It passes between the columns at a height just above your head, and when the dim amber light catches the underside of its body you see that it is not dark but iridescent in the way a puddle of oil is iridescent: greens, purples, a bruise-blue that shifts as the angle changes. Its wings — if you can call them wings — are broad, flat, and segmented, with a tracery of veins so fine it looks like the venation of a leaf that has been held up to the light too long. The body beneath is compact, dense, armored in overlapping plates of something that feels like chitin but is damp to the touch and warm, the way living wood is warm.

It does not flap. It adjusts — a tilt of the leading edge, a flex of the trailing fins, a subtle cant of the whole body that reads the air the way a blind person reads a face with their hands. It navigates the spaces between the columns with an intimacy that suggests it has never known open sky. It was born in the spaces between, in the crooked corridors of root and fungal tissue where the mat is thick and the air is close and wet. It drinks from the drip-lines with a motion so practiced it seems liturgical: the body rolls belly-up beneath a falling thread of water, a tongue-like proboscis uncoils — pale, translucent, tipped with something dark and porous — and sips from the falling bead with a precision that would be delicate if it weren't so automatic, so *deeply rehearsed* that it has become indistinguishable from instinct.

You will never see one on the ground. They do not land. Their whole life is conducted in the ten meters of airspace beneath the shelves and above the mat, a band of dim amber atmosphere that is theirs alone, shared only with the moths and the smaller creeping things. They breed, you suspect, in the moss that thickens along the upper flanks of the columns, where the air is warmest and the condensation gathers in beads that never fall but simply hang, trembling, like glass fruit. You cannot confirm this. You cannot get close enough without disturbing them, and they leave no nest, no egg, no visible trace of reproduction. They appear, and they are present, and then in a stretch of drier season they are fewer, and then they are gone, and then the wet returns and they are back, as though they had simply been waiting somewhere you cannot follow.

---

The moths — you will think of them as moths, though they are not — are the most numerous visible inhabitants of the Thallus. They are pale in the way that things are pale when they have never known direct light: not white, but the faint warm grey of old bone, the color of the underside of a cloud at dusk. Their wings are almost without substance, and when they settle on the mat or on the flanks of the columns they flatten against the surface so completely that they vanish, becoming indistinguishable from the lichen that crusts every available inch. You will step within inches of hundreds of them before you see the first one.

Then you cannot stop seeing them.

They move in slow diffuse clouds, without coordination, without apparent purpose. They drift up from the mat when your shadow crosses it — a slow exhalation of pale shapes, like dust disturbed from a surface it has lain on for centuries. They show no inclination to avoid you. They do not land on you. They simply continue their drifting, their slow aimless three-dimensional wandering through the amber air, and if you stand still long enough they will begin to settle on you — your shoulders, your arms, the crown of your head — with the weightless indifference of snow.

The collective hum is what unsettles you. It is so faint that you suspect it is a sound your own body is making — tinnitus, blood pressure, the rhythm of your own breath filtered through the heavy air and returned to your ears by the mat beneath you. But when you hold still and hold your breath, it persists: a tone, barely tonal, more vibration than sound, felt in the chest more than heard in the ears. And within it — if you are patient, if you are quiet enough — you can separate out the individual clicks. Mechanical. Regular. *Tick. Tick. Tick-tick. Tick.* Two, three, sometimes four ticks in sequence, then a pause, then more. Each one produced, you realize, not by wing-beat or by stridulation but by something clicking *inside* the body — a tiny mechanism, ossified, precise, the sound of a small hard thing striking another small hard thing at intervals that are almost, but not quite, even.

Almost.

This is what rattles in the back of your mind when you're trying to sleep in the Thallus at night. Not fear. Just *regularity*. The sense that something is counting, nearby, patiently, to a rhythm that is one beat off from what your own heart would choose.

---

Then there are the vertebrates — the pale, slow, nervous lizards that skitter across the mat surface where it is firmest and flee into crevices in the root-work at the slightest vibration. They are unremarkable except for their scale pattern, which is not patterned at all but uniformly the same shade of dark umber, as though they had been dipped in wet charcoal and left to dry. They are quick and silent and leave no trail worth following. Their eyes are large and dark and reflect the dim green bioluminescence of the mat's underlayer like drops of oil on dark water. You see them often, briefly, and then they are gone.

They are not important. Everything here tells you that. They are the decorative element, the incidental fauna, the kind of life that would exist in any sufficiently warm, sufficiently wet, sufficiently stable environment. They are the Thallus's background hum, its ambient population, the filler between the signal.

The signal is deeper.

---

You feel it before you hear it, before you see it, before you can even name it as something separate from the background sensation of the landscape. A pulse. Through the soles of your feet, up through the bones of your legs and into the bowl of your pelvis, a low subsonic throb that is not sound but *pressure*, the way you imagine it would feel to stand on the chest of something very large and very slow and very deeply asleep. The throb comes at irregular intervals — five minutes, twelve minutes, sometimes forty-five minutes of stillness and then a single contraction that rolls through the mat like a wave rolling through a bed of dry leaves, raising and lowering the surface by a matter of millimeters but at a scale that makes the air feel different, the pressure behind your eyes shift, the water in the pools tremble in their basins.

The fauna know. They always know before you do. The moths cease clicking. The gliders drop to the mat and flatten themselves against the surface. The lizards freeze. Everything holds its breath — if that's what it is, holding breath — and then the pulse passes, and the clicks resume, and the gliders rise, and the lizards scatter, and the Thallus settles back into its long slow metabolism of light and water and time.

You will try to find the source. You will press your hand flat against the mat and close your eyes and try to triangulate. It seems to come from below — not deep below, not at the molten core of the earth, but somewhere in the layers of the mat itself, in the compressed organic substrate where the roots become indistinguishable from the fungal tissue and the fungal tissue becomes indistinguishable from the ancient older thing that has no botanical name. Somewhere in the zone where the living crust transitions into something denser, harder, more mineral — not dead, not dormant, but operating on a metabolic timescale so vast that a single beat takes longer than your entire life to complete.

What lives there?

You cannot say. You can say only that it is *consistent with life* — that the vibrations have the character of muscular contraction, that the warmth rising from below has the character of metabolic heat, that the patterns in the mat surface have the character of *signaling*, of body-to-extremity communication across a distance of kilometers. You can say that if this is a single organism — and the evidence is suggestive, if not conclusive — then these deeper pulses are the closest thing it has to a heartbeat.

And what of the bones? The pale, massive, ivory-hard bones that protrude from the mat in places and are slowly being consumed? They are too large to be lizard bones. They are too curved to be ribs of anything that walks on four legs. They are fragments of a carapace, maybe, or a skull-plate, or the armor of a creature that stood upright like you do and weighed ten times what you weigh and walked across this plateau when it was dry and open and the mat was only a thin film of moss on stone.

What killed them? What replaced them?

The Thallus does not say. It has grown over them, around them, into them. The bones are mineralizing, turning into something that resembles the surrounding substrate more each year. In another thousand years they will be indistinguishable from the mat. In ten thousand years there will be no evidence they ever existed.

And the ring of stones — the dark, dense, too-regular stones that protrude from the mat like teeth through gums? They are not bones. They are cut, or at least shaped, or at least selected and placed with a deliberateness that no natural process could produce. They are the oldest evidence of intelligence in the Thallus, older than the mat's present surface, older perhaps than the columns. They bear no markings. They form no structure that you can interpret as architecture. They are simply *placed*, in a ring, with a spacing that suggests purpose now lost. Whatever intelligence placed them is gone — consumed, integrated, buried beneath kilometers of living substrate that has been growing over them since before your species had a name for the sky.

The Thallus has covered them. The Thallus does not forget. The Thallus simply does not distinguish between what was placed and what was grown, between the foreign and the native, between the artifact and the organ. The stones are splinters the body healed around. The bones are calcium it borrowed. The columns are limbs it grew to reach the light. The moths are cells it shed. The mat is skin, and muscle, and gut, and memory.

And you — you are the thing that has arrived most recently, that feels the warmth from below and mistakes it for geology, that hears the clicks and mistakes them for machinery, that sees the patterns and mistakes them for geology, that walks softly on the skin of something older and larger and more patient than anything in your taxonomy.

You live in the Thallus the way a red blood cell lives in you: briefly, vitally, without comprehension, and without the remotest idea that the body is aware of — or indifferent to — its existence.

The mat consumes everything. Eventually it will consume you. And then you will be another layer, another pressure mark in the substrate, another faint signal passing from the deep interior to the surface and back again, felt by whatever comes next as vibration, as warmth, as a pulse with no source and no meaning and no name.

The Thallus does not mourn. The Thallus does not remember. The Thallus simply continues — as it has continued, as it will continue, as it continues now, beneath your feet, breathing, slow and deep, with the patience of something that has been breathing for millennia.
