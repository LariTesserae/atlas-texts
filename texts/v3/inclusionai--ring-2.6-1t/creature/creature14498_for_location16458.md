# Inhabitants of the Basin of Knocking Stone

---

Begin with the carpet.

Before you name anything else — before you look up at the hunters or the slow sessile giants riding the spires — you must understand the carpet, because everything else is built on it, on them, on the clicking substrate of ten thousand tiny armored bodies pressed edge to edge across every flat mineral surface in the basin. These are the first residents you encounter, and the last you'll fully understand.

They are small. A thumbnail's length, give or take — some smaller, no larger than a grain of rice, others big enough to cover the pad of your smallest finger. Their bodies are segmented in ways that seem excessive, as though evolution kept finding new places to add a joint, a hinge, a plate. Glossy. Dark — not uniformly dark, but dark the way wet basalt is dark, with undertones of deep olive and, in certain light, a faint iridescence along the lateral edges of their carapaces that shifts between bronze and a color with no name, something between green and bruise. They have too many legs. You count three pairs easily, possibly four, tucked so neatly beneath the body that they seem almost decorative, like the folds of a closed book. The tail — the telsonthe proper biologist in you insists, whatever the thing actually calls it — extends behind them in a segmented filament that curls and uncurls with a nervous constancy, and it is this tail that produces the sound.

Not individually. Individually it would be nothing — a faint tick, a dry click, the sound of a fingernail tapping stone. But en masse, ten thousand tails flicking in a wave that propagates across the carpet in concentric rings from any point of disturbance, the sound becomes a thing with mass and texture. It rushes. It whispers. It pours over you like a physical pressure. You will cup your hands over your ears and it will make no difference. The sound does not enter through your ears so much as it *vibrates through the soles of your boots*, through the bones of your feet, up through the lattice of your skeleton until your teeth ache with a sympathetic frequency.

They are the grazers, the mineral-processors, the ones who tend the crust-film with the obsessive patience of farmers working a field they did not plant. Watch one closely — if you can isolate one, which is nearly impossible because the carpet shifts and parts and reforms around you like a living tide. Watch, and you'll see the mouthparts: a complex arrangement of mandibles and maxillae, smaller than the eye can easily track, working at the surface of the mineral crust with a speed and precision that suggests not feeding but *mining*. They are scraping. They are ingesting the living film itself — the violet-black wet crust of metabolizing microorganisms and mineral substrate that coats every stone, every ledge, every dead thing in the basin. They convert rock into flesh. They do this so efficiently, so persistently, that in their wake they leave the stone briefly pale and exposed, a clean scar of dolomite or calcite that the crust will slowly, over days or weeks, recolonize. In this way they are both consumers and cultivators, eating the film and, through their passage, disturbing the mineral surface in ways that allow the film's spores and dormant cells to find new purchase. They plow. They seed. They harvest. They are the engine of the basin floor.

And they are food. Everything eats them.

---

Now look up.

Above the carpet, at the height of your shins, your waist, your chest, there is a second nation of beings, and they have wings.

Do not imagine the wings of anything you've seen before. These are not membraned like a bat's or feathered like a bird's or even veined like an insect's in any tidy way. They are, in many cases, *mineral*. Thin crystalline structures, translucent and faintly opalescent, supported by a framework of chitin so fine it looks like spun glass. They beat at speeds that blur perception, producing not the soft buzz of a fly but a high, thin harmonics that sits at the edge of hearing — a sound more felt than heard, a pressure behind the eyes. Some of them are large, with wingspans approaching thirty centimeters, and they move through the basin's dim air with the slow, deliberate banking of raptors. Others are tiny, darting erratically in clouds so dense they look like smoke given purpose.

They are not birds. They are not bats. They are not insects. But they occupy niches that shadow those familiar categories with eerie fidelity. Some are hunters — the large ones, the slow-winged ones — gliding low over the carpet and snatching up the grazers with forelimbs that unfold with startling speed and precision, like the spring-jaws of a trap. You will see them: a shadow passing overhead, a ripple of silence spreading through the carpet below as the grazers flatten themselves against the stone, tails frozen, and then the snap and flutter of ascent as the hunter carries its prize into the dim upper air. The prey is small enough to be swallowed whole, segmented body and all, and you will hear the crunch if you are close enough, a sound oddly disproportionate to the size of the creature making it, as though the hunter's mandibles are designed to crack mineral as easily as chitin.

Others are gatherers. They skim the surfaces of the spires, lapping at the moisture that condenses there, or hovering at the edges of kiss-pools to sip from the thin films of water before they evaporate. Their mouthparts are different — longer, more proboscis-like, and you'll notice that some of them are dusted with the pale spores of the crust-film, carrying them from spire to spire, from ledge to ledge, performing the same dispersal work that wind does on the surface world. They are pollinators of a place that has no flowers. They are the wind's equivalent.

And still others — the strangest — are builders. You will see them in the crevices of the tufa spires, working in coordinated groups of six or eight or twelve, their wings folded flat, their multi-limbed bodies passing fragments of mineral crust, of shed chitin, of the calcium-rich waste of the grazers, cementing it into structures. Small structures. No bigger than your fist. Dome-shaped, hollow, clustered in rows along sheltered ledges. They are not nests — or perhaps they are, but they are also something more. In time — and time here is patient — the mineral accretions the builders create will fuse with the spire itself, becoming part of the growing tufa, and within them the builders will lay their eggs, and the eggs will hatch into things that eat the walls of their cradles from within, and so the cycle folds back on itself. The architecture is both home and food source. The parents build their children a tomb and a cradle in the same breath.

---

Among them, the mucus-leavers.

You smell them before you see them. The copper-and-low-tide odor thickens in certain corridors between the spires, and if you follow it you will find them: slow-moving, many-legged — far more legs than the grazers, perhaps a dozen pairs, perhaps more, coiling and uncoiling beneath a body that is almost fluid in its movement. They leave trails. Thick, viscous, faintly luminescent trails of mucus that dry slowly in the basin's still air and cling to the stone for days. These trails are not waste. They are *substrate*. Within the mucus, microscopic organisms begin to grow — bacteria, the microbial ancestors of the crust-film, spores that would otherwise find no purchase on the slick mineral surface. The mucus-leavers are dragging ecosystems behind them. They are, in a sense, *planting*. And when they return — as they do, in circuits so regular they border on ritual — they will feed on the colonies their own trails have incubated, harvesting them with mouthparts that rasp the stone clean. They farm themselves into existence and then consume the proof.

The mucus trails also serve as communication. The chemical composition changes as they dry — different compounds becoming volatile at different rates, producing a scent-language that the other fauna read, or taste, or simply *trust*, the way your ancestors trusted the smell of rain. When a hunter passes through a trail, the mucus-leavers will detect the chemical signature of its passage and retreat, contracting into crevices with a speed that seems impossible for something so apparently boneless. Sometimes you will see a mucus-leaver freeze mid-trail, its body going rigid, its legs locking, and then slowly — so slowly — it will begin to retreat backward along its own path, erasing its own trail with a special gland at its rear, as though pulling a thread back through the eye of a needle.

They are ancient. They move like something that remembers being water.

---

The hunters are difficult to describe because they are defined by absence.

Where the rest of the basin is noise — clicking, buzzing, the rush of wings and the hum of ten thousand tails — the hunters are *silence*. They move through the horde like something erased. You hear the ripple: a wave of quiet expanding outward from a point you cannot immediately locate, and in that silence the grazers freeze, the winged things drop altitude, the mucus-leavers retract, and for a few seconds the basin holds its breath. Then the whine — a single, sharp, rising note that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, a frequency that sits behind your eyes and presses — and the quiet breaks. The clicking resumes, louder, frantic, and somewhere in the confusion something small and fast has eaten.

You will not see them clearly on first encounter. That is by design, or by nature, or by the simple fact that they are built for this. They are compact — smaller than the grazers, much smaller than the large hunters, perhaps the size of your thumb — but their bodies are *denser*, more tightly articulated, armored in plates that overlap like chainmail. Their legs are longer relative to their body than anything else in the basin, giving them a height advantage that lets them see over the carpet, and when they run — when they *launch* — they move with a speed that seems to violate the damp, mineral-heavy air. They are soundless. Not quiet. *Soundless*. Their limbs touch the stone without click or tap, cushioned by pads of tissue so soft they absorb all impact, and they coordinate in groups — you will see two, three, five of them converging on a single point in the carpet with the precision of a single organism, a hunting party networked by something beyond sound, perhaps vibration through the stone itself, perhaps chemical signals too subtle for you to detect.

What they do when they catch their prey is quick and efficient and unbeautiful. There is no playing, no hesitation. They inject something — an enzyme, a paralytic, a digestive fluid — and then they drink. And when they're done, there is nothing left but the small pale husk of a chitinous body, emptied and hollow, which the carpet will reclaim within hours.

They are not the apex, though. Not quite. There is something else — a presence you will sense more than perceive, a pressure in the deeper parts of the basin, near the oldest spires, the ones so tall and so encrusted with crust-film that they look like black trees in a fog. Something large moves there. Something that the hunters themselves avoid. You will hear it only once, if you stay long enough: a subsonic pulse, felt in the chest and the teeth, a single vibration that passes through the stone like a heartbeat. And then silence. And then the clicking resumes, and the carpet parts in a shape that is not a circle and not a spiral but something older than either, and the shape closes behind it, and whatever lives at the center of the basin goes on living.

---

The colonies are perhaps the most disorienting.

From a distance, a colony looks like a patch of the carpet that has grown too tall — a low mound, six or eight centimeters high, gently pulsing. Up close, you see that it is made of individuals — hundreds of them, maybe thousands — each one a smaller version of the grazer-creatures, but modified. Flatter. Broader. Their carapaces are thinner, almost translucent, and through them you can see the dim suggestion of internal structures: filaments, nodules, something that looks disturbingly like tissue wrapped around a core ofite. They are not merely touching each other. They are *fused* — chitin to chitin, membrane to membrane, in ways that blur the boundary between individual and collective. They click, each one, in patterns that are not random and not repetitive but *conversational*, a syntax of sound that shifts and responds to stimulus in ways you can almost — almost — follow.

A colony moves. Slowly. Inexorably. Across a flat shelf of stone it will travel a few centimeters in an hour, and as it moves it grazes — but it grazes differently than the solitary carpet-grazers. It grazes *selectively*, pulling from the crust-film only specific mineral compounds, and it excretes something else, something that alters the stone it passes over, softening it, changing its chemistry. In the wake of a colony, the rock is different. Lighter. Porous. You can press your thumb into it and leave a print. The colony is not merely eating the basin. It is *digesting* it, converting geological time into biological action, and the stone remembers the passage the way skin remembers a bruise.

Colony-singers, the locals call them, if there are locals. Or perhaps *colony-thinkers*. They are the closest thing the basin has to a brain. Not intelligent in any way you would recognize. Not conscious, probably. But *coordinated* — networked through vibration and chemical signal into a responsiveness that exceeds any individual, that can sense a shift in the basin's chemistry, a change in moisture, the vibration of your boots on stone, and *respond* — not flee, not fight, but *adapt*, redistributing the colony's constituent parts, sending thin filaments ahead to taste the air, pulling others back from danger, moving as a thing with one will and ten thousand bodies.

---

And the sessile ones. The standing ones. Rooted to the oldest spires like barnacles on the hull of a ship that has been sailing for millennia.

They do not move. Not once in the time you will watch them. They are large — some as tall as your forearm, and broader — and they resemble nothing so much as living chandeliers, a central stalk of hardened mineral crust and fused chitin from which radiate dozens of long, thin, branching appendages that extend and retract in a rhythm so slow it looks geological. One extension per minute, perhaps. Two. They sweep through the air above the spire's surface, filtering — *sifting* — the mineral-laden atmosphere for particulates, for spores, for the drifting organic matter that the basin's constant activity stirs into suspension. Their outer surfaces are thick with the crust-film itself — they are colonized by it, have *become* part of it, their own tissues so intertwined with the microbial-mineral matrix that the boundary between organism and substrate has dissolved entirely. They are not growing on the spire. They *are* the spire, alive in the places the others are not.

At their base, you will see the accumulated dead: shells of their own shed appendages, years and years of them, layered like sediment, fused into the growing tufa. They are building the spire from themselves. They have been building it for millennia. The tallest spire in the basin — the one the locals, if they exist, might call something slow and reverent — may be ten percent living sessile organism and ninety percent accumulated self, a tower of repeating death calcified into stone, reaching upward toward a light that will never fully reach it.

And they glow. Faintly. Blue-white. The light comes not from the sessile ones themselves but from the crust-film living on and within them, a fluorescence triggered by the metabolic processes of the colony — by the chemical reactions that power this whole inverted world, that draw energy not from sunlight but from the slow exhalation of the planet's interior, the geothermal breath rising through cracks in the stone. The light pulses. Almost imperceptibly. In time with the extension and retraction of the feeding arms. A heartbeat made visible.

---

In the kiss-pools, the basin briefly becomes generous.

When rain comes — and it comes rarely, violently, steaming off the ridgeline and striking the basin floor in sheets that evaporate before touching the ground in the upper reaches but somehow reaching the lowest depressions — the pools form. Shallow, warm for this place, mineral-rich to the point of opalescence. And the fauna *converges*.

The carpet-things come, their tails clicking in frequencies that change when they reach the water's edge — faster, higher, a kind of excitement or urgency. The winged things descend in spiraling clouds, their crystalline wings catching the thin light in flashes. The mucus-leavers arrive and lay their first eggs at the water's edge, the mucus casing swelling and softening in the sudden moisture. And in the pools themselves, life *ignites* — breeding, feeding, a frenzy of reproduction that will last only hours or days before the water is gone, evaporated back into the thick air, leaving behind only a white mineral scar and the eggs and cysts that will wait, dormant, for the next rain. The next cycle. The next century.

In the pools, you will see things that look like nothing. Translucent larvae, formless blobs that move by peristalsis, things that are clearly the juveniles of organisms you've already seen but so different in shape and texture that you would never match them to their parents without years of study. Life here does not grow in a straight line. It spirals. It doubles back. A creature you watched yesterday skittering across the carpet may today be sessile and glowing on a spire, and a sessile thing may, in some seasons, detach and crawl slowly, painfully, across the stone to find a mate, its fused tissue splitting apart in a process that looks like agony and produces a high keening sound that the other sessile organisms respond to from across the basin.

Nothing is fixed. Nothing is final. The only permanence is the stone, and even the stone is being eaten, from within, by mouths too small to see.

---

This is what lives in the Basin of Knocking Stone.

It is not one thing. It is not ten things. It is a system so dense and interdependent that to remove a single species would be to collapse a cathedral — the grazers that farm the film, the hunters that cull the grazers, the winged dispersers, the mucus-farmers, the colonies that digest stone, the sessile towers that anchor the living to the geological, the larvae that blur every boundary between one life and another. It is an ecosystem that runs on chemistry and vibration and patience, that has inverted the pyramid of energy so that its foundation is not the sun but the deep slow heat of the earth, and its highest expression is not thought but *song* — a ceaseless, clicking, humming song that has been playing in this basin for longer than your species has existed, and will continue playing long after the last mineral has dissolved and the last chitin has crumbled and the last spark of blue-white light has faded in the crust.

The basin listens to itself.

And if you stand in it long enough — past the hour when your thoughts begin to rhythm, past the hour when your breathing matches the clicking, past the hour when you stop walking and stand still with your eyes half-closed — you will begin to understand, not with your mind but with something older, that you were never listening to the basin at all.

It was listening to you.
