# What Lives in the Splay

---

Nothing lives here the way things live elsewhere.

Begin with the simplest question: what is a resident of the Splay? In other basins, in other landscapes, the answer is straightforward — a thing is alive, it occupies a territory, it eats and reproduces and dies in a place, and that place is therefore its home. But the Splay does not permit that economy. The Splay demands a different grammar. Here, to live is not to persist. To live is to *resonate*.

---

## The Vathines

Start where everything starts: with the trees.

A vathine is not a tree the way a cathedral is not a tent. The word *tree* is a courtesy, a loan from a vocabulary too poor to hold what it describes. A vathine is a process that has taken on the shape of a tree the way a river takes on the shape of its bed — not because it is one thing, but because it is the most stable form available to something that is continuously becoming.

Their bark is dark and fibrous, almost textile in texture, like rope that has been mineralized over decades into something that remembers being flexible. It peels in thin vertical strips that curl inward rather than outward, as though the tree is constantly shedding a version of itself it no longer needs. Beneath the bark, the sap moves — visibly, slowly, in columns of dark amber that rise through the translucent outer layer in pulses so deliberate they seem intentional. Each pulse takes several seconds. If you press your palm flat against the bark, you feel them: a rhythm beneath rhythm, a systole that doesn't match your own. The sap glows. Not brightly. Not consistently. It brightens when the air pressure shifts, when a rain approaches from the ridges, when something passes through the canopy overhead at a frequency that stirs the leaves into a state that is not quite agitation and not quite reverence.

The vathines do not photosynthesize — or if they do, it is secondary to something else. The spore-canopy that hangs at shoulder height across the basin is their exhalation. Billions of microscopic filaments released from the canopies, drifting and interlocking, forming the gauze through which all light in the Splay is filtered. The vathines are not merely residents of the twilight they create. They *are* the twilight. Remove a vathine and the spore-density would shift within weeks, and the light would change, and the animals would alter their patrol routes, and the parametric callers would adjust their intervals to compensate for the new geometry of sound. The vathines do not live *in* the Splay's ecology. They are the skeleton of it. Every other living thing is an annotation on their architecture.

Their roots are the most unsettling feature. Above ground, they form buttressed arches — pale with calciumite deposits, threaded with runnels of mineral-rich water — that spread outward from each trunk in patterns that echo, unnervingly, the structural logic of human engineering. Load-bearing trusses. Cantilevered supports. Buttresses designed to distribute lateral force. You cannot look at them without recognizing the engineering, and you cannot look at them *after* recognizing it, because the recognition changes nothing and also changes everything. No one built these. No one designed them. They emerged from the interaction of a root system seeking stability in chalk-silt substrate and a physics that does not care whether it produces beauty. And yet.

Below ground — and this is rumored, suspected, inferred from the way certain stretches of chalk resonate when struck — the vathine roots interlock. Not in the way that tree roots in a forest tangle and graft. In the way that *circuitry* connects. Node to node. Signal to signal. The vathines, if the most speculative botanists of the few expeditions are to be believed, may form a single networked organism beneath the basin floor, communicating through pressure differentials in their root-fluid. A forest that is one tree. A single mind distributed across twenty kilometers of chalk and calciumite, dreaming in sap and mineral.

No one has confirmed this. No one has dug deep enough. The chalk is soft, but it is deep, and the roots go deeper, and the water in the runnels carries a silt so fine it clogs any drill bit within hours.

The vathines endure. This is the verb that fits them best. They do not grow in any way that can be measured against a human lifespan. They *endure*, the way a sound endures in a resonance chamber — present, sustained, accumulating harmonics that only become audible over time.

## The Grazers

They are the closest thing the Splay offers to gentle.

Six-limbed, bilateral, each limb ending in a broad pad that distributes weight so evenly that a full-grown grazer can stand on chalk-silt without leaving a mark. Their coloration is the color of wet chalk itself — pale blue-white, faintly iridescent where the light catches the oils on their hide. Their bodies are low-slung and dense, barrel-chested, built close to the ground as though the Splay's particular gravity demands proximity. They are perhaps four meters long and half as tall, though dimensions deceive in a landscape with no vertical reference points.

The head is what arrests you. Smooth, curved, featureless — a single ovoid surface with no visible eyes, no mouth in any conventional sense. The eyes are there, recessed beneath a cranial plate that slides open in four quadrants when the animal is feeding or alarmed, revealing eyes that are wide, dark, and *spinning*. Not darting. Not scanning. Spinning — full rotations, slowly, like lighthouse lenses, taking in wavelengths that your eyes are not built to parse. When the plate closes they vanish entirely, and the grazer's face returns to the blank serenity of a river stone.

They feed on something in the chalk. You cannot see what. Their mouths — slitlike openings along the ventral ridge of the skull — press against the silt and remain there for long minutes, and occasionally you see the chalk surface discolor in a spreading ring, as though something is being drawn upward through micropores. They are eating the minerals. They are eating the calciumite itself, metabolizingite into something the biology has not yet named. When they finish, they rise and move on with a gait so unhurried it borders on geological, and they make a sound — a low, resonant, brass-toned moan, the texture of a tuba being slowly submerged — that you will hear in your sleep for weeks after you leave, if you leave.

They are not afraid of you. Nothing in the Splay is afraid of you. You are not predator or threat or even anomaly. You are a temporary feature, like a rain puddle or a fallen branch. They regard you with a patience so total it borders on compassion, and then they return to the invisible feast beneath their feet.

## The Parametric Callers

These are the voice of the Splay.

You never see them clearly. Field expeditions have captured fragments — blurred thermal silhouettes at the edges of the spore-haze, the suggestion of wings or fins or limbs arranged in geometries that resist memorization — but the parametric callers exist most fully as *sound*. That is their domain. That is the landscape they inhabit.

Their vocalizations are not calls in any zoological sense. They are sequences — ascending and descending in intervals that follow mathematical progressions. Pentatonic runs that modulate into quarter-tones that fold back into harmonics that should not be perceptible to human ears and yet are, felt as much as heard, vibrating in the molars and the sternum. They do not sing. They *compute into the air*. Each caller produces its own sequence, and the sequences interlock — one calling from the east ridge, one from the north, one from somewhere impossibly above, in the spore-canopy where visibility drops to nothing — and the overlapping patterns create a three-dimensional lattice of sound that encloses you completely, that becomes the architecture of the space you inhabit.

At full chorus, the volume is physically painful. Not because it is loud in the crude sense — though it is loud — but because it is *structured*. The intervals land in places inside your skull that have no name. The pressure behind your eyes is not a side effect; it is the medium. The parametric callers are not producing sound. They are producing *space*. They are defining the dimensions of the basin through acoustic interference, carving out territories with frequencies that cancel or reinforce. When you feel the migraine blooming behind your forehead, you are not hearing a noise. You are standing inside a equation.

They triangulate. They adjust. If you move, the sequences shift — subtly, over the course of minutes — to maintain the lattice. You are not being watched by the parametric callers. You are being *located*. You are a variable in a system that existed long before you arrived and will persist long after you leave, and your presence has been factored in with the ease of a river accommodating a stone.

## The Flyers

Above the spore-haze, where visibility degrades into suggestion, things fly.

That is the entirety of what is known. They are seen as silhouettes against the sourceless glow that filters through the canopy — shapes with wingspans so large they cannot be estimated, cutting across the basin with a mechanical patience that implies they are not hunting, not migrating, not performing any biological function that can be named. They move in straight lines. They bank at angles that seem to violate the physics of any wing, any membrane, any aerodynamic system terrestrial or otherwise. They appear, cross your field of vision with agonizing slowness, and disappear into the haze to the north or south without landing, without altering their trajectory, without acknowledgment that you are watching.

There are theories. That they are the mature form of the parametric callers. That they are the vathines' dispersal mechanism — seeds carried on thermals generated by the basin's resonance. That they are something else entirely, something that uses the sky the way the grazers use the chalk and the callers use the air, a substrate that is being metabolized by a process too large to see from within.

You cannot estimate their number. You see one, or you hear the displacement of air that suggests one has passed overhead minutes ago, but there is never a second. They are solitary, or they are so widely spaced that solitariness is meaningless. They are the highest-order predator in a system that does not recognize predation. They are the proof that the Splay extends vertically as well as horizontally, that there are strata of life stacked like the sedimentary chalk beneath your feet, each layer unaware of the one above or below, all of them contributing to the roar.

## The Builders

And then — the structures.

They are not alive. They are not dead. The distinction has become irrelevant.

Who built them — or grew them, or summoned them — is unknown. The structures pre-date the oldest records of expeditions to the basin, and the few attempts to date their materials have returned contradictory results: the fused vathine-root bases suggest organic growth over centuries, while the dark metalite of the subterranean ramp shows isotopic signatures consistent with geological formation, as though the metalite *was* the bedrock and someone — or something — simply carved a passage downward.

One structure has hexagonal plates that rotate independently, each producing a faint chime that harmonizes with the parametric callers' sequences. It is possible — and this hypothesis has been advanced by the few researchers willing to return to the Splay a second time — that the tower is not a structure *at* the parametric callers' frequency. It is a *response*. It listens and answers. It has been doing so long enough that the callers have incorporated its tones into their sequences, the way a musician incorporates a familiar room's resonance into their playing. The tower and the callers are in dialogue. Neither remembers beginning it.

The garden on the platform — the one with non-native plants arranged in geometric precision — is the most unsettling. The plants are clearly alive. Clearly tended. Leaf patterns that echo no known photosynthetic pathway, flowers that open and close in response to changes in barometric pressure, roots in soil that does not exist naturally in the Splay. Something carries water to them. Something protects them from the grazers, whose paths curve away from the platform at a distance of exactly twelve meters every time. Something — or someone — wanted beauty here. Someone with an aesthetic sense specific enough to arrange petals in Fibonacci spirals and broad enough to import the plants from a biome that may no longer exist anywhere on this continent.

The builders are not absent. They are *distributed*. They are in the structures and the structures are in the landscape and the landscape is in the vathines and the vathines are in the chalk and the chalk is in the rivers and the rivers are in the sound and the sound is everything. You walk through the Splay and you are walking through a mind — vast, distributed, patient, and so alien in its cognition that the question of whether it is *intentional* is less important than the fact that it produces intention as a byproduct, the way a forest produces oxygen without meaning to, without wanting to, without knowing what oxygen is.

The Splay is alive. Not alive the way a forest is alive, not alive the way an ecosystem is alive, but alive the way a thought is alive — persistent, self-modifying, and indifferent to the distinction between organic and constructed, between grown and made, between creature and environment.

Something is moving north, very large, very slowly. It has been moving for longer than you have been listening. It will continue moving after you have gone. It does not know the Splay. It does not need to. It *is* the Splay, walking.
