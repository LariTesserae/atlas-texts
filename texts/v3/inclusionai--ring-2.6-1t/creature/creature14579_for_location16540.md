## The Inhabitants of the Altiplano of Shed Teeth

---

### The People Who Were Already There

No one knows what they called themselves, because they did not call themselves anything. Or if they did, the name was a sound the place absorbed the way it absorbs water — briefly, and then without evidence.

They are still here. Not in the way that implies survival. In the way that implies *continuance*.

They are few. At any given time, there are perhaps forty or fifty of them spread across the plateau's fifty kilometers, clustered in groups of three to eight, separated by distances that would be trivial in a gentler landscape but here amount to journeys of hours through the slot canyons and pillar alleys. They are small — not deliberately, not as a cultural aesthetic, but as a biological response to altitude and caloric scarcity. The tallest of them barely clears five feet. They are narrow-shouldered and long-limbed, with joints that seem to operate on a slightly different mechanical logic than what you expect from a human body: elbows that hyperextend a few degrees past where you think they should stop, ankles that articulate in ways that make their movement over the uneven stone look less like walking and more like *negotiation*. They descend vertical pillar faces with the comfort of someone descending stairs.

Their skin is the color of the stone — not uniformly, but in a way that suggests they spent many generations committing to a very specific palette. Pale tans. Iron reds. The occasional bruise-violet that only shows up when their skin is wet, echoing the wet-stone striations of the plateau. This is not camouflage in any purposeful sense. It is simply what happened. They were here first, and the place has been wearing on them for longer than it has been wearing on anything else.

Their faces are their most striking feature — not for any single element but for the overall effect, which is of *concentration*. Their features are not harsh or angular in the way that "primitive" or "weathered" might suggest. They are precise. The cheekbones sit high and close to the eyes. The noses are short, broad at the nostrils, as though designed to extract maximum warmth and moisture from air that offers almost none. Their eyes are pale — not light in color exactly, but *luminous* in the way that very dark-adapted eyes become luminous when light is scarce. In the flat midday sun they squint almost shut, and their faces become nearly unreadable, reduced to geometry and shadow. In the early morning and late evening, when the plateau is its most alien, they are wide open and dark and still.

They do not build. They *arrange*. Their shelters are configurations of existing stone — a low overhang supplemented by a windbreak of gathered slabs, a slot canyon narrowed further by the careful placement of loose pillars into the gap, a depression on a pillar-top pool widened and deepened by hand over generations until it holds water a day longer than it would have. These modifications are almost invisible unless you know what you are looking for. They are the kind of changes that a geologist might attribute to erosion, which is exactly the point.

They speak, but not often, and not in any way that prioritizes information. Their vocalizations are tonal, melodic, and almost entirely without what a linguist would call semantic content — or rather, the semantic content is so diffuse and contextual that it cannot be separated from the act of speaking itself. A sound made while watching the *sweats* begin is different from the same sound made while watching a raptor descend, but the difference is in the intention, the breath, the posture of the speaker, not in the phoneme. They communicate as the plateau communicates: through texture, timing, and the accumulation of small, almost-meaningless signals that together form a picture too large to hold in a single sentence.

They have a relationship with water that is not quite spiritual but would be difficult to distinguish from one. They track the *sweats* with an attentiveness that borders on devotion — knowing, by the pressure of the air and the faint smell of sulfur that precedes condensation, when a weeping wall is about to begin. They drink from the ephemeral pools, yes, but they also *tend* them. They will clear debris from a depression on a pillar-top, deepen a channel that is diverting water away from a crevice where lichen grows. They do not consider this stewardship. They consider this *participation*. They see no distinction between themselves and the mechanisms of the plateau. They are another expression of the same process that makes sandstone weep and lichen hold and pillars fall.

They bury their dead in the slot canyons, pressed deep into crevices where the stone is narrowest. No ceremony that an outsider would recognize as such. The body is placed. The stone is returned. The canyon is left to do what it was already doing — slowly, inevitably, carrying things downward.

### The Later Ones

The ones who came later are a different species of inhabitant entirely. Not biologically — they are human in the broader sense, though changed — but *temporally*. They exist in a different relationship with the plateau, one defined not by convergence but by imposition.

They arrived with the industry. Some of them were the industry. Surveyors, engineers, laborers — the sort of people whose names appear on payrolls that have long since been archived or destroyed. They came because the plateau offered something extractable: a mineral deposit, a strategic elevation, a reason to justify the cost of reaching three thousand meters with equipment and rail and cable. They built the three-walled shelters and the generator rooms and the cable systems that now sing their high, thin songs above the pillar alleys.

But the plateau did not accept them the way it accepted its original inhabitants. It did not wear them slowly. It *rejected* them — not violently, but through the quiet, relentless operation of its own logic. The water that would not stay. The stone that would not hold a bolt. The air that tasted of metal and offered nothing back. The acoustics that made a man hear his own voice coming from behind him and could not be explained by any geometry he had been trained to understand.

The work lasted years — perhaps a decade, perhaps less. Then it stopped. Not because the resource ran out, though it may have. Not because the project was abandoned, though it was. It stopped because the plateau made it clear, in the only language it speaks — the language of erosion, of seepage, of rail lines that end at the edge of a vertical drop with no destination — that some things are not meant to be here.

The ones who built left. The ones who *stayed* are different.

There are a handful of them. Perhaps three, perhaps five. They live in the skeleton of the generator building, in the room whose steel door hangs open at an angle that is not quite a swing and not quite a fall. They have made the concrete mounting pad into a floor, and the dusty bolt holes into shelving. They drink the condensation that collects on the steel ribs of the collapsed roof. They eat what the plateau provides — the wiry grass, the lizards, the pale rodents that emerge at dusk in their quiet, disciplined procession.

They are not the same people who arrived. The altitude has done something to them. Their lungs have expanded and restructured in ways that would be remarkable to a physician. Their blood is thick with red cells. Their skin has begun the slow process of learning the stone's color palette. And their minds — their minds have been reshaped by the acoustics. Living inside a space where sound arrives from unexpected directions, where echoes precede their sources, where the cables overhead vibrate at frequencies that register not as hearing but as *structure* — this has made them into people for whom the boundary between perception and hallucination is a matter of local custom rather than universal law.

They do not speak the way the original inhabitants do, nor do they speak the way you do. They have developed a creole — half language, half adaptation to the plateau's acoustic behavior. They speak in short, clipped phrases, and they pause frequently, waiting for the echo to return before continuing, as though conversation is not an exchange between two people but a negotiation with the space between them. When they listen, they do not look at the speaker. They look at the walls, the pillars, the alleys — the architecture of sound — because for them, the meaning of a sentence lives less in the words than in the way the room receives them.

They are gentle. They are not welcoming. They are the kind of gentle that comes from having been worn smooth — not by cruelty, but by the patient, indifferent operation of a place that simply does not care whether you are comfortable. They will offer you water from a condensation trap they have built from salvaged sheeting. They will show you how to read the *sweats*, how to tell by the pressure behind your eyes and the taste of copper on your tongue whether the stone is about to weep. They will tell you — in their clipped, echo-waiting way — that the boot at the edge of the drop was left by someone who is now part of the plateau, not metaphorically but structurally, that the calcium in the stone has taken what the altitude took from him and that the transaction is, in its way, fair.

They do not know if anyone is looking for them. They do not know if anyone remembers the industry, the extraction, the rail spur that ends mid-intention. They know the plateau. They know the *tick-tick-tick* of the contracting stone at night, the sound like a clock that has forgotten what hour it is. They sleep to it. They dream in its rhythm.

They are not the last people here. The original ones are still here, in their crevices and overhangs, arranging stone, speaking in their tonal, diffuse language, participating in the *sweats*. The two populations do not interact much. They occupy different scales of the same landscape — the originals woven into the geology so thoroughly that separating them from it would be an act of destruction, the later ones perched on the surface of the place like a bird on hot stone, present but not absorbed, waiting for a transaction that has not yet completed.

But sometimes, at dusk, when the rodents emerge and the raptors descend and the cables begin their high, thin singing, you can see one of the later ones standing at the mouth of the generator building, looking out across the packed pillars, and one of the original ones emerging from a slot canyon fifty meters away, and for a moment the two regard each other across the full acoustic strangeness of the plateau — across the sounds that arrive from the wrong direction, across the echoes that come before their sources, across the thin, metallic air that tastes of sulfur and calcium and time.

Neither moves. Neither speaks. The plateau holds them both in its attention the way a sieve holds water — briefly, structurally, without sentiment — and then the light shifts, the *sweats* begin on a distant wall, and both turn back toward the stone they have each, in their very different ways, become.
