## Who Lives Here

---

**First, the people — or what remains of the idea of people.**

You will not find them immediately. You may walk the vine-bridges for hours, run your hand along walls that remember the shape of tools, press your palm against a door arch that scarred itself shut around a purpose no one now recalls — and you will not find them. But they are here. Not as bodies. Not as voices. As something older than either.

They live in the grain.

Look closely at a grown wall, two trunks merged over centuries into a single seamless surface, and you will see it: the grain of the wood, sometimes, forms patterns. Not random. Not the chaotic whorls you'd expect from decades of unconstrained growth. These are *structured*. Spirals that resolve into near-angles. Repeating geometries that fall just short of repetition — close enough to suggest intention, far enough to deny it. They are almost like script. Almost like a language pressed into the body of the tree while the tree was still young and impressionable, still soft enough to hold the memory of a hand pressing against it from the inside.

The people who built here understood something that most builders do not: that a structure is not opposed to the organism that grows around it. That if you are patient enough — if you are patient on the timescale of the wood — the organism will *adopt* the structure, and the structure will become part of the organism, and neither will be the worse for it. They did not build walls. They made *incisions* — shaped the young wood while it was still forming, and the wood healed around the shape and kept it, the way a bone heals around a pin. They did not write on the walls. They *trained* the walls to remember. And the walls remember still, in a language of growth-ring and scar-tissue that no one living can fully read, but that the place itself speaks fluently, in the slow sub-bass groan of a trunk shifting its weight at the edge of perception.

There are signs of habitation. A platform near the waterline — one of the oldest, thick with moss and threaded through with fungal mycelium the color of old bone — still holds the impression of a hearth. Not carved. *Grown.* A ring of slightly darker wood where, centuries ago, someone built a fire on a living surface, and the heat changed the chemistry of the growth around it, and the tree kept that knowledge in its rings like a scar that refuses to fade. You can still feel the warmth if you press your hand flat against it. Not real warmth. Memory of warmth. The wood's memory of warmth.

And there are the walkways. The vine-bridges between the great trunks, still intact, still swaying faintly, still strong. Some of them are so old that the original vines have been replaced by their own descendants — the originals seeded, died, rotted, and the growth-habit they imprinted on the space between two trunks continues as if the vines themselves remember the architecture. Every year, new growth fills the old pattern. Every year, the bridge rebuilds itself from its own decay.

No one built these things for permanence. They built them for *participation.* They knew the forest would take everything they offered and make it its own, and they trusted that process, and they were right.

So: the people are here. In the walls. In the walkways. In the way a root-platform, when you step on it, gives not like dead wood but like something that *notices* you — flexing at a speed too slow to see but real enough to feel in the soles of your feet, a kind of proprioceptive awareness distributed across centuries of growth. You cannot say whether this is mechanical or intentional. You cannot say where the architecture ends and the inhabitant begins. Maybe they never could.

---

**Then the arboreal ones.**

You have seen them already — or rather, you have seen *evidence* of them. A branch that shifts when nothing touches it. A vine-bridge that sways a moment before the wind reaches it. A small carved object — no, not carved, *grown* — sitting on a platform, shaped like a cup or a bowl, with the grain of the wood following contours no natural growth would produce. Someone made this. Someone with hands. But the wood is still alive, still growing, and the cup is slowly thickening around its own purpose, and in another hundred years it will be indistinguishable from the trunk it sits on.

The arboreal creatures are not human. Do not make that mistake. They are tall — four feet, perhaps five when stretched — and slender in a way that suggests not fragility but an economy of mass, every limb proportioned for a life spent in the canopy. Their limbs are long and articulate, double-jointed at the elbows and knees in a way that lets them drape over branches and vine-bridges with a boneless ease that is deeply unsettling to watch. Their hands have six fingers — or rather, four fingers and two opposable thumbs, one on each side, giving them a grip that is not like holding but like *clamping.* They climb the way water climbs a surface: completely, without hesitation, as if gravity were a suggestion they politely declined.

Their skin is mottled — green-brown and grey-brown, the exact colors of the lichens and wet bark that surround them. This is not camouflage in the way you understand camouflage. It is not a strategy for hiding. It is a *consequence* — generations of creatures living in a place where the visual texture of the body matches the visual texture of the world, and the advantage was not so much in avoiding predators as in *belonging.* They look like the place looks. When a creature sits motionless on a root-platform with its knees drawn up and its long fingers resting on its shins, you look at it and then you do not look at it, the way you do not look at a knot in the wood or a curtain of moss. It is there and it is part of the place and your eye passes over it because the place has taught you to see this way.

Their faces are broad and flat, with wide-set eyes that are very large — dark, wet, reflective — and set so far apart that their binocular vision, if it functions the way binocular vision usually does, must give them a perception of depth so acute it borders on a different sense entirely. They see the world in overlapping fields, the way a person hears stereo sound. They do not blink. Their nostrils are high on the face and flared, perpetually damp. They make sounds — soft, clicking, vowel-less sounds that travel through the air the way the reflections off the water travel, doubled and warm. You hear them above you and you cannot tell if they are two creatures communicating or one creature thinking out loud.

They are not hostile. They are not friendly. They are *indifferent* in the way that something deeply established is indifferent to something newly arrived. You are a novelty. You will pass. They have seen shapes come and go in the channels — fish schools, amphibians, falling trees, flood surges — and you are another shape, and you will be absorbed into the background the way everything is eventually absorbed into the background here. But they watch. They always watch. Not with suspicion. With the patient, unblinking attention of something that has never had the luxury of not paying attention.

They build, too. But they build the way the forest builds — by training, by waiting, by knowing which vine will thicken and which branch will bear weight and which root, if guided now, will become a bridge in forty years. They work on timescales that make the human sense of project and completion meaningless. A structure begun by a young arboreal creature may not reach usability until that creature is old. And they are old for a long time. You do not know their lifespan. You suspect it is measured in the same units as the trees.

---

**Then the ones in the water.**

The fish move in schools through the flooded root-systems like a single organism — hundreds of iridescent bodies turning in unison, flashing silver and blue-green, the light catching their flanks in synchronized bursts that ripple through the school like a signal. They are not fleeing from you. They are *noting* you. Their movements are too coordinated, too synchronized, to be mere flight. There is an intelligence in the way they turn — not the intelligence of a single mind, but the emergent intelligence of a system, each fish responding to the movements of its nearest neighbors with a precision that feels algorithmic. They move through the root-tangles like thoughts through a mind, following pathways that are not random and not planned but *habitual*, worn into instinct by generations of navigating the same channels.

The amphibians are still. They sit on the root-platforms in the shallows, mottled green-brown, the exact color of the algae that grows on submerged wood, and they do not move. They do not blink. They are so still that you begin to doubt they are alive — until one of them vanishes. Not quickly. *Completely.* One moment it is there, a shape on a root. The next moment there is a ripple and the shape is gone, as if the water had simply absorbed it. They can do this — change the texture and color of their skin to match whatever surface they rest on, and do it so quickly that the transition is invisible to the eye. They are not hiding. They are *being where they are,* so completely that the distinction between body and environment dissolves.

---

**Then the small ones — the insects, the mosses, the fungal threads.**

The dragonflies are the size of your hand, with wings that hold the light and split it into color — amber, violet, green — hovering over the channels with a stillness that is mechanical in its precision. They do not hover the way hummingbirds hover, darting and correcting. They hover the way a held breath hovers — absolutely, as if movement itself has paused around them and they are the still point. Their eyes are enormous, faceted, covering most of the head, and they track you with a mechanical indifference that suggests they see not a person but a pattern — a heat signature, a disruption in the air.

The beetles move along the trunks in deliberate, unhurried arcs, their obsidian shells polished to a mirror finish. They are the color of deep water and they catch no light — they *absorb* it, so that a beetle climbing a trunk looks like a hole in the wood, a small absence moving upward with patient, six-legged certainty.

The mosses are everywhere. They hang from every surface in pale curtains, thick as tapestries, and they are not passive. They grow with a directionality that suggests preference — thicker on the north-facing surfaces where the light is softest, thinner where the canopy lets a shaft of light through, responding to the shifting geometry of the canopy with a patience that makes the human sense of slow seem frantic. They are the color of bone. They smell of rain.

And beneath it all — literally beneath it, in the submerged soil, in the root-tangles, in the waterlogged wood — the fungal networks. You do not see them. You *sense* them. The way the platforms give when you step on them — not like rot, but like responsiveness — is the mycelial threads beneath, holding the soil together, threading through the drowned roots, connecting one tree to another in a web so vast and so ancient that it may constitute the truest architecture of the place. The oldest trees are not individual organisms. They are *nodes* in a fungal system that spans the entire grove, and the communication that passes through that system — chemical, electrical, enacted over hours and days — constitutes a form of awareness that has nothing to do with brains and everything to do with *presence.* The grove knows you are here. Not because it sees you. Because it feels your weight and your warmth and the particular disruption your presence makes in the chemical gradient of the air, and it has felt ten thousand such disruptions before, and it will feel ten thousand after, and to it you are not insignificant and you are not significant — you are *present,* the way a raindrop is present on a leaf, and it knows you in the way it knows everything that touches it, which is to say completely and without emphasis.

---

**And the birds.**

The birds are the sound of the place. Not a part of it — the *sound* of it. They layer their calls in densities that become texture: the high piping of the small insect-eaters like a crystalline static, the booming of the larger birds like a heartbeat under everything, and between and beneath and through all of it a constant, murmuring chatter that fills the space the way water fills a channel. They nest in the canopy, in the bromeliad-pools and the crooks of grown walls, and their nests are woven from the same materials the place is made of — vine, leaf, moss, spider-silk — so that the nests are not *in* the canopy but *of* it. You cannot find a bird's nest here without realizing that the nest is simply a thickening of the architecture, a place where the forest's own materials have been concentrated into a purpose that is both the bird's and the tree's.

Everything here belongs to everything else. The amphibians eat the insects. The fish eat the amphibians' young. The birds eat the fish that leap. The fungal networks feed the roots that feed the canopy that feeds the birds that drop the seeds that feed the trees that grow the walls that shelter the arboreal ones who tend the vines that become the bridges that connect the platforms that hold the creatures that die and rot and feed the fungi that feed the roots again. There is no outside here. There is no edge. There is only this single, vast, slow, turning system — alive, half-drowned, patient beyond any patience you have ever known — and it is full. Full of life. Full of lives. Full of lives that are so intertwined with the place itself that to ask "who lives here" is to misunderstand the question entirely.

*Here* lives here. *Here* is the thing that lives here. And it has been living here so long that it has forgotten where it ends and the grove begins.
