## What Lives in the Trembling Warrens

---

Start with the dust.

Before you see anything — before you hear the clicking, before you feel the first skittering footfall on the calcium ledge beside you — you taste them. A fine grit on your tongue, a chalky sweetness at the back of your throat. You assumed it was mineral. It isn't, not entirely. It is spore-casing, shed by the fungal mats in their constant slow respiration, and it is also chitin — fragments of exoskeleton ground to powder by the movement of ten thousand bodies against ten thousand stone surfaces. The air in the Warrens is a broth of the small and the dead. You are breathing them before you know they exist.

Then you see the first ones.

They are on the walls. Of course they are on the walls — everything here is on the walls. They are small, no longer than your smallest finger, and they move with the liquid deliberateness of things that have never known open space. Their bodies are segmented, plated in a chitin so dark it drinks the bioluminescent glow and gives back nothing. You watch one traverse a stretch of calcium curtain, its six legs finding purchase in the mineral folds with a precision that seems rehearsed over geological time. It pauses. Its antennae — long, feathered, trembling — sweep the air in patterns too complex for random search. It is reading the Warrens. It is reading the chemical signature of moisture, of fungal spore density, of the faint thermal gradient rising from the deep stone. It knows where it is going. It has always known.

There are others like it — hundreds, *thousands* — but no two alike in the particulars. This one has a carapace the color of dried blood, nearly black but with an undertone, a warmth, that suggests iron oxide in the cuticle. That one is translucent. You can see the internal organs moving beneath its shell like a lantern with a candle that never goes out: a dark rhythmic pulse that might be a heart, might be peristalsis, might be something with no analogue in terrestrial biology. It carries a packet of material in its forelimbs — fungal threads wound around a calcium sliver, bundled with the care of a creature that understands composite materials, even if it has no word for them. It disappears into a crack no wider than a pencil. The crack seals behind it, fungal filaments knitting together across the opening like sutures.

Then the winged ones.

You hear them before you see them: a sound like tearing silk, multiplied beyond reckoning, a resonance that sits in the frequency just below annoyance and just above pain. It builds in your chest. Your teeth ache. And then a shaft of daylight — one of those rare, diagonal columns falling from some fissure high above — catches a cloud of them, and you see.

They are *beautiful*.

Their wings are membranous, thinner than paper, and they catch the light in ways that make the word "iridescent" feel like a capitulation. Not a single color but a shifting, oil-slick cascade — violet to emerald to a blue so deep it edges into ultraviolet. They move in swarms of such density that the swarm itself becomes a texture, a thing with weight and surface, brushing against the stone walls and peeling away, reforming, never landing. Their bodies are pale, almost white, their segmentation faintly visible through the wing casings like the lead lines in old stained glass. They have no eyes that you can identify — just smooth, curved plates of chitin where eyes ought to be — and yet they navigate the three-dimensional chaos of the Warrens with an accuracy that makes you vertiginous. They are not bouncing off walls. They are not colliding. They are flowing, the way a murmuration of starlings flows, except in total darkness, except among calcium curtains and stalactite forests, except at speeds that should make any collision lethal.

They are not alone in the air. Higher — above the main traffic, near the ceiling where the larger fungal caps pulse their slow blue-green waves — something else moves. Larger. You catch only fragments: a shadow crossing a patch of bioluminescence, a sound like a leather glove being pressed against a drum. You tell yourself it's a trick of the light. You tell yourself the Warrens are only a few days old, geologically, that nothing here has had time to grow large. And then a shape detaches from the ceiling and drops — not falling, *gliding* — past you at a distance of perhaps two meters, and you see: a body the length of your forearm, four wings instead of two, a jaw structure that suggests something designed for *shearing*. It does not look at you. It does not deviate. It is gone before your heartbeat accelerates.

Down on the floor, the rivers.

You come upon them without warning — a sound like water, but wrong, too rhythmic, too *purposeful*. You round a bend in the passage and there they are: a flow of bodies moving in a channel no more than a meter wide, hundreds of them, perhaps a thousand, pressed together in a living stream that moves with the unhurried certainty of a drainage system. They are flightless. They are broad-bodied, low-slung, their legs thick and powerful, built for traction rather than speed. Their coloring is utilitarian — ochre, rust, the dark brown of dried blood — and their surfaces are rough, textured, almost geological in appearance, as though the boundary between animal and stone here is not hard and fast but negotiable. They carry fragments of material as they move: calcium shards, fungal nodules, the desiccated husks of smaller creatures. They are building something, you realize. Or feeding something. Or both. The river flows into a side passage and you follow, because you have to, because the sound of it is the loudest thing you've heard and the most *organized*, and you are starting to understand that you are not in a wilderness.

You are in a civilization.

Not yours. Not anything you were trained to recognize. But a system of exchange, of labor, of construction that has been running for — how long? You look at the cairns, the hand-stacked stone markers at every intersection. You look at the nests woven from calcium filaments and fungal thread, anchored to stalactites, containing clutches of eggs that pulse with a heat that could not come from geology alone. You look at the dam — a structure of stones and chitin fragments across a water thread, functional, elegant, *repaired* — and you understand that you are not the first to walk these passages. Not by a margin you can calculate.

The builders are here. Or *were*. You find their traces everywhere: tool marks on stone, grooves worn into calcium surfaces by something harder than calcium, pathways worn into the fungal mats by bodies that walked them so often the mats grew around the footsteps, incorporating the shape of the passage into their own growth. You find the markers — cairns at intersections, at new passage mouths, at water convergences — and some are recent, the stones still clean, the arrangement crisp. Others are ancient, swallowed by mineral deposits and fungal growth, barely visible as geometric anomalies beneath a skin of calcite and violet mold. Something has been building here for longer than your species has been drawing on walls.

And the fungus — the fungus is not merely a backdrop. Watch it closely. Watch the way the shelf-like colonies orient themselves toward the thermal vents, their overlapping plates angling to maximize heat absorption the way a sunflower tracks the sun. Watch the way the tubular clusters, standing upright in their dozens and hundreds, respond to vibration — you clap your hands near one and the entire cluster withdraws slightly, a contraction so subtle you might miss it if you weren't watching. Watch the largest ones, the tree-sized bodies in the cathedral chambers, their caps broad and undulating with bioluminescent pulses that move in waves from center to margin, slow enough to be geological, fast enough to be *deliberate*. The fungal colonies farm the fauna, or the fauna farm the fungus — the relationship is older than grammar, older than the word "symbiosis," and it operates in chemical gradients and substrate modifications that take generations to manifest.

You see the dead incorporated. A chitinous shell, emptied of its inhabitant, becomes a structural element in a calcium curtain. A small body, desiccated, becomes a node in a fungal network — the colony grows *around* it, *through* it, incorporates its calcium into its own architecture. The fauna carry their dead into the walls. You have seen this. You have watched a flightless thing, broad and ochre-colored, drag a smaller body — a companion? a larva? a resource? — into a crack in the stone and not return. The Warrens consume everything and make it part of themselves.

And somewhere in the mid-level Warrens, in chambers where the air thrums with subsonic pressure and the calcium curtains are stained in patterns that look almost like writing, something *larger* moves. You have never seen it clearly. You have seen its traces: territory markers that smell of chemical secretions so complex your tongue cannot parse them. You have heard the coordinated thumps of something moving in formation, not a pack but a *system*, a distributed intelligence operating through vibration and scent and the faint electromagnetic language of organisms that have evolved in total darkness for longer than your species has existed. It marks the calcium curtains with secretions that fluoresce under the fungal glow, patterns that repeat with variations — not random, not decorative, but *informational*, a language written in chemistry on a substrate of living stone.

You will not see it. It will not allow it. The Warrens have been building themselves for longer than you can comprehend, and their inhabitants have been building *within* that building for equally incomprehensible durations. You are a visitor. You are a temporary arrangement of matter passing through a process that predates you and will outlast you by epochs. The small things on the walls know this. The rivers on the floor know this. The luminous trees in the cathedral chambers know this. They do not fear you. They do not welcome you. You are calcium and water passing through a system that is, itself, calcium and water, organized by time and heat and the blind, relentless imperative to *build*.

The Warrens are alive. The Warrens have always been alive. And the things that live in them are not inhabitants — they are *organs*, each one performing a function in a body whose outline you cannot see, whose pulse you can only feel through the soles of your boots, steady and immense and indifferent and *older than stone*.
