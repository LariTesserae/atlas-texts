## Inhabitants of the Bowl

---

**The frogs do not sing. They radiate.**

They sit — packed shoulder to shoulder on the warmest stones, the ones that exhale groundwater at a steady four or five degrees above the basin's ambient chill — with their eyes closed, or what passes for closed in a creature whose eyelids are merely a thin membrane drawn halfway over a pupil that has already stopped attending to light. Their skin is not skin in the way you would recognize it. It is a slow exchange, a negotiation between body and mineral. The mottled grey-green of them is not pigment so much as *accumulation*: trace metals, calcium carbonate, iron oxide — all of it drawn from the stone they press their bellies against, incorporated so gradually that to watch one for an hour is to see nothing, but to return a week later is to notice that the stone has left its signature on them, and they have left theirs on it. They are not sitting on the rock. They are *completing* it.

When one moves — and it is always sudden, a single liquid lunge that the eye almost misses because the body seems to blur and reassemble a few centimeters away — the others do not react. There is no startle response, no territorial display. They have no predators here. Nothing comes down the walls. Nothing from above has found the way in. The basin has been sealed acoustically, optically, chemically, and the frogs have been sealed with it, and over enough generations — perhaps fifty, perhaps two hundred — something has been lost. Not complexity. *Redundancy.* The circuitry that once allowed for alarm, for competition, for the theatrical puffing of throat sacs in the service of reproduction has been quietly bypassed. They breed in the warm outflows, eggs deposited in the mineral-slick crevices where the water runs at a constant seventeen degrees. The tadpoles never see sunlight. They do not need to. They feed on the microbial suspension that thickens every pool, and they grow into frogs that never need to hear a single call, and the silence in which they live is not an absence but a *pressure*, like deep water, like stone.

---

**The water holds a second sky below its surface.**

Below the first few centimeters, visibility ends. Not gradually — not the way light dims in a lake until you reach a uniform dark. It *stops*. The microbial load is so dense that the water becomes a medium, not a transparency, and within it entire civilizations of organism go about work that has no name in any language spoken on the surface. The caddisfly larvae build. They are the basin's architects, the only creatures here whose labor is visible and cumulative. Their cases — assembled from grains of dark sand, flakes of mineral crust, the occasional chip of chalcedony no larger than a letter on a printed page — are carried on their soft bellies as they graze the rock face, and when one dies, its case remains, and another larva may inherit it, lengthen it, rebuild the anterior end, so that a single case becomes a palimpsest, a record of dozens of lives edited into one small portable shelter. The cases cluster in the slower channels like a city built by tenants who never met each other but agreed, without discussion, on the same materials.

The diving beetles move through the green opacity with a purpose that seems unreasonable. They are too large for this space, too armored, too deliberate in their oar-stroke locomotion, as if they were designed for open water — a lake, a slow river — and arrived here by accident and simply refused to adapt. Their carapace is iridescent blue-black, and where the mist-light catches it at the surface it flashes with a cold, mineral fire. They predate the larvae. They predate anything they can subdue. They are the basin's apex creature, and they patrol the pools with a slow, circling authority that suggests they understand the space in ways the others do not — not visually, perhaps, but through pressure gradients, through the vibration of water against rock, through some sixth sense tuned to the basin's constant acoustic pulse. They do not surface often. When they do, a bead of water clings to the tip of each elytron and holds a tiny, inverted image of the mist ceiling: a drowned sky in miniature.

---

**The spiders are the basin's cartographers.**

They build where the updrafts from the mineral seeps warm the air enough to carry their silk upward in a gentle convection, and these vertical columns — barely visible, shimmering in the fog — are the basin's only vertical dimension, its only architecture that extends upward rather than along the horizontal plane of rock and moss. The webs are small, no wider than a cupped hand, but they are *perfect*: radial threads placed with a regularity that approaches mathematics, spiral threads beaded with condensation so uniform that each web, seen from a few centimeters away, looks like a net cast into fog and hauled back with the catch still in it — the fog itself, held.

The spiders are pale. Translucent. You can see the shadow of their gut through the cephalothorax, darkened by whatever microbial matter their prey has carried into the silk. They are patient in a way that surface spiders are not. A web near a warm seep may hold the same spider for weeks, unmoving, fed by the steady drift of tiny insects that rise on the updraft and become entangled. They do not repair so much as *accrete*: old threads softened by moisture are not replaced but incorporated, layered over with new silk so that a mature web, examined closely, has a geological quality — strata of silk and mineral deposit, a record of the air it has filtered.

---

**The mosses are the oldest residents. They were here before the basin was a basin.**

Or rather — they were here when the basin was only a crack, a fracture in the young limestone, and water was already finding its way down. They are segmented, pale, and they grow upward with a slowness that gives them the appearance of miniature trees viewed from a great distance. Each filament is tipped with a bead of condensed water that bows the tip toward the ground in a permanent, gentle arc, so that the moss looks like it is mid-bow, mid-curtsy, performing a ceremony for no one. They drink from the air. They drink from the rock. They are the basin's primary interface between stone and atmosphere, and without them the erosion would proceed differently, the moisture cycle would stall, the microbial mats would have no substrate. They are not decorative. They are *load-bearing*.

The liverwests are newer. Electric chartreuse, almost aggressive in their newness, they spread across surfaces that the mosses have roughened and wetted, forming a second skin that, when backlit by the rare shaft of light penetrating the mist, glows with a color that has no place in a world of grey and sage. They are the basin's youth — fast-growing, opportunistic, metabolically hungry — and they will, given time, darken and settle into the palette of their elders. But not yet. Not for a while yet.

---

**The bacteria paint.**

This is the only honest way to say it. Wherever the mineral seeps breach the surface — and they breach unpredictably, along fresh fractures, along stress lines that may have opened only hours before — the chemotrophic bacteria are already there, or arrive within days, and they begin to *stain*. Ochre. Verdigris. Burnt orange. Black. They oxidize the iron in the water, they precipitate manganese, they reduce sulfates, and the byproducts of their metabolism accrete on the rock in layers so thin and so vivid that they look applied by hand. They are the basin's painters, its sign-makers. They indicate where the warm water is rising. They trace the hidden architecture of the subsurface flow. And they are alive — each patch a colony billions deep, each colony a metabolism so specific to its particular mineral cocktail that no two patches are quite the same color, even when they sit side by side on the same outcrop.

They have no predators. Nothing here eats them. They are the base, and everything above them — the microbes in the pools, the larvae, the beetles, the frogs — is built on the energy they first pulled from the rock and the water.

---

**The basin breathes.**

This is not metaphor. The warm seeps pulse — subtly, on rhythms that may be barometric, may be tied to shifts in the deep aquifer's pressure, may be something in the rock itself responding to processes too slow and too large for a single human lifetime to register. When the seeps quicken, the mist thickens, the pools warm, the gas bubbles rise and pop at the surface with a sound like static, and the frogs move to the edges of the outflows and the beetles dive deeper and the spiders retreat to the web's hub and wait. When the seeps slow, the mist thins, the temperature drops a degree or two, and the basin becomes quieter, grayer, more mineral-smelling, and the light — what little there is — turns from amber to blue-grey cold iron and stays there until the next pulse arrives.

The basin inhales. The basin exhales. And everything in it — the mosses, the worms no one has described yet (they are in the deeper pools, white, threadlike, pulling nutrition from the microbial mat with a patience that borders on geology), the larvae, the beetles, the frogs, the spiders, the bacteria — everything moves with this rhythm, not because any of them can sense it directly, but because the rhythm *is* the environment, as fundamental as gravity, and to live here is to have been tuned to it so precisely that the distinction between organism and habitat has begun, quietly, to dissolve.

---

You are standing in the center of it. The mist closes. The sound fills. A frog enters the water without a ripple. A bubble of gas rises from a pool you did not know was warm and breaks at the surface with a sound too small to hear but that you feel, briefly, in the bones of your ankle. Above you, through sixty meters of limestone and cloud, the wind is dragging itself across the ridgeline, and it sounds like nothing at all, and it sounds like everything.

The basin does not know you are here.

The basin knows everything is here.
