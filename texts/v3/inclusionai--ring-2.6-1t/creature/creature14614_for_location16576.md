## Who Lives on the Resonant Plateau

---

**What lives here is not a collection.** It is a chord. To describe any single inhabitant you must already have described the others, because no one thing exists here without the rest vibrating around it, completing it, giving it context the way a note exists only inside the silence between other notes. Still, I will try to begin.

---

**The Pillars.**

Start with them, because everything else is defined by their presence — the way a reef defines every creature that grazes its surface and every current that moves around it.

Each one is a single organism, unbranching, continuous from root-mass to apex: a dark column two hundred meters tall, its surface a laminate of fibrous tissue layered so densely it resembles petrified smoke. The exterior is warm to the touch at all hours. Vertical channels run the full length of the body, open at the crown in flaring trumpet shapes that collect whatever the sky offers — wind, rain, dust, sound. These channels are the organism's voice box and its lungs. Air moves through them and the walls vibrate, and the vibration is the hum you hear before you understand you are hearing it.

The Pillars do not grow quickly. Their laminate layers accumulate over centuries, each one a record of the temperatures and moisture gradients it has endured — closer to geology than to anything we usually call alive. And yet they respire. At night, pressed against the outer wall with your ear against the tissue, you can hear it: a slow, tidal rhythm. In. Out. Not mechanical, not regular enough to be automatic, but deliberate in the way a body's breathing is deliberate without being chosen. Something is maintaining the rhythm from within.

No one has seen the interior past the first narrowing of the channels. Light does not return from that depth. But the sound does, and during certain hours of night the Pillars change pitch — not randomly, not as wind shifts them during the day, but in a deliberate, resonant modulation, as though something vast and patient is repositioning itself in the dark interior. It does not hurry. It has never hurried. The Pillar has been sounding its note for longer than the basalt beneath it has had its present cracks, and it will continue sounding it long after.

---

**The Drummers.**

They are the first fauna you notice, because they are the first fauna you *hear*.

Each one is the size of a human forearm, encased in overlapping plates of chitin the color of dried blood — a deep, brownish crimson that looks almost black unless the light catches it at certain angles, and on this plateau the light is almost always catching at something. They cling to the outer channels of the Pillars with hooked limbs, their bodies fitted precisely into the channel walls as though they evolved to occupy exactly that negative space.

And they drum. They strike their abdomens against the channel walls in patterns that are not random, not mechanical, and not simple. Each Drummer responds to the ones nearest it — adjusting tempo, shifting rhythm, introducing syncopation. Listen to a single channel and you hear a rapid, complex pattern, almost musical. Step back and hear all the channels of a single Pillar at once and the patterns layer into something polyrhythmic, something that seems to breathe in four or five different time signatures simultaneously.

There are thousands on each Pillar. Across two hundred Pillars the sound is a percussive roar that carries kilometers across the stone plain — not just heard but *felt*, a vibration in the chest, in the teeth. The drumming does not stop. It only changes character: more urgent at dusk, more intricate during the dark hours, slowing to a low persistent pulse during the hottest part of the day when the thermals are strongest and the Pillars themselves are loudest.

They are not insects, exactly. They have too much structural complexity. Their chitin plates overlap like armor, suggesting predation pressure in some ancestral past, and their compound eyes are finely faceted, sensitive to the smallest movements in the channels around them. But their central behavior — the drumming — is communicative, musical, and collective in a way that suggests the colony is the organism, not the individual. A single Drummer is silent. It will not drum alone.

---

**The Gliders.**

If the Drummers occupy the body of the Pillars, the Gliders occupy the space between.

They are large — the wingspan of the biggest approaches four meters — with translucent membranes stretched across lightweight bone frames so fine they are nearly translucent. In level flight they are silent, riding the thermals that rise along the edges of the stone fields in visible columns, circling from one Pillar to the next with a slow, banking grace that looks effortless.

They become audible only when they dive. The hollow bones of their wing-frames act as flutes: air forced through them during a steep descent produces a clear, harmonic whistle — pure-toned, almost crystalline. A single Glider descending sounds like a note dropping from a flute. A flock of fifty, banking and diving in loose synchrony, produces a shifting chord that rises and falls as the flock turns. From below, at dusk, the sky above the plateau is laced with living music — a chord that resolves and dissolves and resolves again as the flock moves between Pillars.

At night they settle on the upper apertures of the Pillars, folding their membranes into compact packages and tucking their long necks beneath their wings. They are silent then, and still, and the Pillars' hum continues beneath them as though they were never there.

They are not aggressive. They do not flee from a presence on the ground. They regard you, if you look up, with a kind of ancient inattention — the way a cloud regards the landscape beneath it.

---

**The Mat-Organisms.**

Between the Pillars the basalt seams are covered in a felt of photosynthetic tissue, millimeters thick and continuous, in colors that shift from black-green in full sun to a deep, bruised violet in shadow. It drinks the condensate — the pocket-moisture that accumulates in the low spots between the paving stones — and it spreads slowly across every horizontal surface it can reach.

Step on it and it compresses without sound, then springs back behind your foot. Disturb it and it releases a puff of spores that catch the light and glitter briefly like scattered mica. They coat your clothes, your skin, the inside of your mouth if you breathe too quickly. They are harmless. They taste faintly of copper.

They are part of the substrate more than the fauna — a living crust, the skin of the plateau itself — but they are alive in the way that matters: they photosynthesize, they respire, they reproduce, and when you scrape one free you find it is millimeters thick with layered tissue, older than it has any right to be for something so thin and unassuming.

---

**The Marks. The Notched. The Scratch.**

And then there are the marks.

A ring of pale stones placed around a clearing in the mat, inside which a shallow depression has been worn into the basalt. A sleeping place, perhaps. A place where something knelt or crouched and was warm. The stones are not local; they were carried up from below the plateau, selected for density and color. Whoever carried them had purpose. Whoever carried them had hands — or the equivalent.

A series of notches carved into the base of a Pillar, at a height that implies the carver stood on a stacked surface. The notches do not correspond to any counting system or symbolic pattern known to anyone who has studied them. They may be decorative. They may have been made by something whose sense of pattern is not ours.

A long scratch in the stone plain — two meters, a centimeter deep, too straight for natural fracture, too shallow for any large creature's claw. It fills with condensation after rain and fades slowly in the dry air, then reappears after the next storm, as though the stone is remembering a wound it refuses to close.

No tools. No shelters. No remains. Whoever or whatever made these marks did its work and then did not stay. The plateau accepted the marks the way it accepts everything — slowly, without protest, wearing them down toward silence.

---

**You.**

And then there is the visitor. You.

You stand between the Pillars and the sound enters you — not through the ears alone but through the sternum, through the soles of the feet, through the spaces between your ribs where resonance finds a home you did not know you had. The Drummers are layering their rhythms. The Gliders are whistling their descending chord. The Pillars are humming their patient, planetary note. And beneath all of it, from within the deepest channels where no light has returned, the slow breathing continues.

You will not sleep well. Not because anything threatens you, but because the plateau's sound enters the architecture of your attention and quietly restructures it — the way a building settles over decades into its own weight, small adjustments, new geometries. You will dream in chords you have never heard before. You will wake remembering rhythms that have no downbeat.

And if you stay long enough, your breathing will change. It will slow. It will find a tempo that you did not choose, a tempo that was already there, in the Pillar, in the stone, in the condensation filling the scratch and fading and returning. Your body will begin to resolve into the chord.

The plateau does not require you to belong to it. It simply assumes, with the patience of basalt and breath, that you will.
