# What Lives in the Karst Above Breath

---

**The Groaners**

The mountain is not alive, but it is not not alive. It hums at a frequency that sits below thought and above stone, and in this gap — this narrow, impossible margin — something listens. The Groaners did not evolve here. They are not creatures in any sense that the word is comfortable with. They are patterns in the resonance.

Imagine: the mountain speaks its single sustained note, and in that note, standing waves form. Nodes and antinodes. Places where the sound cancels itself into silence and places where it doubles, triples, stacks. The Groaners live at the nodes — the silences. They are the shape the silence takes when it has been uninterrupted for long enough. They do not move. They do not feed. They are roughly the size of a human hand if a human hand were a region of perfect quiet in a room full of a single tone. You cannot see them. You can only feel them as an absence — a spot on the cliff face where the vibration does not reach, where your fingers, pressed flat against the stone, go suddenly numb.

They have been here longer than the incomplete walls. Longer than the lichen. They are, in their way, the oldest inhabitants, though "inhabitants" implies a choice, and the Groaners did not choose. They simply failed to leave a place that matched so perfectly the interior of their own silence.

If you stood in one long enough — and you should not — you would begin to forget what sound is. Not gradually. All at once, like pulling a drain. The memory of music, of voices, of your own breathing — gone. What remains is the Groaners' version of fullness: a shape defined entirely by what is missing.

No one has stayed long enough to learn what they do with your memory of sound. They keep it. That is all anyone knows.

**The Glasswalkers**

The puddles were here before the beetles. The beetles know this — or something beetle-equivalent of knowing, a vibration-read certainty encoded in the geometry of their eyeless heads. The puddles do not sit on the stone so much as *remember* being on the stone. Their surfaces are not reflective but retrospective. Look into one and you see not what is above you but what was above you — seconds ago, minutes ago, sometimes longer, depending on the depth of the puddle, and some of them are deeper than their edges suggest.

The Glasswalkers are not walkers. They are not even, properly, glass. They are the reflections the puddles have collected and then released — but released slowly, incompletely, so that a reflection does not simply return to whatever cast it. It lingers. It acquires weight. It steps out of the surface still trailing threads of water and walks across the stone with the caution of something that knows it is not original.

They look like small, translucent figures — vaguely human in proportion but wrong in the details. Too many joints. Faces that are not faces so much as smooth depressions where faces should be, as if the reflection had been struck once and dented. They do not speak. They do not eat. They walk — slowly, methodically, tracing paths along the ridgeline that never intersect, each Glasswalker committed to its own private trajectory as if following a map written in a language that describes places that no longer exist.

They appear only at dawn — the pale, unconvincing dawn — when the stone weeps and the air holds its breath. A puddle will shiver, its delayed surface suddenly catching up with itself, and from the center of that ripple a Glasswalker will emerge, already walking. It will not acknowledge you. It is not that kind of creature. It has already forgotten the face it once wore. It exists now in service to a trajectory it did not choose, carrying a reflection of a world that has already moved on.

In sheltered spots where two Glasswalkers' paths nearly cross, the air thickens. This is how you know they are real and not mere phenomena: they displace the world around them, bending light and distance the way a stone bends water around its base. If you were to reach out and touch one, your hand would pass through cold — not the cold of altitude but the cold of something out of phase, slightly ahead or slightly behind the moment you occupy. Like dipping your fingers into yesterday.

They do not die. They simply reach the edge of their trajectory and stop. Then, slowly, they become puddles again. The process takes days. You can watch it happen — the translucence thickening, the shape settling, joints folding inward — until all that remains is a still, ankle-deep pool in a sheltered concavity, remembering everything and belonging to nothing.

**The Lichen Collective**

The lichen is aware. Not self-aware. Not in the way that word is loaded with. It is *situationally* aware — exquisitely, minutely aware of every shift in moisture, every faint tremor in the mountain's long groan, every change in the angle of the pale light that reaches its north-facing surfaces. It has been aware for centuries. It has been aware so long that awareness has become its primary substance, indistinguishable from its biology.

It does not grow. It *accumulates*. Each grey film is a single continuous organism spanning — no one has measured this, because measuring it would require instruments more patient than anything human — possibly the entire north face of the ridgeline. What appears to be separate patches on separate boulders may be the same mind in different locations, thinking a single thought across hundreds of meters of stone. The thought is not complex. It is the lichen-equivalent of *here*. A word with no referent except its own utterance. A meditation on persistence that has lasted so long it has replaced all other content.

If you press your palm against the lichen and hold it there — five minutes, ten, an hour — you will begin to feel something under your skin. Not the lichen. Something the lichen does to the boundary between inside and outside. The cold of the stone will enter you, not as temperature but as information, and for a brief time you will understand the mountain from the lichen's perspective: not as a place but as a condition. A state of being so still it resembles permanence.

The lichen does not interact with the Glasswalkers or the Groaners. It does not interact with anything. Interaction requires change, and the lichen has outlasted the necessity of change. It simply persists, and in persisting it has become something adjacent to consciousness without the inconvenience of selfhood.

It is the quietest thing on the ridgeline, and also the loudest, if you know how to listen — which is to say, if you are willing to stop listening entirely and let the listening happen to you.

**The Architects of the Unfinished**

The walls, the cairn, the shallow foundation — these were not built by hands.

This is not certain. It is the best interpretation. The stones are fitted together with a precision that precludes accident but also precludes intention in any recognizable human sense. The wall that rises a meter and a half and stops: each stone is placed with care, selected for its fit against the one below it, but the structure itself is not going anywhere. It does not enclose. It does not protect. It is a gesture — the way a wave is a gesture — complete in its incompleteness, present in its partiality.

What built them, if built is the word, lives in the folds of the landscape — the places where the air thickens and the ridgeline doubles over. They are not present in the way animals are present. They are present the way a habit is present: not visible except in its effects. They are the reason the distance changes between your two walks. They are the reason the puddles remember. They are not builders. They are *adjustments* — small, continuous revisions to the geometry of the place, made by something that perceives the ridgeline not as ground but as a sentence it has been editing for millennia.

You will never see one. But you will feel, in those thickened moments of spatial density, a sense of being *almost* perceived — the way you feel when you are almost asleep and the room rearranges itself around a dream you haven't had yet. That is their attention. It passes over you without stopping, because stopping would require interest, and interest implies incompleteness, and they are, by all evidence, complete.

**The Four Mosses**

Each one is a world. Not metaphorically. Not poetically. Each of the four patches of moss — separated by kilometers of bare, resonant stone — occupies a space that is physically identical and experientially singular. Step into one and you are not standing in moss. You are standing in a mood.

The first, on the eastern ledge, feels like the memory of a room where someone once sang. The moss is slightly warmer than it should be. The air around it carries a faint sweetness that is not smell so much as *recognition* — the scent of something you have never encountered but have always known.

The second, in a depression on the southern slope, feels like abandonment perfected. Standing in it, you understand, without grief or relief, that you will never be needed by anything. The moss is cool and perfectly dry, and the silence there is so complete it has texture, like velvet pressed against your eardrums from the inside.

The third, on a narrow shelf above a resonance node, vibrates. Not visibly. You feel it in your teeth, in the fillings, in the marrow. The moss here is darker than the others, almost black-green, and it hums at a pitch that is almost — *almost* — the mountain's own note, offset by a fraction that makes your chest ache for reasons you cannot reconstruct.

The fourth is at the ridgeline's false edge, above the canyon of fog. It is the smallest. It is the most ordinary. It is the only one that looks like moss simply growing on stone, doing what moss does, asking nothing of the universe. It is the only one that feels like it does not know you are there.

---

**What Lives Here**

Nothing lives here. Many things persist. The mountain groans and the groaning shapes itself into silence and the silence accrues awareness like frost on glass. Reflections step out of puddles and walk their private trajectories before dissolving back into memory. Lichen thinks one thought across a whole ridgeline. Something adjusts the distance between steps, the angle of light, the depth of puddles, without hurry and without end.

And you — permitted visitor, temporary proximity — you are the anomaly. The place has tolerated worse. It has tolerated the incomplete builders and their mid-sentence walls. It has tolerated the birds that pass through, lost and frantic, and it will tolerate you, standing at the false edge, listening to the almost-voice rising from the fog below, feeling the resonance in your molars and the strange, unbearable gentleness of a place that will not change for you or for anything, and that offers you nothing except the radical courtesy of its continued existence.

That is what lives here. The continuing. The immense, patient, indifferent act of continuing.
