## What Lives in the Sunken Verdance

---

The smallest lives here are the loudest.

The thumbnail frogs — pale green, translucent-backed, no bigger than a thumbnail pressed to the warm stone — are the first to notice you. They do not flee. They inflate their throats in a pulse too quiet to be song, too rhythmic to be alarm. A communication, perhaps, meant for each other but thrown off-course by the stone: the sound bends in the humid air, arrives at your ear from the wrong direction, and by the time you've turned toward it, the frog on the leaf beneath your gaze has gone silent and the sound is coming from behind your knee. They live in colonies of thirty, forty, pressed shoulder to shoulder on the undersides of broad leaves near the water — a congregation of damp green coins, their small throats beating in shifts so that the chorus never fully stops. They are not singing. They are agreeing. *The water is warm. The stone is dry enough. The light is this color again today.*

Below them, the beetles move with the unhurried authority of creatures that have never known a predator larger than the bird that comes no deeper than the gorge's mouth. Their carapaces are not simply iridescent — they are wet-dark, oil-slick, the color of a puddle reflecting a gasoline rainbow at the lip of a parking lot, except here the color is alive and deliberate, a warning or a claim: *I have been here longer than light has had the right color to reach me.* They walk the rock faces in slow patrols, antennae reading the moisture gradient, the mineral signature of the wall, the faint chemical traces left by the millipedes who passed hours or days before. The beetles do not eat the millipedes. The millipedes do not eat the beetles. They share the stone the way tenants share a building — crossing paths in the hallway, never in the kitchen, never in the bedroom.

The millipedes. They are the most beautiful of the small ones, if beauty is the word for something so unhurried it seems to predate the concept of hurry. Mahogany, segmented, each body ring inscribed with a fine longitudinal groove that catches the amber light and holds it like a liquid. They move through the root network as though following a schedule — and perhaps they are, a chemical schedule laid down in pheromone traces so old and so deeply embedded in the stone-smell that they have become part of the air itself. You could, if you pressed your nose to the ground and breathed slowly enough, taste their passage: a faint musk, organic, not unpleasant, like damp cedar. They are as long as your forearm. They have no eyes worth speaking of. They do not need them. The roots are their roads, the stone their compass, and the chemistries of the deep wall are their map.

In the pools — the ones rimmed with that porcelain-thin mineral lace that took four hundred years to build — the catfish rest. Not sleep. Rest. Their bodies are pale, almost translucent in the clear water, and their whiskers — four, six, eight per fish — fan out in slow oscillations that read the water the way the beetles' antennae read the air. They are not hunting. They are not resting. They are *monitoring*. A catfish in a rimstone pool is a living sensor array, its entire body an instrument for measuring the temperature, mineral content, and microbial density of the water it has lived in since before the forest above was a forest. They do not move unless something moves beneath them — a shrimp too close, a current shift, a vibration from footsteps at the pool's edge. When they move, it is with a slow muscular authority that displaces the water just enough to send a shiver across the iridescent surface film.

That film — the iridescence — is not oil. It is alive. A community of photosynthetic microorganisms so thin they are measured in microns, a living skin on the surface of still water that breathes and reproduces and dies and is reborn in the span of a single warm afternoon. They are the reason the pools catch light and break it into colors that don't have names. The greens are not green. The purples are not purple. They are colors that exist only here, in this specific water, under this specific angle of refracted light, and if you blink — if you look away and look back — they have shifted, because the angle of your head has changed, and the light has bent differently through the humid air, and the organisms have drifted a quarter-turn on the surface current of your breath.

The shrimp have translucent bodies. Through them you can see the dark thread of their gut, the pale rhythm of their heart. Their spines are red — not bright, not decorative, but the deep red of iron, of blood held up to dim light. They are the pool's undertakers and maintenance staff, picking at the stone surfaces with meticulous legs, consuming the biofilm, the dead microorganisms, the mineral crusts that flake from the limestone. They clean the pools the way the mosses clean the stone, the way the roots clean the air. Everything here has a custodial function. Nothing is merely alive.

The geckos are the acrobats. They move on vertical stone and upside-down on root masses with the casual precision of creatures for whom gravity is a suggestion politely declined. Their feet are broad, padded, scored with lamellae so fine they can grip a film of water. They eat the beetles — the small ones, the ones that haven't yet grown to full oil-slick grandeur — and in doing so they keep the beetle population in the zone the place prefers. Not too many. Not too few. The geckos are regulators, and they are regulated in return: something in the canopy — something you hear but never see, a shadow passing across a gap of green-gold light, a branch that breaks not by falling but by being *placed* — comes down for the geckos at dusk. You find the remains in the morning: a pressed flower (spat out), a scatter of small bones, a clutch of translucent wing-casings. The gecko's life ends in the canopy's mouth, and its body becomes calcium for the stone, nitrogen for the soil, phosphorus for the roots that hold the walls together.

And then there is the sound that never resolves.

The clicking, far ahead in the gorge, where the walls narrow and the light falls to that deep amber, the color of honey held to a candle flame. Bats, the mind says. Echolocation. The biological sonar of creatures navigating in the absolute dark beyond the light's reach. But the clicks are too rhythmic, too evenly spaced, too deliberate. And they do not change as you move toward them. They do not grow louder. They do not shift frequency. They remain exactly the same: *click, click, click* — patient, metronomic, geological in their regularity. Perhaps it is a species of bat that has been here long enough to develop a call pattern indistinguishable from dripping water. Perhaps it is dripping water, hitting a hollow stone that resonates at exactly the frequency of a bat's call. Perhaps the place itself has learned to mimic its inhabitants, or the inhabitants have learned to mimic the place, and the distinction no longer matters, because here, at this depth, in this warm green-dark cathedral of root and stone and patient water, the difference between a living sound and an environmental one has been negotiated away over centuries, over millennia, until what remains is simply *sound* — the gorge speaking to itself in a language that has no words because it predates the need for them.

The ancient fig is the oldest visible resident. Its trunk is invisible, lost somewhere far above in the canopy that filters into shadow and then into mystery. What you see are the buttress roots — enormous, dark, scored with water-channels filled with pale lichen. They have grown together across a wide section of the gorge and formed an arch that looks like architecture, looks like intention, looks like something that was *decided*. The fig does not know it has built a cathedral. The fig is not trying to build anything. It is reaching for light and anchoring itself against the pull of gravity and the slow erosion of the limestone, and the result of these purely mechanical ambitions is a structure so beautiful that it has become the spiritual center of a world it did not choose to create. The orchids have colonized its bark. The geckos hunt along its roots. The bats — or the sound that might be bats — echolocate through its archway. The fig is not a participant in the ecosystem. The fig *is* the ecosystem, or a major organ of it, and everything else lives in the shadow of its patient reaching.

The night-blooming flower whose perfume descends on the convective currents — you will never see it. It is several stories above you, somewhere in the canopy layer that still exists in truncated form along the gorge's rim. Its scent arrives without warning: a sudden sweetness, heavy and narcotic, that fills your lungs and makes the back of your throat ache with something that is not quite memory and not quite longing. The flower blooms at night because the warmth that rises through the convective rolls is strongest then, and it rides those currents the way a message rides a carrier wave — from the unseen world above down into the world below, where it arrives as fragrance alone, a communication with no content except *I am here* and *something is blooming* and *there is more above you than you will ever reach*.

And the remnants — the carved stone, the ceramic, the dark symmetrical object at the bottom of a pool — they are not alive, but they are not dead either. The moss has grown over them. The roots have gripped them. The lime-rich water has mineralized their surfaces until they are becoming stone themselves. The forest is not destroying what came before. It is *digesting* it, slowly, incorporating it into its own body. The carved object at the pool's bottom may have been placed by human hands. It is now held in place by root and silt and the patient accretion of mineral crust. It has been here so long that it has become a feature of the landscape rather than an artifact in it. The fish rest near it. The shrimp clean its surface. It has been given a function it never had when it was new: it is a landmark, a meeting point, a spawning site, a territorial marker. It means something to this place now that it never meant to whoever made it.

Nothing here is simple. Nothing is only what it appears to be. The gorge is not a cave. It is not a ravine. It is not a garden. It is a space that has been colonized by life so thoroughly and for so long that the space itself has become a kind of organism — a slow metabolizing system in which water, stone, root, air, and creature participate in a single circulatory process that has no center and no edge. The fig's roots hold the walls together, the walls hold the moisture, the moisture holds the life, the life holds the walls, and the holding has been going on for so long that none of it can be separated anymore.

You are not visiting this place. You are passing through a body. And the body — vast, patient, amber-lit, breathing in its slow convective rhythm — is not entirely sure you are anything other than a particularly large and temporary mineral deposit, waiting to be incorporated.
