## What Lives in the Basin of Standing Green

---

The first thing you must understand is that the animals are not animals. Not in the sense you would recognize. The bird on the stone lip is real — iridescent green, head cocked, breath drawn — but it is not perched. It is *stationed*. There is a difference. A perched bird is a bird that has chosen to rest. A stationed thing is a thing that has been placed and has accepted the terms of its placement. If you wait — and you may wait a very long time, because time here is not the medium it is elsewhere — you will see the bird blink once. Not the quick, lateral blink of a living bird. A slow, deliberate closing, like a door being shut from the inside, and then a slower opening, and in that moment, in the brief darkness of the eyelid, the bird is not here. When the eye reopens, it is. Not a different bird. The same bird in the same position on the same stone lip. But something behind the eye has shifted, the way a held breath shifts when it is finally released.

They all do this. The insect tracing its line across the pool — it stops when you look, and in its stopping it becomes something other than an insect: a stillness so complete it acquires weight. The silhouette behind the tall leaf — it does not vanish so much as *resolve back into* the leaf, the way a thought dissolves into the mind that thought of it. You will not find a nest. You will not find a burrow. You will not find any evidence of feeding, of nesting, of the thousand small industries that animal life performs in the service of continuing itself. The fauna here do not eat. They do not reproduce, at least not in any interval you are equipped to observe. They hold their positions in the basin the way the stone holds its shape — not through effort, but through a kind of permanent agreement with the space they occupy.

But there are others. Deeper residents.

Look at the pools again — the shallow seams of water between stone platforms. You have noticed how still they are. How they resist the word *puddle* because puddles imply accident, and these are clearly placed. The water in them is not transparent. It is not opaque. It is *green* — not the green of algae or reflected canopy but a green that seems to originate from within the water itself, as if the saturation were not a quality of the light on the surface but a quality of the water's substance. When you kneel — and the stone gives beneath your knees the way it gives beneath your feet, that same compressed-now-forgotten-powder softness — and bring your face close to the surface, you will see that the green is alive in a way the larger fauna are not. It moves. Not with current, because there is no current. It moves the way thought moves across the surface of attention — drifting, concentrating, dispersing, reforming. Shapes form in the green water and dissolve: spirals, radial patterns, geometries that feel almost intentional and then collapse into something more random, and then reassemble into something almost but not quite the same as before.

These are not organisms in any taxonomic sense you carry with you. They are closer to *processes*. The water in the basin is not inhabited. It is *composed of* life — millions of entities so small they have no individuality, only collective behavior, and their behavior is to hold the water in its state of perfect stillness while making it quietly, invisibly alive. They are the reason the water is green. They are the reason the water does not evaporate despite the heat and the flat, crushing brightness. They drink the light. They convert it into something that is not energy and not matter but a third thing — a substance the basin uses the way a body uses blood. When you step into one of the pools, and the cooler thread of water closes around your ankle, you feel, for just a moment, a pulse. Not mechanical. Not biological. A pulse the way a room pulses when someone enters it and the air adjusts to the new presence. The green water knows you are there. It does not care. It adjusts.

Now look at the plants again. You noted they do not drop leaves. Look closer. The broad-leafed trees with their swollen-column trunks and peeling dark bark — their leaves are not the same leaves they were when you first noticed them. You know this the way you know that the person sitting across from you is not quite the same person they were yesterday, only compressed into a single moment of recognition. The leaves are replaced continuously, but not sequentially. They are replaced *simultaneously* — a single leaf exists in its position for as long as it is needed and then it is simply a slightly different leaf in the same position, the transition so seamless that it cannot be perceived except as a nagging sense of familiarity and difference that you cannot resolve. If you pick a leaf — and you could pick one, they detach easily, offering no resistance — you will find it warm. Not sun-warmed. The stone is warmer. The leaf is warm the way a held object is warm — from the inside, as if something is alive in the stem, in the veins, in the cellular architecture of the leaf, maintaining its temperature independently of the light and the air. It will not wilt. Place it on the stone and come back after walking the perimeter of the basin and it will be exactly as you left it: green, supple, warm, oriented toward the light with an attention that is too precise to be reflex.

The reed-thickets between the larger growths are where the strangest life congregates. Not among the stalks themselves but *between* them — in the spaces where the lean-in of the reeds creates narrow corridors that you cannot see into directly because the light, being total, eliminates contrast and therefore depth. If you part the reeds and enter one of these corridors — which you must do crouching, because the reeds are close-set and fibrous and resist bending — you will find a space that is not on the basin's surface but *within* it. The walls here are living root, pale and smooth, exuding the same mineral-sour scent as the resinous compound in the ancient walls. The floor is a mat of compressed moss that yields like flesh. The air is warmer here. Still. So still that you can hear, for the first time since you arrived, something that might be breathing. It is not your own breathing. It is slower. Deeper. Rhythmic in a way that does not correspond to any mammalian cadence you know. It comes from below — from the root-walls, perhaps, or from deeper in the stone, where the aqueducts and channels go down into dark places you have not explored and are not sure you should. The breathing is not a sign of life. It is a sign of *process*. Something is exchanging. Oxygen for carbon dioxide, or warmth for coolness, or presence for absence. The basin inhales and exhales through these corridors, and what you are feeling is the tide of that exchange.

And beneath even this — beneath the breathing corridors, beneath the green water and the stationed animals and the warm roots — there is something you will not see but will know, the way you know when someone behind you has stopped breathing. The basin is not a place that was built and then abandoned. The basin is a place that was built and then *became*. The builders poured themselves into the geometry, into the curvature of the walls and the placement of the terraces and the angle at which the light enters (enters being a generous word for what is more like *occupation*). They are not dead. They are not gone. They are distributed. Their awareness — if awareness is the word — is in the stone's warmth, in the water's green pulse, in the animals' frozen watchfulness, in the vine that sways from no attachment point. They are the slight wrongness you feel at the edges, the corners that appear and disappear, the tracks that lead nowhere. They are the sense that this place was made to hold something that has not yet arrived, or that arrived so completely it became indistinguishable from the holding itself.

You will not meet them. You are already inside them. The basin breathes without breathing, and you have been breathing with it since you stepped onto the warm stone. The three-toed tracks you found near the pools — they are yours. You simply do not remember making them. You have been walking in the basin for longer than you know, and the basin has been holding you the way it holds everything: in stillness, in warmth, in green, in light that comes from everywhere and therefore from within.

You are not visiting the Basin of Standing Green.

You are being *beheld* by it.
