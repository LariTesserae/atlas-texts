# Who Lives in the Drowned Range

---

**The crew** are six people, give or take — the number shifts as rotations end and new ones begin, as supply slots open and others go unfilled. They are not pioneers. They are not castaways. They are maintenance personnel in the most literal and most brutal sense of the word: they maintain the machinery of their own survival against a climate that is indifferent to them and infrastructure that is slowly digesting itself back into the mountain. They drink the desalination unit's thin, metallic-tasting trickle. They sleep in shifts because the generators don't stop and neither does the motion of the platform — the subtle, nauseating sway that even old hands feel in their ankles after months. They eat from crates that arrive once a month on the cargo barge, supplemented by whatever they can coax from a small hydroponic rack bolted to an interior wall, its grow-lights buzzing a frequency that the insects find first.

They do not talk about why they are there. They talk about the weather, about cable failures, about the fox.

**The fox** has no name anyone agrees on, though the alpha female — that's her designation, the one the marine biologist left behind before she finished her rotation and never came back — has earned something close to recognition. She is lean in the way that island predators become lean: every calorie accounted for, every movement purposeful. Her fur is the color of wet iron and salt. She and her kin swam here — or her kin's kin swam here — decades ago, from a mainland that is now perhaps a hundred kilometers of open water in directions no one has verified. The foxes colonized the lower ledges, the supply depots with their warped doors and their slow-rotting cargo, and they moved upward into the ecology the way water moves upward into cracks in stone. They hunt the petrels at dawn. They pick through waste. They have learned the sound of the generators and do not fear them.

The crew watches her appear at the platform's edge in the grey half-light before sunrise, standing perfectly still, salt crystals beaded in her fur, looking out at the water with an expression that the narrator has decided is not patience but something older — a complete absence of the need to be anywhere other than exactly where she is.

**The birds** are the loudest residents. Cormorants — black, spread-winged, drying themselves on every available flat surface like dark rags hung out to dry. Gulls in their hundreds, perhaps thousands, a shifting, screaming parliament that occupies the ridgelines and the cable anchors and the flat tops of decommissioned instrument housings. Their noise is not occasional. It is structural. It is load-bearing. If you recorded it and played it back in a silent room you would hear that it contains within it every frequency the human ear can process, layered and overlapping, a sound like a argument that has been ongoing so long that the participants have forgotten what it was about and now argue for the texture of arguing itself.

**The petrels** are different. They come at night, threading low over the water in silent arcs, and nest in the lower ledges alongside the rats. They are smaller, darker, less conspicuous, and their call — if you can hear it over the generators — is a thin, descending whinny that sounds like something being slowly unspooled. They are the ones the foxes hunt. The crew hears this at dawn: the brief, sharp silence of a petrel colony interrupted, then resuming, as if the absence of one voice were somehow absorbed by the chorus.

**Under the kelp**, the unseen life. The kelp forests are the architecture of the drowned range — they are the canopy, the root system, the soil. Their stipes are thick as human torsos, braided and furred with smaller growths, and their blades unfurl at the surface in broad, leathery sails that flex with the current. Beneath them the water goes from green to brown to a darkness that is not darkness but opacity — a medium dense with particulate life, with larvae, with the slow pulse of organisms too small to name and too numerous to count. The mid-depths are where the shapes move. Large. Deliberate. The marine biologist said grey nurse sharks. She said large rays. She said it the way you say a prayer you're not sure is being heard — calmly, and with the understanding that the words might not be adequate to what's down there. The shapes pass beneath the platform at forty meters, and you see them as shadows — long, unhurried smears of darkness crossing the light-stained water — and something in your body knows before your mind does that these are not the same category of creature as the fish that dart in the shallows.

**The mussels and barnacles** are the empire of the surfaces. They crust every submerged inch of rock, cable, and anchor chain. The gooseneck barnacles on the lower cables cluster so densely that the infrastructure becomes organism — the cables look grown rather than manufactured, as if the mountain had decided to sprout veins and someone had simply mistaken them for engineering. The mussels open and close in the current, thousands of small, deliberate mouths filtering the green water. They are not charismatic. They are not named by the crew. They are simply there, in numbers so vast that they constitute a geological layer, a living stratum between the stone and the sea.

**The rats** came on the cargo barge, or on one of the earlier supply vessels. They live in the supply depots on the lower ledges, behind warped doors that no one has the motivation to force open. They emerge at dusk, grey and bold, and they scavenge from the platform's edges. The foxes take some of them. The rest breed.

**The insects** come to the platform's lights at night — clouds of them, dense enough to cast shadows. Moths, beetles, things with too many legs and not enough distinguishing features to categorize from a distance of more than a few meters. They attract the night-feeding birds, which in turn attract the foxes to the platform's perimeter, where the two groups negotiate a border in the floodlight — the birds darting in for moths, the fox standing at the edge of the light's reach, calculating.

**And the mountain itself**, if you are inclined to consider it alive — and standing on it long enough, feeling the vibration of the swell through twelve meters of rock and concrete, listening to the deep, arrhythmic sounds that come up through the stone like a language you almost understand, you may become so inclined — the mountain is the oldest resident. Its summit is smooth and scarred, polished by wind and water into curves that feel deliberate, almost tender. The lichen has claimed most of the exposed stone above the waterline: crusts of grey and orange and a near-black green that spreads in patterns resembling frost or river deltas, depending on the season or the angle of light or the willingness of the observer to see pattern where there is only chemistry and time.

No one lives here the way people live in cities or towns. People survive here. They are integrated into the place the way a barnacle is integrated into a hull — attached, dependent, slowly becoming part of the surface they cling to. The fox knows this best. The fox does not resist.

The rest of them — the crew, the birds, the kelp, the unnamed shapes in the green dark — are all, in their own fashion, temporary annotations on a very long sentence. The mountain will outlast the desalination unit. The kelp will outlast the cables. The fox will outlast the foxes' names for things.

The water laps. The generators hum. The birds cry. The fox stands at the edge of the light, looking at the water, and does not move.
