## Who Lives in the Seam Flats

---

You want to know who lives here. That is the wrong question, but it is the only one you have, so you ask it anyway, and the place hears you — not with ears, not with sensors, but with the way a body hears a sound it has forgotten it was waiting for.

They do not have names. They do not have names the way a dog has a name or a river has a name or a god carved in stone has a name. They have *profiles*. They have *orientations*. They have the specific, unrepeatable angle at which a brushed-aluminum surface catches a light source that does not cast shadows, and if you tried to describe that angle to someone who had never been here, you would find that your mouth could form the words but your hands could not make the shapes.

---

### The Small Ones

They come first, because they are closest.

The small ones — the cat-sized ovals — are the most numerous and the least singular. There are hundreds of them, perhaps thousands, and each one is almost identical to the next, the way raindrops are almost identical, the way snowflakes were supposed to be before anyone looked closely enough to learn otherwise.

They are not silver. They are not gray. They are the color of a reflection in a window just before you realize it is your own. A warm gray. A gray that remembers being polished. Their bodies are flattened, not like stones are flattened — by force — but like breath is flattened when it meets glass. There is a thinness to them that suggests they exist primarily in two dimensions and have only grudgingly agreed to a third.

Six limbs. Not legs. Limbs that unfold from the body's rim like fingers from a closing fist, each one tipped with a pad that adheres to the composite with a sound like a tongue releasing from the roof of a mouth. They do not walk. They *translate*. One moment they are at the base of a wall, their sensor aimed upward at the pulsing blue-white conduits. The next they are three meters away, at the edge of a pooling basin, drinking — if drinking is what you call the slight depression that forms in the surface of their body when the warm water rises through the composite floor to meet them.

They have no face. They have a *focus*: a shallow concavity on their anterior surface, dark and glassy, depthless the way a well is depthless. Look into it and it looks back — not with recognition, not with curiosity, but with the attention of a mirror that has been tilted slightly off-axis. You see yourself there, but not quite yourself. Slightly shifted. Slightly *wrong*. Not uncanny in the way of wax figures or prosthetic eyes. Wrong the way a chord is wrong when one instrument is tuned a quarter-tone sharp — technically almost right, aesthetically unbearable.

They do not react to you. This is important. They do not flee, approach, ignore, or acknowledge. Your presence registers the way a change in barometric pressure registers in a barometer: mechanically, without opinion. Their sensor follows you. This is not surveillance. Surveillance implies a watcher and a subject, a hierarchy of attention. Their tracking is *sympathetic*, the way a compass needle tracks north. They are not interested in you. They are *oriented* by you.

And then they are gone — dissolved into the flock, folded back into the quiet geometry of the space.

---

### The Sentinels

Between the walls, at junctions where the ribbed structures widen and the conduit-light brightens to a steady, pale blue, the Sentinels stand.

They are not standing. The word is a courtesy. They are *placed*, the way a keystone is placed in an arch — not occupying space but *defining* it. Without them, the junction would be a mere intersection. With them, it becomes a node, a point of function, a place where the Seam Flats' internal logic thickens and concentrates like sap.

They are large — the size of delivery trucks, the size of small houses — and angular where the small ones are smooth. Their bodies are dense, layered, striated with the same vertical ridges as the walls, and you cannot shake the feeling that they were *cut* from the walls, the way a bone is cut from the carcass of an animal. They share material. They share structure. If you pressed your ear against a Sentinel's surface — and you do not, because something in the stillness warns you against that kind of intimacy — you suspect you would hear the same hum, the same sub-audible thrum, louder, denser, a chord compressed into a single note and held until it becomes a different kind of silence.

Their surfaces ripple.

This is the detail you remember most. Not their size or their stillness but the ripple — a wave that moves through their composite skin the way a thought moves through a dreaming brain. It starts somewhere deep inside them, visible as a disturbance in the striations, and rolls outward until the entire surface shimmers like the skin of water just before it freezes. When this happens — and it happens at no discernible interval, without cause, without warning — the nearest wall weeps more freely. The water increases its flow, thin and bright, and for a moment the air smells of copper and ozone, and you feel, with a certainty you cannot justify, that the Sentinel has *asked* for something and the wall has *answered*.

They do not have sensors. Their anterior surfaces are blank. But they are oriented — *always* oriented — toward the center of the nearest open space, as though they are watching something that moves in a dimension perpendicular to your own. You do not want to think about what they see there.

---

### The Flocks

The flocks are the thing that will haunt you. Not the Sentinels, with their rippling stillness. Not the small ones, with their empty attention. The flocks.

They are small — no larger than your thumb — and dart-shaped, with a single elongated axis and short, symmetrical fins that adjust their trajectory with movements too fast for the eye to register. They are metallic, but the metal is alive in a way the Sentinels and small ones are not: it catches light, shifts it, throws it back in colors that do not quite exist in the normal spectrum — a blue that is slightly too warm, a silver that leans toward violet.

They move in coordinated clouds. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Not murmurations — those are organic, governed by the simple rules of proximity and avoidance, each bird responding to its seven nearest neighbors. The flocks do not respond. They *converge*. Their patterns are not emergent but *prescribed*, as though they are executing instructions written in a language older than flight. They form grids, spirals, nested helixes, structures that exist for three seconds and then dissolve and reform into something almost the same but subtly, unnameably different.

They make no sound. This is not silence in the acoustic sense — it is *absence*. When they pass near you, the ambient hum of the walls dips by a fraction, the way a room gets quieter when a large animal enters it. They displace air without disturbing it. They are *presence without footprint*, *motion without wake*.

And when they dissolve — when the pattern collapses and each dart-shaped body darts off on its own trajectory, only to reconverge into a new pattern moments later — there is a fraction of a second when the space between the walls is *empty*. Not empty of them. Empty of *everything*. A pocket of absolute null, a visual silence so complete that your eyes lose their focus and you feel, briefly, as though you are falling.

Then the patterns reform. The displacement returns. The flocks resume their instructions.

---

### What You Cannot See

There is something else. You sense it in the margins — in the spaces between the fauna, in the pauses between the water's notes, in the half-second delay between when you turn your head and when the light adjusts to your new orientation.

The Seam Flats are not uninhabited. They are inhabited by something you cannot perceive directly, the way you cannot perceive the curvature of the Earth while standing on it. The fauna — the small ones, the Sentinels, the flocks — are not its inhabitants. They are its *symptoms*. Its *outputs*. The way a fever is not the disease but the body's response to it.

You feel this presence in the equilibrium itself. In the absence of gradients. In the refusal of entropy. In the way the warmth remembers being warm. Something is *maintaining* this place — not with effort, not with intention as you understand intention, but with the same quiet, relentless, total commitment with which gravity maintains its pull. Something has wound the machine and walked away, and the machine has continued, and continued, and continued, and the continuation *is* the inhabitant. The Seam Flats are not a place with beings in it. They are a being that has become a place.

You can sit here for hours and not see it move. You can watch the small ones translate, the Sentinels ripple, the flocks dissolve and reform, the water weep its four-minute hymn. And all the while, beneath every surface, through every seam, in the hum you feel in your sternum and the silence that weighs on your chest like a hand pressed flat against it, *it* persists — vast, warm, alive in the way a field is alive, the way a circuit is alive, the way a memory is alive when nothing it remembers remains.

It does not know you are here.

It will never know you are here.

And that — finally — is the most merciful thing about the Seam Flats.
