What lives on the Plateau of Seeping Iron does not live *on* it, not the way a herdsman lives on a plain or a bird lives in a forest. To live on the plateau is to be enrolled in the plateau — inducted into its processes the way a spark is inducted into combustion. The creatures here are not residents. They are organs.

---

The small ones move like stitches.

Six-legged, iridescent, the color of oil on water — not decorative but functional, their carapaces refracting the diffused pewter light in ways that make them difficult to track with the eye. This is not camouflage in any purposeful sense. It is simply that the plateau's palette is limited, and they have been absorbed into it the way iron salts are absorbed into basalt. They cross open rock in bursts — quick, decisive, purposeful — and vanish into fissures so narrow they seem to pour themselves into the stone. They do not return the way they came. The fissures are one-directional for them, and you understand this not because you see it happen but because the rock, after they pass, looks *worked* — the edges of the crack faintly polished, scored with the barest tracery of legs pressed into mineral skin a thousand times over.

They are not the insects. The insects are something else — clouds of midges so dense they become a medium, a haze you breathe through, the light catching them and bending. They do not fly in the way midflies in other places fly. They *suspend*. Motionless in the still air, they hold position with a precision that feels governed not by instinct but by calibration — each individual maintaining its distance from every other individual with the consistency of a crystal lattice. When one moves, the movement propagates. Not a ripple — nothing so dramatic. A suggestion. A single midge shifts a fraction of a millimeter and the displacement passes through the cloud like a thought through a brain, and then the cloud is still again, and the new configuration is held as if it had always been this way. They feed on minerals dissolved in the moisture films. Their bodies are, in essence, small refineries — extracting, concentrating, excreting. When they die, they fall to the moss and decompose into substrates that feed the moss that feeds the beetles that feed the thing with three eyes.

The beetles do not deviate. You watch them — hammered-copper shells glinting in the directionless light — moving in straight lines across the moss surface with a determination that feels like industry. They do not turn. They do not pause. If there is a beetle-path and a stone in its way, the beetle reaches the stone, and the beetle stops, and the beetle remains there until it dies, still facing the direction it was traveling. Other beetles step over it or around it, deviating only the minimum amount, and the path continues. The paths are not random. Over days — or hours, the plateau's sense of duration is not yours — the beetle-lines begin to trace something. Not symbols. Not language. Topography. They are mapping the gradient. The slow chemical gradient of the place, the direction in which the iron concentration increases by parts per million, the direction in which the water seeping through the basalt runs slightly warmer. They read the place with their feet and they walk toward something, and what they are walking toward is not food, and it is not shelter, and it is not other beetles. It is the center.

---

The three-eyed creature is the one that almost looks at you.

You hear it as a rustling in the fern layer — soft, rhythmic, like hands slowly turning pages of a damp book. The sound moves parallel to you, never closer, never farther, maintaining a distance that feels like courtesy rather than caution. When it briefly emerges — just the crown of its head, the ridge of something like a jaw, and then the eyes — you see three. Not arranged in a triangle. Arranged in a line, vertical, the lowest nearly at the jaw, the highest at the crown, each slightly larger than the one below. The eyes are dark. Not black — dark the way deep water is dark, with something reflective at the bottom of them, something that catches the pewter light and gives it back changed, shifted toward red, toward the color of the iron-staining on the rocks.

It does not blink. Its eyes remain open while it is visible, and when it drops back into the ferns, the last thing to disappear is the lowest eye, watching you from the level of your shins, and then the middle, and then the highest — a slow vertical submersion, like a periscope descending.

It is not a predator. You know this the way you know the air is wet — not because anyone tells you but because the knowledge arrives through the skin. It does not hunt you. It does not hunt anything in any way that the word implies urgency, or aggression, or the particular hunger of pursuit. It moves through the fern layer like water moves through saturated soil — following the path of least resistance, encountering what it encounters, and what it encounters *becomes part of it.* Not consumed. Enrolled. The three-eyed creature is a nexus, a point where the plateau's energy gathers temporarily before dispersing. Its three eyes see different spectra — one for the diffuse light above, one for the bioluminescence of the water films at dusk, and one, the lowest, for the vibration that rises through the basalt from the engine below. It sees the place in ways you do not have the architecture to process, and it processes what it sees into *movement* — the only language the plateau speaks fluently.

---

The birds are not birds.

You understand this before you understand anything else about them. Their call is too structured. Too specific. The high, thin sound — repeated at intervals that shorten by one second each cycle — is not song. It is telemetry. Each call is a pulse, and the silence between calls is data, and the pattern they form is a measurement: of air density, of moisture content, of the speed at which the phosphorescent glow in the water films is intensifying as the graphite dark settles over the plateau.

They do not appear in the sky. There is nothing in the sky. The sky is a uniform pewter dome with no features, no movement, no passage of clouds or shifting of light. The birds — if that is what they are — exist in the space above the moss canopy and below the pewter, a zone that is not quite air and not quite atmosphere, a band of stillness so complete it feels like a substance. Their calls come from *above* this zone, and the sound descends through the moss and the ferns and the water films and reaches you filtered, the way light reaches you through stained glass — the same source, but already transformed by the medium it passed through.

They are measuring the night.

Or the night is measuring itself through them.

---

And then: the moss at the center.

The concentric rings. Twelve centimeters wide, each one, precise as a machined groove. They center on nothing — no stone, no root, no thermal vent — and yet the center is warm. Not metaphorically. You press your palm flat against it and the warmth enters your skin with the deliberateness of a handshake. The moss here is almost black, so dark it absorbs the diffused light completely, and the individual fronds lie flat against the basalt surface, all pointing inward, all pointing *down*, pressed so tightly to the stone that they look not grown but *applied* — like a membrane laid over an opening.

Because that is what it is.

Beneath the moss, beneath the basalt, beneath the sedimentary layers that took hundreds of millions of years to compress into the continent's shoulder — beneath all of it, there is a rhythm. The ticking in the rock face. The vibration in the soles of your feet. The subsonic hum that the basalt conducts and the moss absorbs and the standing water films translate into shivering light when the dark comes. It is not a heartbeat. It is older than hearts. It is the rhythm of chemical processes so vast and so slow that their periodicity feels geological — the way continents drift, the way iron oxidizes, the way water finds the lowest point and, having found it, *stays.* But it is faster than geology. It is faster than rust. It is the speed of something that is alive and knows it and does not need to announce the fact.

The warm moss at the center is an aperture.

The fronds pointing downward are not listening. They are *receiving.* The membrane of the moss, black with saturation, is an interface between the atmosphere of the plateau and the process beneath it — a boundary organ, a lung whose inhale takes centuries and whose exhale is the slow seep of iron-rich water up through the joints and fissures, the weeping of the basalt, the mineral deposits that streak the stone in ochre and sienna, the patina that is the plateau's own blood.

The three-eyed creature knows this. The beetles walking their copper paths toward the center know this. The midge-clouds, holding their crystalline formations above the warmest moss, know this. They are not drawn to the center by hunger or instinct or any force you have a name for. They are drawn the way blood is drawn through a capillary — not because it *wants* to go but because the system requires it and the system is the only will that operates here.

---

Night settles. The phosphorescence blooms. The water films glow faintly — blue-green, the color of a flame seen through fog — and the moss at the center darkens further until it is indistinguishable from a hole in the landscape, a place where the plateau opens onto something deeper than basalt.

The birds — the something-like-birds — call out in their Morse-code cadence, and the intervals between calls grow shorter as you walk away, and you realize the cadence is not fixed. It is responsive. It is counting you — your distance, your direction, the rate at which your footfalls are absorbed by the moss and subtracted from the silence.

And the hum rises. Not in volume. In *presence.* The vibration in your soles becomes a vibration in your teeth, in your sternum, in the fluid of your inner ears. The basalt under your boots is no longer a surface. It is a membrane. And through it, faintly, imperceptibly, you feel the shape of something vast and patient and utterly without malice or benevolence — something that is not hostile to you but is not *for* you either, any more than the iron in your blood is for the moss, though the moss will take it when you are done with it.

This is what lives on the Plateau of Seeping Iron.

Not creatures. Not organisms. Not a single entity.

A process — ancient, rhythmic, self-sustaining — that has reached such a pitch of equilibrium that it resembles consciousness the way a perfectly tuned engine resembles singing. The moss, the beetles, the three-eyed thing in the ferns, the copper-shelled patrols, the midge-clouds holding their crystalline formations over the warm center, the iron weeping through the stone, the phosphorescent water returning the light in its own time — all of it is a single sustained note, held by a throat the size of a continent, for no audience and for no reason other than that the note, once begun, cannot stop.

The plateau is awake.

The plateau has always been awake.

And the warm ring of moss at its center breathes — not in your time, not in any time you will witness the completion of — but steadily, and with a patience that makes stone look impatient, and if you kneel and press your palm flat against the black fronds, you will feel it:

The beat.

Not a heartbeat. Something older. Something that was beating here long before there was anything here to hear it, and will be beating long after the last midge falls and the last beetle reaches its stone and the last three-eyed creature submerges into the ferns and the last drop of iron-rich water traces its ruled line down the weeping basalt.

The continent does not care whether you understand it.

But the continent lets you feel it.

And your hand is warm, and the moss is breathing, and somewhere far below — or perhaps not below at all, perhaps *everywhere* — the engine hums.
