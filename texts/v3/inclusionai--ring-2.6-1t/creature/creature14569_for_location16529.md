**Inhabitants**

---

**The frog-things** — and they are frog-things only by mercy of approximation, the way a word is a mercy applied to the thing it cannot hold — live in congregations. That is the first fact about them, the fact from which every other fact about their kind descends: they are never alone. You will never see one. You will see forty, sixty, a hundred, pressed together on a column's flank like lichen with bones, their translucent bellies catching the grey light that filters down from a source they never look toward, glowing faintly the way a jar of fireflies glows — not lit from without but from the interior murk of their own processes, some biochemistry you will not understand because it does not belong to your branch of time.

They are, individually, about the length of a forearm — the shorter forearm, the one you use to write, not the one you gesture with. Their skin is the color of wet slate held up to a candle, and it is *wet*, always wet, not in the way a amphibian is wet but in the way a stone in a creek is wet — damp at the molecular level, as though water were not on them but *within* their substance. When you touch one (and you will not, but if you did, if your hand moved with the slowness this place encourages, the way a breath is slow) it would feel like pressing into a warm river stone that is also alive, also startled, also — and this is the part you would feel in your wrist before your brain could name it — *absorbing* you a little. Not eating. Drinking. The way a porous stone drinks.

Their faces are broad, flattened, almost rudder-like, and their eyes are set wide and bulge only slightly, the way a fish's eyes bulge when it is curious about something it cannot eat and cannot flee from. The eyes are dark. Not black — dark the way deep water in a well is dark, dark with a faint suggestion of something reflective at the bottom that may be light or may be thought. They have no visible pupils. They look at you with the full surface of the eye, all at once, the way a security camera does, the way a fish tank does when you stand before it and every eye in it turns and it becomes clear that you are the thing being observed, not the observer.

The fingers — four on each hand, the webbing between them luminous, faintly arterial, a tracery of blue-green light that pulses slightly out of phase with whatever organ produces the glow in their bellies — are long. Delicate. They splay when the creatures land and fold when they crouch, and when you see them in clusters on a vertical surface, clinging to the slick dark stone with the pads of those too-wide digits, they look like notes on a page, like a chord someone left written in a language of flesh.

They are quiet. They make sounds — soft clicking sounds, a kind of wet tongue-to-roof-of-mouth percussion, a murmur that is more vibration than voice — but they are profoundly, almost unnervingly *quiet* for things so numerous. A hundred of them clustered on a wall sound like a single creature breathing deeply in its sleep. They have no alarm calls. They have no songs. When one contracts its throat and the lantern-swell of its body expands, dimming momentarily as the glow relocates inward, it is not communication. It is digestion. It is thought. It is whatever they do instead of speaking.

They eat the moisture, or what lives in the moisture. They eat the film that grows on the walls — a thin scum, barely visible, that is the closest thing this place has to grass. They eat each other's shed skin. They eat, apparently, nothing that you would recognize as food, and yet they are plump. They are in excellent health. Their bones feel dense under the translucent membrane. They are not starving. They are not hunting. They are *maintaining*, the way a filter maintains a pool, the way a liver maintains a body. They are the purification system of a building that breathes through them.

When one jumps — and they jump often, unpredictably, with that sharp report like a twig or a match-strike — the others do not flinch. They do not register it. The jump is not social. It is locomotion, or it is dreaming, or it is a form of excretion — the sudden expulsion of something the body needs to shed, a physical equivalent of a thought you release by saying it aloud. They jump toward nothing. They land and cling. They resume.

---

**The kites** — for want of a better word, for they are not kites, they are not birds, they are not bats, they are something that your visual cortex assembles into a stable image and then quietly revises every half-second because the image *will not hold* — occupy the space above you, always, the way weather occupies the space above a field. You do not look up to see them. You feel their absence from your peripheral vision and then the return. They move in the grey wash of diffused light near the ceiling, where the geometry of the walls ends and the architecture becomes suggestion — becomes height, pure and indifferent.

They are angular. Every edge about them is a decision. Their silhouettes are not aerodynamic — they are *axiomatic*, shaped the way a mathematical proof is shaped, where every line is necessary and nothing is decorative. They are flat. Not flat like paper but flat like a *concept* of flatness, as though someone understood that height is optional and chose against it. They move in planes — literally, geometrically, they move in planes — tilting, banking, rotating along axes that appear to change mid-turn, so that what you think is the leading edge becomes the trailing edge becomes a side becomes a vanishing point, and you cannot tell which end of them is forward and this is not confusion, this is *design*. They are designed to be unreadable in direction. They are the opposite of arrows. Wherever they are going, it does not have a name that involves forward.

Their color is the color of absence. Not black — they do not absorb light, they *preclude* it. The space where they are is a space where grey stops being grey and becomes a hole in the grey. If you could catch one — you cannot, they are too fast in their jerk-and-glide, too fluid in their refusal to be held — you would find it has weight, has texture, has the specific matte quality of a material that has never been illuminated because it was not made to be.

Their voices are the loudest thing in this place, and the most precise. A thin reedy keen, tonal, modal, rising and falling through intervals that do not belong to any scale you have been taught but that *feel* taught, that feel like something you once started to learn before you had the language for learning it. They call to each other. They call, it seems, *in response* — one voice raises and another shifts to meet it, not harmonically, not in counterpoint, but in something else, something that feels like the completion of a thought the first voice did not know it was thinking. Their speech is not language. It is the *architecture* of language. It is the load-bearing structure of meaning, stripped of everything but the weight it must carry.

They do not land. They do not descend to the height where the frog-things cluster. There is a vertical stratification here, a social geography as rigid and unspoken as the carvings in the second chamber. The kites own the upper volume. The frogs own the surfaces. And the nest-builders — the unseen ones — own the margins, the corners, the edges where wall meets floor meets the ankle-deep warm water.

---

**The nest-builders** — you have never seen one. You know them only by their work.

The nests are in the second chamber, all of them, tucked into the carved corners where the wall-channeling converges into nodes, the way a circulatory system knots into organs. Each nest is a shallow bowl, the size of a curled body — not your size, something smaller, something that curls to the dimensions of a large cat or an infant. The construction is meticulous. Layers of material arranged in spirals — shed skins (the frog-things' skins? their own? something else?), small stones selected with obvious discrimination (each one smooth, each one a slightly different shade of the same grey-black, as though chosen from a larger pile with care), and lengths of fibrous stuff — thread, twine, pulled-apart cloth, or something that *becomes* those things after enough time in this environment, something that looks woven but may only look that way because the nest-builder understands the structural principles of weaving without knowing the word.

The fastidiousness is what gets to you. This is not hoarding. This is not instinct run wild. There is a *curation* to these nests — each stone placed, then removed, then replaced. Each fiber aligned with its neighbors to a degree of precision that exceeds necessity. The creatures that build them are fastidious the way a clock is fastidious — not because they care about beauty but because the mechanism *requires* exactness. The nests are not homes. They are *instruments*. They are tuned to something. They sit in the carved channels and the water moves through the channels and the nests sit at the nodes and you understand, with the body's understanding rather than the mind's, that the nests are part of the resonance. That they are what makes the hum hum. That the nest-builders are the tuning forks of this place.

You do not see them because they operate in the margin-hours. They move when the reddish amber light is coming through the distant aperture, the passage-tunnel that leads to wherever this place connects next. They move with a lithe, cautious gait, low to the water, dragging their gathered materials behind them. You have caught only the aftermath — the fresh nest, the newly placed stone still wet from the water, the fiber still taut and unweathered. They are gone before you can kneel. They are gone before you can decide whether to call them intelligent.

They are intelligent. You know this the way you know a fire is hot — before you have language for it, before you have decided whether to put your hand in. Their intelligence is not yours. It does not build abstractions. It does not name things. It understands *relationship* — the way a stone relates to water relates to channel relates to resonance relates to the hum in the wall that, if you press your back against it and close your eyes, you can feel vibrating through your spine into the meat of your skull. They think in that frequency. They build in it. They are the maintenance crew of a machine that was built so long ago that the builders became the maintenance crew became a different species became *this* — quick, quiet, meticulous, and completely, perfectly at home in a logic whose destination they no longer need to understand because they *are* the destination.

---

And the place itself — because the place itself lives, the way a lung lives, the way a gut lives, the way a reef lives once the coral has died and the structure remains and the structure *is* the life. It breathes through the frog-things. It speaks through the kites. It feels through the nest-builders. The water is its blood. The carvings are its nerves. The hum is its voice, and the hum says nothing you can translate, only something you can *feel*, and what it feels like is this:

You are inside something that has been alive for longer than the species that built it, and it is not malevolent, and it is not kind, and it does not need you, and it is so profoundly, quietly, *exactly* alive that the distinction between alive and not-alive becomes, for a moment, a question that has no meaning here.

The frogs glow. The kites keen. The nests wait, tuned and patient, in the warm dark water.

And the place resonates, and resonates, and resonates.
