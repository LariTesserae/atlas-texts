## What Lives on the Lithic Steppe

---

### I. The Mat

The first and oldest inhabitant is the one no one can properly name.

It does not stand so much as it *exists* — a continuous sheet of grey-green tissue pressed against the mineral skin of the plateau like a hand pressed over a wound, except the wound is the geology itself and the hand is slowly healing it. From above, on the rare days when the atmospheric clarity allows an unbroken view, it looks less like vegetation than like a textile: woven, felted, patterned with darker veins that branch and re-branch in geometries that are almost but not quite fractal, almost but not quite random. Close up, the surface is furrier than it appears — a dense nap of filamentous structures, each no thicker than a human hair, rising from a tough, spongy substrate that resists compression the way a closed sponge does. When you cut into it — and the few researchers who have done so report this with a kind of reluctant awe — it does not tear. It separates along planes, like muscle, revealing interior channels through which a clear, faintly viscous fluid moves with visible, agonizing slowness.

The mat has no flowers. It has no seeds. It has no recognizable leaves, stems, or roots in any botanical sense. What it has instead is a modularity so thoroughgoing that individual organisms cannot be distinguished from the colony. A square meter of mat, excised and placed in a sterile container, will continue to exhibit electrochemical gradients, rhythmic contractions of its internal fluid, and — most unsettlingly — directed growth toward a source of mineral moisture, even when no one in the laboratory can identify anything that qualifies as a sensory organ. The biologists who first studied the samples published papers in which they referred to the mat as a "superorganism," then revised that to "distributed colonial system," then, in later and more candid drafts, simply called it *the mat* and left the taxonomy open, as if they understood that naming it would mean pretending they understood it.

It pulses. Not visibly — the movement is too slow, too deep in the tissue. But you can feel it, standing barefoot or pressing a palm flat against the surface on a still night: a rhythm more geological than biological, a systole and diastole measured in what feels like weeks. Whether this is a heartbeat, a breath, or something else entirely — a metabolic cycle no human science has yet characterized — is a question the mat does not answer.

What it does do is *colonize*. It colonizes with the patience of erosion. It sends filaments into cracks in rock. It grows up the concrete feet of industrial pylons. It threads into the porous seams of corrugated metal. On the southern perimeter of the processing compound, where a section of conveyor belt has been motionless for weeks, the mat has already begun to coat the rubber surface with a thin, grey-green film, and in the joints where the belt folds, tufts of vertical filaments — pale, translucent, swaying in a breeze that does not exist — have begun to emerge. Given enough time, and the mat operates on a scale where "enough time" is measured in centuries or not at all, it will digest this machinery the way a body digests a splinter: by growing over it, into it, through it, until the foreign object is incorporated into the living architecture and becomes indistinguishable from the geology it once sat upon.

### II. The Beetles

They are the most visible fauna on the plateau, though "visible" is generous — they are small, dark, and so deliberate in their movements that they often appear to be part of the mat's texture until one of them pauses, lifts its body on jointed legs, and extends its antennae in a slow, scanning arc.

The beetles are flat — dorsoventrally compressed, as though they have been pressed by the same geological forces that raised the plateau. Their exoskeletons are a deep, matte black that seems to absorb the intense light rather than reflect it, and their surfaces are not smooth but microsculptured: fine ridges and pits that, under magnification, reveal a patterning so regular it approaches engineering. They move along the mat's surface with a mechanical gait — left-right-left-right, each step identical in length, each pause identical in duration — as though following an algorithm so old it has been encoded not in genes but in habit, in muscle memory inherited from a lineage so ancient that the beetles themselves have forgotten its origin.

At regular intervals — observers estimate every ninety seconds, though the timing is not exact — a beetle will stop, plant its legs wide, and extend its antennae forward in a slow, trembling arc. The antennae are long relative to the body, segmented, and they move with a delicacy that seems disproportionate to the beetle's otherwise plodding demeanor. They are tasting the air. Or tasting the mat beneath them. Or tasting something else — some chemical gradient, some electrochemical residue of the colony's vast and silent communication network — that no instrument has been sensitive enough to detect.

No one has observed them eating. No one has observed them mating. They appear to do nothing but walk, pause, taste, and walk again, their black bodies moving across the grey-green mat like punctuation marks in a sentence too long to read in a single lifetime.

### III. The Grey Shapes

They have been seen exactly four times.

Each sighting was by a different person, in a different part of the plateau, and the descriptions are consistent to a degree that excludes hallucination but inconsistent in ways that frustrate any attempt at classification.

The shape is large — larger than any bird on the plateau, though it is silhouetted against the sky at heights where the dwarf trees thin out and the vertical filaments of colonized water stand at their tallest. It has long legs, or something like long legs: structures that extend below a body too indistinct to resolve, moving with a fluid, unhurried gait through the upper filaments as though wading through water. It does not fly. It does not walk. It *progresses*, the way smoke progresses, the way heat shimmer progresses — with a coherence that suggests intention but a form that refuses to hold still.

Each observer has reported the same detail: the shape did not react to their presence. It did not flee, turn, or pause. It continued its slow passage through the filaments with what one worker described, in a report that was quietly removed from the camp's shared files, as "the patience of something that doesn't know it's being watched because watching is not something it does."

Whether the shapes are animals, or projections of the mat's deeper processes, or some third thing that has no name in the available taxonomies, is a question no one on the plateau is willing to answer definitively. The beetles do not react to them. The mat does not change its rhythm when they pass. They are, in the most literal sense, part of the landscape — as native to the place as the seepage, as the condensation, as the slow and patient pulse beneath the surface.

### IV. The Metallic Insects

These are newcomers.

They appeared within six months of the first drilling operations, drawn by the vibrations, the heat, the chemical signature of disturbed earth and processed water. They are insects in the broadest sense — segmented bodies, jointed limbs, wings — but they have adapted to the industrial environment with a speed that alarms the biologists and does not alarm anyone else, because no one is paying attention.

They are small, iridescent, and their exoskeletons reflect light in colors that do not exist in nature: metallic blues, greens that shift to copper in motion, sudden flashes of violet that appear when a cloud of them turns in the air and catches the sun. They are louder than the plateau's native insects. They congregate on the drill pylons in densities that obscure the metal surface beneath them, and when they take flight — which they do in response to changes in the drilling rhythm, as though they can feel the shift in vibration through the ground — the sound they produce is a high, static hiss that the workers have learned to ignore the way city dwellers learn to ignore traffic.

They are not harmful. They do not bite. They do not appear to eat the mat, or the workers, or each other. Under observation, they feed on the mineral-rich standing water that pools at drill sites, their mouthparts adapted — with an adaptive speed that should be impossible in any conventional evolutionary framework — to extract nutrients from mineral leachate. They are, in effect, processing the same materials the industrial operation is processing, and the thought occurs to more than one observer that the insects and the machines are performing the same task, one biologically and one mechanically, and that the insects are better at it.

### V. The Dwarf Trees

They anchor the ridgelines.

That is their only apparent function — to hold the highest edges of the plateau in place, their root systems (if that word can be applied to organisms that share more structural grammar with the mat than with any tree in any conventional forest) gripping the thin, newly formed soil in a grip that is geological in its tenacity. They are small, gnarled, and they do not grow so much as they *persist*, year after year, adding perhaps a centimeter of height in a decade, their trunks twisted into shapes that suggest wind, or weight, or both.

Their leaves are small, waxy, and dark. In the morning, during the condensation cycle, they are the loudest things on the plateau — not individually, but collectively, as the water that has formed on every surface overnight drips from their branches in a fine, continuous patter that fills the air with white noise so dense it erases all other sound. For two to three hours, the dwarf trees are the voice of the steppe, speaking in a language of falling water that no one has translated.

### VI. The Water

It is not alive, but it behaves as though it is.

The seepage — the continent-wide wetness that saturates the substrate from below — has no source. No river feeds it. No aquifer has been identified. The water simply *is*, rising through the mat's capillary network, condensing on every surface, pooling where the mat has been disturbed, dripping when the air cools. It is the circulatory system of the plateau, or at least it serves that function, and without it the mat would die within weeks, the beetles would follow, the dwarf trees would crack and topple, and the metallic insects would vanish into whatever void they emerged from.

Where the drills have removed the mat, the water pools and turns grey-brown with mineral leachate. At the edges of these pools, the mat has begun to colonize the water itself — sending up thin vertical filaments that sway in unison, a mass movement so uniform it resembles breath, as though the colony is inhaling through the surface of the standing water. This is not metaphor. The filaments do move in a rhythm that correlates with the deeper pulse of the mat, as though the water has become an extension of the organism's respiratory system.

### VII. The Camp

Forty people, give or take, rotating on fourteen-day shifts.

They are not inhabitants so much as they are *incidents* — temporary interruptions in the mat's slow, patient expansion. Their prefabricated units sit on elevated steel platforms, the ground-mat already colonizing the gaps between pylons, wicking moisture up into the walls, leaving grey-green stains on every surface in contact with the earth. The workers have learned to elevate everything: their boots, their tools, their food stores. Anything left on the ground for more than a few hours will show the first signs of colonization — a faint dampness, a thin crust of living tissue, a pulse so faint it could be imagined but cannot.

They do not talk about the night. They do not talk about the way the ground feels warmer than the air, or the way the stars cast shadows, or the way the mat glows faintly under that enormous, unsympathetic sky — a glow they call *the shimmer* and attribute to bioluminescence, though no one has successfully measured it, and no one has successfully explained why it feels, standing in it, like standing inside a thought that is not your own.

---

### VIII. What Lives Here

The question is poorly formed.

The Lithic Steppe does not *have* inhabitants the way a forest has inhabitants, or a city, or an ocean. It *is* an inhabitant — the mat, the water, the beetles, the trees, the metallic insects, the shapes that may not be shapes, all expressions of a single, vast, incompletely formed organism that is still building itself, still integrating, still learning how to be alive on a scale that human frameworks cannot contain.

The camp is temporary. The drilling is temporary. The conveyor, already sagging, already greening at its edges, will be absorbed within a decade or two, or three, or a century — the mat does not keep time in ways that accommodate human impatience. The plateau itself, young and unfinished, still finding its shape, still reaching upward — this is not a place where life exists.

It is a place where life *is still happening*. And it has been happening, with a patience that predates language and will outlast every structure on its surface, for longer than anyone can say.

The ground breathes. The beetles walk. The filaments sway. The water rises.

And the mat knits on, closing over every wound, every footprint, every drill-hole, every human footstep — slowly, without drama, with the unshakeable patience of something that cannot be hurried because it has already decided where it is going, and it has known for a very, very long time.
