# Inhabitants of the Becoming Place

---

Begin with the builders, because they are the center of things here — not by size or fierceness, but by *presence*. They are the ones who made the room you're standing in into a world.

You see them first as movement: a scuttle of amber along the flank of the central mound. Then one pauses, and the light — that dim, honeyed light — catches the overlapping plates of their thorax, and you realize they are beautiful in the way that well-worn things are beautiful. The chitin is layered like hammered metal, darker at the extremities, almost black at the tarsal tips where the stone has polished them. Up close, each individual is slightly different: some lean more copper-bronze, others toward deep umber; some have faint pale striations along the abdomen, others are unmarked. They are not identical. They have what might, in a more familiar animal, be called personality — not temperament exactly, but *consistency of manner*. One you watch for a long time carries its soil pellets with a kind of brisk economy, three at a time, stacked on its forelimbs. Another works alone at the base of the eastern wall, slowly and methodically re-weaving a section of tunnel entrance with fresh resin, its movements so precise they almost look like calligraphy.

They are not blind. Their heads — small, rounded, fitted with antennae that never stop moving — orient toward you when you first enter, then lose interest. You are furniture. You are geology. You are a warm surface that occasionally exhales carbon dioxide. You are irrelevant to the project underway. And the project is everything. The mound behind them is not a nest in the simple sense. It is infrastructure: ventilation shafts that open and close in response to humidity, brood chambers maintained at a precise temperature by the metabolic warmth of the occupants, larders where fungal cultivars are kept damp with that self-generated resin. They farm, in their way. The pale fungal mats that spread in concentric rings across the floor may not be wild — they may be tended, fed, shaped by the builders' waste and the deliberate moisture they carry in. You cannot be sure. But the proximity is not coincidence. The two organisms — builder and fungus — have arranged themselves into a mutualism so old and so settled that it looks like instinct. It probably is.

---

The worms come later. You don't find them — they find you, or rather, they find the pools.

They appear after the stone has sweated overnight, when the cupped depressions in the floor have filled with that warm, clear water. At first you see only a stirring on the surface — a tremor without source — and then one draws itself up, fully, and you can see it: a thread of living glass, no thicker than a horsehair, pale enough that you can watch the peristaltic pulse of its gut through its skin. It has no eyes you can identify, no discernible head or tail — just a body that moves by contracting, a blind question mark drawn across the surface of the water, tasting the air.

They are soft-bodied but not fragile; when one encounters the surface tension of the pool, it simply passes through, submerging like a thought completing itself. Underwater — if you could see them there — they likely graze on the bacterial films that coat every wet surface in this place, converting the stone's mineral exhalation into something the next link in the chain can consume. They do not linger. Within minutes of appearing, they begin to retreat, drawing themselves back into hairline fractures in the rock, pulling their translucence after them until the pool is still again and you wonder if they were ever there at all.

They surface two or three at a time. Never more. They do not cluster and they do not compete. The place has room enough, and they know it.

---

The predator is not beautiful in the way the builders are. It is beautiful the way a drawn blade is beautiful.

It waits near the water. Always near the water. It folds itself flat against the stone — and it is very flat, body and limbs pressed close, like a leaf that has been ironed — and it becomes almost indistinguishable from a piece of dark, polished shale. Its coloring is deliberate: slate-grey mottled with veins of darker pigment that mimic the mineral striations in the surrounding rock. Its forelimbs are what give it away: modified, elongated, tipped with small hooked structures that are clearly not for walking. They are for holding. When it moves, it does not scurry like the builders; it *glides*, a flat ripple of controlled motion, and it can stop at any point in that motion and become still again with a completeness that is almost mechanical.

It is patient. You watch one for the better part of an hour and it does not move more than twice. Once, a worm surfaces in the pool six inches from where the predator crouches. The predator does not react — not visibly. But something in the set of its limbs shifts, a subtle redistribution of tension, like a spring compressing. The worm begins to retreat. The predator's forelimbs flick — a single, fast, precise motion — and the worm is gone beneath the surface, seized, pulled under. The pool settles. The predator unfolds itself a fraction more, resets, and waits.

It is not cruel. It is not kind. It is *exact*. Its patience is not suffering; it is what its body was made for.

---

The iridescent ones are the strangest, at first, because they seem to contradict the place. Everything here is soft and low and dim, and then one of these creatures moves through a patch of fungal mat and the light catches it — a sudden wash of color that is not from the stone, not from the moss, not from the water. Blue-green, purple, a flicker of gold. It comes from their wings: wide, folded close against their backs like cloaks or mantles, overlapped like the scales of a moth but thinner, almost membranous, and shot through with a structural color that shifts as they turn.

They are flightless. The wings are vestigial — not useless, because they serve as display surfaces and possibly thermal regulators, but they will never carry the animal aloft in this low-ceilinged basin. It doesn't try. It walks with a slow, dignified gait through the fungal mats, its mouthparts methodically clipping spores from the surfaces. Where it feeds, it leaves behind clean geometric patterns — arcs and parallelograms and near-perfect circles of denuded substrate, as though it has an architect's instinct and a draftsman's restraint. The patterns fade as the fungus regrows, but they persist long enough that you can follow the path of a single individual across the colony and reconstruct its route like a sentence written in absence.

They are solitary. Quiet. Their footsteps make no sound on the stone.

---

And then — something else.

You do not know what to call it. You are not sure it has a name.

You catch it at the margins: the upper vegetation, the ferns, the tall mosses that rise to the height of your knee. A displacement. A rustle that is too large and too fluid for any of the creatures you've already catalogued. A shadow that moves against the movement of your own eyes, always in the corner, always withdrawing. You lean in and it is gone. You wait and nothing comes.

The only evidence is the sound — not a footstep but a *presence*, a low vibration that travels through the stone into the soles of your feet before it reaches your ears. Something resonant and slow, like a cello note played under water. The builders do not react to it. The predators do not crouch. The worms do not retreat. Whatever it is, it belongs here as fully as they do.

You glimpse it once: a soft body, pale, a foot or perhaps a little more, multi-limbed — not the six-legged architecture of the others but *many-limbed*, each one tapering to a delicate, translucent tip that brushes the vegetation as it passes without breaking it. It moves the way water moves through tall grass: unhurried, continuous, without decision because it does not need to decide. It is not hunting. It is not foraging, at least not in any way you recognize. It is *patrolling*, perhaps. Or *reading* the place with senses you do not have the vocabulary for. It slides into the upper canopy of moss and is simply gone, the way a thought leaves your mind after you've finished thinking it.

You will not see it again. But the vibration returns, once, late in your time there — felt, not heard — and you understand that it is aware of you in the same broad, peripheral way that you are aware of the weather. You are a presence. You are a warm object. You are here, and it knows, and it does not mind.

---

The builders work.

The worms surface and vanish.

The predator crouches.

The iridescent ones write their geometry in absence across the fungal mats.

The soft thing in the periphery passes, and the stone hums.

None of them know they are inside something that is still being born. They live in the becoming the way fish live in water — completely, without separation, without the need for a word for what surrounds them. They are not early arrivals. They are not late. They arrived the way the damp arrived: gradually, completely, as if they had always been part of the architecture of the place, fused into its formation at a level below choice, below instinct, in the deep grammar of how a new world assembles its first inhabitants the way it assembles its first stones — one particle, one filament, one living body at a time.

You step back toward the rim of the basin, and the light above is brighter than you remember, and the wind has a different smell. You look down once into the warm, still air of the place, and the builders are carrying something — a pellet of dark soil, a drop of resin — along a path you will never walk.

The becoming continues.

It does not notice you leave.
