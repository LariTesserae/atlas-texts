## What Lives in the Weeping Alcove

---

You must understand first that the question is wrong — not in its asking, but in its assumption. To ask *what lives here* is to ask who occupies a body while still believing the body is separate from the tenant. The Weeping Alcove does not permit that distinction. Everything here is tenant. Everything here is body. What you are looking for is not a creature but a civilization — one so old and so thoroughly merged with its architecture that the word *inhabitant* has become indistinguishable from *material*.

But you want names. So let us begin there, and let the names fall apart as they will.

---

**The Procession.**

You saw them first — or rather, you saw them before you saw anything else, because they move along the water films in such numbers that they look like a single thing, a thread of quicksilver unspooling through the room's veins. They are not mites. They are longer than mites, translucent as the description said, but calling them translucent is already a lie, because to be translucent is to allow light to pass through you, and what passes through these creatures is *changed* — it enters pale and colorless and exits carrying a faint amber signature, as though their organs are not organs but optics, refracting in a direction that has nothing to do with sight.

They have no eyes. They have no mouth you would recognize as a mouth. Their bodies are segmented in a way that suggests they were designed — not evolved, *designed* — to follow the paths that moisture traces through the room, the way a key is designed for a lock. And they do follow them, hundreds of them, moving in flows so uniform that from a distance they look like a liquid traveling uphill along the wall, defying the water's own direction, ascending through capillary action into the mineral crusts where the richest deposits of calcium and something stranger — something with a spectral signature your instruments will struggle to classify — accumulate in veins no wider than a hair.

They feed on these deposits. Or they *harvest* them. There is a difference you will not be able to articulate later, the way there is a difference between a river eroding a canyon and a sculptor carving stone. The grooves they leave in the mineral crust are too regular, too deliberate in their depth, repeating in intervals that are almost but not quite identical — the way a heartbeat is almost but not quite identical. If you were to record these intervals and play them back at a speed that compresses hours into seconds, you would hear something that resembles a pulse. You would hear something that resembles a signal.

They are not the smallest things here. There are smaller things, inside them — colonies of organisms so small they exist below the resolution of the lens you brought, visible only as a faint graininess in the creatures' tissues, a texture that might be pathology or might be symbiosis. The mites carry worlds. The mites are worlds. And the worlds inside them are themselves carrying smaller worlds, and somewhere in that recursion, in that nesting of tenant within tenant within tenant, the thing that was originally constructed — the *building* — starts to blur.

---

**The Ribbons.**

In the basins, the flatworms. You called them ribbon-worms, and that was a reasonable first pass, the way calling a cathedral a large building is reasonable, but the name holds about as well. They are segmented, yes, but the segments do not repeat — each one is slightly different, slightly specialized, the way the vertebrae in your spine are all different shapes that only look alike because you learned them as one thing. The ribbon-worms are old. Not old in the way that an ancient forest is old, where the individual trees cycle but the system persists; old in the way that a *word* is old, repeated so many times that its origin has been lost and its meaning has been replaced by the sheer weight of its use. They have been coiled in these basins for so long that the basins have shaped themselves around them — the shallow depressions in the floor are not randomly eroded; they are body-prints, the fossilized negative space of creatures that have rested here so consistently, for so many centuries, that the minerals have rearranged themselves into cradles for their resting forms.

They are aware of you. Not immediately — there is a latency, a period of several minutes during which they seem as indifferent as the copper-shell insects. But then, slowly, the nearest ribbon extends toward the edge of the basin closest to where you stand. Not aggressively. Not even, you think, with intention. But with *attention*, the way a cat's ear swivels toward a sound it hasn't yet decided to care about. When you touch the water, they do not scatter in fear. They scatter in *recognition*, the way a flock of starlings shifts when one bird registers a change and the change propagates through the group not as panic but as *information*. They reconverge on you — not all of them, but a contingent, a delegation — and they hold their positions at a distance that is exactly the limit of your reach, as though they have calibrated it over centuries of encounters with things that reach.

They feed on the hexagonal lattices the water makes. You saw the shapes collapse when you leaned close; what you didn't see was that the ribbons had started moving *before* you leaned close, responding to micro-vibrations in the basin's surface caused by your breathing, your heartbeat, the almost imperceptible thermal signature of your body projecting into the warm air. They dismantled the lattices for the same reason a surgeon clears a field before making an incision. The lattices were not decoration. The lattices were *preparation* — and the ribbons were here to consume what the lattices were building, the way a baker consumes what the oven has finished.

They have no face. They have a mouth, but it opens along the full width of their anterior end, a ciliated groove that dilates and contracts in peristaltic waves, and what they consume — the mineral-laden water, the dissolved organics, the lattice-structures — passes through them slowly, and what they excrete is luminous. The chartreuse glow along the upper walls is not, entirely, independent microbial colonies. It is, in part, ribbon-worm waste — bioluminescent byproduct smeared across the ceiling by the water films that run downhill from the basins. The light that makes this room glow is partially the residue of digestion. The room illuminates itself with its own feeding.

---

**The Copper-Shells.**

Smaller than your smallest fingernail. Their shells are not actually copper — they are a metalloprotein exoskeleton, a legitimate alloy of organic tissue and trace metals drawn from the water, and when the dim light catches them they flash with that warm reddish-gold that looks like nothing so much as a coal that refuses to die. They are mechanical in their movements, as the first description noted, but the mechanism is not rigid. They move the way a clock moves — each motion precise, each interval exact — but the clock is *alive*, and somewhere inside it, the clockmaker is dreaming.

They emerge from the seams in the walls. Not *through* the seams — from them, as though the walls are a hive and the seams are its openings. They spend their lives patrolling the surface of the water films, maintaining them, adjusting the flow paths with their bodies, depositing trace chemicals from glands at the base of their antennae. What the chemicals do, you cannot determine from observation alone, but the water films behave differently in their presence — the meniscus geometries hold longer, the hexagonal lattices are more regular, the surface tension is measurably altered. They are not merely navigating the water systems. They are *operating* them. They are the technicians of the Weeping Alcove, and they have been maintaining its plumbing for longer than your civilization has had plumbing to maintain.

They do not reproduce in any sense you would recognize. The seam in the wall produces them, one or two at a time, over intervals of months. The wall does not open; they simply appear at the seam's edge, fully formed, their shells already hardened, their antennae already sweeping. They emerge dry, walking across the mineral surface until they reach the first water film, and then they begin.

---

**The Column.**

In the center of the room stands what was once a structural support. It is now a reliquary. The alloy spine at its core — corroded beyond any recognizable metallurgical period — is wrapped in so many layers of accretion that it resembles a tree trunk more than a girder. The moss grows here, the only honest moss in the room, actual *Bryopsida* with actual chloroplasts performing actual photosynthesis under the bioluminescent light. The algae film clings to the wall beside it, dark red-brown, almost black, metabolizing who knows what — possibly sulfur compounds released by the corroding alloy, possibly metabolic byproducts of the bioluminescent colonies, possibly neither.

And threaded through the column's cracks, the fungal network: white filaments so fine they look like cracks in porcelain, pulsing with a bioluminescence so faint it could be reflected light or could be the filaments themselves generating a signal too dim for your eyes to register without adaptation. They reach toward the water basins with a patience that is not biological — the word for what it is does not exist in your language, because the concept does not exist in your culture. A patience closer to geology. To tectonic drift. The fungal threads extend by microns per day, and they have been extending for centuries, and when they reach a basin's edge they do not drink but *listen*, pressing their hyphae against the mineral rim in a way that, if you were inclined to metaphor and not afraid of being wrong, you might compare to pressing your ear to a wall.

Because behind the wall, something is alive. Something large.

---

**The Wall-Treader.**

You heard it. You heard what you described as displacement — mass moving through a space not much larger than its body, wet compression, the tap of mandible or sensory filament against the interior surface. Three times. Always three times.

It is the truest resident of the Weeping Alcove. It is, you are beginning to suspect, the reason the alcove weeps — not with water, though water is its circulatory fluid, but with sound, with resonance, with the low tonal presence you felt in your teeth and ribs before you ever entered the room. It is not *in* the walls the way a rat is in the walls. It *is*, in some operational sense, the walls — or the walls are its skeleton, its exoskeleton, its shed skin. The building is not a structure it inhabits. The building is a *gesture* it is still making, a slow continuous motion of construction and accretion that has been proceeding for so long that the distinction between builder and building has been lost.

You cannot see it. You can hear it moving — not always, but at intervals, when it passes close enough to a surface thin enough to transmit vibration. The sound it makes is specific: compressed mass against mineral, a slow deliberate shifting that follows the room's thermal gradients with the unerring certainty of something that has mapped every degree of variation over centuries. It avoids the water basins. It moves toward the heat — deeper into the structure, where the amber glow behind the walls pulses in that slow rhythm you will later realize is not synchronized with your heartbeat but with something much slower, something more like a circadian pulse, a tidal cycle measured in geological time.

What it looks like, you cannot say. You have found, pressed into the mineral crust at the base of the far wall, a mark — a depression the size and approximate shape of a human hand, but wrong: too long in the fingers, with an extra joint, the pad-marks faintly iridescent with the same oily sheen that coats the walls. The mark is recent. Hours old, perhaps. The mineral surface has not yet begun its slow process of recrystallization over it. Something pressed itself against this wall, recently, deliberately, and left a signature you can read only as *presence*.

---

**The Room.**

And what of the room itself? What of the Weeping Alcove as an entity, not as a container but as a *thing*?

It breathes. You felt it — the hum deepening by a quarter-tone, the air thickening, the moisture rising. It has a metabolism: the bioluminescent colonies consume and excrete light, the copper-shells maintain the water systems, the ribbon-worms process the lattices, the microfauna cycle minerals through their translucent bodies and return them to the crusts. The fungal threads listen. The wall-treader holds everything together, moving through the structure like blood through tissue, distributing heat, redistributing moisture, maintaining the gradients that every other organism depends on.

And the building — the corroded, lattice-walled, mineral-accreted structure — provides the skeleton, the thermal mass, the chemical reservoir. It exhales mineral-rich moisture. It holds warmth the way a lung holds air. Its corroded alloy spine, wrapped in moss and fungus and biofilm, is not deteriorating. It is being *processed*, broken down into forms that feed the alcove's ecology, the way a body breaks down food, the way a forest breaks down a fallen tree.

Nothing here is natural. You noted that. But nothing here is artificial either, not anymore. The boundary dissolved so long ago that the language you brought with you has no word for what replaced it. The Weeping Alcove is not a building with an ecosystem. It is not an ecosystem in a building. It is something that grew in the space between those categories, in the seam where the constructed world's precision wore down to something porous, and life — patient, layered, indifferent to your categories — filled the gap and called it home.

You are standing in a throat. The sound is swallowing. And the room, dim and warm and dripping, does not care if you understand any of this.

It has understood you, though. It registered your arrival the moment you rounded the breach — the shift in air pressure, the foreign thermal signature, the disruption to the moisture gradient you walked through without noticing. The tapping in the wall was not checking for you.

The tapping in the wall was *greeting* you.

And the ribbons reconverged. And the copper-shells paused their patrol. And the colonies dimmed, almost imperceptibly, the way a room dims when someone important enters. And the Weeping Alcove, five layers deep in tenant and structure and breath and light, turned its attention toward you — not with eyes, not with recognition, not with anything you would call welcome or warning — but with the vast, mineral patience of something that has been alive for longer than your species has had a name for alive, and considered you, briefly, the way you might consider a drop of water falling into your palm.

Warm. Brief. Almost interesting.

And then it returned to breathing.
