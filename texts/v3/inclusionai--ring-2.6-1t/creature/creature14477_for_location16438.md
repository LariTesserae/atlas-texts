## Inhabitants of the Glosspit

---

**The People**

They are not what you expect. That is the first thing.

You expect — given the darkness of the approach, given the narrowness of the throat through which you must squeeze, given the press of cold stone against your shoulders — you expect something diminished. Something pale and hunched and narrow-eyed, something that has retreated from the world and been hollowed out by the retreat. But they are not hollowed. They are *filled*. They move through the Glosspit the way water moves through stone: slowly, completely, as though they have always been there and will never leave, as though the concept of departure is a language they once knew and have let go disuse.

They are short. Not stunted — *compact*. Dense-bodied, thick-limbed, with wide flat feet that move soundlessly over the moss. Their skin is the color of the shelfall in low light: a pale warmth, the shade of amber held up to flame, though some of them — particularly those who spend long hours in the upper galleries tending the fern-curtains or working the copper veins — have a greyish cast to their complexion, as though the stone has begun to claim them. Their hair is dark and coarse, worn long by most and often threaded with small things: a twig, a feather, a bone pin, a strand of dried thrum-grass. There is no ceremony in this. It simply is how hair is kept. It is kept because it grows, and what grows is tended, and what is tended becomes ornament without anyone deciding that it should.

Their faces are broad, with low foreheads and strong jaws. Their eyes — and this is the feature most often remarked upon by visitors, though few would admit it — are unsettlingly steady. Not fixed, not staring, but *settled*. There is a stillness behind their gaze that has nothing to do with stupidity and everything to do with patience. They look at you the way the shelfall looks at you: with a kind of ancient, mineral attention that does not waver, does not flinch, does not try to be anything. They blink slowly. Their mouths are quiet.

They speak, but not often, and not at length. Their language is percussive and low — vowels that sit in the back of the throat, consonants that are more breath than sound. A word may take three seconds to say and mean something that would take a paragraph in any surface tongue. They have words for the different qualities of silence in different parts of the chamber. They have a word for the moment when the condensation on the walls begins to thin, a phenomenon that occurs so rarely that most visitors never witness it, but the people here recognize it the way a sailor recognizes a shift in the swell. They have a word for the particular silence of the moot-shelf when the entire community is seated and nothing is forthcoming. That word does not translate. It is not a word for emptiness. It is a word for *fullness that has not yet decided what it is full of*.

They do not greet visitors with words. They greet them with stillness. You enter a dwelling and they look up. They regard you. And then they return to what they were doing — mending a tool, grinding shelfall into powder, nursing a child, sitting with closed eyes and breathing in a rhythm so slow it seems like rest but is, you come to understand, something closer to practice.

They are not hostile. They are not welcoming. They are *present*. This is a distinction that takes days to understand and longer to stop reaching for.

**The Children**

The children are the only ones who run. They move through the settlement in sudden eruptions of motion — darting through doorways, scrambling up the sides of structures, sliding down the angles of the shelf-wall into the moss below. They are loud by Glosspit standards. Their voices rise in sharp, brief arcs — not laughter exactly, not shouting, but something in between, a kind of vocalized momentum, the sound of energy that has nowhere to spend itself and so spends itself in sound alone.

They are watched, but only loosely. The people here do not corral their young. A child may wander to the edge of the shelf and look down into the void, and the adults nearby will not move. This has been remarked upon as dangerous, by outsiders. It is not dangerous. The children do not fall. They come to the edge, they look, and they come back. They learn the boundary the way they learn everything else: by being immersed in it until it becomes indistinguishable from themselves.

The children play a game that involves stacking fragments of shelfall — the chips and flakes that break off naturally from the walls — into small towers on the moss. The towers always fall. The children always rebuild. There is no competition. There is no winner. There is only the stacking and the falling and the stacking again, which, if you watch it long enough, begins to look less like a game and more like a kind of prayer — or perhaps a kind of science. Perhaps those are the same thing here.

**The Thieves**

They are everywhere and nowhere.

The *thieves* — and no one has offered a name for them that translates cleanly; the nearest approximation is something like *the small ones who are already there* — are creatures of extraordinary and unsettling grace. They are perhaps nine inches from nose to tail, with bodies that are more fur than form: dense, silver-grey pelts that seem to absorb light, and eyes that are too large for their skulls, liquid and dark and set wide apart, giving them a permanent expression of startled attention. Their ears are almost comically large, translucent at the tips, perpetually rotating in the direction of sound the way a flower tracks the sun.

They move without friction. A thief can cross a room in which twenty people are sitting and not one of them will notice until it is sitting on their knee. They appear — this is the only word for it — they *appear*, squat and composed, on a table beside a cup of broth, and no one can say where they came from. They sit calmly, grooming their whiskers with their small, four-fingered paws, and after a time they vanish with the same silence. They leave nothing behind. No scuff marks in the moss. No displaced air. Only a faint impression, like the memory of weight.

They eat insects. They are tolerated, even encouraged. Families will leave scraps of food near their doorways specifically for the thieves, though this is done without ceremony — a piece of dried thrum-bread placed on a stone, and by morning it will be gone, and by morning a thief will have appeared on someone's shoulder during the evening meal, as though the exchange were not an exchange at all but simply the natural circulation of matter through the Glosspit's quiet ecosystem.

They are not pets. They are not wild. They are something else — something that has achieved a relationship with the human population so thorough and so deeply mutual that the boundary between domestication and coexistence has simply dissolved. A Glosspit child grows up with thieves crawling through their hair. An elder dies with a thief curled against their chest. Neither seems surprised by the other's presence. Neither needs to name it.

**The Birds**

In the highest places — in the crevices where the shelfall meets the raw stone, in the hairline fractures that climb toward some unseen ceiling far overhead — the birds live.

They are black. Predominantly. But "black" is an insult to what their feathers do. In the ambient glow of the shelfall their plumage shifts through iridescent blues, deep greens, and purples so rich they seem to contain actual depth, as though the color were not a surface but a corridor you could fall into. The effect is most pronounced when they move — a glint of wing, a flash of throat — and it fades when they are still, as though their color were a kind of motion, something the feathers only *do* rather than *are*.

They are small — no larger than a fist — and compact, with short rounded wings and strong, grasping feet. They do not build what could be called nests. They roost in the crevices, pressed against the stone in rows, their feathers overlapping like scales, their heads tucked beneath their wings, and they give every appearance of being not alive but carved — small sculptural forms embedded in the rock, glinting faintly, indistinguishable from a cluster of mineral growth if not for the occasional blink.

They make almost no sound. A faint rustle when they shift position. A soft, papery exhalation when they take flight. Their flight is not flight as commonly understood. They do not soar. They *hop-glide* — short, arcing bursts between crevices, their wings beating in quick, almost soundless flurries, their bodies tracing paths that look less like trajectories and more like the way sparks drift upward from a low flame: purposive, brief, beautiful, and then gone.

The people prize their feathers. They wear them in their hair, woven into cord, inlaid in the mortar of their doorframes. They will not explain why. Offer a theory — decoration, ritual, status, superstition — and you will be met with a look so gently uncomprehending that you will realize you have not asked a question so much as revealed your own impoverishment. The feathers are there. The feathers are worn. The birds are there. The birds are not disturbed. This is enough. This is the whole of the relationship, and the whole of the relationship is enough.

**The Insects**

The beetles move as though they are considering a purchase.

Opalescent-shelled, thumbnail-sized, they travel across the moss in slow, deliberate paths, their antennae sweeping in careful arcs, as though they are inspecting each blade of moss, each pebble, each stray flake of shelfall with the meticulous attention of someone examining fruit at a market. They are not hurried. They are not sluggish. They are *deliberate*. Their shells catch the ambient glow of the crystals and break it into faint rainbows — a color that does not exist on the surface, a color that exists only here, in this light, on these backs, and that you begin to think of as belonging to this place in some way that a color should not belong to a place but somehow does.

The crickets are another matter.

Pale. Eyeless. They live in the deeper moss, in the thickest carpets where the darkness is almost complete, and they produce a sound — if you can call it a sound — that is beneath hearing. It is a vibration that enters through the floor, through the soles of your feet, and sits in your sternum like a hollow hum. You feel it before you know it is there. You feel it in the spaces between heartbeats. At night, lying on the moss in the Glosspit, you become aware of a resonance in your own body that does not belong to you, a frequency that your skeleton seems to recognize and your mind does not. The eyeless crickets are singing. You are the instrument.

**The Moss, the Ferns, the Fungal Lights, the Grüm-Grass**

These are alive. Not in the sense that visitors initially assume — not as backdrop, not as scenery, but as *population*. The moss is a living carpet so dense and so deep that it has its own temperature, its own moisture, its own ecology. Step on it and it does not compress. It *yields*. It accepts your weight the way a conversation accepts a new voice: with a brief adjustment, and then a continued hum. In some places the moss is so thick that the people have hollowed rooms into it — not dug, exactly, but *shaped*, pressing the living walls into alcoves and sleeping hollows that slowly reknit themselves over time.

The ferns are the architecture of the upper world — or the upper half, at least. They grow in enormous curtains from the crevices and ledges high above, hanging down like green-gray sheets, perpetually damp, swaying with a motion so slight it might be actual breath. Their fronds are long and fibrous, used by the people for weaving, for rope, for the walls of their dwellings. They harvest the fronds carefully, taking only what has browned at the tip, leaving the growing heart untouched. This is not a policy. It is simply what one does, the way one does not step directly on the moss if it can be avoided. Not out of reverence. Out of *kinship*.

The thrum-grass grows in the wetter zones near the side-channels, in thick spongy carpets of triangular stems that give off a faint metallic smell when crushed. It is gathered and dried and woven into mats and baskets. When it burns — and it does burn, slowly and with a steady, low heat — its smoke has a quality that the people find agreeable. It is the smell of the food-hall. It is the smell of home.

And the fungi. The fungi are the strangest living things in the Glosspit, and perhaps the most beautiful.

They rise from the moss in clusters: translucent stalks, some no taller than a finger, others reaching waist-height, pale and ghostly, faintly luminous in colors that range from a blue-tinged white to a deep, bruised violet. They do not glow brightly. They glow the way a bruise glows when you press it — from the inside, softly, insistently, as though something within them is perpetually on the verge of being spoken. They are not eaten. They are not used. They are simply *there*, growing and glowing and fading and growing again, and the people move among them with the casual indifference of someone walking past a lamppost. The visitors, though, stop. The visitors always stop. They crouch beside the fungal clusters and watch the light pulse faintly within, and they feel something they will not later be able to name — something close to recognition, as though they have seen this light before, in a dream, or in the last moment before sleep, or in the brief instant after a candle is blown out when the afterimage still burns on the inside of the eyelids.

The fungi do not care. The fungi glow for the fungi. And the Glosspit glows for them, and through them, and because of them — a world folded inward, alive with ten thousand small and unhurried lives, all of them glowing, all of them quiet, all of them saying: *we are here, and this is enough, and this is enough, and this is enough.*
