## The Inhabitants of Karstheld

---

You will not see them at first. That is not quite right — you will not *recognize* them at first. They are there. They have always been there. The settlement was not built *for* them in the way you might think; it was built *by* them, which is a different thing entirely. A nest is not built for the bird. It is built by the bird, and then the bird fits itself into the shape it has made.

Start with the hands.

There are handprints on the walls — not painted, not carved, but *stained*. The mineral paste that daubs the light-channels along the vertical seams of the rock is not inert. It responds to pressure, to warmth, to the oils in skin. Whoever touches it leaves a mark that glows faintly for a few hours before fading, a dull amber handprint slowly going dark, like a bruise resolving. You will see them everywhere once you know to look: on the edges of doorframes, on the undersides of catwalks, on the pipes where someone gripped them while welding or threading or simply steadying themselves in the tight corridors. Some of the prints are large — broader than a human palm, with the spacing of fingers that are not quite fingers, not quite tentacles, but something in between that has not yet committed to either name. Some are small. Some are overlapping, one on top of another, as though many individuals passed through the same narrow space, or as though one individual passed through the same narrow space many times.

The prints are warm when you touch them. They are still warm. Some of them.

---

They are not one thing.

Resist the urge to name them. The mind wants a species the way the eye wants a horizon — cleanly, all at once. But what lives in the Warrens is *fractured* across roles, the way a factory floor is fractured into stations. Or perhaps the way a single mind is fractured across tasks — you do not have four arms, but sometimes when you are building something complex, it feels like you do, like your attention has split and each half is operating independently, each hand knowing what the other is doing without being told. They are like that, but more so.

There are those who work the pipes. You will hear them before you see them — not clearly, but in the rhythm of the settlement itself. A scuff of booted feet that are too wide and set too far apart. The scrape of a tool against stone that is not chiseling but *listening*, the way a doctor listens to a chest. They move through the corridors in sequences — never alone, never in crowds, always in pairs or triads, spaced so precisely that they seem to be measuring the tunnels as they walk through them. Their shadows are wrong: too tall, too narrow, moving at a slight offset from the bodies that cast them, as though the light-source — the walls themselves — does not agree on where it is.

There are those who tend the moss. These are smaller, closer to the ground. You will catch a glimpse of them only in peripheral vision — a crouched shape near a steam vent, adjusting something too small to see, a filament of pale growth held between fingers that are too long and too careful. They are the ones who have left the fungal fruiting bodies. You assumed those grew on their own. They did not. They are cultivated — planted in cracks, watered with the warm mineral water, tended with the patience of a practice that predates agriculture by longer than you would like to guess. The ovoids pulse because they are alive, and they are alive because someone feeds them, and someone feeds them because whatever lives here is *processing* something through them. You can taste it if you stand too close to the fruiting bodies for too long: a faint electrical tingle on the tongue, the smell of ozone cut with that overripe sweetness, as though the organisms are converting the waste heat of the generators into something else. Into what, you cannot say.

There are those who build. These are the ones you can almost understand. They work in the dead hours — you will know when the dead hours come, because the thrumming of the generators will shift in pitch, dropping half a tone, and the lingering quality of the sound in the air will double, so that every noise you make exists in two times at once, the present and a half-second ghost that is not an echo but a *memory* the stone keeps. The builders leave their tools in rows along the walls — instruments you do not recognize, some of them curved, some jointed like insect limbs, some that seem to vibrate when you approach them. They do not look up when you enter a room. They do not stop. They work with a coordination that is not mechanical, not choreographed, but *shared* — as though each of them can feel what the others are reaching for before the reach happens. You will watch one of them fit a section of pipe into a joint that has no visible threading, no tolerance, no gap between the inner and outer diameters. It simply *sits*, and the other workers nod — or rather, they make a small, downward tilt of the head that might be a nod or might be something their necks do because their heads are shaped differently and the motion is only analogous.

---

They see you. There is no question of this. The mineral light does not illuminate evenly — it responds to heat, to motion, to the disruption of air, and every time you move, the corridor you are in brightens slightly, the paste along the walls warming and glowing a shade more, as though the place is *looking at you through its own skin*. They have accounted for this. You notice, after a while, that the handprints you have been following — the large ones, the ones you assumed were workers — are not the freshest prints in the settlement. The freshest prints are smaller. Closer together. Five fingers, maybe six, pressed into the light-channel paste at the height of a child's hand — but children who make no sound, who do not appear in any corridor you can find, whose prints simply *arrive* ahead of you as though they have been placed there by someone walking backwards, facing you, already watching.

They do not speak. But the place speaks for them.

You will notice this before you understand it. A pipe shudders in a pattern that is not mechanical — not the random vibration of fluid under pressure, but a *cadence*, a repetition with intervals that are almost rhythmic, almost language. The steam vents hiss in sequences. The generators shift their throb in ways that are too structured to be mechanical and too low to hear clearly, felt only as a change in the pressure behind your sternum, a tightness, a release, a tightness again. The settling is wrong. The settling is deliberate. You stand in the narrow alley between two leaning structures and you feel the sound of your own breathing linger in the air past where it should have dissipated, and for one moment — just one — it overlaps with something else: a faint, warm exhalation that is not yours, that comes from the wall itself, from behind the mineral paste, from somewhere in the stone where the handprints are freshest and the light glows lowest.

It is not a voice. It is the *shape* of a voice, the way a fossil is the shape of a thing that once moved.

---

They are not hostile. This is important. You will feel the urge to be afraid — the silence, the wrongness of the architecture, the way the handprints watch you, the acoustic residue that makes every sound into a sentence that hasn't finished yet. All of this will make you want to draw your weapon, or back toward the access shaft, or call out and demand to know what you have found.

Do not do these things. Not yet.

They are *working*. The settlement is young. The generators are still reaching operating temperature. The moss is still colonizing. The fruiting bodies are still calibrating their pulse. The pipes are still learning the rhythm of whatever they were built to carry. Everything is in its first iteration, and they are tending it with the focused, quiet intensity of people — of *things* — who have been building toward this moment for longer than the freshness of the stone suggests.

They are aware of you in the way you might be aware of rain arriving while you are focused on a task — peripheral, noted, not interrupting. They do not approach. They do not flee. They continue to work in their paired triads, in their crouched tending of the cultivated fungi, in their silent fitting of pipes into joints that should not fit. And from time to time, one of them will pause, tilt its head in that almost-nod, and press a hand flat against the wall, and the wall will glow where it touches, and the glow will linger after the hand is gone, and in that lingering you will read something that is not language and not gesture but occupies the space between:

*Acknowledgment. Surveillance. Patience.*

They are becoming what they will become. So is the place. So, if you stay long enough, might you.
