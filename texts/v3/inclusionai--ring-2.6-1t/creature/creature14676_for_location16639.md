## What Lives in the Settleplain

---

It would be easy to say nothing lives here. It would be wrong, but it would be easy — and the Settleplain has already punished enough certainties to deserve that the wrongness be examined closely.

What lives here is, first and most fundamentally, the ground.

Not metaphorically. Not poetically. The ground is alive in the way a lung is alive — not because it breathes, but because the exchange has been so total and so prolonged that the boundary between the breathing and the breathed has disappeared. You step onto what you think is mud and you are stepping onto a civilization of organisms so dense, so layered, so entangled across deep time, that biology and geology have merged into a single substance that belongs to neither category and has outlived the usefulness of both. Bacteria, archaea, fungal mycelia, mineral colonies — trillions of distinct lineages, each with its own evolutionary history, each now so interwoven with the others and with the inorganic matrix they've colonized that they have become, functionally, a material. A living stone. A thinking mud. A membrane stretched across the bones of a world that finished collapsing before your species had a name for collapse, let alone a word for what comes after.

This is the first layer of inhabitation, and it is the deepest, and it is the one that *you* walk on rather than *in*, which is the only reason you don't immediately recognize it as alive. If it were a forest floor, you would smell decomposition and understand. If it were a coral reef, you would see motion and understand. But here the life is so compressed, so saturated, so thoroughly distributed across every cubic millimeter that it has become invisible precisely by being omnipresent. You are walking on a surface that is, at every point, more organism than mineral, and you call it ground because your language hasn't caught up with what your feet are telling you.

---

Above — or rather, on top of, which is the only directional word that still works here — the lichens.

They deserve their own silence, but the Settleplain doesn't grant silence to anything, so here are the lichens: unnamed, in every language. Colors that exist in the place between colors — a silver-green that could be the memory of jade, a rust that could be the memory of copper or blood, and a blue so dark it swallows the light that falls on it and gives back only a faint iridescence, a shimmer, as if something just beneath the surface were trying to signal you in a wavelength your eyes were never built for. They grow in fractal whorls across every surface — the ground, the ghost-trunks, the partial walls, the bones of fauna that died so long ago they've become part of the conglomerate. They are the last chromatic vocabulary in a world that has mostly surrendered to pewter and bruise-dark and the dim amber of whatever light leaks up from beneath.

They are tentative. They are apologetic. They cover everything, and yet you can stand in the middle of the Settleplain and feel, somehow, that they are fragile — that any disruption, any sudden commitment to existing too loudly, would send them retreating into whatever dormancy allows them to survive in a place that has stopped caring whether anything survives. They do not flower. They do not fruit. They do not perform any of the rituals that your biology teaches you to expect from a living thing that has found a niche. They simply persist, in fractal silence, coating, colonizing, being present at the resolution of a microscope.

---

Then the trees, or what the trees left behind.

What remains are mineral trunks — calcium silicate, hollow, resonant. They stand in sparse clusters, three or five or sometimes just one, rising no more than two or three meters above the universal wet, and they look like the masts of ships that sank on dry land in an epoch so distant that dry land hadn't yet been invented. The Settleplain's atmosphere condenses on them constantly, and moss — another tentative occupant — grows inside their hollows, another layer in the palimpsest. They do not photosynthesize. They do not grow. They stand, and they resonate, and when the deep sound of the place passes through them — which it does, ceaselessly — they vibrate at frequencies you feel in your sternum rather than hear, and for a moment the trunk is a tuning fork for a world that stopped making music a very long time ago.

They are alive in the way a scar is alive. Present, functional, shaped by something that happened once and keeps happening, in attenuated form, forever.

---

The fauna.

You will not see them if you are looking. This is not a metaphor. This is the single most important fact about the fauna of the Settleplain: they are invisible to intentional observation. You must be looking at something else — a ghost-trunk, a hollow, the texture of the wet ground — and then, at the periphery, a shape will detach itself from the dimness. Low. Broad. Half-submerged. Moving with the slow deliberation of a thought arriving at its own conclusion. It will have too many limbs, or not enough, or limbs that exist only when you aren't counting them. Your eyes will slide off its edges as if they've been oiled, as if the creature has coated itself in a thin film of the same perceptual uncertainty that governs distances and time here.

It will pass through the space you occupied a moment before. The space will briefly become louder.

This is not metaphor either. The displacement of a body that has learned to fold itself around perception creates a vacuum — not physical, but acoustic — and the roar of the place rushes in to fill it, and for a half-second the chord shifts, and something in your chest registers the shift as absence, or loss, or the memory of almost-seeing.

They are rare, but the place is not barren. They are rare because they have learned — over aeons so vast they have become geological features — that *being seen* here is a liability. What hunts in the Settleplain hunts by noticing, by the sudden registration of *something that is not the Settleplain*, and so the fauna have become the Settleplain. They are the place's way of being alive without announcing it. They exist in the gaps between perception, and they are not hiding — they have simply become so integrated with the texture of the place that the act of perceiving them requires a kind of attention the place itself does not encourage.

No one has touched one. No one has reported what it feels like, because the people who stay long enough to try have usually stopped reporting in the conventional sense. They start humming the chord. They start bending around distances. They begin, slowly and without noticing, to become another layer in the crust.

---

The structures.

They do not live. But they *presence*, which may be a different verb for the same activity the fauna perform. A wall that stands seven courses high and then simply declines to be a wall. A foundation that extends forty meters in one direction and refuses all others. A single arch that spans nothing with an architectural patience that borders on devotion. They are made of the same mineral-conglomerate as the ground, and the question of whether they were built from the landscape or into it is meaningless — it presupposes a boundary between construction and substance that the Settleplain dissolved before your species existed.

Inside them, sometimes, a hollow. Fist-sized. Perfectly smooth. Warm to the touch — not hot, but warm the way a living thing is warm, the way bread is warm, the way a sleeping animal is warm. They do not contain anything. They are simply *present*, like closed eyes, like held breath, like the pause between two halves of a chord. They appear without pattern. They serve no identifiable function. They are the most intimate objects in a place that has outgrown intimacy, and yet they are the closest thing the Settleplain offers to an invitation — not to enter, not to understand, but simply to place your hand against the warmth and feel that something, somewhere, sometime, was making space for you.

---

And then there are the long-term occupants, though *occupants* implies a choice, and *long-term* implies a clock, neither of which survives sustained exposure to the Settleplain.

They are — or they were — whatever walked into this place and did not leave. Or could not leave. Or discovered, over weeks and months and years, that the distance between inside and outside had stopped being a fact and had become, like everything else here, a suggestion. They report — in halting voices, in fragments recorded on devices that function intermittently and record with increasing unreliability — that the place adjusts to them. That over time, distances stabilize, but only around *their* bodies, as if the Settleplain is learning the shape of their presence and conforming itself to it, the way a river conforms to a stone. That the time-stutters become less disorienting, not because they diminish, but because the self that experiences them becomes more permeable, more capable of holding contradiction without flinching.

They do not age in any conventional sense. They do not starve — the ground provides, in the way the ground provides everywhere, through the membrane, through the wet, through the warm composite that is no longer food or substrate or flesh but simply *material*, interchangeable, democratic. They lose the edges of themselves first: the certainty of where their skin ends and the air begins. Then the certainty of where their thoughts end and the sound begins. Then the certainty that the sound is external at all.

And then they are just another layer in the crust. Another warmth inside a hollow. Another frequency in the chord. Another presence that has folded itself into the dense, ancient, saturated, roaring membrane of a place that was finished with edges before the first stone was eroded into softness, and has been waiting — patiently, without intention, without language, without anything that could be called desire — for company.
