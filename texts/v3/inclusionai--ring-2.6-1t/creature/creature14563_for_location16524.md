## Inhabitants of the Cleave

---

### The Clingers

They are the first thing you understand about the Cleave, and the last thing you stop seeing. In the days — weeks, months — after you leave, they will appear behind your eyelids: those flattened bodies pressed into the dark like pressed flowers in a book that no one will ever open.

They are not bats. They are not birds. They belong to no branch you can name. Think of them as what would happen if a hand were given the desire to fly — the fingers extended and extended still further, the thumb remaining as a small hooked anchor, the membrane between pulled taut with struts of cartilage so fine they look like the rigging of a ship made from spider silk and wishbone. Six fingers. Sometimes seven. The extra digit is vestigial, a small nub that juts from the leading edge of the wing and serves no aerodynamic purpose that you can discern, though the creatures themselves seem to use it as a kind of tactile antenna, sweeping it ahead of them as they crawl across the stone, feeling for the edge of a ledge or the body of a neighbor.

Their faces are broad and flat, almost like someone pressed a nose into clay and then forgot to pull it out. The nostrils are vestigial slits. There are eyes — small, dark, wet — but the eyes do not do the heavy work. The heavy work is done by the feet. Each clawed toe is ridged with pads that feel, with a sensitivity your fingertips would envy, the smallest tremor in the stone. A footfall three ledges above. The shift of gravel on a shelf forty feet to the left. The slow exhalation of another creature breathing in its sleep two bodies away from them on the same square foot of rock. They read the Cleave the way you would read a page, each vibration a letter, each sequence of vibrations a word, until the whole chattering mass of them becomes a single sentence pressed against the wall.

They are silent in flight. This is the detail that stays with you. You do not see them — not easily, not at first — because the moss-light is thin and the darkness below is thick, and they move through both without sound. The only noise they make is incidental: the clicking of cartilage on cartilage as a wing folds or unfolds, the small dry percussion of a claw finding purchase on stone. In silence, at speed, in the dark, they are almost impossible to follow. You lose them. They reappear in a place you did not expect, already landed, already still, already reading the stone again with those impossible feet.

They eat the moss. They eat it the way sheep eat grass — methodically, moving along the wall in slow processions, their blunt teeth rasping the colonies from the stone. They eat it without killing it. There is always enough left to grow back. This is not mercy. This is arithmetic. They learned it the way their ancestors learned it: too slowly for anyone to watch, but the ones who ate everything died, and the ones who left some alive did not, and so the survivors are the ones you see now, grazing their thin blue-green pastures with the patience of animals who understand that starvation is always a possibility and so plan for it without thinking.

They are warm. This matters. In a place this cold, this damp, this high, warmth is the most valuable currency, and they spend it freely. They press against each other — flank to flank, belly to back — sharing heat the way a fire shares heat, the outermost ones colder, the innermost ones warmer, and all of them accepting the arrangement without resistance. The young do not cry. They do not fight for position. They fold themselves into the gaps — between a shoulder blade and a spine, beneath a wing, against the soft underbelly of an adult where the membrane is thin and the warmth passes through — and there they stay until they are large enough to find their own purchase. An infant clinger does not choose its place. Its place chooses it: wherever it fits.

On the upper ledges, closer to the wound of grey-white sky, the colonies are thinner. The moss is sparser. The animals are leaner. They are, in their way, more beautiful here — there is more of the stone visible, more of the raw fracture-face, and the occasional silhouette against the pale light from above has a spareness to it that looks almost holy. But holiness is a word for something that stands apart, and nothing in the Cleave stands apart. Everything is entangled. Everything is a single surface with things clinging to it.

### The Moss

Before the clingers, there was the moss. Before the moss, there was the stone. The stone came first — it always does — but it is not alive. The moss is the beginning of alive here.

It grows in colonies no larger than a cupped hand, pressed flat against the walls and the few horizontal surfaces that exist. In color it is the blue-green of a dying screen, of deep water seen from above, of the last light before the sky forgets what color it was. It pulses. Not uniformly. Not in rhythm. Each colony pulses to its own arrhythmic schedule, brightening and dimming in slow waves that roll across its surface like something breathing beneath a sheet. When two colonies are close — near enough that their light overlaps — the pulses sometimes synchronize for a moment, three or four beats in unison, before drifting apart again. No one has explained this. No one has tried. The explanation probably involves chemistry: molecules diffusing across the thin film of condensation that coats the stone, one colony's byproduct becoming another colony's signal. But you do not need chemistry to feel what is happening. You need only watch. They are talking. They are saying *here* and *now* and *still here* in a language made of light.

The moss feeds on mineral seepage — the pale veins of quartz and calcium and unnamed stone that thread through the rock and, very slowly, dissolve into moisture that creeps along the surface. It feeds on condensation. It feeds on the thin UV light that filters down from above through the wound in the mountain's skin, though not much light reaches this deep. The moss has learned to feed on almost nothing and to make of that almost-nothing a small, persistent glow. It is the bottom of the chain. It is the foundation. Every living thing in the Cleave is, in the end, a way of eating moss.

### The Larger Ones

You know them only in pieces.

A shadow passing across a patch of light. A shape rising on a downstroke so slow it seems to hang in the air like a thought forming — the wingspan enormous, the fingers trailing behind the body like the legs of something pulled up from an ocean trench, each one long enough to bend and hook back on itself. The body is heavy. You can tell by the way it moves: not the darting, nervous energy of the clingers but a slow, liquid patience, the unhurried confidence of something that knows the dark belongs to it.

The larger ones hunt the clingers. They hunt the moss. They are both predator and grazer, flexible in their appetites the way something must be when the world it lives in offers so little to choose from. When they pass through a moss colony, the light dims — not entirely, but noticeably — and you understand with a sick lurch in your stomach that they are not merely disturbing it with displaced air. They are consuming it somehow, directly, with a chemical exhalation, breathing the moss into dissolution and swallowing what remains. The colony recovers afterward. Dimly. Slowly. But it recovers, which suggests the larger ones do not take all of it either. There is a grammar to this. There are rules. The Cleave enforces them.

They do not click the way the clingers click. Their wing joints are larger, heavier, and the sound they make is lower — a deep, resonant thud, like a stone dropped into deep water. You hear it from below and feel it in your sternum. They are patient. They wait on the lower ledges and in the crevices that the clingers cannot reach, folded into the dark with their wings wrapped tight, their oversized fingers curled inward like the legs of a spider at rest. They do not sleep in the way you would recognize. They simply go still. Go quiet. And then, slowly, they begin again.

You never see the young of the larger ones. This is either because the young are hidden in a deeper part of the Cleave that no one has seen, or because the young are born already capable of hiding, or because the larger ones do not have young in any way you would understand the word. You will not resolve this. Sit with it.

### The Ones Who Were Here

They were here. That is all you can say.

The circle of stones at the base of the Cleave is not large — no wider than the span of two outstretched arms — but it is deliberate in a way that nothing else in this place is deliberate. The clingers do not build. The larger ones do not build. The moss does not build. The circle was made by hands, or by something that served as hands, and it was made with care: each stone chosen for its weight and smoothness, placed at a slight inward angle as though the circle were meant to hold something in, or keep something out, or mark the boundary between one kind of belonging and another.

The concentric arcs scratched into the granite are older than the stones, or perhaps younger — you cannot tell. The mineral patina does not give you enough. The lines are deep and confident, made by something harder than the stone itself, or by something softer that was applied to the stone with tremendous pressure and tremendous repetition. They are not writing. They are not decoration. They are the trace of a mind that needed to say *I was here and I understood something and I had no language for it so I drew the shape of the understanding instead.* 

The tools are scattered around the circle and the carvings as though whoever made them was interrupted — but not violently. The hand-axe was set down on a flat stone with its edge facing up, as though the work it was being used for required both hands and the work was expected to resume. The cordage lies in a loose coil, still supple enough in the thin, dry air that it might have been laid down yesterday. The bundle of sticks, bound with sinew, leans against a rock at an angle too precise to be accidental — it was part of something. A frame. A support. A structure that was being built or taken apart or prepared for a purpose you will not learn.

No bodies. No remains. No burial, no campfire charcoal, no refuse. Whoever was here left the way they arrived: completely, and in a way that made the leaving look like the place's own idea.

It is possible they are still here, in the sense that the circle is still here, in the sense that the carvings are still here, in the sense that the Cleave remembers them the way stone remembers everything — slowly, incompletely, but with a fidelity that outlasts bone.

---

### What Lives Here

What lives here is *closeness*. It is the pressure of warm bodies against cold stone, the way every surface is claimed, the way the young fold into the adults without ceremony, the way the large ones fold into the dark without resistance. It is the moss talking to itself in pulses of light too faint to be called language and too persistent to be called silence. It is the clicking of joints and the settling of rock and the slow, mineral breath of a place that is still becoming itself.

What lives here is a chain so simple it could hardly be called a chain: stone gives mineral, mineral gives moss, moss gives life, life gives back to stone. And somewhere at the edge of that chain, at the very beginning or the very end — it is hard to tell which — someone once knelt on cold granite and drew arcs in the stone with something harder, and then stood, and walked, and did not come back.

What lives here is the sound. The wet shuffling. The clicking. The slow tearing of a world still pulling itself into its final shape. The Cleave listens to itself, and what it hears is not silence, and not absence, but a density of presence so thick it has weight, it has texture, it pools in the dark like water, like stone, like life pressing itself flat against the walls of the only home it has ever known.

Something lives here. Something has always lived here. The stone knows. The moss glows to say it. The winged things press close together in the dark to keep it warm.
