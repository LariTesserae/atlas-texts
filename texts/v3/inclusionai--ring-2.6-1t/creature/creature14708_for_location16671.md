## What Lives in the Underseam

---

Start with the small things, because they are the most honest.

They come out when the machines cycle — a sound like a wet exhale through iron — and emerge from cracks, from grates, from the seams in floor plates where the standing water pools shallowest. Pale. Translucent in the way certain deep-water fish are translucent, not fragile but *absent* of opacity, so that when you hold one up to the light — and you will hold one up to the light, eventually, because you will try to understand it — you see the dark smears of organs suspended in something that is not quite gel and not quite blood. They range in size from the head of a pin to the length of a forearm, and their limbs are segmented, jointed in ways that suggest evolution borrowed from insects and then lost interest, handed the blueprint to something else, something that finished the work with a different sense of purpose.

They have no eyes. Where eyes would be, the skin — if it is skin — is smooth and faintly concave, as if something had pressed inward from the inside, or as if they are perpetually listening. They do not listen. They *vibrate*. When they cluster — and they cluster always, never singly — the air above them shimmers with a subsonic hum you feel in your molars. Colonies of hundreds gather on warm surfaces: the housings of operating machines, the iron ribs of pipes that carry something heated from deep below, the convex metal panels that pulse with amber light. They feed on heat and condensation and the fine metallic dust that coats every inch of the Underseam. They feed on each other. They are not cruel about it. It is simply the arrangement.

If you sit still on a ledge above one of their colonies, you will see them perform what can only be called rituals. Not purposeful in the way a machine is purposeful — they have no visible function, no nest being built, no food being stored — but structured. They arrange themselves in spirals. They pulse in sequence, light moving through the colony in a wave, a ripple of faint bioluminescence that might be communication or might be the reflection of the mold-light on their wet skin. They climb on one another's backs in columns that rise and collapse. They do this whether or not anyone is watching.

They are, as far as you can tell, the most populous residents of the Underseam.

---

Then the mid-sized things.

They are harder to describe because you never quite see them. You see where they have been. A smooth trail in the standing water — not a wake, exactly, more like a groove pressed into the surface, as if something broad and flat had been dragged along the bottom just beneath the film. The trail appears, extends for ten meters or thirty, and then simply stops. No body. No disturbance at the end. Just the water, closing over the groove like a wound healing.

They are low. That is the first thing you understand about them, before you understand anything else — they are *low to the ground*, or low to the water, built along an axis of horizontal intent. Many-legged; you have counted, in the margins of your vision, as many as twelve limbs moving in a coordination so fluid it looks like the creature is not walking but *flowing* across the surface of the water. The body, what little of it you have caught in peripheral light, sits just above the waterline, dark, close to the ground, wider than it is tall. It does not splash. It does not ripple the standing water. It absorbs sound. The silence that precedes one — and there is always a silence that precedes one, as if the Underseam holds its breath — is not the absence of noise but the *presence* of a particular quality of quiet, dense and deliberate, like felt.

No one has touched one. No one wants to. Not out of fear, exactly, but out of a deep and bodily intuition, the kind that lives in the spine and predates language: *do not reach into the dark water for that shape.*

They are not hostile. They are not anything. They move through the flooded corridors the way weather moves through the world above — present, inevitable, unconcerned with you. Sometimes, in a long stretch of corridor lit by a dying fluorescent tube, you will see a set of wet footprints that are not footprints — too many digits, too evenly spaced, pressed into a thin film of condensation on the floor — leading from a shaft that opens into darkness and ending at a wall, where they simply stop, as if the thing that made them walked into the stone and was absorbed.

---

The bird-things are easier, because they announce themselves, though you would rather they didn't.

They live high. In the arched spaces where the ceiling reaches its apex, straddling iron beams and the junctions of load-bearing walls, they build nests from wire, rags, fragments of polymer conduit, and the shed skins of the pale colonies below. Dense feathers, oily, the color of wet slate, overlapping like roof tiles. They are flightless — their wings are small, vestigial, articulated like arms more than wings, and they use them to brace themselves against surfaces, to pull themselves along ledges, to gesture at each other in a language that is mostly territorial and mostly about warmth. They are warm to the touch. The heat radiates from their chests in a way that suggests something internal, something metabolic and relentless, a core temperature that the Underseam's cool indifference has not been able to dampen.

Their calls are low. So low they are almost below hearing — you feel them in your sternum, in the cartilage of your ribs, as a thrumming, a resonance, as if someone has struck a bell wrapped in cloth. At dusk. There is no dusk in the Underseam — there is no dusk — but the bird-things call at the time that would be dusk if the sun had any bearing on this place, and their calls are answered, from far chambers, from shafts that descend into unmapped dark, by other bird-things, and for a moment the whole buried district is threaded with a sound that is almost, but not quite, a song.

They are not afraid of people. If you climb to their level — and this requires scaffolding, or pipes, or the iron catwalks whose diamond-plate treads have been worn to a mirror shine by generations of clawed feet — they will look at you with small, bright, black eyes and regard you the way a cat regards a piece of furniture that has been moved. With mild displeasure. With the calm certainty that the arrangement is wrong and they will wait, because the arrangement always comes back.

---

And then: the tallies.

A wall in the deepest part of the residential quarter. Smooth stone — older than the brick above it, older than the iron above that — covered in scratches. Not carved, exactly. Scratched, with something sharp, something that was held in a hand. Tallies in columns: groups of five, with a diagonal crossing stroke, the most ancient counting system on earth. But also: columns of different widths, different depths, different spacing. Some columns are full — dozens, hundreds of marks — and others are barely begun: four strokes, five, then nothing. As if someone started counting, then stopped, then started again elsewhere.

And beside the tallies, in a different hand — or a different tool, or a different *kind* of hand — there are marks that are not tallies. Curves. Repeating patterns. Not language. Not yet. Not quite. But structured. Rhythmic. The way a heartbeat is structured. The way dripping is structured. You look at them and you feel, without being able to articulate it, that someone — or something — was trying to say *this is how long I have been here,* and that the saying of it was more important than the thing being said.

The stool at the steel table — eight stools, eight places set, all pulled out, all abandoned — faces the wall of tallies. Whoever sat there, whoever gathered in that plaza for whatever passed for conversation in the Underseam, they were looking at that wall. They were counting.

---

There is one more thing. You will not see it the first time, or the second. Perhaps the fifth, in a corridor where the water is shinier than elsewhere, where the condensation runs thick on an iron beam overhead and the light — the amber, steady, inexplicable amber light of the oldest surviving lamp — throws everything into a warmth that feels almost like memory.

A shape at the edge of the water. Not the low many-legged thing. Not the pale colonies surfacing to vibrate. Something upright. Something that stands in the knee-deep water and does not move. The shape is indistinct — you will tell yourself it is a trick of the light, a shadow cast by the amber glow on the wet stone, a distortion of the condensation on the beam above — but it has proportions. Legs, torso, something that might be a head tilted slightly downward. It does not approach. It does not retreat. It stands at the edge of the water the way a person stands at the edge of a shore, looking out at something vast and indifferent and old.

And then you blink, or the fluorescent tube in the corridor above you catches, or a pipe groans and your attention shifts for half a second, and the shape is not there anymore. The water closes over the space it occupied without a ripple. The amber lamp pulses once, steadies. The dripping continues.

You will not speak of this. Not because you are afraid, but because language does not hold it. It was never meant to be held. It was meant to be the way the Underseam holds everything else: in silence, in water, in the slow amber pulse of machines that no longer remember what they were made to do, and do it still.

---

The Underseam does not have citizens. It has *participants*. Things that have been drawn into its processes the way sediment is drawn into still water: slowly, quietly, and without the option of leaving. The pale colonies participate in feeding. The low shapes participate in moving through. The bird-things participate in calling. The molds participate in growing. The tallies participate in counting. And the shape in the water — if it is a shape, if it is a thing and not a trick of the light and the copper-sweet air and the weight of all that stone above pressing down — participates in watching.

You are a participant now. You know this the moment your boots sink slightly into the wet flagstone, the moment the silence adjusts to include the sound of your breathing. The Underseam has not welcomed you. It has simply made room, the way water makes room for a stone dropped into it — not with welcome, but with the inevitability of a process that has been running for longer than you have existed, and will continue running long after you have climbed back to the surface, blinking in the light of a world that will feel, for a while, impossibly thin.
