## What Lives in the Quiet Loud

---

They do not notice you. That is the first and most important thing.

You could stand in the middle of a shelf and remain still for an hour, and nothing would alter its course around you. The rivers of clicking things would part around your boots and close behind you. The grazers on the wall would not shift their ancient gaze. The fliers would pass through the space you occupy as though you were a column of stone — not hostile, not friendly, simply *unregistered*. You are not in their world. You are in the same room as their world, and their world does not acknowledge yours. This is what allows you to watch.

---

Begin with the ones underfoot.

They are the most numerous and the least considered, which is exactly how they prefer it — not that preference is the right word. Instinct, then. Habit written into the body so deeply it has become architecture. The small ones — the finger-joint-sized ones — move in sheets. You will look at a stretch of rubble and see it seething, a carpet of dark segmented bodies catching the blue glow in their varnished plates, and you will think *insect*, but that word is too small and too terrestrial for what you are seeing. These are creatures built for a world that has no up and no down, only surface. Their legs — six, eight, it varies — work in waves that ripple from head to tail with a coordination that looks choreographed but is chemical: pheromone trails laid down and read and followed with a fidelity that would shame any compass.

They click as they walk. Not mechanically — not like the ticking of a clock — but with a brittle, organic quality, as though their bodies are made of the same material as the stone they cross. And in a sense they are. Their chitin is calcareous, mineral-dark, shot through with faint veins of quartz that glitter when the bioluminescence catches them right. They are dressed in the same stone they walk upon. They are the gorge wearing legs.

The larger ones — the forearm-sized — are a different register of the same song. They move alone or in loose pairs, not in the rivers of the smaller kind. Their bodies are heavier, more plated, and they move with a deliberateness that borders on ceremony. Each leg is placed. Each pause is intentional. They will find a seep — a thin dark thread of water weeping from a crack — and they will lower their heads to it and remain there for minutes, drinking with a slow, rhythmic motion of their mouthparts, and you will understand that in this gorge, to drink is to pray. The water is the source of everything. The mosses grow along it. The glow-things cluster near it. The grazers patrol its trails. And these large clicking ones — the ones you notice least because they move so carefully — they are the keepers of the water's path, mapping its course in their bodies, carrying the memory of where it has been and therefore where it will be again.

---

Now look at the walls.

The grazers are impossible to see at first because they do not move the way you expect living things to move. They move the way weather moves. You glance at a rock face and see a broad, darkened shape — maybe the size of your hand, maybe twice that — and you think it is a stain, a shadow, a trick of the glow. And then, minutes later, when you glance again, it has shifted. Not far. An inch, maybe two. And you realize it has been moving this entire time, with a patience so total it has become indistinguishable from stillness.

They are heavy. You can tell by the way they cling — broad, muscular feet with suction pads the color of old rubber, pressed flat against the stone with a grip so tenacious that even the glow-things seem to grow around them rather than beneath them. Their bodies are soft, pale, almost translucent where the light passes through the thinner edges of their mantle. They have no eyes you can find, or at least none that answer to the word. They orient by the gradient of the glow on the wall — they move toward it, slowly, inexorably, grazing the luminous colonies with a rasping mouth that leaves a trail of slime.

And this slime — this is the part no one tells you about, the part you discover only by accident, by kneeling too close to a wall and catching the light at the right angle. The trails glow. Faintly. A ghostly afterimage of the organism's passage, visible for minutes before it fades. The grazers carry the light with them. They eat it and they secrete it, altered somehow, shifted in wavelength, so that the walls of the gorge are not merely lit from within by the stationary colonies but are also *traced* by the slow passage of the grazers — a palimpsest of movement written in blue fire. The gorge remembers everything that touches it.

Higher up, in the overhangs and the narrows where the walls pinch together and the air thickens with moisture, the colony-dwellers hold their positions like gargoyles on a cathedral. Mussel-shaped. Barnacled. Shells of dark iridescent blue so deep they look like bruises in the rock. They open and close in slow rhythm — not in response to anything you can see, not to feed, not to hide, but simply because opening and closing is what they *do*, the way a heart beats. They filter the damp air through gill-like structures inside their shells, drawing from it nutrients that exist only as vapor, as trace mineral suspended in moisture. They are the ones who eat the air itself. They are why the gorge breathes.

Their shells, if you find a shed one — rare but not impossible — are impossibly light, layered like nacre, and they refract the bioluminescent glow into colors that should not exist in a place with no sun. Tiny rainbows. Fleeting, gone as soon as you move.

---

But it is the fliers who define this place. They are its voice, its motion, its nervous system. They are the ones who make the sound you heard before you saw anything — the clicking, the chittering, the overlapping calls that fill the gorge like water fills a basin.

The small ones dart. That is the only word for it. They appear at the edge of your vision and vanish before you can focus, quick and angular, moving in trajectories that seem random but are not — each one following invisible highways through the shafts of pale light, each one avoiding the others with a precision that looks like grace but is really mathematics. They are the gnats, the sparks, the pixel-flutter of a world too complex to render all at once. Their wings are nearly transparent, veined with the faintest tracery of luminescence that catches when they cross through a glow-shaft, so that for a moment they look like shards of stained glass thrown into the air.

The large ones are another matter entirely.

You hear them before you see them — a slow, resonant beating, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. Their wings are broad, leathery, folded along bodies that are surprisingly compact, armored in a way the smaller fliers are not. They do not dart. They *process*. They move in slow, deliberate circuits through the upper reaches of the gorge, high enough that the ledges blur beneath them, navigating by some combination of echo and glow that you will never fully understand. When one passes through a light-shaft, you see it whole for perhaps two seconds: the dark shape, the spread of wings, the blur of motion — and then it is gone, and the shaft is empty again, and you are left with an afterimage that burns at the center of your vision for minutes.

They are the nearest thing this gorge has to large mammals. They are the ones who, when they land, cause the smaller creatures to scatter. They are the ones whose silence, when they pass over a section of wall, makes the gorge hold its breath. Not because the other creatures are afraid — or not only because of that. But because the large fliers carry with them a displacement of air, a shift in the pressure and temperature and sound, that the gorge's life has evolved to read. A shadow. A downdraft. A momentary drop in the hum. And everything, for a half-second, goes quiet.

Then it rebuilds.

---

And beneath all of this, holding it all up, feeding it all, the glow-things persist.

They are the closest thing the gorge has to a soul. Not because they are beautiful, though they are — not because they are alive in some dramatic, recognizable way, but because they are *present* in a way nothing else is. They coat the walls in thin veils, crusts, films — you cannot quite call them colonies because a colony implies individual things working together, and the glow-things seem less like individuals and more like a single substance that has chosen to exist in many places at once. They are not plant. Not animal. Not fungus. They are something older than that decision. They sit on the stone and they convert the mineral energy of the gorge — the slow chemistry of water and rock and pressure and time — into light and biomass. They are the foundation. They are the reason anything else can live here at all.

And they pulse.

Rhythmically. In patterns that shift and reform, wave after wave, like a conversation conducted in light. Whether they are communicating with each other or simply responding to the chemistry of their environment, you cannot tell. But the rhythm is there. It is constant. And when you sit in the gorge long enough — hours, maybe, just watching the walls — you begin to feel that the rhythm is not *in* the glow-things but *around* them, that they are keeping time with something larger, something you cannot hear or see but can only sense as a pressure in your chest, a resonance in your teeth.

The gorge hums. The glow-things pulse. The water drips. And everything that lives here — the clicking multitudes, the patient grazers, the armored fliers, the filter-feeding shells, the shadow-predators you never quite see — all of them are part of this rhythm. All of them are the gorge expressing itself in muscle and chitin and light.

You are not in their world. But for a moment, standing there with the sound filling your chest and the blue glow washing over the walls and the slow procession of ancient wings circling above you in the dim — for a moment, the boundary thins. And you feel it: not that you belong here, but that here belongs to the same silence you carry inside you. The same deep, mineral patience. The same quiet loud.

Nothing has changed. Nothing will.
