## Inhabitants of the Pérambe

---

### I. The Coronal

To say the Coronal *live* is to use the word the way you'd use *weather* — not as a description of a thing but as an admission that something is happening at a scale too large and too slow to be pinned down. They do not behave like organisms. They do not grow in any way a biologist could confirm. They do not visibly respire. And yet they are, in the most fundamental and unnegotiable sense, alive: they metabolize, they persist, they transform what they touch, and on the longest of timescales, they *reproduce* — though reproduction here means something closer to inheritance than birth, a seed released only upon death, carrying forward an architecture of cellulose and mineral uptake that has been refining itself for longer than anything human has stood upright.

What they are, most precisely, is *colossal*. Each one is a columnar trunk-complex — not a single stem but a braided lattice of woody tissue, amber-dark, spiraling inward with a slow deliberateness that recalls the logic of nautilus shells or the growth patterns of galaxies. The bark plates are the color and texture of ancient architectural stone, and this is not merely a resemblance. Over the millennia the Coronal and what is built upon and within them have exchanged traits, the way two languages trading words eventually begin to share grammar. The bark fissures into courses. The root buttresses widen into plinths. The branch-cantilevers extend with the structural logic of flying buttresses, and in their crooks, where secondary growth has filled the angles with dense living wood, rooms have formed — humid, warm, threaded with fungal infrastructure that functions, unmistakably, as insulation and plumbing.

They are patient in a way that has no analogy. A Coronal standing at the edge of Derove may have been standing there when the substrate of the plateau was still warm with the heat of whatever geological event raised it. Its roots descend thirty meters into the gneiss, and beneath that — no one knows. The drill teams that tried to find out reported that the boreholes, once they passed a certain depth, simply *arrived elsewhere*. Not collapsed. Not deflected. Arrived. As though the root system had been conducting them, the way a vascular system conducts a tracer dye.

The oldest Coronal are the ones the settlement is built around, and the newer ones ring them outward in a pattern that, seen from above, resembles dendritic crystallization — or a nervous system. Whether this is coincidence, ecology, or spatial-temporal causality no one can say. The surveyors have given the largest individuals names — the Amber Column, the Pewter Spire, the Twelve Galleries — and these names are the only coordinates anyone trusts, because the distances between them shift. Slowly. Rhythmically. Just below the threshold of perception, just above the threshold of measurement. The plateau breathes, and the Coronal breathe with it, and whether they are breathing *in* or *out* determines whether Derove is compact or vast on any given week.

Inside the oldest individuals, the hollows extend deep. Vertical shafts connect to horizontal galleries that branch and rebranch in patterns that echo — but do not exactly replicate — the architecture of the buildings the settlers have improvised within them. Vapor barriers line the walls. Electric lights string along the ceilings like bioluminescent analogues of the mosses growing outside. The air inside is thick — warm, humid, threaded with the chemical scent of active fungal networks and the faint ozone tang of something else, something that the chemical analysis teams have never been able to identify with certainty. It is not dangerous. It is not exactly safe either. It is simply *present*, the way a heartbeat is present: not a thing you can isolate, but a rhythm you cannot ignore.

The Coronal do not respond to stimuli. They do not bend toward light or retract from damage or signal to one another in any way that instruments can detect. And yet there is a persistent, unconfirmable sense — reported by almost every long-term resident of the interior hollows — that the trees are *aware*. Not sentient, perhaps, not in any way that maps onto animal cognition. But aware in the way a vast, distributed system is aware: each root-tip a sensor, each hollow a chamber, each branch-cantilever an antenna reading the wind for information the way a radio reads the electromagnetic spectrum. The Coronal know what is happening on the plateau. They have always known. They simply have no mechanism for letting anyone know that they know, and no one has been able to determine whether this is because they cannot communicate or because they see no reason to.

### II. The People of Derove

They are not one people. They arrived from elsewhere — from lowland cities, from coastal processing towns, from the agricultural communes of the southern river basins — and they arrived over several generations, drawn by the thermal extraction contracts, the mineral rights, the quiet promise that the plateau offered work that was difficult but steady. They are engineers, mechanics, surveyors, geochemists, logistics coordinators, and the administrative class that keeps all of these people paid and fed and marginally functional in a place where the ground itself cannot be trusted to stay where it was yesterday.

There are between eight hundred and twelve hundred of them at any given time. They live in the gaps — in the spaces between Coronal columns where the branch-cantilevers form natural walls and the dew collects in perpetual pools. Some inhabit the low, thick-walled buildings of cut gneiss and salvaged metal that have been constructed into the root systems as though the trees simply coughed up rooms over the centuries and someone moved in. Others — the ones attached to the older, larger Coronal — live *inside*, in the vertical hollows and horizontal galleries, following electric lights upward through trunk-complexes that do not acknowledge the distinction between inside and outside in any way that human architecture would recognize.

They are pragmatic. They do not talk about the spatial anomalies in social settings. This is not denial; it is a survival strategy born of decades of coexistence with an environment that refuses to hold still. The most experienced residents develop what the settlement's occupational psychologists — themselves pragmatic people — have termed "flexible certainty": the ability to walk a route they have walked a hundred times, note that the distance has changed by some unmeasurable fraction, and adjust without stopping, without looking back, without the microsecond of hesitation that betrays the fear of not knowing whether the world is stable.

Children grow up here. They learn, earlier than children anywhere else on the continent, that space is not a fixed property of the world but a negotiation between the objects in it. They develop an uncanny sense of orientation — not magnetic, not landmark-based, but relational, keyed to the Coronal themselves, the only things on the plateau that do not move. Derove's children navigate by tree, the way sailors navigate by star. And like sailors, they learn that the frame of reference itself is in motion, and that the skill is not in resisting that motion but in feeling it, the way a sailor feels the set of the current beneath the hull.

They are not insular. They are not unfriendly. They are simply *tired*, in the way that people who spend their working lives maintaining infrastructure in a fundamentally unstable environment become tired — not of the work itself, but of the quiet, constant negotiation with a world that will not hold still. They drink in the communal mess halls. They trade shifts. They maintain the steel-grate roads between nodes, re-tensioning the electrical lines, replacing the expansion joints in the pipelines that flex with the plateau's slow spatial breathing. They have a dry, understated humor. They call the fluctuations "the Breathing." They call the fog "the ceiling." They call the time they spend walking between nodes "the space between thoughts," because sometimes, mid-walk, there is a moment — just a moment — where the landscape seems to hesitate, as though it is deciding where to put itself, and the walker feels, for an instant, that they are not on a landscape at all but inside a mind that is thinking them into position.

They do not say this out loud.

### III. The Crustaceans of the Dew-Pools

In the hollows where the branch-cantilevers curve downward and condensation pools in perpetual gleam, there live small creatures — no larger than a thumbnail — that are the closest thing the Pérambe has to a conventional fauna. They are translucent. Their internal anatomy is faintly visible through their carapaces: a tracery of gut, a dark knot of neural tissue, filaments of gill-like structures that extract oxygen from the humid air. They are crustaceans, though their taxonomy resists classification; their closest known relatives are freshwater species found nowhere within a thousand kilometers of the plateau. The working hypothesis is that their ancestors were trapped in the Coronal's hollows millennia ago, when the trees sealed themselves during some long-forgotten climatic event, and that they have been evolving in isolation — in the perpetual twilight of living wood and standing water — ever since.

They are pale. Almost colorless. In the bioluminescent glow of the mosses that line the dew-pool margins, they appear as faint shapes moving through light, like thoughts drifting through a half-asleep mind. They feed on the fungal networks that thread through the walls of the hollows, and in turn they are fed upon by nothing — there are no predators on the plateau large enough to hunt them. They exist in a kind of biological stasis, population cycling gently between boom and scarcity in time with the condensation cycle of the Coronal's canopy.

No one studies them closely. They are too small, too obscure, and too difficult to culture outside their dew-pools to attract sustained attention. But the children of Derove sometimes collect them in glass jars and hold them up to the electric lights, watching their translucent bodies glow faintly with the reflected luminescence of the mosses, and in this small, unremarkable act — a child holding a jar of ancient, light-drunk creatures in a tree that is older than language — something of the Pérambe's essence is briefly, perfectly visible.

### IV. The Fog

The fog does not roll in. It *settles*, as the description has it — but to say this is to describe a meteorological event, and the fog is not, or not only, a meteorological event. It is the most pervasive and least acknowledged resident of the plateau. It comes in from somewhere — no one has ever identified the source — and it drapes itself across the gneiss flats with the deliberateness of a hand pulling a cloth over a table.

It has weight. This is the first thing you notice, before the visual obscuration, before the damp chill. The fog on the Pérambe presses against the skin with a specificity that fog elsewhere does not. It is not uniform. One breath is sweet with condensation, tasting of clean mineral moisture. The next carries iron, dryite, and something faintly chemical — the same unnamed substance that scents the interior of the oldest Coronal hollows. It is as though the fog is not a single atmospheric phenomenon but a *medium*, carrying information from one part of the plateau to another, the way the chemical signals in a forest carry news of damage or decay from tree to tree, except here the medium is the atmosphere itself, and the information it carries is not about damage.

It is about *shape*.

There is a hypothesis — whispered, not published, advanced by a geochemist named Lüska Vord who spent eleven years in the interior hollows before leaving the plateau and refusing to speak publicly about her findings — that the fog is not condensation at all, not in the conventional sense, but an exhalation. That it is produced by the Coronal themselves, a metabolic byproduct of whatever process allows them to metabolize distance and substrate and light into the vast, breathing architecture they have become. That the plateau is, in this sense, a lung, and the fog is its breath made visible.

The hypothesis explains the settling. The fog does not roll because it is not a weather system. It is an emission. It falls to the ground because it is heavier than the air it displaces, dense with particulate and moisture and whatever else the Coronal exhale when they process the raw substrate of the peneplain into the living lattice of their trunks. It settles the way ash settles after a fire — not falling so much as *arriving*, having already been in the air and now completing a journey that began deep inside something vast and old and patient.

No one has tested this hypothesis. No one wants to. It would require sampling the fog at its source, and no one has been willing to enter the deepest hollows of the oldest Coronal — the ones where the air is thickest, where the lights flicker in patterns that almost resemble language, where the walls are warm to the touch and the sound of the wind through the upper branches is loudest and most structured, most like a voice trying to form a word it has forgotten the meaning of.

### V. The Sound

And the sound — the sound is alive.

Not metaphorically. Not poetically. The people of Derove, who are pragmatic and precise and have spent generations measuring and categorizing everything about their environment, will tell you — quietly, in the communal mess halls, late in the shift when the industrial noise has dimmed — that the sound of the Coronal at dusk is *organized*. That it is not random, not the product of wind interacting with branch-cantilevers in any way that wind and wood should produce. That the pitches shift in patterns that are too complex and too consistent to be the result of turbulence, and that the patterns repeat — not identically, but structurally, the way sentences repeat in structure while varying in content.

It sounds, they say, like a room full of people speaking in a language just beyond comprehension. Like something alive, thinking.

The wind moves through the upper galleries of the Coronal, and the branch-cantilevers — each one a slightly different length, a slightly different angle, a slightly different resonance — produce tones that overlap and interfere and harmonize in ways that no instrument of human construction could replicate without extraordinary engineering. The result is a chord. Sustained. Shifting. Alive with overtones that seem to emerge from the interaction of dozens of individual voices and yet dissolve when you try to isolate any single one.

At night, when the industrial nodes quiet and the thermal vents reduce their output and the only human sound is the murmur of conversation carried through the fog from one settlement node to the next, the chord becomes the dominant presence on the plateau. It fills the flat expanse of the peneplain the way water fills a bowl — completely, quietly, with a pressure that you feel in your chest before you hear it with your ears.

The new arrivals say it keeps them awake. The long-term residents say it keeps them *awake* — that there is a difference, and that the distinction matters. To hear the Coronal sounding is to be aware, in the most fundamental and unshakeable way, that you are standing inside something that is thinking. Not beneath it. Not beside it. Inside it. The way a thought lives inside the mind that produces it — not separate, not separable, but nested within a larger architecture of awareness that extends upward through the columnar trunks and outward through the branch-cantilevers and downward through the roots that grip the gneiss and drink from whatever stratum lies beneath, where the rules of distance dissolve and the plateau breathes and the fog settles and the sound rises and the sound rises and the sound —

*— the sound is alive.*
