---

**The Living**

Nothing here was born of gentleness. Everything that lives on the plateau was born of pressure, of mineral water threading through young stone, of heat rising from a planet that has not yet finished cooling the wound that made this place. And so the life here does not resemble life as it is usually pictured — the soft green narrative of root and leaf and wing. It is something older in feeling, something that remembers, in the architecture of its cells, that the world was once only chemistry and violence and slow cooling glass.

---

**The bacteria come first, because they must.**

They are not, strictly speaking, alive in the way that the word is usually meant. They do not move with purpose. They do not eat in the way that you understand eating. They *metabolize* — sulfur, iron, the trace minerals that leach endlessly from the young rock — converting heat and chemical imbalance into the faintest possible surplus of energy. They are less like organisms and more like a process that happens to persist. A reaction that learned, improbably, to continue.

They form films. Veils. Patches of discoloration along the wet seams of stone where warm water seeps. Viewed closely — and you must get close, close enough that the cold enters your knees through the granite — they look like oil on water, or like the skin that forms on a wound. Black, iridescent, faintly trembling when the water moves beneath them. They are the absolute foundation of everything that follows. Everything. They are the reason anything else exists here, and they do not care.

---

**Then the worms.**

They are small — no longer than the segment of a finger — and they are pale in the way that things become pale when they have never known sunlight: not white, exactly, but the color of uncooked dough, of wet string, of something that has been soaked too long and has begun to lose its conviction about being a particular color. Their bodies are translucent, or nearly so. If you hold one — though you should not, and the place will not let you hold one easily, the foxes and the birds see to that — you would see the dark thread of their gut, the faint pulse of fluid moving through it. They move through the wet crevices of rock in the warmest seams, where geothermal heat keeps the stone above freezing even in the deepest hours before dawn. They feed on the bacterial films, sliding over them with a slow muscular contraction that is less like crawling and more like *dissolving into* the stone and then reconstituting on the other side.

They are legless, blind, and ancient in manner. They do not flee. When the stone shifts and a boulder settles into a new position, they simply reroute, finding the new warm seam, continuing. There is nothing in their behavior that suggests urgency. The plateau is young, but they are patient in a way that makes the plateau feel old.

---

**The arthropods are their neighbors and, occasionally, their predators.**

They are harder to see. Their exoskeletons are the color of wet ash, flattened against the rock so closely that they seem to be part of it — a rough edge, a flake of stone that moved when it shouldn't have. They have too many legs. Not dramatically so, but enough that the eye, when you finally catch one moving, registers the wrongness: eight, ten, and always at least two of them not doing the same thing as the others, exploring, testing, tasting the air with a kind of chemical anxiety. Their antennae are long, pale, constantly in motion, reading the moisture gradients in the air the way you would read a sign.

They move in coordinated ways that suggest not intelligence but something adjacent to it — a distributed awareness, each individual responding to the movements of its neighbors with a timing so precise it looks rehearsed. When one finds a rich seep of warm mineral water, the others arrive within minutes. They do not communicate in any way you could hear. The sound they make is the dry whisper of too many small legs on glassy stone, and it is this — not sight, not scent — that the foxes listen for.

---

**The foxes.**

They are the most beautiful thing on the plateau, and there is no one here to confirm or deny this, and the foxes do not care.

They are small — smaller than you expect, though their fur makes them look larger when you first see them against the dark rock. Silver. Not white, not grey, but a specific and luminous silver that catches the diffused light and holds it, so that when one stands on a ridge at dusk (dusk being indistinguishable from midday here, but darker) it glows faintly, like a held breath. Their fur is dense, oily, beaded with moisture almost always, so that they look as though they have just emerged from water even when they have been sitting still for hours.

Their paws are wide, furred between the pads, and they move over the wet stone with a sureness that is almost contemptuous of gravity. They do not slip. They do not hesitate. They pick their way along the edges of seeps and stream channels with a stillness that is not caution but *method* — they will stop and remain motionless for minutes at a time, head slightly tilted, ears reading some frequency you cannot hear, and then they will move one paw forward, then another, and the whole animal flows into a new position as though the stone had simply opened for it.

They hunt the worms and arthropods. They are patient beyond what should be reasonable for a mammal at this altitude, where the metabolism should be screaming at them constantly to eat, to move, to burn. But the foxes burn slowly. They fold themselves against the rock in sheltered crevices when the cold deepens past a certain point, curling their tails over their muzzles, and they wait. They can be seen at any hour — there are no hours here, not truly — but they seem most present in the early part of what might be called morning, when the condensation is heaviest and the seeps are warmest and the prey is most active.

They watch you. This is the thing that stays with you. You will see one, perhaps two — they move in pairs more often than alone — and you will understand that they have been watching you for longer than you have been aware of them. Their eyes are pale, pale yellow, almost without visible pupil, set wide in their heads so that they have the appearance of something that sees in all directions at once. They do not blink. They do not start. They look at you the way a mechanism looks at the thing it has been designed to process: with attention, but without surprise.

---

**The raptors** are another matter. They are built for this place — the updrafts along the plateau's edges, the cold dense air, the ceaseless wind finding its way through the fissures. They ride the thermals that rise from the geothermal vents with a stillness of wing that makes them look bolted into the sky. They are dark above, pale below, and they circle slowly enough that you can watch a single bird for an hour and not perceive that it has completed even a quarter of one turn.

They are not large. If you held out your arms, their wingspan would nearly match your reach, but they are lean, compact, built for precision rather than power. Their calls are the high, thin keen you hear from the moment you step onto the plateau — not a scream, not a cry, but a sustained note, almost tonal, as if the bird were testing the air and finding it resonant. They hunt the foxes' leftovers, the small mammals, the slow. They are patient in a way that echoes the foxes, but the foxes' patience is stillness and the raptors' patience is *motion held so steady it becomes indistinguishable from rest.*

---

**In the caves,** the small mammals — vole-like, though smaller, with flattened skulls and eyes that reflect light like wet stones — live in colonies that smell of ammonia and old fur. They nest in the deepest chambers where the temperature holds constant, raising litters in a darkness so complete that their fur has begun, in some populations, to lose its pigment. They are not remarkable. They are warm-blooded persistence, the mammalian insistence on continuing in a place that offers no particular reason to continue. They gnaw on the roots of the few plants that grow near the warmest vents — the cyanobacterial crust, when it thickens, forms a rubbery mat that the mammals scrape away and eat, not for nutrition so much as for the moisture it holds. They are drinking the ground. They are alive because the ground weeps.

---

**And the amphibians.** The amphibians are impossible and do not care.

They are small — the largest no longer than your thumb — with dark, almost black skin that is not smooth but finely granular, like wet sandpaper. They live in the warmest of the mineral springs, the ones where water rises from deep enough in the earth that it arrives at the surface at temperatures the plateau would otherwise never allow. The pools they inhabit are no larger than washbasins, ringed with mineral deposits — verdigris, ochre, that rust-orange the color of old blood — and the water in them is so clear and still that looking into one is like looking down into a dark room with a polished floor.

They have no voice. They vocalize, if at all, through vibrations felt more than heard, a subsonic hum that you sense in your sternum when you crouch near the pool's edge and hold very still. They breed in the warm crevices at the pool's margin, laying their eggs in small gelatinous clusters that adhere to the stone like drops of amber. The young do not pass through a larval stage. They emerge complete — tiny, dark, already mobile — and scatter into the wet stone within hours of hatching, disappearing into the crevices with the same uncanny sureness that the foxes display, navigating a world they have never seen by senses they were born already possessing.

They cannot survive outside the warm springs. At this altitude, away from the geothermal warmth, the cold would kill them within hours. And yet they are here. They have been here, the surveyors' notes say — or rather, they imply, in the bewildered margins, in the sketches of unfamiliar tracks found on rock that should support nothing — for longer than anyone can account for. How they arrived is not a question the amphibians entertain. They are here. The springs are warm. The stone is wet. These facts are enough.

---

You will stand on this plateau and you will hear the chorus — the wind in the fissures, the high keen of the raptors, the dry whisper of ten thousand small legs on glassy stone, the faint percussion of water finding its way through new rock — and you will understand that this place is not an ecosystem. That word is too gentle, too botanical, too burdened with the memory of forests and meadows and the comfortable green rot of temperate decay.

This is an *engine.*

It runs on heat and mineral water and sulfur and time. Every creature here is a gear, a piston, a valve. The bacteria convert stone into food. The worms and arthropods convert bacteria into motion. The foxes and raptors convert motion into stillness — the stillness of a predator at rest, of a full belly, of energy stored against the cold. The amphibians convert warmth into life so immediately and completely that the process looks, from the outside, like magic. And the whole machine hums, indifferent to your presence, indifferent to your absence, running with the mechanical patience of something that was not built but *compressed* — forced into existence by the same pressure and heat that forced the rock beneath your feet into being.

You are standing inside something that is alive the way an engine is alive. Not softly. Not gently. Not with the quiet dignity of a forest or the cheerful excess of a meadow. But with the fierce, mineral, iron-blooded persistence of a world that was, only a few thousand years ago, only *fire*, and that has not yet decided whether it would rather be fire again.

You are standing inside something that was once nothing but pressure and heat and time. It is not yet finished. But it is already alive.
