## What Lives on the Weeping Terrace

---

### The People of the Basin

They did not build this place. That much is apparent the moment you walk among them — not from what they've made, but from what they haven't presumed to make. Sixteen shelters, low and heavy-roofed, arranged in a loose ring that mirrors the ring the animals make at dusk, as though the settlement were a translation of something it once saw happen in the open ground and chose, without deciding, to belong to.

There are perhaps forty of them. But forty is one of those numbers that breathes. In the wet season there are more — someone returns from a ridgeline circuit with a leather bag and a story, and a wall gets rebuilt, and a new lean-to appears against the southern slope where a young pair has decided — or inherited — that it is time to be a household. In the drier months the count drops. People leave on long walks along the crests, sometimes for days, following animal trails or mineral veins or simply a feeling that the ridge needs watching. They always come back. They always come back carrying things in their hands.

They are not a people who explain themselves. You will not be told their name for themselves, because they do not seem to believe that a name is the kind of thing one gives. You might hear them called by each other in low, clipped utterances — not names exactly, but positions. *The one who mends the channels. The one whose daughter tends the pools. The one who was on the ridge when the stone wept last.* Identity here is not a possession. It is a relationship, and it is always expressed in terms of what someone is doing, or has done, or is about to do.

They are short — not stunted, but compact, as though the thin air and the cool stone have agreed on a sensible economy of body. Their hands are large and sure. Their eyes are a washed-out brown, pale as the wood they build with, and they have the particular steadiness of people who have never once needed to be convinced that the world is older and larger than they are. They do not look at you with curiosity. They look at you the way they look at the weather — acknowledging that it has arrived, assessing what it intends, and then returning to what they were doing.

They wear hides — the leather cured with a paste made from the copper-tasting resin of the twisted trees along the southern ridge, which gives it a faint greenish cast and a smell like rain on old pennies. Over this they drape woven mats of the blue-green moss-stuff, still faintly cool and damp, still exhaling, so that a person approaching the settlement in the haze looks like a slow-moving tuft of the basin floor, which may be exactly the point.

Their tools are all hand-shaped: flaked-stone blades with hafts of pale hardwood, bone awls curved to the shape of a thumb, wooden mallets worn smooth at the striking face. They have copper — small pins, thin wire, a few sheet-patches hammered flat and stitched into hide for reinforcement — but there is no fire hot enough here to smelt. They know copper exists higher, up in the sharp ridgelines where the stone is still too young and too angry to hold anything. They have not gone to get it. Not because they are afraid. Because they have made a decision, or inherited one, about what this place will give and what it will not, and they do not push. You do not push a basin that is still becoming.

Their days are structured around the stone's weeping. When the rock darkens along the northern slope — when the water beads along the grain boundaries and begins its slow, aimless crawl downward — the settlement stops. This is not ritual. It is not superstition. It is the behavior of people who have learned to read a landscape that operates on a timescale just slow enough to be almost comprehensible, and just fast enough to matter. They watch. They wait. And when the seepage stops, whatever they were doing resumes, as though a sentence has been finished that no one bothered to speak aloud.

They eat the star-shaped fruit of the moss-mats, and they drink from the pools — cupping the water in their hands and drinking in small, careful sips, the way the animals drink, though no one acknowledges the resemblance. The water tastes of almost nothing, with a mineral sweetness underneath that lingers on the tongue and seems, if you drink enough of it, to make your thoughts move more slowly. The people do not comment on this. Their thoughts already move at the speed the basin prefers.

They are not unhappy. They are not happy, either. Those categories do not seem to apply. What they have is something more like alignment — the sense of a joint that has found its socket, a riverbed that has finally found its gradient. They live inside the basin's indifference the way a note lives inside a chord: not singled out, not separable, but present and necessary in a way they would not need to explain.

### The Six-Eyed Ones

The ungulate-shaped creatures. The ones whose name would be a translation and therefore a lie.

They come into being as a pressure change before they come into being as sight. You feel them first — a faint displacement of air, a low collective exhale, the sound of sixty bodies parting the stillness of the moss. Then the shapes resolve: long-legged, long-necked, heavy-headed, moving in a loose drift that has the coherence of smoke and the patience of erosion. Their bodies are dark — so dark they look like holes cut in the dim air of the basin, and only when they pass near a stone-flower do you see their edges, lit by the amber glow, and realize they are solid, warm, alive.

The heads are too large. That is the first thing anyone notices, and it is the thing the people of the settlement do not mention, because they noticed it generations ago and found that there was nothing to say. The skull is broad and flat, the jaw set forward, and from the lower mandible extends a proboscis — not a trunk, not exactly, but a muscular, tubular organ, pale and slightly ridged, that uncoils downward to touch the surface of the pools without breaking it. They drink through it in slow, rhythmic pulls, and the water rises inside the proboscis and is gone, and the surface of the pool barely trembles. It is, the people will tell you if you ask and if they decide you are a person it is worth answering, a mechanical grace. The word they use for it does not translate, but it contains the ideas of *correct motion* and *unearned certainty*.

And the eyes. Six of them. Arranged in crescents — three on each side of the broad skull — so that each face has a field of vision that must encompass nearly everything at once. The eyes are pale amber, reflective, the exact color of the stone-flowers at dusk, and they do not blink. They do not need to. There is a nictitating membrane, nearly transparent, that passes laterally across the full crescent of each set, and you will see it move sometimes — a slow, horizontal wipe, like a cloth drawn across a window — and then the amber is bright and steady again.

They do not flee. This is crucial. They do not flee. When you step near them — and you can step near them, they pass through the settlement at a distance of an arm's length, close enough to feel the warmth rising from their hides — they do not startle. They turn. Slowly. With the unhurried deliberation of something that has never had reason to hurry, they redirect their long necks and regard you with three eyes on each side, and you feel, without being able to articulate it, that you are being assessed by a system of perception that does not have a word for *threat*. You are simply — present. Registered. Moved on.

And then they are past you, and the moss exhales where their bodies pressed it, and the silence rushes back in to fill the shape they left.

At dusk — the dimming, not the departure — they gather. All of them. Forty, sixty, eighty, drifting from the edges of the basin toward the open centre until they form a broad circle, facing outward, motionless. Their amber eyes catch what light there is and return it. From above it would look like a ring of small fires burning low and patient in the grey air. No one here has seen it from above. No one intends to. The ridgelines are too sharp, too new, too willing to cut. The people know the shape the animals make at dusk the way they know the shape of their own breathing — not because they have seen it, but because they stand inside it.

What it means, if it means anything, is not discussed. The circle is not a ceremony. It is not a defense. It is six dozen animals standing in a ring and looking outward at a world they have survived without explaining, and if that is a metaphor it is one the basin made and not the people, and the people are not interested in claiming it.

### The Pool-Dwellers

In the southern pools — three, four, at most five of them, each no larger than a dining table, fed by the weeping stone and ringed by porous mineral rims that grow millimetre by millimetre — there are creatures that are not fish.

They are translucent. Flattened. Their bodies are thin enough that in certain light they are nearly invisible, and you see them not as shapes but as absences — darker patches against the pale stone bottom, moving in slow spirals that never intersect, never collide, maintaining a spacing so precise it seems mathematical. They have mouths that are too wide, proportionally, and they graze the pool bottoms with a slow, sweeping motion, their filamentous feeding structures combing the mineral surface for what they find nourishing. You see three. You see five. You see a dozen.

You never see one.

This is not a rule you are told. It is a pattern you notice, and then you notice it is absolute. They exist in minimums. Their movements are synchronized — not mechanically, not like a school of fish responding to a predator, but with the slow, dreamy coordination of something connected by a signal too subtle to detect. When you look away and look back, they have shifted position in unison, and the pattern they trace is different but the rhythm is the same, as though they are all reading from a score that is being written in real time by something in the water itself.

When you step to the pool's edge they scatter. Not in panic — they are never in panic. They withdraw with the same unhurried certainty as the six-eyed ones on land, a deliberate tidal retreat, and they settle into new spirals at the pool's centre within seconds, and resume their grazing as though your presence were a minor fluctuation in the system, acknowledged and absorbed.

Beneath them, something larger moves.

You can only see it as a displacement in the water. A slow lateral shift of the surface — not a ripple, not a wave, but a dimpling, as though a broad, flat shape were passing beneath the centre of the pool on a circuit that takes it to each pool in turn. It takes a full day. The water dims slightly as it passes, as if a shadow had fallen from below, and then the light returns and the tadpole-things resume their spirals and the mineral rim grows another fraction of a millimetre and the weeping stone on the northern slope gathers another bead of water and sends it down.

No one has ever seen what it is. The settlement was built around the pools, not despite them. The decision — whatever it was, whenever it was made — happened a very long time ago.

### The Small, Quick Ones

At dusk, when the multi-legged iridescent creatures emerge from the rubble, the basin changes its frequency.

They are small — no larger than a hand — with eight legs, sometimes ten, arranged in a configuration that suggests they are less descended from something than *convergent* with it, as though the idea of having too many legs arrived independently, a solution refined by a system that did not need to be elegant, only effective. Their bodies are flat and iridescent, opalescent, shifting between deep violet, bronze, and the oily rainbow-color of water running over a dark stone. They make no sound at all, and they move with a liquid precision through the angular talus that would cut anything soft, their limbs finding purchase on surfaces that look like they would offer none.

They hunt the insects that emerge from the warm cracks in the stone. You can see those insects sometimes — small, pale, almost transparent — drawn to the heat radiating from deep fissures where the geology is still settling, still cooling, still giving off the warmth of its own formation. The iridescent ones follow the heat. They emerge at the margin of dusk, when the dimming light makes their colors sharpest, and they flow through the rubble like oil across water, and when they find an insect they are fast, and it is over quickly, and there is nothing dramatic about it.

They are beautiful in the way that something is beautiful when it has no use for you. You watch them and you admire them and they do not know you exist, and this is perhaps the purest form of beauty available on the Weeping Terrace: a life so completely absorbed in its own ancient economy that it does not even register yours.

### What Lives Here, Ultimately

What lives on the Weeping Terrace lives in a covenant written before language, enforced by sharpness and patience in equal measure. The six-eyed ones drift. The pool-things spiral. The iridescent hunters flow through the rubble. The people watch and mend and harvest just enough. The stone weeps when it decides. The moss exhales. Something beneath the water passes in a circuit older than the channels that were built to follow it.

Nothing here is hostile. Nothing is welcoming. Everything is *present* — fully, completely, without reservation — and the presence of each thing sustains the others in ways that do not need to be named to be understood. The settlement at the basin's centre is not a kingdom. It is a punctuation mark in a sentence the land is still writing. The animals are not wildlife. They are not *wild* at all, because the concept of wild requires a boundary between *here* and *not here*, and the Weeping Terrace has no such boundary. It is all one system: geological, biological, hydrological, human. A basin that is still becoming, in which everything that lives is simply another expression of the same slow, indifferent becoming.

You stand there. The stone is warm underfoot. The filaments hang motionless and almost — almost — you swear you can see them settle. The silence hums. And you understand, without quite being able to say it, that you are not observing this place.

You are being permitted to witness it.
