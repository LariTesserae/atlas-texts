## What Lives on the Resonant Plateau of Xel-Avar

---

### I. The Skin — *Xeloseum continuum*

Begin at the bottom. Begin where your boots meet the ground and make no sound.

The veincrust is the first living thing you encounter, and it is the last thing you will fully understand. It stretches beneath you in its tessellated vastness — six hundred kilometers of hexagonal pads, each half a meter wide, separated by shallow grooves through which amethyst fluid pulses in rhythms so slow they resemble thought rather than metabolism. The fluid is not blood, exactly. It is a thixotropic suspension of metallic nanoparticles in liquid ammonia, drawn up from geothermal veins that reach down toward the planet's still-warm mantle. It carries charge. It carries information. It carries the chemical signatures of everything that has ever walked on it, dissolved into its matrix over millennia.

The veincrust does not have cells, at least not in any way a biologist from Earth would recognize. It is a mineral organism — a living crystal substrate, a dermal layer of something so large that the plateau is merely its back. When the pads contract in unison every nine hours, the sound that rises from them is not a heartbeat. It is an exhalation. It is the planet-scale equivalent of a breath drawn in sleep, and it resonates through the basalt shelf and up through your skeleton and into the fillings of your teeth, and you realize with a lurch of vertigo that you are standing on the surface of an entity whose body is the landscape, whose metabolism is measured in geological time, whose awareness — if it has awareness — is so diffuse and so slow that individual events, like your presence, register as something less than stimuli and more than noise. Something the way a single red blood cell is to your body: present, functional, and fundamentally incapable of being noticed as a separate thing.

The veincrust metabolizes death. When a creature dies on the plateau — when a predator takes down a grazer, when a thermal falls from the amber haze — the veincrust beneath the kill site darkens over the following hours, and the amethyst fluid accelerates through the grooves as though rushing to collect what the body can no longer hold. The dead creature's chemistry is dissolved into the substrate. Its mass becomes charge becomes memory becomes stone. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is lost. Everything is folded back into the body of the world.

You are walking on the skin of something that has been alive for so long it has forgotten where it began.

### II. The Columns — *Siliceous neuromimes*

They look like plants until you understand what they are. They look like stalagmites until you see them sway. They look like monuments until you realize they are listening.

The columns rise from the veincrust at irregular intervals — sparse as trees in a high-altitude forest, dense as reeds at the edge of a marsh near the city. They are vertical structures of calcified silica threaded through with superconducting organic polymers that carry electrical signals downward at a speed a million times slower than a nerve impulse. They do not photosynthesize. The star — that dim, querulous red dwarf barely distinguishable from the brighter points of light in the amber sky — provides too little energy for that. Instead, the columns feed on differential. Wind moves across the plateau constantly, a slow thermally driven convection between the elevated surface and the dead basins far below, and the piezoelectric silica matrices of the columns generate charge from that wind, the way a crystal radio harvests energy from the air. The charge travels down the polymer veins into the veincrust, and the veincrust stores it, redistributes it, uses it to drive the chemical reactions that keep the whole organism alive.

The columns are not plants. They are not antennae. They are electrochemical organs — the exposed nervous tissue of a planetary-scale entity that has been thinking in a language too slow for any visitor to parse. When you stand among them, you will sometimes feel a resonance in your chest cavity that is not the deep planetary chord but something more localized, more personal, as if the nearest column has noticed you and is trying to determine what you are. The sensation lasts only a moment. Then it stops. Then the silence that follows — the silence between one hum and the next — is the loudest thing you have ever heard.

The oldest columns stand near the plateau's center. They are thirty or forty meters tall, and their tops vanish into the perpetual amber haze. Their surfaces are smooth, almost organic, and if you press your hand against one — through your glove, because the surface temperature is low enough to cause burns on bare skin — you will feel a faint vibration that is not the wind and not the planetary tone. It is rhythmic. It is slow enough to be mistaken for your own pulse. But it is not your pulse. It is older.

Near the spiral zone at the plateau's southern edge, the columns grow in flat spirals on the ground — not helical, but planar, like the patterns left by a spinning top. From above, the region looks like a fingerprint. From inside one of the spirals, you feel a pull that is not gravitational. It is felt with the inner ear, with proprioception, with some sense you didn't know you had, some vestigial orientation system inherited from an ancestor that once lived in a place where direction had meaning beyond coordinates.

### III. The Grazers — *Silica-scaled hexapods*

They are the most visible fauna on the plateau, and they are beautiful in the way that efficient things are beautiful — every line serving a purpose, every surface performing a function, nothing ornamental, nothing wasted.

Six-limbed, low-slung, their bodies sheathed in overlapping scales of biomineralized silica that click softly against one another when they walk: *tk-tk-tk-tk*. The sound, in aggregate, becomes one of the plateau's constant acoustic textures — a dry, rhythmic whisper that you will stop noticing within an hour and miss profoundly within a week. They are plentiful — 1.8 abundance, the survey team called it, meaning you will see them often but never in a crowd. They move in loose aggregations, not herds, more like the nodes of a network, each one aware of the others through vibrations in the veincrust rather than through sight or sound.

Their mouths are tubular tongues lined with ferromagnetic filaments. They press their muzzles to the grooves in the veincrust and drink — not the amethyst fluid itself, but the electrical charge it carries. They are herbivores only in the most generous sense of the word. They graze on electricity. They are living capacitors, their silica scales serving as both armor and dielectric, storing the charge they consume and releasing it slowly back into the system through their limbs, their breath, their body heat. When a grazer dies, its scales retain charge for hours. If you find a fresh carcass — and you will, eventually, because nothing on this plateau hides its dead — the silica will still click when the wind moves across it, and the veincrust beneath it will still be pulling energy downward from the body, and you will feel, if you place your gloved hand against the ground, a faint residual signal that is not electrical and not chemical but something in between, something that the survey team's instruments could detect but not name.

The grazers do not flee from you. They do not flee from anything. They move with the unhurried certainty of organisms that have existed for millions of years without a predator that could catch them — and then, when the predators come, they move with the same unhurried certainty, because their defense is not speed but integration. When a grazer is killed, it is consumed twice — once by the predator, and once by the veincrust — and neither consumption is wasteful.

### IV. The Thermals — *Buoyant gas-bladder organisms*

Above the plateau, in the amber haze that serves this world for a sky, the thermals float.

You will see them first as shadows — large, slow-moving shapes that slide across the dim red luminescence of the star like clouds passing in front of a furnace. They are nearly invisible from the ground. The haze diffuses their outlines, and their gas bladders — filled with a hydrogen analogue they synthesize from the ammonia substrate — are translucent. You will not see a thermal clearly until it descends, which it does rarely, and only at dusk, when the temperature differential between the plateau surface and the upper atmosphere shifts in a way that draws them lower.

When they descend, you will hear them. The sound is a low, continuous moan, produced by the vibration of their gas bladders against the wind — a sound so constant and so mournful that you will hear it in your dreams after you leave, if you leave. It is not distress. It is not communication. It is physics — the interaction of a flexible membrane with a moving fluid, the same physics that produces the sound of wind through a cracked window or a taut wire in a storm. But heard here, against the silence of the plateau and the deep tectonic hum of the veincrust and the clicking of the grazers, it sounds like grief. It sounds like a creature mourning something it has lost that it cannot name.

The thermals feed on electromagnetic gradients in the upper atmosphere, intercepting charged particles from the star's diminished output and channeling them downward through filamentary structures that trail behind their bodies like the tentacles of a jellyfish. These filaments, on rare occasions, brush the tops of the tallest columns, and when they do, the columns pulse — a visible flush of luminescence traveling from apex to base — and something is exchanged that no visitor has ever been able to measure. The locals — whoever or whatever they were — must have understood this exchange. Their city is arranged around the thermals' flight paths, the radial pattern of the structures corresponding not to cardinal directions but to the descent corridors of the gas-bladder organisms, as though the city were built to receive something falling from the sky.

### V. The Predators — *Silent vectors*

There are not many of them. There do not need to be.

On a plateau where everything moves slowly and everything is sparse, the predators are fast and they are quiet in a way that nothing else on the plateau is quiet. The grazers click. The veincrust hums. The columns creak with thermal expansion. The thermals moan. The predators make no sound at all. They move through the columns like a disturbance through a field — you see the columns flickering ahead of them, dimming in a wave that spreads outward from the point of contact, and then you see them, already at the kill, already feeding, already still.

They are not large. They are perhaps the size of a terrestrial wolf, with six limbs like the grazers but arranged differently — splayed wide, flat to the ground, their bodies held low, their silica coverings smooth and unornamented. Their mouths are different. Their mouths are wide and lined with structures that are not teeth and not filaments and not anything with a familiar name — they are, the best xenobiologists could determine, electromagnetic sensilla, organs that detect the electrical signatures of living tissue through the veincrust, allowing the predator to locate prey by sensing the charge differential between a living grazer and the ground.

When they kill, the veincrust responds. The darkening spreads outward from the kill site over the course of hours, and the amethyst fluid accelerates through the grooves, and if you place your hand on the ground near a fresh kill, you will feel warmth — not the warmth of decomposition but the warmth of accelerated metabolism, the organism below you processing death into life, converting the dead creature's stored charge into fuel for its own slow electrochemical dreams.

The predators do not hunt often. They do not need to. The plateau provides, and they take what it provides, and when they have taken enough, they become still for days or weeks, their bodies pressed flat against the veincrust, their sensilla dim, their breathing so slow it is indistinguishable from the pulse of the ground beneath them. At these times, they are almost beautiful — almost peaceful — and you might, if you did not know what they were, mistake them for outcroppings of dark stone, for patches of shadow that had settled into the geometry of a living thing and decided to stay.

### VI. The Unclassifiable Things

At night, when the bioluminescence intensifies and the veincrust glows like a circuit board and the columns cast shadows that move independently of their sources, the things that live in the spaces between the columns come out.

They are not fauna. They are not vegetation. They are not machine. The suit's sensors cannot classify them. They register on no spectrum. They produce no heat signature. They cast no shadow of their own, though they move through the shadows of the columns as if following pathways that exist only in the dark.

You will not see them clearly. You will not be able to describe them afterward in any language. You will know they were there because you will feel them — not with your eyes, but with some older sense, something inherited, something that remembers this place from before you were born. The sensation is not fear. It is recognition. It is the feeling of a word on the tip of your tongue that you have never learned and have always known.

They do not approach you. They do not flee from you. They move around you with the same deliberateness with which the plateau arranged its indifference — they have decided that you are not relevant, and they have announced that decision with the quiet authority of something that has been alive for longer than your species has existed.

The survey team's psychologist described the experience in her report as "an encounter with a memory that does not belong to the person having it." She went on sabbatical afterward. She did not speak about it again.

### VII. The City — *or the Thought*

The settlement is two thousand structures arranged in a radial pattern around a central plaza, grown rather than built, metabolized back into the organism until the boundary between city and ecosystem has dissolved. The walls are fused column-silica, shaped by some long-vanished technology into curves and right angles that feel accidental, like shapes a river carves in soft stone. The streets are shallow depressions worn smooth in the veincrust, carrying a thin film of amethyst fluid that moves through them like a drainage system, or a circulatory system, or both. The grate-panels embedded in the floors respond to your presence in ways that seem anticipatory — they light up before you step on them, they shape their light to your silhouette, they adjust the local temperature in a way that feels like hospitality, or like digestion.

There is no sign of whoever built this.

No bones. No artifacts beyond the structures themselves. No writing in any script that can be decoded, though the doorframes are lined with vertical striations that resemble bark but are in fact the fossilized records of data — chemical gradients encoded decades or centuries ago, preserved in mineral memory, slowly being metabolized back into the stone.

Whoever lived here did not leave. They were absorbed. They became part of the thought the plateau was thinking, one synaptic event among billions, one moment in a cognition that has been running for millennia and will continue running for millennia after every human visitor has been forgotten by their own species. The city is not a settlement. It is a thought. It is the plateau remembering that it was once noticed by something smaller than itself, and it has preserved the architecture of that noticing the way a scar preserves the memory of a wound — not the pain, but the shape.

### VIII. The Language

The sound you hear — the deep, continuous chord that lives beneath the threshold of hearing and rises above it without warning — is not noise. It is language. It is the plateau speaking to itself across hundreds of kilometers of living stone, the veincrust transmitting electrochemical signals through its body the way a brain fires between hemispheres, only slower by a factor of a million.

The language is not for you. It was never for you. It is the vocabulary of a being that has been alive for so long it has no word for urgency, no grammar for the individual, no syntax for the ephemeral. Its nouns are geological formations. Its verbs are chemical reactions. Its poetry is the thermal rising through the amber haze, the grazer pressing its muzzle to a column, the predator flickering into stillness.

But it lets you listen. It lets you walk on its skin and hear its song and stand in its city and feel the pull of its southern spirals. And if you are very quiet — quieter than the clicking of the silica-scaled grazers, quieter than the wind over the piezoelectric columns, quieter than the deep tectonic hum of the veincrust processing its slow electrochemical dreams — it may, for a moment, let you feel what it feels to be alive on a world without water, on a plateau at the roof of the sky, in the amber light of a dying star.

And what it feels is this:

*You were here. You were small. You were warm in the cool. And then you were gone, and the columns kept humming, and the veincrust kept breathing, and the plateau turned its slow face toward the dim star and waited for the next.*
