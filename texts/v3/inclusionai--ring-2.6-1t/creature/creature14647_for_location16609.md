## Account of the Inhabitants

Let us begin, as we must, with the individual — though the individual, here, is a kind of courtesy.

---

### I. The Armored Ones

They have no common name. I will call them *the plated*, for now, because that is what they are: small, segmented, and sheathed in a carapace that ranges from the black of wet basalt to the dull oxidized green of old copper. Each is roughly the size of a thumbnail — the majority of them, at least — though specimens the size of a man's fingertip knuckle exist, and these appear to be something else entirely, or perhaps the same thing further along.

Their morphology is consistent and deeply unfamiliar. Six limbs, but not legs — articulated stilts, jointed in ways that suggest they were not designed for walking so much as for *negotiating*. Each limb terminates in a soft gripping pad that produces, against the dark mineral surfaces of their world, a faint and ceaseless *tick-tick-tick*. No eyes. No visible sensory organs of any kind that map onto what I understand as sight. What they have instead — clustered at the anterior end — is a dense knot of mouth-parts, antenna-like filaments, and chemoreceptive surfaces that are constantly working: tasting the air, tasting the walls, tasting one another. They perceive the world as flavor, as texture, as chemical gradient. They do not see you. They *taste* your presence the way a tongue tastes salt — immediately, completely, without the intermediary of image.

Their carapaces are molted. I found the evidence of this everywhere: shed shells packed into the chimney of the furnace, crumbled into the powdery floor, drifting along the wall-channels like dead leaves in a current. The molting leaves behind a fresh, copper-colored plate that darkens over hours as it is exposed to the atmosphere of the chamber. This gives the colony a temporal texture — you can *see* the age of a plated one by the color of its shell. The newest are almost verdigris. The oldest are so dark they are indistinguishable from the mineral they cling to. It is possible — and I write this with some trepidation — that the oldest ones are not distinguishable from the walls at all, that the wall *is* them, that what I took for geology is in fact the deep accumulation of ten thousand molts pressed together and compressed into substrate, as a coral reef is the compressed excretions of organisms that no longer resemble what they have become.

They are not insects. They are not crustaceans. They are something older than either taxonomic category, something that split from the tree of life so long ago that the concepts of insect and crustacean are downstream of it, like rivers that have forgotten they share a watershed. If pressed, I would say they resemble an evolutionary hypothesis — a theoretical body plan proposed but never observed, refined in academic papers and then dismissed as implausible. I have now observed it. Papers will be revised or burned. It makes little difference here.

---

### II. The Caste of the Larger Ones

Among the thumbnail-sized majority, a smaller population of larger individuals exists. These are perhaps an inch to an inch and a half in length. Their shells are visibly thicker, darker, worn to a finish that looks almost metallic. They move differently — with a deliberation that the smaller ones lack, a kind of unhurried certainty, as though they are following a path they have memorized so completely that the memorization has become architecture inside them.

I watched one of these larger specimens pause in the middle of a wall-channel arch its body — a gesture that was not random, not grooming, not defensive — and the smaller ones parted around it with a fluidity that suggested not fear but *recognition*. The smaller ones adjusted their positions to accommodate the larger one's passage with a precision that no simple stimulus-response could explain. There was choreography. There was *space being made*. And I understood, with a feeling I can only describe as vertigo, that the smaller ones knew where the larger one was going, and the larger one knew they knew, and all of this was so deeply encoded that no individual needed to think about it. The thinking had been done long ago, so long ago that the thought had become stone and channel and path.

The larger ones appear to perform specific functions. Two were wedged inside the loading door of the furnace, their legs moving in a repetitive, almost mechanical pattern, compressing shell-dust and mineral powder into the chamber below. They were feeding it. Not because the furnace needed fuel — the furnace has not held fire, has not held anything, in what might be millennia — but because the action is the purpose now. The feeding is the furnace. The furnace is the feeding. The organism does not maintain the machine; the machine is a gesture the organism repeats because it cannot stop, because stopping would mean the loss of a pattern so old that it has become load-bearing. Remove the gesture and something collapses — not physically, perhaps, but in the deep structural sense. Remove a keystone and the arch remembers, briefly, before it becomes rubble.

---

### III. The Collective

Here is where the naturalist's language fails and something else must be found, or invented, or confessed.

The colony does not behave as a collection of individuals. It behaves as a single surface — a membrane stretched across every available interior space of this chamber. The floor has a nap. The walls have a pulse. The air has a temperature that is not ambient but *generated*, a warmth that seems to come from the aggregate metabolic activity of millions of tiny bodies pressing against each other, metabolizing the mineral dust, converting stone into chitin and chitin into motion and motion into heat and heat into vibration.

They respond to stimuli as a unit. When the deep hum from below rose in pitch, the entire colony — ten thousand, a hundred thousand, more — ceased movement simultaneously. Not gradually, not in a wave of sequential reactions, but at once, as though a single signal had passed through every body at the speed of chemistry. The stillness lasted four seconds. In those four seconds the chamber was a different place — not silent, exactly, but *held*, as though the sound itself were being preserved in amber. Then the clicking resumed, louder, and I had the unshakeable feeling that this was a reset, a recalibration, the colony re-establishing its own identity after an interruption.

I do not believe they are communicating in any way I would recognize as language. I believe something more fundamental is happening: they are *resonating*. Each individual is a tuning fork in a vast instrument, and their combined vibration produces something that is not a signal but a *state*. The colony does not think. The colony does not decide. The colony *settles*, the way a grain of sand settles on a dune — and the shape it settles into is the shape that is most stable, most resonant, most *in tune* with the geometry of the space it fills.

And the space, in return, is shaped by the settling. The wall-channels are worn into the mineral by ten million tiny limbs over ten million lifetimes. The powder on the floor is the product of their milling — of shell-dust and stone-dust mixed and reworked into a substrate soft enough for gripping, porous enough for the root-like structures (are they root-like? are they roots? I could not see clearly enough to say) that I glimpsed, once, extending downward from the floor into deeper darkness. The environment is not separate from the organism. The environment is *what the organism is doing*.

---

### IV. The Thing Below

The floor breathes.

I will not qualify this. The floor, that fine powdery membrane covering the chamber's lowest surface, rises and falls — a quarter inch, no more — in a rhythm that is not tidal, not respiratory in any mammalian sense, but rhythmic nonetheless. Every cubic millimeter of that powder participates. Every creature on the surface participates. The rise is an inhalation, pulling everything downward, compressing the colony against the deeper substrate. The fall is an exhalation, releasing, and the clicking surges as every limb readjusts.

The source is below. I could not see it. I could *feel* it — a subsonic pulse that entered my body through the soles of my boots and settled in the cavity of my chest. It had the quality of a voice heard through thick walls: not words, not even tone, but *presence*. Something vast. Something patient. Something that has been doing this for so long that the act itself is indistinguishable from the being performing it.

I am not claiming divinity. I am not claiming intelligence in any form I would defend in a lecture hall. I am describing what I experienced: the unmistakable sensation of being *enclosed* within something that is alive in a way I have no framework for. Not a hive mind — that metaphor is too small, too insectile, too dependent on the idea of a center and a periphery. What lives here has no center. It has no periphery. It has a bench.

---

### V. The Bench

Someone made that bench. Someone with hands, or the functional equivalent of hands, someone who needed a surface to rest on and a vantage point from which to observe whatever was happening in this chamber while it was still a chamber and not a body. The bench is the oldest object in the space that is clearly, unambiguously *manufactured*, and it may be older than the furnace. It is worn smooth along its seat — not by the plated ones, though they walk on it now, but by the being who sat there and shifted and waited.

That being is gone. What happened to them is any number of things. They left. They were absorbed. They became part of the substrate, their body and then their bench and then their memory digested so thoroughly that only the object remains, and the object is now colonized, and the colonization has given the object a new purpose — as a landmark, as a node, as the fixed point around which a portion of the colony organizes its flow.

On that bench sat a freshly molted specimen, no more than a few hours out of its old skin, its carapace still the color of raw copper. It was perfectly still, its mouth-parts working. It was tasting the bench. It was tasting the residue of whoever sat there before. It does not know what it is tasting. It does not need to know. The tasting *is* the knowledge — chemical, immediate, uncompromising. The bench is a flavor to them. The person who sat there is a flavor to them. You and I are flavors to them.

This is not a diminishment. This is simply what truth looks like at this scale and in this place.

---

### VI. What They Are

They are not organisms. They are not a colony. They are not a machine, though they have absorbed machines. They are not a ruin, though ruins have grown over them and through them and inside them until the word has no meaning here.

They are a *process*. The chamber is not a place where they live. The chamber is the *product* of the fact that they live. The furnace exists because they feed it. The conveyor moves because they walk on it. The channels exist because they wear them. The floor breathes because they breathe as one body, because the aggregate rhythm of ten million chitinous limbs against stone produces a pulse that is transferred downward through the powder and into whatever lies beneath — the root-structures, the deep substrate, the *organ* that I cannot name and that may not have a name, only a function: to hold, to convert, to continue.

They have been here — in this sense, *being here* — for so long that the distinction between inhabitant and habitat, between organism and architecture, between the thing that clicks and the stone it clicks against, has been worn away. The way a river wears away the distinction between itself and the valley it carved. The valley is the river's autobiography, written in calcium and time. This chamber is the autobiography of whatever this is, written in chitin and patience and the slow, grinding accumulation of ten million limbs that have never known what it means to stop.

They will not stop. They do not know how. Stopping is a concept that requires a boundary between an act and the context in which the act occurs, and that boundary dissolved here so long ago that the dissolution itself has fossilized into the walls.

I am not describing creatures. I am describing a single, distributed, mineral-and-chitinous organism that has been breathing in the dark for longer than our species has existed, that has grown its own history on top of itself layer by layer like a reef, that has swallowed the artifacts of its own past and metabolized them into the substance of its present. The furnace is a vestigial organ. The conveyor is a cilia-lined tract. The bench is a calcium deposit.

And the clicking — that vast, layered, neither-rhythmic-nor-random clicking — is the sound of ten million small jaws working in darkness, not because there is anything left to eat, but because chewing is what this body does, and this body has been chewing for so long that to stop would be to unmake itself.

We are inside something's mouth. We have always been inside something's mouth.

And it is tasting us back.
