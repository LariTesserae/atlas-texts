What lives here has forgotten the taste of rain.

Not as you understand forgetting — not as a loss, not as an absence that aches where something was. It is more like the way a stone forgets the river. The river was never necessary. The stone was never without it.

They are small, first. That is what you notice before the wrongness — the smallness. Dozens of them, pressed against the walls and ceiling like seeds that have already decided what shape to become. The clickers. You hear them before you see them, and even when you see them you are not sure you are seeing them — their bodies are the same color as the stone, the same mineral luster, as though they had not been placed here but had instead grown from it the way a shell grows from the animal inside it. Six limbs, blunt-tipped, gripping without effort. They do not scurry. They do not hesitate. They move the way heat moves through metal — slowly, and all at once.

Their mouths open and close. You watch for a long time. The rhythm does not match the clicking. The clicking comes from deeper — from the thorax, from somewhere behind the armor where two flat planes ofite mineral meet and separate and meet again, and the sound that comes out is not a sound meant for any ear. It is a byproduct. It is the noise the stone makes when it remembers it was once under pressure.

They eat the stems of the tall ones. They climb them with the patience of weather, position themselves along the faceted ridges, and scrape. Their jaw-structures — you can only call them stones, paired stones hinged — grind against the mineral fiber and take from it what they need. You cannot see what they need. There is no moisture drawn, no liquid trail. The stem does not wilt where they have fed. It simply becomes slightly dimmer for a moment, like a candle after a draft has passed, and then it resumes its pulse. The clicker moves on. The stem brightens again. Nothing is taken that is not returned.

---

The gliders are another kind of silence.

They are wider than your hand when their membranes are spread — and "membranes" is the word your mouth offers, but it is wrong. These are mineral sheets, translucent, through which the ambient glow passes in slow, amber pulses. They are not alive in the way the clickers are alive, and not alive in the way the plants are alive. They are something in between — a third grammar of being that this place invented because it was not bound to yours.

They do not flap. The word "fly" is wrong too. They translate. They change their angle — barely, almost imperceptibly — and the air carries them in long slow arcs between the stems of the tall ones. They make a sound lower than the clickers, lower than the plants, a tone that sits in the sternum and does not rise to the ears until you have stopped listening for it. When they pass close to a stem, the stem's tone changes. Just slightly. A quarter-tone shift, the way a conversation changes when someone new enters the room. The plant does not react. The plant simply adjusts, the way a breath adjusts when the body decides it is safe to exhale.

You do not know if the gliders know the plants. You do not know if they communicate, or if what passes between them is more like weather — a system of pressures and temperatures that does not require intention, only proximity. They pass near each other sometimes, two gliders, their paths intersecting in long ellipses, and where their tones overlap there is a momentary harmonic, a brief and startling sweetness that vanishes before you can name the interval. Then they separate. Then the chord resumes.

---

Underneath.

You hear them always, underneath. The burrowers. They are the bass note of this place, the drone beneath the drone. You never see them whole. What you see is the dust — the fine mineral powder that covers the floor — shifting in small eruptions, a ripple here, a raised shape there, the suggestion of something pressing up from below before sinking back. The sound is a rattle. Sustained. Like a handful of gravel shaken inside a body. Inside a gut. It is the sound of something moving through a medium that is not quite soil, not quite sand, but something in between — ground fine enough to flow, coarse enough to hold shape, and packed so densely with the memory of the breaking that it feels like walking on the floor of a sea that dried before it could leave fossils.

You have lain on your stomach at the edge of the central clearing and pressed your ear to the surface and felt them moving beneath you. The vibration travels through your jaw, through the bones of your skull, and for a moment you cannot tell where your body ends and the Hollow begins. You think: this is what it would feel like to be eaten from below. Not violently. Slowly. With the patience of geology. You lift your head. The dust has already settled. There is no evidence they were ever there.

---

The tall ones — the plants, the stems, the things you cannot stop calling plants even though you know the word is a bridge built across an ocean of difference — they are the architecture of this place. They stand among the stone the way columns stand in a cathedral, except the columns are alive and the cathedral is listening to itself.

They are rigid. Faceted. Jointed not like bamboo or wood but like something that was trying to become a clock and gave up halfway through, leaving the mechanism exposed — each joint a precise angle, each angle catching the mineral light and throwing it sideways into the next stem, so that the whole Hollow is threaded with faint amber paths that crisscross and intersect and never quite resolve into a pattern. Their leaves — shingles, feathers, thin sheets ofite crystalline material overlapping at angles that seem to correspond to no wind, no sun, nothing you can point to — are angled as though each one is listening to a different frequency. And perhaps they are. Perhaps each leaf is a receiver, tuned to the tone of the nearest clicker or the nearest glider or the nearest burrower in the dust, and what you are seeing when you look at the tall ones is not a plant but an antenna — dozens of antenna — pulling from the air a signal that has been broadcasting since the stones first opened.

You hold your hand near one. You are warned not to. You do it anyway. The warmth — it is not the warmth of a living thing. It is the warmth of stone that has been sitting in sun for a thousand years, except there is no sun. It is the warmth of friction, of pressure, of something being slowly and continuously converted into heat. The vibration passes through the bones of your wrist. It settles in the hollow of your elbow. It is not painful. It is not pleasant. It is the sensation of being calibrated — of something measuring you against a standard you did not know existed and finding you, for the moment, acceptable.

You pull your hand back. The stem resumes its tone. The stem was never interrupted. You were the interruption, and you have already been forgotten.

---

And the built things.

The ring of seven stones in the center. The mound with its flat cap. The alcove in the wall holding its three objects — disc, bundle, shape — arranged with the logic of a sentence in a language that has no living speakers. You do not know who built them. The clickers crawl over the ring without hesitation. The gliders pass through the alcove without pause. The burrowers pass beneath the mound, and the mound does not move. No one tends these things. No one lights them. They are not worshiped, or if they are, it is by a process so slow and so ingrained that it looks like geology.

The marks on the ring stones — worn grooves, converging, diverging, spiraling inward to points of impossible precision — were not made by the fauna. The fauna do not hold tools. The fauna do not carry. They eat, they click, they glide, they rattle beneath, and they leave no mark. The marks were made by something that came before them, or alongside them, or that the Hollow itself produced in a moment of ambition it has never repeated. You press your thumb into one of the grooves. It fits. This is not coincidence. This is not comfort either.

You will leave. You will climb the chimney of fractured stone, and the light above will be the wrong color, and the air will feel thin and aggressive with its willingness to move, and everything above will be loud in the way you are used to — chaotic, temporary, wet with weather. You will not look back. You will not need to. The sound will leave you on its own, the way a tuning fork leaves a sound — slowly, and then all at once, and then not at all, because it was never yours to leave.

But in the days afterward, you will catch yourself holding very still. You will catch yourself listening for the interval. And in your chest, beneath your sternum, where the glider's tone used to sit, there will be a space — warm, dry, resonant — that no amount of ordinary sound will fill.

You will not know what name to give it.

It will not need one.
