## What Lives in the High Verdance

---

What lives here has forgotten — or never learned — the contract most life observes with its environment: that you adapt to the world as it is, and in return the world lets you stay. The inhabitants of the High Verdance do not adapt. They *participate*. They are not residents. They are symptoms.

---

**The Rootbinders**

You smell them before you see them, and you feel them before you understand them. The Rootbinders are the oldest continuous organisms on the plateau — not individual plants but networked supercolonies of rootstock and rhizome and fungal filament that stretch across kilometers of rock, stitching stone to stone in a living mesh that is part architecture, part circulatory system, part slow argument with gravity.

A Rootbinder does not have a body in any useful sense. It has *zones*. In one zone, you encounter what looks like a wall of root — thick as a human thigh, dark as wet iron, braided and re-braided over decades into patterns that are too regular to be accidental and too irregular to be engineered. The surface is warm to the touch, and if you press your ear to it, you hear a low, rhythmic wet clicking, like a tongue pressed against the roof of a closed mouth. In another zone — perhaps a hundred meters away, perhaps a kilometer — the same organism expresses itself as a carpet of moss so dense and uniform that it looks like poured green felt, and when you step on it the ground yields slightly, not like soil but like muscle tensing beneath skin.

Rootbinders are not conscious in any way that maps onto animal experience. But they are not unconscious either. They occupy a middle category that the plateau seems to have invented for itself: *processes that behave like intentions without harboring them*. A Rootbinder will grow toward warmth. It will grow toward vibration. It will grow around an obstacle rather than through it, not because it cannot break stone — it can, given enough time, and enough time is something the High Verdance provides in abundance — but because the alternative path was available, and the old growth receded and the new growth filled the channel, and the result looks so much like a decision that the word *decision* starts to feel like a reasonable description for what happened.

The moss they shed is the foundation of everything. Nothing else really grows in the deep soil of the plateau. Fingers of moss first, packing stone dust and condensed vapor into a substrate thin enough to tear but sturdy enough to hold. Then the ferns arrive, then the low-canopied trees that are really just moss and root grown ambitious, then the climbing things, and suddenly there is a forest, and it is all one Rootbinder expressing different aspects of itself in different light.

**The Tonnerres**

The name means nothing. It is a sound-whale word, an attempt at onomatopoeia by a language not designed for them. They are the largest animals on the plateau. They live in the deep vegetation, below the lowest canopy layer, in a dim that never fully lifts.

You do not see a Tonnerre. You experience the *absence* of a Tonnerre — a displacement in the fern wall, a slow parting of green, a hush that falls over the insects and the rustlers and the small membrane-winged gliders, as though the local ecology collectively holds its breath. Then there is a shape: not clear, not sharp, but volumetric — a mass, maybe four meters long, maybe more, moving with the slow deliberation of something that knows that nothing on this plateau is in a hurry to eat it. Its body is covered in overlapping plates of something between chitin and bark, dark green and wet, and it parts the vegetation not by force but by *suggestion* — the plants yield to it the way water yields to a hand pressed slowly through it, closing again behind the movement.

They pulse. That is the only honest description. Deep, subsonic vibrations that you feel in your sternum and your molars and the soles of your feet, a rhythm that is not a heartbeat but something adjacent — something broader, something that communicates *state* rather than *content*. A low, steady pulse: I am here. A quickened double-pulse: there is a change in the ground. A long, rising tone that seems to come from everywhere at once and fades into the geothermal hum of the plateau itself: something we have no word for, but the Tonnerres understand, and the Rootbinders thrum in reply.

Whether the Tonnerres are individuals is an open question. They are never seen in pairs. They are never seen young. There is, supposedly, a juvenile somewhere — a smaller shape glimpsed once through three layers of canopy — but the sighting is disputed. It may have been a rock that shifted. The plateau shifts rocks.

---

**The Six-Legs**

Colonies of thousands. They live on every exposed rock face and cliff edge, a crawling iridescence that turns from copper to violet to deep blue depending on the angle of light, which on the plateau is always indirect, always filtered through haze and canopy and the occasional unlikely shaft of sky that punches through a gap in the cloud-layers and sends a column of pale gold slanting down into the green gloom. In that light, a Six-Leg colony looks like a stained-glass window that someone has pasted onto a cliff.

They are not insects. They have six legs, yes, and a segmented body, yes, and a carapace, yes. But the segmentation is wrong — too many joints in the wrong places, too much flexibility where an exoskeleton should be rigid. They fold into crevices in ways that suggest they are not the right shape for their own bodies, or that their shape is a negotiation with the surface they are currently climbing, moment to moment, as though their anatomy is a suggestion rather than a blueprint.

They are the most responsive creatures on the plateau. A shadow passes — a glider overhead, a cloud-edge briefly obscuring the haze — and the colony flattens, every individual pressing itself to the rock in a wave that moves with visible speed from the top of the formation to the bottom. A steam vent hisses nearby, and they scatter, not in panic but in order, flowing into cracks like mercury into fissures, and then flowing back out a minute later when the vent settles. They have no visible eyes. They orient by vibration, by heat gradient, by some sense that does not have a ready translation into human physiology. When the ground shifts — when the plateau breathes and a crack opens or a slab tilts — they are the first to know. They are pressed flat against the stone a full second before the movement begins.

They farm. That is the simplest way to say it. Certain colonies maintain patches of moss on the rock faces they inhabit, trimming it back when it grows too thick, aerating it with tiny clawed forelimbs, removing competing fungal growths with a precision that looks, again, far too deliberate. They harvest condensation. They harvest heat, clustering over thermal cracks in the rock and absorbing radiant warmth in shifts, so that the individuals on the interior of the cluster rotate outward and the ones on the exterior rotate inward, a slow revolving that keeps the colony's temperature perfectly, obsessively even.

They have no sound. They have no visible means of communication. And yet a colony of three thousand individuals moves as one organism, making decisions that no individual could make alone, pursuing goals that no individual could have. They are proof that consciousness is optional.

**The Gliders**

The membrane-winged gliders ride the thermals that rise from geothermal vents, circling in their slow, patient loops, and they are the closest thing the plateau has to poetry made flesh.

They are large — wingspan perhaps two meters — and their wings are translucent, veined with structures that look like the fossilized vasculature of a leaf. They are pale, almost colorless, and in certain light they are nearly invisible: just a ripple in the air, a disturbance where the haze thins for a moment and then thickens again. Their single notes — one every few minutes, sometimes longer, sometimes never — are high and clear and so perfectly suited to the thick air that they seem to hang there indefinitely, neither decaying nor traveling, simply *existing* in a fixed point in space until the air absorbs them.

They are not birds. They lay no eggs. They have no nests. They eat, it is believed, on airborne spores and the condensation that forms on their wing membranes overnight, which they lap up with a tongue that unfurls like a strip of wet silk. They are profoundly indifferent to observers. A glider will pass within arm's reach and regard you — if regarding is the word — with eyes that are enormous, black, and contain no detectable focus. They see, perhaps. Or they sense the displacement of air that a standing human creates and find it uninteresting.

What they do, aside from circling and singing, is unknown. They are not observed nesting, not observed feeding in any conventional sense, not observed interacting with one another except in the rarest cases where two gliders cross a thermal column at the same altitude and briefly, casually, almost accidentally, their wingtips touch — a contact so light that it might be imagined — before they drift apart on separate loops.

They are, it has been proposed, the plateau's *sensors*. The membranes of their wings are thin enough to register barometric changes too fine for any instrument. Their notes may be responses to subsurface vibration, a way of translating geological data into the air. They may be the listening apparatus of a place that is otherwise deaf to its own processes. This is speculation. The plateau offers no confirmation. The gliders offer no denial. They circle.

**The Underfoot**

This is not a name. It is a category of inference.

Under the moss, under the thin soil, under the decomposing mat of fern-frond and leaf and dead Rootbinder membrane, there is *motion*. It is constant. It is not the scuttle of insects or the burrow of worms. It is deeper. It is a vibration that travels through the ground in complex, overlapping rhythms, and when you lie face-down on the moss — and you must do this to feel it properly, pressing your cheek against the wet velvet, breathing in chlorophyll and mineral warmth — you realize that the motion is not *beneath* you. It is *around* you. It is in every direction, at every depth, a three-dimensional tremor that the moss translates into touch, that the Rootbinders translate into growth, that the Six-Legs translate into their obsessive thermoregulatory dances.

What makes it is unknown. Whether it is alive is uncertain. It has patterns, but not repeating ones. It has rhythms, but not fixed ones. It is the cardiovascular system of the plateau, perhaps, or the resonance of the machines operating through stone and root and water, or the subsonic speech of the Tonnerres heard at a remove, translated through too many intermediary media to recognize.

It is alive enough. It will respond to weight. Step onto a patch of moss and the motion beneath you shifts — not ceasing, not fleeing, but *reconfiguring*, like a crowd parting for a stranger, and then closing behind you, and then forgetting you were ever there.

**The Machines**

They live here and they do not live here and this ambiguity is their defining characteristic.

The piston assembly at the warm spring drives its pump without fuel, without maintenance, without any hand to oil its joints — and the oil, if you check, is not oil but something dark and mineral-slick that the plateau itself seems to produce, weeping it from the same fissures that generate the steam. The flywheel turns without load, but if you watch it long enough — hours, perhaps, or days — you will notice that its speed is not constant. It accelerates and decelerates in patterns that correspond, if you chart them, to the subsonic pulses of the Tonnerres. You will not believe this until you have charted it. You will not believe it afterward, either, but you will not be able to unsee the correlation.

The conveyor belt carrying dark mineral fragments uphill does not merely function. It *functions efficiently*. Its speed adjusts to load — heavier loads slow it slightly, lighter ones speed it up, and this adjustment is not mechanical in the conventional sense, because the motor is a simple thing, a brute-force rotary engine of a design so old it predates standardization. The efficiency is emergent, arising from some interaction between the motor's output, the load's mass, the angle of the belt, and something else — something in the bedrock beneath the motor's housing that may be providing supplemental energy in a form that has no name in conventional engineering.

The machine at the gorge's edge — rotating arms, measurement-or-cutting — turns with a slow, precise regularity that no longer corresponds to any observable environmental input. It responds to nothing. It produces nothing. It simply operates, and the ferns grow around it and through it and are not impeded, and the moss colonizes its surfaces and does not interfere with its motion, and the two have reached an equilibrium that looks like intimacy, or at least like a very old cohabitation.

Do the machines live? They persist. They self-repair, in the sense that the plateau self-repairs around them — cracks in housings are sealed with mineral deposits, corroded surfaces are colonized by rootbind-filament that prevents further oxidation, joints that should have seized decades ago continue to turn because the Rootbinders have grown into them and now provide structural support that the original engineering lacked. They maintain themselves by becoming part of the biome. They have been assimilated, or they have assimilated, or the distinction has dissolved.

---

**The Plateau Itself**

This is the hypothesis that begins as a joke and ends as the only model that fits: the High Verdance is alive. Not alive in the way a forest is alive, or the way a superorganism is alive, but alive in the way a *mind* is alive — processing, adjusting, rendering itself in multiple tenses, uncertain of its own boundaries, continuously revising its own ontology.

The gravity fluctuations are its uncertainty. The temporal overlap is its memory and its anticipation operating simultaneously without sequence. The ground-shifting is its restlessness. The machines are its *projections* into the physical — the only parts of it that are legible to instruments, the only parts that correspond to the world as it is conventionally understood. Everything else — the Rootbinders, the Tonnerres, the Six-Legs, the Gliders, the Underfoot — is the fauna of a consciousness that is building itself in real time, in all directions, without knowing what it is becoming, or whether the becoming will settle into something stable or continue to fluctuate indefinitely between states.

You stand on the ridge. The haze closes in. The rock hums beneath your feet — not a vibration, not a sound, but a *presence*, warm and cautious and vast, and for a moment you feel it looking back at you with an attention so diffuse and ancient and slow that your own consciousness registers it not as thought but as weather. As pressure. As the sense that you are standing on the surface of something that is dreaming, and that the dream is not about you, but that you are in it, and that for now, that is enough, and the ridge holds, and the plateau breathes, and the haze extends in every direction to a horizon that is not a boundary but a process, still unfolding, still becoming, still pushing itself upward out of the planet's body with the slow, undecided violence of a birth.

It has not decided whether to finish.

Neither have you.
