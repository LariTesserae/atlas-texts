## Who Lives in Qaar-Vhen

You would know them by their hands first.

The people of Qaar-Vhen have hands that are never fully dry. Not wet — not the sogginess of someone who has spent too long in water — but perpetually damp, the way river stones are damp after a rain that never quite stops. Their fingerprints are softened. Their grip is sure but gentle; they take things the way you'd take a ripe fruit from a stem, with a slight turning pressure rather than a clamp. Children's hands are the same. Old people's hands are the same. The dampness is not a condition here. It is simply what skin is.

---

Their eyes are unusually sensitive to gradation. In a place where the light never exceeds the color of weak tea, where shadows are soft and short and the sun is a rumor behind gauze, you do not develop the ability to see in brightness — you develop the ability to see *difference*. A Qaar-Vhenner can tell you, by half a glance, whether the bronze light has shifted warmer or cooler this hour. They read the micro-weather of the condensation towers the way a farmer elsewhere reads the sky. When the towers cycle down and the air clears slightly, they look upward — not toward any horizon, since the ridgelines enclosing the basin are low and unremarkable, but toward the canopy of walkways and towers and bridging infrastructure where the clearest light gathers — with an attentiveness that resembles gratitude.

They are not, by outside standards, good at seeing far things. A visitor from a brighter world will notice that Qaar-Vhenners tend to stand closer than expected when speaking, not from any cultural intimacy but because the dimness has trained them to read faces at a distance of two arm-lengths or less. Beyond that, things blur.

---

They have no word for "animal."

Not a clever circumlocution. Not a shooing-away gesture when the topic comes up. The concept simply does not live in their language the way the concept of "wall" or "water" or "warmth" does. They have a rich vocabulary for types of root, stages of fungal bloom, the textures of condensate and the tones of pump-sound at different phases of the cycle. They can name forty varieties of the broad-leafed wetland vegetation by the specific quality of their shade. But if you ask what a dog is — if you use the complex apparatus of outside language to describe a creature with a nervous system that moves through the world with intention — they will listen with the same attentiveness they would give to a description of a color they cannot see.

Children do not ask about animals. It would not occur to them. The world is plants and fungi and water and iron and people. This is not a deprivation to them. It is not even a category of thought. When visitors from outside — and they do come, occasionally, drawn by the condensation trade or by the town's peculiar quiet — bring animals through, the children cluster around them with an astonishment that is genuine but mild, the way a child from elsewhere might react to an unusually shaped seashell.

The adults are less charmed. Not hostile. Just uneasy. Something about a creature that moves with its own decision, that has eyes that track and bodies that shift weight — it unsettles them in a way they cannot fully articulate. It produces too much *unpredictability* in a world that is, by their standards, exquisitely knowable. The pumps cycle. The towers hiss. The fungus glows when you walk beneath it. The water in the Drenn Chamber rises and falls. Everything here has its rhythm. A living creature that is not bound to any of those rhythms feels, to a Qaar-Vhenner, like a wrong note in music they have heard all their lives.

---

They eat plants, fungi, and algae.

There is a staple mash — they have a dozen names for it, none of them translatable — made from the broad wetland tubers that grow just below the waterline, pounded smooth and mixed with a fermented algal paste harvested from the canals. It is dense, slightly bitter, and warm from the geothermal heat that rises through the basin floor. It is eaten at every meal, supplemented by whatever is currently fruiting: the heavy-petaled flowers that open in the rare bright hours produce a seed-pod that can be roasted, and the shelf fungi that grow on the tower infrastructure can be sliced thin and dried into something with a chewiness and umami concentration that startles people from drier worlds.

Meals are communal, almost without exception. Food is prepared in shared kitchens built into the structural cores of the walkways — long, narrow spaces where the residual heat from the pump machinery does most of the cooking. The kitchens are always warm and smell of tuber-mash and copper and the faint sweetness of fungal spores. People eat in shifts, because the pumps never stop and neither does work, but the shifts are social occasions. There is a graciousness to Qaar-Vhenner meals that outsiders sometimes mistake for ritual. It is not ritual. It is simply that in a place where silence is made of machinery and the living world is green and mute and rooted in place, the act of eating together is the most complex social event available. So they make much of it. They talk during meals. They are funny — dry, quiet, observational humor, attuned to the smallest changes in a world that changes slowly.

---

They are a people shaped by sound.

In the same way that the absence of animals has left a gap in their concept of the living world, the constant presence of machinery has given them a relationship to sound that is difficult to convey to someone from a quieter place. Qaar-Vhenners do not *listen* to the pumps the way you or I might listen to music or a river. The pumps are not background. They are not foreground. They are *medium* — the substance in which thought moves, the way air is the substance in which birds move for people elsewhere.

They can diagnose a mechanical fault by ear with startling precision. A pump running half a cycle late. A condensation fin that has warped and is producing a slightly higher tone. A section of canal-wall where the water-flow has changed pitch by a quarter-tone and indicates a blockage two turns away. This is not specialized knowledge. It is literacy. Children grow up learning to listen the way children elsewhere learn to read.

Music, where it exists, is percussive and low. Qaar-Vhenner song, on the rare occasions it is performed — at festivals, at the close of the annual canal purge, at certain funerals — uses the sounds of the town itself. Striking metal surfaces at specific points along the walkway network produces tones that resonate through the iron skeleton of the settlement. A skilled performer can, by striking a sequence of beams, columns, and pipe-junctions, essentially *play the town*. The effect is unlike any instrument from a brighter world. It is deep and damp and oddly beautiful — a song of pressure and water and iron vibrating in a bowl of living root.

---

They are — to outside eyes — physically unremarkable. Average height, sturdy build, dark hair, brown eyes in various shades. Skin that is faintly greenish from decades of ambient algal spore and copper particulate. They move with a careful economy, an efficiency of gesture that comes from living in narrow walkways on surfaces that are never fully dry. They are not quick. They are not slow. They are *precise*, the way water finding its level is precise.

Their emotional lives are harder to describe. They are not cold. They are not stoic. But they have a quality that visitors sometimes interpret as indifference and is actually something closer to *patience*. In a world where everything is wet and warm and humming and nothing with a nervous system ever dies because nothing with a nervous system ever lived, the ordinary dramas of elsewhere — urgency, alarm, the sharp surprise of the unexpected — have a smaller place. Things change slowly. The towers cycle. The vegetation grows. The condensate patterns form and dissolve. The Drenn Chamber rises and falls. In that context, a strong emotion is like a stone dropped into a slow, deep river: it makes its impression, but the current is patient and the stone will be covered in silt before long.

This is not apathy. It is a kind of deep, collective calm that comes from living inside a system that is older than anyone's memory and will continue longer than anyone's life. The pumps are older than the oldest resident. The basin is older than the pumps. The roots hold everything together with an indifference to human intention that is neither hostile nor kind. The Qaar-Vhenners have, over generations, learned to live inside that indifference the way the thread-fungi live on the underside of the walkways — not in submission, not in defiance, but in a steady, bioluminescent companionship with something that does not need them.

---

They are generous to visitors, within limits.

They will feed you. They will show you the condensation towers and explain the pump cycles with a pride that is genuine but understated, the way you might explain the operation of your own hands. They will tell you about the condensate patterns and the walkway glow and the Drenn Chamber — always with the caveat that these are just things, just textures of life, not problems to be solved.

But they will not ask you to stay.

And visitors who stay too long — longer than a season, say — begin to change in small, hard-to-name ways. They start waking at the pump-cycle rather than to light. They begin to hear the pitch-change in the water before they can name what they've heard. The dimness stops feeling like a deprivation and starts feeling like a kindness, the way a familiar room feels kind after a long journey. The absence of birdsong stops being eerie and becomes, instead, a clean space — a room in the house of sound where something else can live.

And sometimes — not always, but sometimes — a visitor who has been in Qaar-Vhen too long will be found standing at the edge of the wetland, at the margin where the built world gives way to the open dark of the vegetation, looking out into the fog-shapes of the root-forest with an expression that is not longing and not contentment but something in between. Something unfinished. Something that has not been named because there is no word for it in a language that has no word for animal, because the animal part of a person — the part that wants to chase, to flee, to hear a bird cry out across an open sky and feel the catch in the chest that says *I am alive and so is that other thing and we are different and it matters* — that part goes quiet in Qaar-Vhen, not because it dies but because there is nothing for it to push against.

And the people of Qaar-Vhen, who have never had that part to quiet, look at these visitors with a kind of calm puzzlement, the way you might look at someone crying in a place where no one has ever cried.

They do not comfort. They do not explain. They simply wait, the way everything in Qaar-Vhen waits — in the warm damp dimness, among the roots and the iron and the water that is always rising and never quite reaching — for the next phase of the cycle.
