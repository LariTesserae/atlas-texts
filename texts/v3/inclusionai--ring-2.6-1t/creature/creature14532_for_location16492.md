## The Inhabitants of the Columned Reach

---

### The People

They call themselves something that does not translate cleanly into any neighboring tongue — a word that hovers between *we who remain* and *the root system.* Outsiders, on the rare occasions they have stumbled into the basin and stumbled back out, have called them the Mossborn, the Rootfolk, the Reachmen. None of these names are correct. None of them are wrong enough to be entirely useless.

What is certain is this: they have been here long enough to become a feature of the landscape in the way that buttress roots and shelf fungus are features of the landscape — not placed, but *grown.* The earliest stories they tell are not about arrival. They are about recognition. *We were here, and then we noticed.* That is the shape of their origin. Whether it is true or not is, in a sense, irrelevant. They inhabit their origin story the way the basin inhabits the ridgeline: it fits, it holds, the water runs along its channels, and no other shape would explain what is present.

There are perhaps forty of them. The number is not fixed — it breathes. Children come in clusters, like fruiting seasons. Elders leave slowly, thinning into the settlement the way old trees hollow and become habitat for smaller, stranger life. No one is abandoned, but neither is death treated as a disruption. It is taken as a settling — the way sediment settles after the water slows. The basin knows this rhythm. The people have made it their own.

### Bodies

You see their bodies before you see their faces.

They are compact. Dense in the way that things are dense when every calorie costs something to extract from wet soil and steep ground. Broad hands with short fingers that grip vines and split cane with a precision that looks almost mechanical until you notice the variation — the way each hand adapts to the particular twist of a root or the particular tension of a wet lash. Their grip is not performed. It is ambient, like the way the canopy holds rain.

Their skin is dark — not uniformly so, but in the way that lichen is dark: layered, shaded, responsive. Sun-dark beneath sun-exposed ridges, lighter where cloth or canopy covers them, and everywhere faintly damp. They smell of woodsmoke and plantain sap and something else — a faintly fermented sweetness, like overripe forest fruit carried in the pores. This smell is not unpleasant. It is the smell of a body metabolizing a place rather than merely living in it.

Their movement through the forest floor is the most startling thing about them. Where you stumble — on root, on moss, on the slick uneven surface that shifts underfoot like a living thing — they move with the practiced negligence of people who have never had the concept of *rough terrain.* Every surface beneath their bare feet is legible to them. They know which moss is spongy and which is firm. They know which root will hold weight and which is mid-decay. A child of perhaps five years walks ahead of you on a path you cannot see, bare feet pressing into the green substrate with an economy of pressure that suggests the ground is speaking to her and she is listening.

They are not tall. Average stature hovers around what you would call short in your own frame of reference, though the word feels wrong applied to them, the way *short* feels wrong applied to a moss-covered boulder. They are proportioned for the basin. Their center of gravity is low. Their joints are flexible in ways that suggest generations of climbing, crouching, threading through spaces built for smaller bodies or for bodies that simply never stand fully upright when there is something to lean against, something to brace a shoulder against, something to drape an arm over while working.

Their faces are broad-boned, with strong jaws and wide-set eyes that are almost universally dark brown so deep it reads as black in low canopy light. Their hair — thick, tightly coiled — is worn in ways that change with age and status. Children's hair is cropped close or left wild. Adults wear it longer, often threaded through with small bones, seeds, strips of dried vine, or coils of hammered copper that must be traded from elsewhere. The eldest among them — and there are always eldest among them, they live longer than you would expect given the conditions — wear their hair loose and down, falling past the shoulders, thick as moss. It is possible this is a choice. It is also possible the hair simply outlives the impulse to cut it, and they find the arrangement satisfactory.

### Dwellings and Domestic Life

The homes do not have doors in the way you would recognize. They have openings — archways cut from woven heartwood poles, sized for a body to pass through by ducking and turning slightly, a shape that is not hostile but is not permissive either. It asks something of you. *You must become slightly smaller to enter this space.* The effect, whether intentional or not, is that you cannot bring your full upright posture inside. You meet these people on their terms, in their architecture, which is to say: in a posture of slight accommodation.

Inside, the dwelling is dim. Light enters through gaps in the roofing — deliberate gaps, layered with overlapping fronds that shed rain but admit a grey-green glow. The floor is the ground itself: root mat and moss, compressed to something almost soft, covered with woven mats of stripped palm fiber. Against the walls — which are little more than the outer poles of the structure, daubed with clay and alive with the inevitable green of moss that has begun colonizing the surface — there are sleeping platforms, raised a foot or two off the ground. These are not beds in any comfortable sense you would recognize. They are broad woven platforms, stiff, covered with layered fronds and furs that are so saturated with use they have become almost felted. They are warm. They smell of bodies and woodsmoke and something faintly fungal, like the underside of a shelf mushroom.

The domestic rhythm is organized around water.

This is the first thing you understand when you watch them move through a day. Water is the metronome. The cisterns fill and are checked and emptied into cooking vessels and washing basins and storage gourds. The pool behind the dam is monitored — not with instruments but with attention, the way you might monitor a fire or a sleeping child. When the pool rises too high, the dam is reinforced. When the seepage from the weeping rocks slows in drier periods, the channels are cleared of accumulated leaf matter with the same patient, practiced hands that weave baskets and tie lashings.

Food is gathered in a rotation that follows no single calendar but seems to follow every calendar at once — the fruiting cycles of canopy trees, the breeding cycles of the fish and frogs in the pool, the emergence patterns of insects, the readiness of root vegetables that are cultivated in small clearings where the canopy has been deliberately thinned to allow light. They grow things. Not in rows, not in fields, but in *patches* — a spiral of tuberous plants around a young hardwood, a cluster of fruiting shrubs nursed in the light-gap left by a fallen tree, a ring of edible ferns at the base of a buttress root. Everything they cultivate is interplanted with things they do not cultivate but encourage: medicinal herbs, shade-tolerant greens, fungi that fruit on the rotting heartwood of particular tree species they seem to favor in their planting.

They smoke meat over the cobble-lined fire pits with a patience that borders on ceremony. The fish — dull-scaled, silver-bellied things pulled from the tannin-dark pool with woven traps — are split and laid on racks above a low, steady smoke from particular wood species they select with care. The smell pervades the settlement. It is not the sharp, acrid smoke of a campfire but something deeper, sweeter, almost floral. The meat takes days. They do not rush it. While it smokes, they work on other things. While it smokes, the basin smokes with them.

### Society (Such As It Is)

There are no chiefs. There are no councils. There are, instead, *practitioners.*

The old woman who checks the cisterns each morning, running her fingers along the waterline of each hollowed log with the focused attention of someone reading text — she is a practitioner. The man who maintains the rope bridges, testing each vine-lashing with a pull of his hand, replacing fibers that have begun to weaken under the slow insistence of moisture and weight — he is a practitioner. The woman who oversees the smoking of meat, who knows by smell alone when the wood is right and when the heat needs adjusting, who moves between the fire pits with the unhurried precision of someone who has never once burned a batch — she is a practitioner.

Authority here is not wielded. It is *exercised* — in the older sense of the word, meaning it is put to use, applied through competence, recognized but not ritualized. Disputes, of which there seem to be remarkably few, are resolved through a process that looks, from the outside, like extended conversation conducted at low volume while doing something practical — weaving, mending, scraping fibers. There is no raised voice in the settlement. This is not a rule they enforce. It is a threshold they have crossed. The ambient volume of the basin — that immense, layered drone of water and insect and wind and leaf — renders shouting not merely unnecessary but absurd. You cannot compete with a waterfall's throat. You learn, eventually, to speak underneath it.

Children are everywhere and nowhere. They appear at the edges of your vision — crouched on a root watching something in the leaf litter, draped over a branch reading for fruit, following at a distance with an attention that is not quite shyness and not quite curiosity but something closer to *assessment.* They learn by proximity. They are not formally taught so much as allowed to exist in the presence of competence until they absorb it, the way the walls absorb moss. By the time they are eight or nine, they are functional members of the settlement's rhythm, contributing in small but real ways — carrying water, sorting drying fish, learning the particular hand-grip for splitting bamboo.

### The Relationship

The most important thing to understand about the people of the Columned Reach is that they do not see themselves as separate from the basin.

This is not mysticism. It is not philosophy. It is simply the conclusion that an extended family of forty humans, living for an unknowable number of generations inside a self-sustaining ecosystem that is large enough to support them and small enough to be understood in its entirety, will inevitably arrive at: *we are part of this, in the way that a stomach is part of a body, or a root is part of a tree.* They take from the basin. The basin regenerates. They take again. The cycle does not need to be honored. It simply *is*, the way gravity simply is. They have built their entire civilization around the principle of not breaking what is already working.

This is visible in everything. It is visible in the way their dwellings are shaped around growing trunks rather than cutting them. It is visible in the way they harvest from the forest in rotation, never stripping a single area, always leaving enough for regrowth. It is visible in the way their waste returns to the substrate — composted, incorporated, unceremonious. The boundary between settlement and forest is not a wall. It is a gradient. At the edges of the dwellings, the human-made dissolves into the grown: a wall becomes a trunk, a pathway becomes a root network, a cistern's overflow becomes a stream that feeds the pool that feeds the dam that holds the water that seeps into the ground that feeds the roots that hold the trees that hold the canopy that holds the water that condenses and falls and feeds the cisterns again.

They are not innocent. They are not noble savages. They are forty human beings living in a very wet place, and they have gotten very, very good at it. They have preferences and opinions and attachments and small jealousies and private joys. They argue about whose turn it is to clean the fish traps. They have favorites among the cooking pots. They sing — quietly, always quietly, because the basin does not require volume — while they work, and the songs are old and do not have words you would recognize, though they have rhythm, and the rhythm sounds, if you close your eyes, like dripping water finding its way down a root.

### The Fauna Between and Among

The animals are not separate from the people. They are not pets, nor are they pests. They are *co-inhabitants* — present, active, indifferent, tolerated, sometimes encouraged, sometimes eaten, always accounted for.

The birds in the canopy — the ones with the iridescent throat patches and the sharp overlapping calls — are not kept but they are not shooed away. The settlement exists within their territory, and they within it, an arrangement negotiated over generations through mutual familiarity. The birds nest in the trees that grow through and around the dwellings. They eat the fruiting branches that overhang the walkways. Their droppings fall on the clay-daub walls. No one cleans the droppings quickly. The droppings feed the mosses that feed the insects that feed the birds. No one tracks this cycle. Everyone participates in it.

The iridescent-headed waterfowl in the pool behind the dam — it is seen and noted. It is not domesticated, but the dam is built in a way that accommodates its presence: a shallow lip where it can enter and exit freely, a section of bank kept clear for nesting. Whether this was designed or simply emerged from decades of coexistence is a question that would not occur to the settlement's inhabitants. The answer, in any case, is both.

Beneath the structures, in the spaces between root buttresses and packed earth, live the things that the people do not speak about with fear or distaste but with the neutral acknowledgment of neighbors. Large spiders with webs strung between posts. The armored millipedes that emerge at dusk and process in slow, dignified lines across the pathways. The pale termite-like colonies whose ridged towers appear on selected logs — logs that the settlement has chosen *not* to use for building, because these colonies are understood to be performing work: breaking down cellulose, returning nutrients to the substrate, performing the slow alchemy of decomposition that the entire basin depends on. You do not kick over a termite mound here. You might as well kick over a compost heap. You might as well kick over your own stomach.

And the large things — the dark, heavy shapes that move through the understory fifty paces beyond the settlement's edge with a slow, grunting deliberation — these are acknowledged with a particular kind of attention. Not fear, not habituation, but the respect you give to something that could harm you but has not yet and likely will not, because the arrangement works. The settlement does not expand into the deep forest. The deep forest does not come into the settlement. The boundary is maintained by mutual indifference and the peculiar, unspoken agreement that exists between a large predator and a small community that has made itself useful to the landscape rather than adversarial within it.

The evening I imagine — because you have not yet been told there is an evening, but there is, the basin dims and cools and the sound changes as the diurnal creatures pull back and the nocturnal ones begin their shift — the evening has the people gathered near the fire pits, the cobble-stones still holding the day's warmth, the smoke threading upward through gaps in the canopy and vanishing into the grey. Children sit close to adults. The water in the cisterns catches the last light and holds it. Something in the pool has begun its rhythmic thrumming — that sub-bass you feel in your sternum before you locate the source. The frogs, enormous-throated, singing from their floating logs.

No one speaks above a murmur. The insects are rebuilding their wall of sound. A bird calls once from deep in the canopy — a single clear note above the drone, like a pin dropping in a cathedral — and is answered, seconds later, from a direction you cannot identify.

This is what lives in the Columned Reach. Not creatures, not specimens, not wildlife. *Inhabitants.* All of them. The moss and the millipedes and the people and the frogs and the unseen things chewing in the rotting logs. A single, breathing concentration of the normal. A place that has reached equilibrium and called it home.
