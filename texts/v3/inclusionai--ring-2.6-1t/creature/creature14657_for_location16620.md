## What Lives in the Cut

---

Start with the people.

Not because they are the most interesting things living in the Column of Calcara — they almost certainly are not — but because they are the loudest, and loud things are easiest to find when you arrive somewhere new and frightened and still smelling of the train.

They are, first of all, dry. Everything about them is dry. Their skin has the matte, papery quality of cured leather, even in the young ones. Their lips are always cracked. Their eyes are bright with a mineral clarity — not soft, not wet, but sharp, the way a freshly fractured crystal face is sharp, catching light at angles you did not expect. They blink less than you are used to. Their eyelashes are caked withite dust, perpetually, the way someone from a wet world might have perpetually damp hair.

They walk a particular way. Flat-footed. Weight forward on the ball of the foot, heels settling last, always testing. New arrivals shuffle and stumble on the uneven scaffold platforms, the corrugated metal walkways, the sheer bare rock faces that serve as paths. But after a few weeks — a month, two months — you stop looking down. Your feet learn the column the way a pianist's hands learn a keyboard: not through thought but through pressure, through the reading of texture through leather and rubber and callused skin. A miner who has spent five years in the Cut can tell you what level he is on, approximately, by the feel of the stone underfoot. He cannot explain how. He does not try.

Their hands are remarkable. Gnarled, sure-fingered, mapped with tiny white scars from calccrete splinters — which are not splinters, really, but crystalline fractures that leave wounds which do not bleed normally but instead seal over with a thin, glassy membrane, almost like a mineral scab. The older workers' hands look, at a glance, less like hands and more like particularly expressive pieces of the column itself. They grip things with an economy of motion that looks almost lazy until you see them working — threading bolts in a pressurized vent with fingers that seem to know the threads by touch, guiding a hoist cable around a drum while carrying on a conversation and chewing a strip of dried chemofungus with the same mouth.

They are not a people who waste gesture. The Cut does not allow it. Space is narrow. A flailing elbow can send someone off a walkway. A sudden movement near a ventilation shaft can be swallowed by the updraft and never seen again. So they move with a careful, compressed efficiency, and their expressiveness comes in other ways — in the angle of a tilted respirator mask during conversation (the equivalent of a raised eyebrow), in the specific rhythm of knuckles tapped against a railing (a greeting, a farewell, a lie, depending on the number and tempo), in the low, murmuring half-voices they use, because full voices carry strangely in the column and are considered, if not rude, then at least conspicuously amateurish.

There are four thousand of them. There are also perhaps a thousand more than that, if you count the workers who arrive on the ore trains from the basin settlements, work six-month contracts, and leave before their faces have fully changed. These contract laborers are the ones who most often speak of the Cut in the same terms: *it gets into you*. Not poetically. Descriptively. The calccrete dust. It is in the lungs of everyone who has spent more than a month breathing un-filtered air in the lower levels. It is in the creases of their knuckles and the folds of their eyelids. Geologists would tell you it is chemically inert. The people of the Cut do not care what geologists would tell you. They have seen the dust in a dead man's open palm, settled into the whorls of his fingerprints like sediment in a riverbed, and they know that the column takes its people, slowly, and makes them part of itself.

---

Now go smaller.

The skitterlings.

You will hear them before you see them, if you are on the lower walkways at the right hour — the dim hours, the hours that are not quite night but not anything you would call day, when the phosphorescence of the stone is brightest and everything glows in shades of dull amber and greenish bone. A clicking. Not random. Rhythmic enough to feel like communication, arrhythmic enough to feel alive.

They pour from the cracks. The column's surface is, up close, crazed with fissures — some the width of a finger, some barely wide enough to pass a wire through. The skitterlings live in these fissures, packed against the stone in copper-colored rows, their exoskeletons clattering against the walls of their burrows like coins in a pocket. When they emerge, they do not rush. They pour, the way smoke emerges from a chimney: one, then a few, then a stream, then a flood, until the entire wall of a walkway seems to shiver with their movement.

They are beautiful, in the way that things designed entirely by function are beautiful, with no wasted line and no decoration beyond the sheer gleam of their chitin, which catches the phosphorescent light and throws it back in coppery flashes. Six legs, usually, though you will occasionally see one with eight or ten — mutations, the biologists say, or perhaps adaptive variation, no one is sure. Their bodies are segmented, flattened, built low to the stone. They can run across a vertical face of raw calccrete without hesitation, clinging with hooked tarsi that grip the crystal planes of the stone as if they were made for exactly this purpose. Perhaps they were.

They feed on rustbloom, which they scrape from stone with jaw structures that move with a speed that blurs the eye. They feed on each other. They feed on anything organic — and in a place where organic matter is so scarce that a torn glove left on a walkway will be reduced to thread and leather paste within a week, the skitterlings are a reminder that life does not require softness. It requires only pressure, and time, and something to react with.

---

Above the skitterlings, a different kind of life.

The harness-jackers. The size of a large dog, if a dog had been redesigned by someone who had never seen a dog but had read a very precise description. Four arms, each ending in a hooked, three-jointed claw that can grip a scaffold rail, a ventilation pipe, or a writhing fistful of skitterlings with equal ease. Wide, flat bodies, segmented like a pill bug's but armored in a darker chitin — black-green, almost — with a pattern of ridged crests along the back that vibrate audibly when the creature is agitated, producing a sound like a thumb run along the teeth of a fine comb.

They are solitary. They are territorial. And they are, for reasons no one fully understands, obsessed with the refinery vents.

The vents, which exhaust the heat and toxic byproducts of calccrete processing, accumulate mineral deposits on their exteriors — crystallized effluent that clogs the mesh filters and reduces airflow. Within days of cleaning, the deposits return. Within hours of returning, a harness-jacker will have scaled the vent housing and begun methodically scraping them away with its claws, consuming the mineral crust with a focus and regularity that borders on mechanical. The refinery operators, who are practical people, noticed early on that a vent with a resident jacker stayed clear far longer than one without. So they began leaving crushedite crystal on the rooftops near the vents. The jackers take the offerings, and in return, they stay. They keep the vents clear.

No one calls this domestication. No one calls it symbiosis, either. An older rail operator, a woman named Maren who had lived in the Cut for nineteen years and spoke with the quiet authority of someone who had survived five dynamite collapses and two ventilation failures, once described it as follows: *"We leave them a plate. They leave us breathing. I don't need to know what they call it."*

---

And above the jackers — above everything, really — the thermals.

You cannot describe a thermal by looking at one, because looking is not how you experience a thermal. You experience a thermal the way you experience a change in air pressure before a storm, or the way you experience silence in a room full of people who have simultaneously stopped speaking. It is a presence felt before it is seen.

You feel the air move first. A warm updraft from somewhere deep within the column, rising through shafts and conduits and open spaces, pushing against you from below like the breath of something vast and patient. Then, if you are looking up — and everyone in the Cut, eventually, looks up — you see a shape. Pale. Vast. The color of smoke, or perhaps of something that exists just on the other side of visibility, a color your eyes almost have a word for but do not. The body is membranous, translucent, and it shifts as it moves in a way that suggests not flight but *drifting* — the slow, purposeful drift of a cloud that has decided, for reasons of its own, to move in a particular direction.

They have no eyes. At least, no structure that anyone has been able to identify as an eye. No mouth, either. What they have, on their undersides — and you must be very far below them, looking up, which means you must be at the upper levels, standing on a platform near the crown of the column and looking into the open space above — are spirals. Intake spirals, vast and ciliated, opening and closing in slow rhythms that suggest respiration, or perhaps feeding, or perhaps something for which there is no human analog. They draw air through these spirals, filtering particulate —ite dust, calccrete microcrystals, the chemical effluvium of the refineries — and they do something with it. No one knows what. The filtered material vanishes into the body of the thermal and is not observed again.

The sound. The sound is what makes the thermals sacred.

It is not loud. It is not even, strictly speaking, a sound — not the way that a stamp mill or a human voice is a sound. It is a tone, low and resonant, that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, from the column itself, from the air, from the interior of your own chest. It sustains. It holds a single, deep note for minutes at a time, shifting almost imperceptibly in pitch, the way a cello note shifts when the player moves their finger a fraction of a millimeter. Acoustic instruments could not produce it. Natural phenomena — wind, resonance, vibration — do not fully explain it. The people of the Cut have tried. The geologists have tried. The instrument-makers and the musicians who have come up on the ore train and smelled the dust and heard the note and understood immediately that no concert hall on the planet could replicate it have tried. No one explains it. No one can.

What is known: the thermals do not respond to stimuli. They do not react to light, to sound, to the presence of people directly beneath them. They drift in slow, overlapping circuits near the crown of the column, occasionally rising higher, occasionally descending, their paths intersecting and diverging with a grace that looks choreographed but cannot be, because there is no intelligence visible to direct it. They are, by every measurable criterion, weather. They are, by every felt criterion, alive.

No one harms a thermal. No one would know how.

---

Now go deeper.

Below the lowest platforms — below the reinforced walkways and the emergency supply caches and the bolted-shut hatches that bear faded warning signs in three different handwriting styles — there is a shaft. The deep core. It drops through the column like a throat, narrowing as it descends, the walls transitioning from engineered stone to raw calccrete to something rougher, older, with striations that suggest not geological formation but *growth*. Rings, if you like. Like the rings of a tree, except that trees require rain and soil and centuries of sunlight, and none of those things have existed here for longer than anyone can reckon.

No one goes down the deep core. People go *to* the deep core. Workers on decompression shifts have reported hearing, on the lowest level — Level Seven, which is the last level before the shaft becomes impassable — a vibration. Not the ambient geological hum that everyone hears everywhere in the Cut. This is different. It is rhythmic. It is slow. It matches, if you press your hand to the wall and close your eyes, the rhythm of your own heartbeat. Not exactly. Close enough to be a coincidence. Close enough to be unsettling.

The burrowing worms live somewhere in the walls at this depth, or nearby. No one has seen one whole. Only their vibrations, transmitted through the stone, perceived as a subsonic pressure in the jaw, the teeth, the inner ear. Occasionally, on Level Six, someone finds a cast — a hollow impression in the rock, sinuous, a few centimeters across, as if a flexible thing had pressed itself into soft stone and then moved on, leaving its shape behind. The casts are catalogued. They are photographed. No one can say with certainty what made them.

And higher — not up, but *inward*, through the column's labyrinthine interior passages that the miners and engineers have mapped in fragments and no one has mapped whole — there are the flicker-clouds. Something moves through the upper chambers in coordinated masses, not quite solid, not quite gaseous, their bodies emitting pulses of pale bioluminescence that ripple through the group in patterns that resemble, distantly, language. They are gone before you can reach them. All that remains is the afterimage: a bright, branching pattern burned into your vision for seconds afterward, like looking at the sun, if the sun had written something you almost understood.

---

And beneath all of this, underlying all of this, holding all of this together: the column itself.

The question — the question that the geologists argue about in the bare room with the bolted table and the single lamp, the question that the miners avoid and the thermals seem to orbit and the skitterlings seem to serve — is whether the column is alive.

Not alive the way the skitterlings are alive, or the harness-jackers, or even the thermals, which at least move with apparent purpose. Alive the way a *system* is alive. The way a reef is alive, or a forest soil, or the slow metabolic churn of a single organism too large to see from the inside. The calccrete, with itsite vascular analogs. The rustbloom, feeding on chemical energy and glowing as it does. The vibration, the deep geological tone, the rhythm that matches a heartbeat. The way the column grows — not quickly, not visibly, but measurably, millimeter by millimeter, year by year, calccrete depositing on calccrete in patterns that look, if you are the sort of person who looks at mineral formations and sees intention, like rings. Like growth rings. Like the record of a life.

No one answers this question. Everyone who lives in the Cut has an opinion. No one's opinion settles it.

What matters is this: the ore is good. The rail runs on schedule. The dust settles. The water reclamation keeps running, recycling breath and condensation and the thin biological moisture of four thousand people pressed against the stone, and the column holds them the way a lung holds air — compact, warm, alive, or close enough.

The Cut holds.

And at night — which is not night, not really, but the dimming of the mineral glow, the amber-green light fading to deep bronze and then to near-darkness, broken only by the arcs and gas mantles — the skitterlings pour from the walls. The harness-jackers crouch on the railings, their crests vibrating. Somewhere above, a thermal turns in its slow orbit and the organ-note swells in the chests of everyone in the Cut, whether they hear it or only feel it, whether they know it is sacred or only know it is old.

And somewhere below, impossibly deep, something hums back.
