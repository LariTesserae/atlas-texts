## What Lives in the Young Hollow

---

The people who live here have become, in ways they do not fully recognize, people of stone. Not by choice — no one sat down and decided to become mineral — but slowly, the way water becomes the shape of whatever holds it. Their hands are rough in a way their hands above ground were not. Their eyes have adjusted to a light that has no source, and sometimes, when a newcomer arrives from the upper passages, they watch that person blink and squint and turn their face upward, and they feel a recognition that is not quite pity and not quite pride. They have been changed by the place without announcing that they intended to be.

There are perhaps forty of them. No one has counted — counting the whole population has never seemed necessary, the way no one counts the number of stones in a wall. They are families, mostly, drawn from a handful of settlements that no longer hold together above. Some came alone. One old woman, the diggers say, simply appeared one morning at the passage mouth with a bundle on her back and sat down at the edge of the central fire-ring and said nothing until someone handed her a bowl of water, at which point she nodded once and never spoke of where she had been. She tends the fungal shelves in the lower tunnels now, checking their glow the way a shepherd checks eyes in the dark. No one asked her to. No one stopped her.

The children are the most fully adapted. They move through the passages with a confidence that would be recklessness above ground — swinging around tight corners, ducking under low ceilings, leaping gaps where the floor drops a half-step — all without looking. They have never known the open sky, though some of the older children, the ones with sharp memories or parents who talk too freely, carry a kind of phantom image: a vast, flat brightness that they cannot describe except by what it is not. They call it *the wide-thing*. They are afraid of it and curious about it in equal measure, which is perhaps the most honest thing any of the Hollow's inhabitants feel about anything.

The children gather in the unassigned chamber more than anyone else. They sense it before the adults do — something in the geometry of the space, the way sound sits in it without fleeing. They have started to use it without permission, without announcement. A few of them drag flat stones into a rough circle and sit there and talk in the low, careful way their parents have taught them, though what they say is not what their parents would say. They tell stories about the place. They have begun, without knowing they have begun, to give it a history.

The adults watch. They do not correct the children's stories. They listen the way one listens to something growing — something that might be a useful plant or might be a crack in the foundation, and which has not yet made its intentions clear.

The diggers are a class unto themselves, though no one would call it a class. They are simply the ones who go deeper. They come back with their faces pale under the stone-light, their pupils blown wide, speaking little for the first hour after surfacing into the tunnels. They know the rock the way a sailor knows the sea — not as an adversary, but as a medium with moods. They know which stone will split clean and which will crumble, which vein of limestone hides a hollow space beneath and which is solid to the core. They have opinions about the rock that they express in a shorthand so compressed it barely qualifies as language: a grunt, a flick of the wrist, a held-up hand that means *not here, try three spans to the left*. They are the ones who found the hum. Or rather, they are the ones who first said something about it, and then everyone else realized they had been hearing it too.

There is a young digger — not the youngest, but young enough that her knuckles are still smooth and her eyes have not yet taken on the far-look of the deep workers — who digs a little differently from the others. Where they follow the stone's grain, she sometimes pushes against it, testing. Where they accept a fracture as a boundary, she pauses at it and taps, listens, taps again. The others watch her without concern but also without indifference. She reminds them of something. They cannot name it. It is perhaps simply the memory of the first person who drove a flint axe into the rock above and decided to go down.

The water-keepers are quieter people, more methodical. They know the channels the way the diggers know the stone — not emotionally, but with a precision that borders on intimacy. They clear the silt every rotation, and they have learned to read the water's behavior the way the diggers read the rock. When the flow thins near the east passage, they know a crack has shifted upstream. When the pool grows cloudy for no reason, they check the upper fissures for new debris. They are the ones who notice things first: a change in temperature, a new damp spot, a mineral smell that wasn't there before. They are the Hollow's nervous system, and they know it, though they would never say so.

The fire-tenders might be the most important people in the place, though they would deny it. The central fire-ring is never allowed to go out. Not that fire is strictly necessary for warmth — the Hollow holds its temperature with a stubborn consistency — but it is necessary for something else. The people gather there in the dim hours, the hours when the reflective panels are covered and the stone-light dims to its faintest, and they sit in a loose ring and talk or don't talk and tend small personal fires with sticks and stare into the coals and feel, without articulating it, that the fire is the thing that separates a shelter from a home. The fire-tenders feed it. They choose the wood carefully — root-wood, mostly, brought down from above, dried slowly in the Hollow's cool air until it burns with a steady, smokeless patience. They know which wood crackles and which wood sighs and which wood throws sparks in a way that makes children gasp.

There is no leader. There is the council of five, but the five shift, and their authority is the kind that exists only because no one has contested it. They are not rulers. They are more like the place where two currents of water meet — a point of convergence, not a source of force. Disputes are rare. When they come, they are settled the way everything else is settled in the Hollow: through conversation that goes on too long, through silences that mean more than words, through someone finally standing up and walking to the disputed alcove and looking at it and then walking back and saying, quietly, something that both parties can accept.

---

The grey lizards are not pets. They have never been pets. They arrived — or perhaps they were always here, and the people only noticed them after a while, the way you notice a sound that has been there all along. They are small enough to cup in a hand, but no one cups them. They are too fast for that, and too indifferent. They bask on cooking stones and dart into crevices when feet approach, and they return when the feet are gone, and this pattern has repeated so consistently that the children have named individuals — there is the one with the crooked tail, there is the pair that always appear together, there is the large one that sits on the warmest stone in the cooking area and does not move even when the fire is lit nearby. The lizards eat insects. The children insist that the lizards also listen to things. The adults smile at this. But some of them, late at night, when the fire is low and the hum is loud, look at the lizards on their warm stones and think: *yes. They are listening.*

The beetles are armoured and unconcerned. They appear in the cooking areas and near the water channels, small and dark and clicking faintly when disturbed. No one tries to remove them. No one tries to keep them. They are simply there, the way the stone is simply there, and their presence is recorded by no one because it has never seemed remarkable. If one were to ask a child, *what is a beetle?*, the child would say: *it is a small hard thing that clicks and lives near the water.* The child would not understand why this needed explaining.

The winged thing near the pool — no one agrees on what it is. It appears at irregular intervals, always at the pool's edge, always briefly. It is larger than the beetles, dark, and moves in a way that suggests wings but too fast to confirm. It has never been caught. No one tries to catch it. There is an unspoken agreement, not articulated but understood, that some things in the Hollow are better met with attention than with hands. The settlers watch it the way they watch everything — with a proprietary care that has no word for itself. *We see you,* their watching says. *You are part of this.*

---

The moss is the oldest living thing in the Hollow, though it grows so slowly that the word *living* feels almost aggressive. It spreads in dark, wet patches along the water lines, thick as felt, the colour of deep earth after rain. It smells ancient. The settlers walk on it, lean against it, let their hands rest on it without thinking. It is the closest thing the Hollow has to a carpet, a floor-covering, a softness. It makes the stone feel less like stone. The settlers do not know this is what it does, but they would recognize the absence of it instantly, the way you recognize the absence of a sound you did not know you were hearing.

The fungal shelves glow. They glow faintly, steadily, in the passages where the air is thick and the light from above has never reached. They are bone-white, slightly translucent at the edges, and they pulse — so faintly that you must watch for a long time before you are certain the pulse is real. The lower workers use them as markers. *Past the third shelf, the passage forks.* *Where the glow is brightest, the air is bad.* The shelves do not care. They grow, they glow, they persist. They are the oldest residents of the deep Hollow, older than the people, older perhaps than the hum, and they will remain when everything else has been rebuilt or abandoned or forgotten. They are patient in a way that is not patience at all but something older — something closer to the patience of stone, or of light travelling through stone, or of water finding, drop by drop, the path of least resistance through a wall that did not know it had a weakness.

---

And then there is the shoot.

The pale shoot in the crack in the floor. The one that was scraped back twice and returned both times. It is the most contested thing in the Hollow, though no one has raised their voice over it. Some say it should be allowed to grow — *look at it, how it bends toward the light, it is trying so hard, let it try*. Others say it will crack the stone if it is not stopped — *it does not know what it is becoming, and the stone knows itself, and the stone does not want this.* No decision has been made. No one with authority has weighed in. The shoot grows. It is not yet identifiable as anything. Its leaves are scales, its stem is pale, its roots are somewhere in the crack where no one can see. It wants something it has never had. It bends toward the fissure-light with the conviction of a thing that has confused wanting with existing.

It will be named eventually. Everything is named eventually. But not yet. Not yet.

---

The hum from below does not live. It does not grow, it does not die, it does not eat or reproduce or respond to being observed. It is not a creature, not a force, not a message. But the people feel it in the mornings when they wake in their alcoves and press their palms flat against the stone floor, steadying themselves before standing. They feel it in the soles of their feet. It is there — not warm, not cold, just *present*, like a second heartbeat beneath the first.

Some of the settlers say it is the Hollow itself. Not its voice — it has no voice — but its awareness. They do not say this as metaphor. They say it the way they say the water runs downhill: as a fact that needs no further explanation. The Hollow is aware of them. They are aware of the Hollow. The work of coexistence goes on, stone by stone, day by unday, in the steady, breathing dark.
