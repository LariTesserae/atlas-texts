## The Residents of the Condensation Plate

You don't see them all at once. You don't see them at first at all, only the evidence of them — the faint warmth that comes from a corridor too regular in its geometry to be natural, the amber glow behind a wall that is not lit but is luminous, the way certain stones seem to shift, almost imperceptibly, when you're not looking directly at them. And then one steps into view — or, more accurately, one allows itself to be seen — and you understand that everything about the Condensation Plate that felt *deliberate* was deliberate precisely because of them.

They are tall. Not towering, but tall in the way that a column is tall — vertical, proportioned for a landscape defined by ridges and verticals. Their limbs are long, but not the gracile length of the plateau's fauna. Thicker. More articulated. Four upper limbs, though the lower pair are shorter and broader, used for bracing and climbing the interior lattices of the settlements rather than the open plain. They walk on two, always, but in a way that suggests the other two limbs are never fully idle — trailing, resting, ready, like a third pair of hands that simply prefers not to be used for walking.

Their skin is the color of the stone, and this is not coincidence. It is a matte dark grey that carries the same faint iridescence as the fauna, though subtler — visible only when the fog thins and the pale light comes in at an angle. It is not hide, as the ungulate-creatures' is; it is smoother, thinner, almost translucent at the extremities, where you can see the dark threadwork of capillaries beneath the surface. Their bodies radiate heat slowly and unevenly — you feel them before you see them in darkness, a warmth that comes in patches, like sun through cloud. They are not warm-blooded in any way you would recognize. They are *regulated* — their internal temperature a careful negotiation between the geothermal warmth beneath the stone and the cold condensing air above, maintained by the same molecular infrastructure that heats their homes.

Their faces are angular, but not sharp. The bones are visible in structure — a long, narrow skull, high cheekbones that catch the amber light, a mouth that is wide but rarely opened wide, flanked by sensory organs that are not eyes in the terrestrial sense but serve the purpose in a broader spectrum. Their eyes — or the organs you take for eyes — are deep-set, dark, and reflective in the way that still water is reflective. In the low light of the settlement interiors they seem to glow faintly, not with luminescence but with an *efficiency of absorption*, as though they are drinking in every photon rather than wasting one. When they look at you, there is an unsettling patience in it. Not coldness. Not warmth. Something prior to both — a quality of attention that belongs to a being whose relationship to time is negotiated in geological terms.

They speak, but not always with sound. They have voices — low, resonant, pitched to carry in the stone corridors without echo, harmonics shaped to complement the subsonic hum of the infrastructure — but much of their communication happens through touch and proximity. Hands pressed against shared walls. Limbs intertwined in the amber-lit communal spaces in configurations that look like sculpture, like architecture, like a language written in the body. When they speak aloud, their words are few and deliberate, each one given the weight of a stone set down. They do not chatter. The thin air, the thin ecology, the thin margin between survival and entropy — these have not made them sparse out of hardship, but out of philosophy. They believe — or perhaps they simply know — that every utterance changes the atmospheric balance in a room. That every gesture of excess is a small betrayal of the equilibrium that keeps them alive.

The young are different, of course. Small — but not fragile. Dense-bodied, with oversized sensory organs and a restless energy that the adults seem to find endearing the way you might find a geological event endearing: a landslide reshaping a familiar ridge, interesting, destructive, and not your problem. The young climb the vertical interiors with all six limbs, making use of the fourth pair that the adults have largely repurposed. They are louder. They experiment with sound, with light, with material — pulling at the crystalline fibers in the walls, tasting the condensation with tongues that are more sensitive than anything you can imagine. They are tolerated. They are watched. They are the only evidence you see of emotion in this place — something wilder and less controlled than the adults project, flickering across their young faces in expressions that are almost mammalian, almost recognizable, almost *yours*, before they are taught to be still.

And there is teaching. There is deep, sustained, patient teaching. It is the primary social act. Knowledge here is not stored in writing or in any medium you would call digital. It is stored in the stone itself — in the configuration of the infrastructure, in the molecular arrangement of the walls, in the precise calibration of crystal matrices that the young must learn to read the way you would learn to read a book. A fully educated resident of the Condensation Plate can press a hand against any wall, any surface, and read the history of the settlement's atmospheric processing, its water cycles, its thermal management, the record of every repair, every modification, every seasonal adjustment — all encoded in the structure of the material. This is not metaphor. They do this. It takes decades. A master of this art is called *takhalis* — a word that translates, imperfectly, as "one who has read the house."

They are not numerous. Across the entire plateau — hundreds of kilometers of stone, fog, and silence — there may be three or four thousand of them, distributed across the five settlements and their outlier stations with the same deliberate precision as the fauna. They do not breed quickly. They do not die quickly. Their metabolisms are slow, tuned to the slow processes of the young world beneath them. A resident may live for centuries — the oldest among them, the ones who remember the first cooling of the stone, are not revered so much as *consulted*, brought to the growing chambers to press their hands against newly formed walls and read what the geology is telling them about the plateau's future.

They do not fear you, exactly. But they do not need you. Their technology is so seamlessly integrated into the world that your presence on the plateau is the equivalent of a single organism arriving on a mountain that has been alive for millennia. They observe you with the same attentive patience with which they observe everything — the fog banks, the fauna, the slow adjustments of the ridges. They will come to you when they are ready, not before. And when they do, they will want to know one thing: whether you have read your own house. Whether you understand the material you live within. Whether you have taken the time to learn the language of the stone beneath your feet, the air around your body, the water you drink.

Because to them, these are not resources. They are not environment. They are the text of a book they have been reading for centuries, and they will not stop reading it simply because someone new has arrived on the page.
