## Who Lives in the Woven Height

---

**I. The Secretors — whom no one names because no one has ever needed to**

They are the reason the place exists. Not the reason it began — no one remembers the reason it began, and the oldest chambers, the ones buried so deep that the air there is thick as broth and tastes of something ancestral, hold no record of a first builder. But they are the reason it continues.

Imagine — if the word still does its work — a body the length of your forearm, pale in the way deep water is pale, translucent at the edges so that you can see the slow movement inside: material being processed, broken down, reconstituted. They have no face you would recognize as a face. A mouth, yes, but a mouth that is more of an event — an opening that appears when the body decides to secrete, a pore that dilates and releases a substance the color of wet bone. That substance hardens. It always hardens. It begins as something between a thread and a secretion, soft as mucilage when it first touches air, and then it stiffens over hours, over days, binding itself to whatever it touches — the walls, the ceiling, the bodies of others who have secreted in the same place before them. They build in layers. They build in shifts that last for centuries. A single secretion-thread, once deposited, may be felt by a hundred thousand descendants before it is buried under a hundred thousand more.

They do not know they are building. This is not a metaphor for humility. It is a statement about the interior life. The question of whether they are aware of the scale of what they do is a question that belongs to a different kind of mind — a mind that measures things, that distinguishes between small and large, between the local and the cumulative. They lack that faculty. What they have instead is a sensation that scholars of the place — and there are none, because there is no one here to study the place, only those who live inside it and therefore cannot see it — might describe as *rightness*. They feel, in the nerve-bundles that pass for their cognition, that the place where they secrete is the place they should be secreting. They feel that the material they leave is needed. They feel this the way you feel that the next breath is needed. Not as knowledge. As condition.

Their bodies are warm. They are always warm. They press themselves against the growing wall and they stay there, sometimes for years, sometimes for the whole length of what would be called a life if the concept of life-as-a-contained-thing made sense here. But it doesn't. Here, a life is not contained. It leaks into the walls. It is absorbed. The you-that-was-you becomes texture. Becomes corridor. Becomes the thing your daughter walks through on her way to the place where she, too, will press herself against the wall and disappear.

---

**II. The Columns — the armored ones**

You feel them before you see them, which is to say you feel them before you see anything, because the vibration arrives through the floor, through the base of your skull, through the dense bone of your inner ear. A cadence. Not marching — nothing so organized — but a rhythm of shared momentum, a unanimity of direction that requires no signal, no command, only the inherited knowledge of where to go and what to carry.

They move in files three bodies wide. They are plated — iridescent, yes, the way certain beetles are iridescent, but here the iridescence is not decorative, it is functional: the overlapping plates flex against one another and produce the low harmonic that, combined with ten thousand other sources, composes the texture of sound that saturates the place. They cannot stop producing it. Their plates are always moving. When they are still, which is almost never, the silence from their bodies is the closest the Woven Height comes to quiet, and even then the quiet is relative, is full of all the other quiets, the other vibrations, the other textures of living presence.

They carry things. Not with forelimbs, not with anything you would call hands. They carry with their bodies — food boluses larger than their own heads, larvae wrapped in secreted silk, fragments of wall that need to be relocated from a place of surplus to a place of need. The things they carry are not objects to them. They are extensions of the body's purpose in the moment of carrying. A beetle of this kind, bearing a morsel of fungal matter the size of a fist, does not experience the morsel as separate from the task of walking, and the task of walking is not separate from the sensation of being in the correct place in the column, and the column is not separate from the corridor, and the corridor is not separate from the self. This is not mysticism. It is just what it is like to be a body whose cognition is distributed between its own physiology and the architecture it moves through.

They have no eyes that see light. They perceive through vibration, through chemical gradients in the air, through the minute textural differences between surfaces they press their legs against. A corridor is not a visual space to them. It is a taste, a smell, a haptic memory encoded in the chitin of their tarsi. They know where they are the way you know where your hand is without looking at it.

Their lives are short — weeks, perhaps, in the scale of the place. But the columns do not end when the individuals end. The column is older than any of its members. It has been walking since before the chamber it passes through finished expanding. It will be walking after the last member of the current generation has been absorbed into the walls. It is not a metaphor to say the column is immortal. It is a literal description. The column is the place's circulatory system. It does not die because it is not, in the strictest sense, alive. It is motion that has achieved persistence.

---

**III. The Murmuration**

In the upper chambers — upper being a relative term, meaning the ones closest to the outer surface where the dim blue-green threads of actual sky filter down through the colonized surface and the air is slightly less thick — there are flyers.

They are not birds. They are not bats. They are not anything whose name would give you the comfort of recognition. They have wings, yes, but wings that are less like wings and more like veils, membranes stretched between elongated finger-like bones that are themselves part of the wall when the creature is at rest. In flight they are chaos made beautiful by the fact that chaos, at sufficient scale and sufficient uniformity, looks like order. They move in bodies of ten thousand, fifty thousand, more. They fill a chamber the size of a cathedral and the sound of their wings is the loudest single voice in the roar.

What they are doing is not flight in the way you understand flight. It is not travel from one place to another. It is a kind of breathing. They rise and fall in thermal columns generated by the metabolic heat of the deep core. They ride the updraft of warm air seeping upward through the walls and they spread out and they fold together and they shed — constantly shedding — down and chaff and the translucent husks of their previous moltings, and all of this falling material joins the substrate below, becomes soil, becomes wall, becomes the next generation's foundation. They are not flying. They are metabolizing the air. They are converting warmth into material.

In the midst of the murmuration, there is no individual. There is only the murmuration. A single creature pulled from the flock would be a shape without a name, a body with no verb. It exists fully only in the collective motion. When one turns, the turn passes through the flock like a wave through water, and the wave is not communication — it is coupling, physical, the way one pendulum affects another through shared air displacement. They feel the wingbeat of their nearest thousand neighbors and that feeling is thought.

Sometimes, in the deepest quiet the place allows, you can hear them not as a roar but as a whisper, the distant susurration of ten thousand membranes moving through air that is too warm and too still. It sounds like something being remembered.

---

**IV. The Deep Ones**

Far below, in the chambers where the oldest construction exists — where the architecture of species long extinct has been encased, absorbed, built over, and is now held in place by a million generations of successors — there are presences.

You cannot see them. You hear them. The sound is different down there. It is not the overlapping cacophony of the surface corridors but a slow, subsonic groaning, a vibration so low it is less a sound than a pressure on the organs. It comes from everywhere and nowhere. It comes from the walls themselves.

The Deep Ones are sessile. Rooted. They were born as mobile creatures — small, pale, seeking — and they found the place that was right and they attached themselves to it and they have not moved since and they will not move until they die, which may be in a thousand years, which may be in ten thousand, because time down here is not time. Time down here is geology.

They grow into the walls. Their bodies fuse with the secreted matrix of their ancestors until the boundary between organism and architecture simply ceases to have meaning. A Deep One is a chamber. A chamber is a Deep One. Its organs are distributed through the wall — respiratory filaments threading through the matrix like mycelium through soil, neural clusters embedded in calcified layers that pulse with electrochemical signals so slow that a single thought might take a season to complete. What does a Deep One think about? Not in the way you think about things, with subjects and objects and the small stage of attention where the self performs. A Deep One thinks the way a mountain thinks, which is to say it registers. It holds. It processes the chemical information carried by the water that seeps through its body, the vibrations of the columns passing through its walls, the slow temperature shifts of the air. It is a sensor. It is a node. It is a keeper of the deep memory of the place — not memory as narrative, but memory as structure, encoded in the geometry of the walls themselves, in the specific arrangement of materials that only a Deep One can read.

They exude warmth. The deepest chambers are the warmest, and the warmth comes from them — from their respiration, from the slow decomposition of their own tissues at the margins, from the fermentation of materials sealed within their bodies over centuries. They are the place's furnace and its conscience both, though conscience is too generous a word for what is closer to a gravitational field.

---

**V. The Quick — the ones who rush**

Against the vast, patient, geological metabolism of the place, there is one current of urgency. The small ones. The ones whose lives last days.

They are insects in the loose sense — small, many-legged, armored in chitin thin as paper, and they move through the narrow passages at a pace that would be reckless if the place had any concept of recklessness. They do not hesitate. They do not pause. They carry fragments of material too small for the larger builders, spores, nutrient pellets, the shed skins of larvae that need to be relocated to specific chambers where the temperature and humidity are calibrated to a precision only they can sense.

Their lives are a single errand. They are born in one chamber, they run, they deliver, they die. Some of them never see the builders they serve. Some of them navigate passages so narrow that their bodies compress slightly as they pass through, their exoskeletons flexing, their legs working in a blur. They are the closest the place comes to adrenaline, to ambition, to the desperate scrabble of a life that knows it is short. And yet they are not frantic. They are fast, but their speed is smooth, purposeful, as if the urgency has been refined over millennia into pure motion — the minimum energy required to complete the task before the body fails.

When one of them dies — and they die constantly, their small corpses joining the substrate, their chitin softening, becoming paste, becoming wall — the place absorbs it without noticing. A single death in the Woven Height is not an event. It is a molecule reorganized. But the current persists. There is always another one running through the narrow passage, always another set of legs ticking against the floor, always another fragment arriving at the place where it is needed.

---

**VI. The Green — the ones who hold**

And threaded through all of it, through every wall and corridor and chamber, the green.

Mosses first — thin films of them, barely visible, dark emerald against the pale architecture, holding moisture against the surface with a tenacity that borders on chemical intimacy. Lichens after them, crusting the oldest surfaces, their symbiosis of fungus and alga a mirror of the larger symbiosis of the place itself — two organisms so intertwined that neither exists without the other, producing together a substance neither could produce alone.

Then the ferns, in the places where light is sufficient — the outer corridors, the vaults near the surface, the ledges where condensation pools. Their fronds uncurl slowly, over weeks, and when they die they do not fall. They remain, stiffening, becoming structural, their cellulose fibers merging with the secreted matrix until a fern stem and a wall segment are the same piece of matter, distinguishable only by the green of the living tissue still visible at the core.

The vines are the most aggressive. They thread through the construction like sutures, their tendrils seeking cracks, gaps, weaknesses, and when they find them they fill them, thickening, hardening, their woody tissue calcifying until the vine is a beam and the beam is a vine and there is no answer to the question of which built which. There are archways that exist because a plant grew in a curve and the builders followed, filling in the space beneath with their matrix. There are buttresses that are half wood, half chitin, half fiber, half resin, in ratios that shift over the centuries as one tissue slowly replaces another.

The plants do not compete with the architecture. They improve it. They add tensile strength where the builders' material is strong in compression. They add water transport where the builders' material absorbs and holds. They add flexibility where the builders' material is brittle. They are the place's immune system, its repair mechanism, its slow-motion response to stress and fracture. And in return, the place gives them everything — structure, moisture, the residual warmth of ten thousand metabolisms, and a substrate so rich in nutrients that they grow at rates that would be extraordinary in any other environment.

Together, animal and vegetable, secretion and growth, chitin and cellulose, the makers and the holders, they compose a material that has no name in any language that names things separately. It is not wood. It is not chitin. It is not stone. It is something that only this place has ever produced, in this slow, patient, ceaseless accumulation, and it is the only substance in the world that is simultaneously alive, dead, and architectural — that is to say, it is the only substance in the world that is honest about what everything is made of.

---

**VII. What it means to live here**

Nothing here lives *in* the place the way a creature lives in a habitat. Everything here lives *as* the place. The beetle is the corridor it walks through. The fern is the arch it clings to. The Deep One is the chamber it has become. The murmuration is the weather it generates. The quick ones are the blood that moves through the body's smallest veins.

There is no outside to look at this place from and understand it. There is only inside, and inside is not a location but a condition — the condition of being made of the same material as everything else, of being part of the same slow, continuous act of construction that has been ongoing since before the memory of any creature here, and will continue long after the last of them has been absorbed into the walls and become texture and become foundation and become the raw material of whatever unimaginable thing the next ten thousand years will build on top.

The sound never stops. The warmth never fades. The walls breathe. The whole mass pulses with a metabolism older than language, older than species, older than the concept of a self separate from a world. And in the warm dark, in the high basin where no sky is visible because the sky has been replaced by ten thousand overlapping lives layered on top of one another for longer than anyone can count, the building continues. It has always continued. It will always continue.

This is what lives here. This is what has always lived here.

Not creatures. Not organisms. Not a habitat.

A single, continuous, breathing act of construction — alive, and aware of itself, in a way that has no need for words.
