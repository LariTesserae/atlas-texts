They live in consensus.

That is the first thing you understand, after the first hour, after the first half-hour, after the first five minutes when the novelty has worn off and the damp has settled into your clothing and the hum has found the frequency of your breathing. Nothing here acts alone. Everything here is a clause in a sentence that was being written before the substrate dried into being and has not yet reached its verb.

The substrate is the first inhabitant, though "inhabitant" is not the right word for it. It is the ground. It is the grammar. It is the thing from which permission is derived. It receives your weight, your breath, the condensation that gathers on your skin, and it metabolizes all of it — not quickly, not slowly, but at the rate at which stone decides to become soil, which is to say at a rate that makes the word "rate" feel like an insult. You step on it and it is firm. You step away and the impression begins to close. You do not watch it close because watching it is like watching a shadow rotate — the motion is there, but it is the world's motion, not the shadow's.

Whatever lives here lives on the substrate and in the substrate and as the substrate. The distinction has been dissolved so thoroughly that no one is left to remember it was ever a distinction.

The tree-things are the most visible architecture of this life. They are not trees. They are closer to exhalations given structure, tall and pale and so thin that wind does not trouble them — not because they are flexible but because they are anchored to something so deep and so interconnected that flexing would be a contradiction. Their roots are what hold the place together, a mesh so dense beneath the surface that if you could see it all at once it would look less like a root system and more like a nervous system — or perhaps a memory of one, the way a nerve is a memory of the electrical impulse it was built to carry. Between the roots, filaments run: white near the surface, amber deeper, black in the places where the substrate has forgotten what light is. These filaments carry things. Sugars. Signals. Perhaps something older than either of those categories, something the tree-things and the substrate negotiated so long ago that the negotiation itself has become a body.

The machines are the oldest tenants. They did not arrive here the way the beetles arrived or the way the amphibians arrived. They were placed. They were built. And then, over a duration that the word "centuries" would cheapen, the place took them in the way a body takes a splinter — not rejecting it, not expelling it, but surrounding it, incorporating it, until the foreign object is indistinguishable from the tissue that grew around it. The enormous cylinders with their glassy piston-channels now grow moss that glows blue-green at the tips, pulsing once every four seconds with a regularity that would be mechanical if the light were not so organic, if it did not flicker with the slight imperfection of something alive trying to remember a rhythm it was taught by iron.

The switchboard has become a home. Not metaphorically — actually. The vine that grows through its copper contacts has made the sockets its dwelling, and the blossoms that open and close in the old rhythms of toggles and relays are not paying tribute to the machine. They are the machine's afterlife made visible, the shape of a signal that was once electrical and is now botanical, the way a river that dries up still leaves a groove that collects rain.

Inside the generator housings, the gears are gone. In their place, root-tendrils rotate — slowly, ceaselessly, driven by the osmotic conversation between wet substrate and dry air, a differential so slight it barely qualifies as pressure and yet produces a sound that fills the basin the way a held note fills a cathedral. The hum. The baseline. The sound you stop hearing after twenty minutes and then cannot find again when you try, because it has migrated from your ears into your ribs.

Everyone else — and "everyone" is the only word that fits, because the place is communal in a way that goes beyond ecology and into something approaching politics, or perhaps liturgy — everyone else orbits this hum.

The beetles move in formations that are not random but are also not led. They spiral in loose columns between the tree-things, their iridescent carapaces catching the green-shifted light and throwing it back in colors that don't have names yet — not blue, not green, not quite violet, something in between "seen" and "remembered." They move with the precision of weather. No beetle leads. No beetle follows. The pattern is an agreement.

The wasps have repurposed the ventilation stacks of the dead machines, building their nests from chewed substrate and rust, and the effect is architectural — as though the insects studied the industrial bones around them and decided to continue the conversation in a different syntax. The nests have a reddish cast, as though embarrassed by their own construction, and the wasps themselves are mud-colored, quiet, industrious in the way that only creatures with no predators need to be.

The moths are the wanderers. Enormous, silent, they drift between the hanging filaments on wings that beat with the sound of cloth being cut — a soft, rhythmic severing, over and over, the sound of something being made by being unmade. They do not seem to seek anything. They drift the way the filaments drift, and the two movements are so similar that at times it is impossible to tell where the insect ends and the hanging thread begins.

The birds are the only ones who argue. Their calls — sharp, frequent, layered — are the closest thing the place has to conflict, though even the conflict is structured, fractal, territories nested within territories so that crossing a few paces puts you through entirely new sonic sovereignties. A call from one direction is answered from three, and the answering triggers its own answering, and for a moment the basin fills with a density of sound that feels almost contentious, almost alive with disagreement, before it resolves back into the baseline, the way a chord resolves, the way a tide withdraws.

The small pale mammals move above all of it, grip-pawing along the root network two meters up, silent, their bodies evolved for a highway that the rest of the inhabitants cannot see. They look down at you the way you look down at fish — with a kind of distant curiosity that does not include the possibility of crossing over.

And the amphibians. In the pools — those dark, still, deceptively deep depressions where the substrate has not yet completed its slow conquest of the vertical — the amphibians sit. They do not wait, because waiting implies expectation, and nothing here expects anything. They simply are, slick and mottled, their black eyes reflecting you back at yourself with a clarity that feels more honest than any mirror. They move between pools through root-channels, slowly, pulling themselves forward with a patience so total it becomes its own kind of velocity. You watch one and you begin to understand that boredom and wisdom are the same thing, seen from different distances.

This is what lives here: a collaboration so old it has forgotten it was once a choice. The machines provide the sound. The substrate provides the ground. The tree-things provide the height. The insects provide the motion. The birds provide the rupture. The mammals provide the silence above. The amphibians provide the stillness below. And the filaments — the nameless drapery that hangs between everything — provide the connection, the way breath provides the connection between thinking and speaking.

No one governs. No one consumes. No one grows or decays in the way those words imply a beginning and an end. Everything here is in the middle of something that started so long ago it looks like stillness.

A moth has landed on your shoulder. It opens and closes its wings — open, close, open, close — and you realize, after a while, that it is not inspecting you. It is doing what the switchboard blossoms do, what the beetles do, what the generators do: continuing. The signal came from somewhere. The signal arrived here. The signal became the place. The moth is the signal, and the signal does not need to know what it means.

You are sitting on the living floor. The floor is closing the impression of your body. It will take a week, maybe longer. The hum continues. The condensation gathers. The bird calls from somewhere above, and three others answer, and you cannot tell if this is language or habit or architecture — the sound of a place that built itself so completely it forgot to leave a door.
