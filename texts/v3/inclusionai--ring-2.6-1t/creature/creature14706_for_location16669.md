## What Lives on the Canopy Plateau

---

The first thing to understand is that the question of who lives here is not quite the right question. The plateau does not host life in the way a forest hosts birds or a pond hosts frogs. It *is* life, in a manner that has outgrown the distinction between organism and environment, and what lives here lives the way a liver cell lives inside a liver — embedded, functional, inseparable from the tissue around it.

But you are asking, so we will begin from the outside and move in.

---

### The Mosses

They are the first living thing you notice, though you will not think of them as living. They fill every gap, every crack in concrete and corroded steel, every concavity in bark and iron where water pools and lingers. They are not the delicate, decorative mosses of temperate forests. These are dense, structural, almost textile — mats an inch thick, sometimes two, with a surface so even it looks extruded. They glow faintly in the diffused light, not green but a shifting grey-green that makes them look like condensation given form.

They are not passive. Watch a mossy patch for long enough and you will see it change — not grow, exactly, but *settle*, the way a mattress settles under a shifting weight. The moss responds to moisture gradients, to vibration, to the thermal signature of a nearby body. It does not flee. It does exactly the opposite: it grows toward the disturbance, thickening in the direction of a footstep, a leaning hand, a dropped tool, filling the space your body leaves in the air with new tissue so slowly that you cannot watch it happen and yet, returning hours later, you can measure the difference with your fingers.

The mosses are the plateau's outermost membrane. They condition the air, hold the moisture, and create the silence. Their density absorbs sound not as foam absorbs sound but as a throat absorbs sound — actively, with muscular tissue that has evolved (or adapted, or been shaped) to deaden vibration as a byproduct of its own metabolism. The silence of the plateau is not an absence. It is a production of the mosses.

Dozens of species, perhaps hundreds, though taxonomy here is almost useless. Some are so specialized they grow only on copper surfaces. Others only on rust. Some form visible fruiting structures — tiny pale stalks no taller than a pinhead that release spores so rarely and so quietly that you may never see one discharge, only the empty, collapsed husk that remains.

---

### The Fungal Layer

Beneath the moss — literally beneath it, in the substrate, in the space between root and rust where condensation pools — there is a fungal stratum that may be the trer brain of the plateau, if it is permissible to call it a brain.

You see it in cross-section when the light angles in: a white-to-cream web of hyphae so dense it looks like spun cotton, but tougher, leathery, with a tensile quality that resists pulling. It binds the roots to the metal, the metal to the concrete, the concrete to the soil that has accumulated in its cracks over decades or centuries. It is the connective tissue, the mycelial grout that holds the entire architecture together. Without it, the plateau would collapse into a heap of rotting iron and strangling vine.

It metabolizes things that should not be metabolizable. The mystery of the warm roots — the ones you find when excavating by hand, roots that are metabolically active in conditions that should produce only cold, sluggish decay — begins here. The fungal network operates a chemosynthetic metabolism of extraordinary antiquity. It feeds on iron oxide. On calcium leached from concrete. On the slow oxidation of metals. It converts rust into energy the way surface plants convert sunlight, and it has been doing so so long that the process is no longer extraordinary to it. It is simply what it does. The warmth of those roots is not residual. It is the warmth of a metabolism that found fuel in the ruins and never stopped burning.

The fungal mats produce fruiting bodies rarely, and when they do, they emerge overnight — pale, shelf-like structures that grow along the undersides of root-beams, sometimes a meter across, with a surface that looks like hammered pewter. They are not poisonous, presumably. They taste like copper and chalk, and they dissolve in your hand within hours of being picked, leaving only a rust-colored stain on your palm that takes days to wash out.

---

### The Root-Tendrils

These are not roots in any conventional sense, though they emerge from root systems and share their tissue. They are exploratory structures — long, pale, wire-thin extensions that reach out from the main vegetal mass into open space, into air, into gaps between pipes and beams where there is nothing to feed on and nothing to anchor to.

They move. Not quickly. A tendril may take days to extend a full arm's length, and weeks to curl around an object. But they move with intention, or at least with a responsiveness that is functionally indistinguishable from intention. Place a metal rod in their vicinity and, over the course of days, a tendril will find it, touch it, test it, and begin to wrap. The wrapping is not random. The tendril palpates the surface of the rod — slowly, with a pressure you can feel if you hold the rod still — and it seems to be reading the object. Assessing. Deciding.

Some tendrils carry moisture. These are the ones that follow the old pipe network most faithfully, tracking the ghost-plumbing of the plateau with a fidelity that suggests they are not merely following the pipes but have incorporated the logic of the plumbing into their own growth patterns. A tendril carrying water will seek out a crack in a pipe and insert itself, merging with the metal, and the water that was flowing through the pipe now flows through the tendril, and the tendril grows thicker.

Other tendrils are sensory. They are thinner, darker, and they do not carry fluid. They wave in the still air — not in response to wind (there is effectively no wind here) but in response to pressure differentials so minute they are below the threshold of human perception. Temperature gradients. Humidity shifts. The displacement of air by a moving body. The tendrils feel you coming before you arrive.

It is not known whether the tendrils are autonomous or extensions of a larger decision-making network. The fashionable term, among the few researchers who have studied the plateau, is *distributed somatic intelligence* — a system in which no single part thinks but the whole body (and it is, arguably, a single body) behaves as though it does. The tendrils are its fingers, its tongue, its peripheral nerves. They are the part of the plateau that is most obviously *doing something*, and yet they may be the least important part.

### The Canal-Lifters

You will not see these unless you look directly up.

In the upper canopy — the dense lattice of branches, aerial roots, and calcified support beams that forms the ceiling of the plateau's interior — there are channels. Vertical channels, mostly, the diameter of a human wrist, that run through fused wood and hardened root-tissue and old electrical conduit. These are the canals, and through them the plateau moves its water.

But the canals are not inert. The walls of the canals are muscular — not metaphorically, but structurally. They are composed of layered root-fiber tissue that can contract and relax, and they do. Slowly. With a rhythm that is not the rhythm of any heartbeat you have ever heard, because it is not a single rhythm. Different canals contract at different rates, in different sequences, and the effect is a kind of slow peristalsis that moves water upward through the system — against gravity, against every expectation, in defiance of the plumbing logic that the original engineers installed.

The canal-lifters are the reason water flows uphill on the plateau. They are not creatures so much as tissue behaving with purpose, the way the lining of your intestine behaves with purpose, without thought, without a brain, but with a specificity and reliability that no amount of uncoordinated growth could produce. If the plateau is a body, the canal-lifters are its esophagus. They move resources from the roots to the canopy, where the canopy breathes, and what it breathes out is moisture, and what that moisture becomes is rain, and what the rain feeds are the roots, and the cycle continues with a completeness that is almost mechanical.

Almost. The canal-lifters adapt. When a channel is damaged — by a falling beam, by your weight on a root-bridge, by anything — adjacent channels dilate to compensate, and within days new tissue has grown to repair the damage, and within weeks the channel is functional again, though its route may have changed, and the water now flows along a slightly different path, and the sound of the pipe you leaned against rings at a slightly different pitch.

### The Visitors

The insect-like thing you encountered — the one with the too-long proboscis and the folding wings — is one of perhaps no more than a dozen mobile species on the entire plateau. It has no common name. It was seen once by a researcher who documented it and then, returning three weeks later to the same root-tendril, found no trace of the creature or anything like it.

It feeds on root-surface secretions — the metabolic byproducts of the fungal-plant network that bead on root surfaces like dew. The dimpling you observed when it pressed its proboscis to the root was not damage. It was the root responding — a localized tissue contraction that increased secretion, the way a nipple responds to stimulation. The root was feeding the insect as deliberately as a parent bird feeds a chick.

Whether the insect is necessary to the plateau — whether it pollinates, whether it distributes, whether its proboscis stimulates secretions that the root needs stimulated — is unknown. It may be a parasite. It may be mutualistic. It may be the plateau's immune response, a creature generated from within the vegetal mass to perform a task the mass cannot perform for itself, and then reabsorbed when its work is done.

Look at the canopy sometimes, very still, very patient, and you may see others. A shape that is not quite a bird — something lower, slower, moving through the branches with the fluidity of something that has never known open sky. A vibration in the root-network that resolves, if you press your palm to the ground and concentrate, into something rhythmic and deliberate and not your own. A warmth, in a place that should be cold, that moves.

---

### The Plateau Itself

In the end, there is only one organism on the Canopy Plateau. It has many parts, many tissues, many metabolisms nested inside each other like Russian dolls made of rust and chlorophyll. The mosses are its skin. The fungi are its connective tissue. The root-tendrils are its sensory apparatus. The canal-lifters are its circulatory system. The insects and the shapes in the canopy are its — what? Offspring? Projections? Dreams?

It is not dying. It is not thriving. It is persisting — in the way a river persists, not because it wants to but because the shape of the land cannot stop it, and it has been flowing so long that the land has been shaped around it, and now they are the same thing, the river and the land, the plateau and the life that has colonized it until the distinction no longer holds.

You are standing on it. You are standing in it. The condensation on your jacket is its breath. The warmth under your hand on the root is its pulse. The silence is its will.

It does not need you here. It did not notice you arrive. And if you stay long enough, you will begin to believe that it will not notice when you leave — that you have already been absorbed, that the cold on your skin has already become part of its system, that the memory of the world outside is thinning, like light through canopy, growing more diffuse, less directional, until it is nothing but illumination without source, everywhere and nowhere, the way light behaves in a place that has forgotten what it means to cast a shadow.

The roots continue their work. The pipes ring faintly. The plateau breathes.
