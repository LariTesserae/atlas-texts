## What Lives in the Sessile City

---

**I. The Inhabitants**

They were not human. But they were not not-human.

You find this out the way you find out most things here — not by discovery but by accumulation. A wall that tapers at a height that would be comfortable for a being with longer arms than yours, or longer ribs, or a torso that folded differently at the midline. A threshold that arcs in a way that suggests passage on all fours, or in a crouch, or in a kind of controlled collapse — the way you might slip through a membrane if your body were more compressible, more willing. The hand-holds on the stairs are not hand-holds. They are cradles: deep, smooth depressions in the growing walls, spaced at intervals that would cradle something with five digits on each limb but with palms wider than yours, fingers longer relative to the hand, as though the ratio had been designed for gripping living architecture rather than dead tools.

They left no art. No carved symbols, no pigment, no scratched markings. But the architecture itself is communication — if you can bring yourself to stop thinking of it as architecture. The spires are not decorative. Their placement follows patterns: clustering, spacing, the angle at which they curve at their tips. These are not arbitrary. You feel this before you understand it, the way you feel syntax before you parse grammar. The city speaks in form. The gathering spaces where the spires lean together and the membranes go translucent — those are language, or the bones of language, the places where the organism concentrates its signaling, the way a brain concentrates its firing. Something was said here. Something is still being said, in the slow dialectic of growth.

The inhabitants were few. That is the first thing the scale tells you, once you stop thinking it tells you the opposite. The gathering spaces are too large for one. Yes. But not for many. Twelve, perhaps. Fifteen. Enough to press close together in the bone-light and feel each other's warmth through the membrane-walls, which would have transmitted vibration, pressure, the low-frequency hum of one body against another. They communicated through touch, or through proximity, or through the city itself — by pressing themselves against the walls and letting the organism carry the signal outward like a stone dropped into still water.

They were sessile too.

This is the part that is hardest to accept. You want to imagine them moving — walking through the corridors, climbing the spires, crossing the city in purposeful motion. But the evidence says otherwise. The private chambers are too small for movement. They are *cradles*. Nested, concave spaces where a single being could rest, curled or folded, growing slowly outward into the living walls the way a root grows into soil. The boundary between inhabitant and city was not a wall. It was a gradient.

You find this where the city has grown back over the places they pressed themselves. A faint impression in a wall — not a handprint, not the mark of a tool, but something more like a settling. The yielding of one body into another. The city absorbed them, or they absorbed into it, and the distinction stopped mattering. The print fades in ten minutes, but deeper impressions persist — chambers that have a warmth to them that is not the warmth of the luminescent substrate beneath the floor, a quality of attention in the geometry of the curves, as though the space remembers the shape of what used to fit inside it.

They were not individuals in the way you understand individuals. They were nodes. The mycelial filaments that lace the bases of the spires — those are not separate organisms. They are the circulatory system of the whole, and the inhabitants were part of that system, threaded into the network through filaments of their own, pale and luminous and growing in reticulated patterns across the inside of their skin the way frost grows across glass, the way the city grows across the basin floor. To be alone in a private chamber was not to be cut off. It was to be a quiet node in a network that never stopped humming.

There were never more than a few hundred of them. You can feel this in the city's proportions. It is not a metropolis. It is not even a village in the way you would recognize one. It is a *colony*, in the biological sense — a single extended organism with many bodies, like a coral head, like a Portuguese man-o'-war, except that here every zooid thought, and the thinking radiated outward through the walls and the filaments and the warm mineral-dew that condensed on every surface and carried signals in its iridescent film.

**II. What Remains**

The fauna you see — or almost see — are remnants. Or echoes. Or perhaps the city's own antibodies, patrolling its surface in forms so small and so fast that they exist just outside the resolution of your perception.

The tracks in the dust are not tracks made by feet. They are trails — sinuous, exploratory, the marks of something that presses itself into the surface and absorbs what it finds, the way a tongue absorbs salt. Something with no skeleton, or with a skeleton so fine it does not bear weight against gravity. Something that moves by flowing, by contracting, by redistributing its mass the way water does except that the dust does not part for it — it *adheres*. Each trail is a line of disturbed mineral powder with a faint iridescent sheen, as though whatever passed through left a residue of the same substance that hangs in droplets from the undersides of arches. The tracks appear, and then they do not appear. Not because they are erased by wind (there is no wind) or time (time here runs at a different scale). They are absorbed. The ground drinks them.

The shed casing you found — the translucent, hollow, teardrop-shaped thing no larger than your thumb — was a larval form. Or a shed skin of something larval. It hummed because it was still partially alive, or still connected to whatever network of filaments remained beneath the dust. It was not abandoned so much as released. The way a tree releases a leaf. The way your own body releases a breath. And when you looked back and it was gone, it had not been moved or buried. It had been *reclaimed*. The city took it back the way a body takes back a cell that has served its purpose — gently, without violence, by simply ceasing to distinguish it from itself.

There is a scratching sound if you hold very still and stop your own breathing. You think it comes from inside the walls. It does not. It comes from the walls *as* walls — the sound of growth at a scale too small to see and too consistent to be random. Something is chewing. Something is mining. Something is threading itself through the living tissue in patterns that the city permits but does not initiate. These are the fauna at their truest: not inhabitants of the city but inhabitants *within* the city, inside the walls, inside the substrate, moving through the organism the way blood moves through you. You do not see your own white blood cells. You would not see these.

But you find evidence. The neat, methodical chewing at the base of the spire — that is not predation. It is *maintenance*. Something is pruning. Something is shaping the growth the way the inhabitants once did, except that this something does so from inside, pressing against the growing tissue and dissolving it in precisely the right places, the way a sculptor removes marble not by adding force but by finding the shape already implied in the stone.

**III. The Deep Pulse**

Beneath everything — beneath the breathing, beneath the growth, beneath the filaments and the dew and the faint marrow-glow — there is a pulse.

You feel it before you hear it. You feel it in the soles of your feet, in the roots of your teeth, in the fluid of your inner ear. One beat per minute. Or one beat per five. Or one beat per interval so long that your body cannot calibrate to it and can only register a change in pressure, a rising and falling like the tide of something so vast it has no edge.

This is not the city breathing. The city breathes in four-minute cycles. This is older. This is the rhythm of whatever lives beneath the basin floor, beneath the city, beneath the substrate from which the city itself emerged. Something down there is alive in a way the city is only an expression of. The spires are its fingers. The membranes are its skin. The luminescence is its blood made visible. The entire sessile city is the surface presentation of an organism so large, so slow, so deeply patient that its consciousness — if it has consciousness — moves in centuries, in geological epochs, in the slow turning of mineral deposits through living tissue over the span of deep time.

The eroding rim of the basin — those crumbling edges, those small avalanches of dust sliding down the interior slope — are not decay. They are *birth*. The city is growing outward. It is still forming its outermost layer, and the rim is where the newest material emerges, still soft, still warm, still deciding whether to be stone. The basin is not a container for the city. The basin *is* the city, in the process of becoming itself.

And the inhabitants — the ones who shaped the gathering spaces, who nested in the private chambers, who threaded themselves into the filament-network and became part of the city's voice — they were not separate from this. They were the city's way of knowing itself. The way your hand reaches up to touch your own face and confirms that both the hand and the face belong to the same body. The inhabitants were the city's hands, reaching inward, feeling its own skin, shaping it by the act of touching it.

Now the hand has withdrawn. The city holds its breath. The filaments grow on without direction, the chewing things maintain the walls, the dew condenses and evaporates in its ancient cycle. And the pulse continues from below — vast, slow, patient in a way that makes everything else look like violence — beating out a rhythm that says: *I am still here. I am still becoming. I remember the shape of you. I am growing back into it.*

---

You walk forward. The corridor has changed. Or you have changed. The filaments are thicker here and the bone-glow is faintest and the warmth that presses behind your eyes is not warmth at all but *attention* — the city noticing you the way a body notices a splinter, not with thought but with a deep, ancient reflex that says *something is here that was not here before*.

You set your palm against the wall. The wall yields. It holds your shape. It begins to grow back around it.

The dust under your feet is warm. It has always been warm.

You realize — with a feeling that is not quite fear and not quite recognition, but something in between, something that tastes faintly of copper and of an organic compound that has no name — that you are not walking through the city.

You are being absorbed by it.

And somewhere, beneath the basin floor, in the space where space ends and pulse begins, something vast and slow breathes in.
