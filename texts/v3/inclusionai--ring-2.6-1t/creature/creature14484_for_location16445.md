## What Lives on the Drowned Plateau

---

They live in the margins. That is the first thing to understand — not the margins of the land, but the margins of everything. The margins of water and stone. The margins of breath and submersion. The margins between what is alive and what is merely persistent.

The people of the plateau are, at first glance, unremarkable in their workaday motions. A woman kneels at the edge of a channel, her forearms slick to the elbow, drawing water in a clay vessel the color of wet charcoal. A man shapes a piece of basalt with a harder stone, chipping flakes in a rhythm so practiced it seems involuntary, like breathing. Children move through ankle-deep water in a loose procession, trailing their fingers along the stalks of the tall growths, which lean toward them like animals expecting to be fed.

But look longer and the details assert themselves — the things that are shaped by the place and, in turn, shape themselves to it.

Their skin is darker than you expect. Not sun-dark — the light here never concentrates enough for that — but mineral-dark, as if the water and the stone have slowly stained them. Their hair is worn long and is almost always damp. They move with a deliberateness that visitors sometimes mistake for slowness, but it is not slow. It is the pace of people who have learned that the water is always negotiating, that a channel that is ankle-deep today may be thigh-deep by evening, and that haste is not the same thing as speed. They move the way the water moves: with purpose, but on a rhythm that belongs to the place rather than to them.

Their bodies are broad-shouldered and long-limbed, not massive but dense — as if the plateau's particular gravity has, over generations, settled into them. Their hands are large, their fingers long and sure. They wade, they swim, they climb the basalt ridges with the ease of creatures that evolved in exactly this environment, which — depending on how far back you trace their stories — may be closer to the truth than it sounds. Their feet are rarely dry, and the leather they wear across the soles is not for warmth but for grip — thin, tight, worn smooth in the places that contact wet stone.

They do not build fires on the water, though they cook with heat. Meals are slow immersions — roots, tubers, the pale flesh of grazers, all submerged in heated clay vessels and left for hours until everything surrenders its substance to the warm liquid, which is consumed as nourishment and as broth and, in some cases, as the medium for the fermented foods that constitute their most valued trade. To share a pot of slowly prepared food is their deepest gesture of trust. The preparation itself is communal, overseen by whoever has the knowledge of timing — and timing here is not measured in minutes but in the slow gradients of warmth that pass through a day.

There are no chiefs. There are elders, and there are speakers — people whose memories are longer, whose rhythmic repetitions of the old stories hold the community's shape together the way the carved marks on standing stones hold information for those who know how to read them. Authority is not imposed. It is recognized, the way you recognize that a particular stone in a channel has been worn smooth by centuries of water and will not move. If an elder speaks, the others listen — not out of obedience, but out of a practiced attentiveness that has survival value in a place where the landscape is always, quietly, in the process of becoming something new.

Their children are everywhere and nowhere. They appear at the edges of channels, on the broad backs of dozing grazers, in the upper branches of the squat ridge-trees, silent as herons. They learn the water's moods before they learn to speak in the conventional sense — a kind of hydrological literacy that operates through immersion and intuition. A child who falls into a channel is not rescued immediately. The others watch. If the child surfaces, they are helped. If the child sinks, the watching becomes closer, sharper — not alarmed, but attentive. The water here is warm. It does not kill quickly. There is time to learn.

They name the water's rhythms. Not with words that correspond to tides — the word for tide is a complex one, borrowed or adapted from whatever language first met an ocean, and it sits uneasily in their speech. They have instead a vocabulary of subtlety: the slow rising is called one thing; the slow falling, another. The moment when a channel begins to deepen has its own word. So does the moment when it stops. There are seventeen distinct terms for the degrees of stillness the water can exhibit, and children learn them the way children elsewhere learn colors.

---

The grazers are patient in a way that makes human patience look like impatience with itself. They stand in the shallows for hours, their broad, splayed feet planted in the algal mats, their muzzles moving slowly across the surface of the water, cropping the green-brown growth with a dexterity that their heavy bodies would not suggest. Their hides are slick and dark — almost black in the dim light, gleaming faintly where the haze condenses on their skin. Their eyes are small, high-set, and perpetually half-lidded, giving them an expression of mild, perpetual skepticism, as if they have seen the water do something implausible and have chosen not to react.

They are not unintelligent. But their intelligence is not the kind that asserts itself. It is ecological, situational, embedded in the rhythms of the place. A grazer knows when a channel is about to deepen — it moves to higher ground before the water arrives, not urgently, but with the certainty of something that has felt the ground shifting beneath its feet. They travel in loose aggregations, never so close together as to suggest herding, never so far apart as to lose contact. Their communication, if that word applies, is almost entirely through vibration — low-frequency pulses transmitted through the water and the stone, felt rather than heard.

Their young are born in the water. The calves — if that word applies — are buoyant for the first weeks of their lives, floating at the surface with an almost absurd serenity, their spindly legs splayed in every direction like a creature that has not yet learned what it is. The adults stand around them in a loose ring, not protectively — there is nothing here to protect them from, most of the time — but in a way that seems to stabilize the water around them, to create a pocket of calm in the gentle flow.

The grazers leave marks in the algal mats where they walk. These marks persist for days. And here is where the place begins to do something that the settlers notice but do not, for reasons of their own, find remarkable: the algal mats thicken in the shape of the footprints, as if the vegetation is remembering the pressure, holding the form of the passage even after the passage itself has moved on. Within a week the marks fade. Within a week the mats have produced new growth, and the memory has been metabolized.

---

The drifters are the oldest things here. Not old in the way that stone is old — they are not geological — but old in the way that deep water is old, old in the way that a very slow thought is old. Their bodies are vast, longer than the largest settlement's longest building, and they move through the deeper channels with a deliberation that makes the water itself seem impatient by comparison. A drift across a single basin takes weeks. Possibly longer. The settlers who have watched them for generations report that some individuals appear never to complete the crossing of a single depression in their lifetimes.

They are filter-feeders. Their mouths open at the surface — wide, slow, unhurried — and close again after what might be minutes or might be hours. They expel breath infrequently. When they do, the sound carries across the water and through the stone: a long, warm exhalation, cracked and resonant, like a door that has swollen in its frame and opens reluctantly. The settlers say that on the quietest nights, when the mist is thick and the air is perfectly still, the groan of a drifter is the closest thing the plateau has to a heartbeat.

Their backs accumulate life. Algae, small organisms, the rare perching bird that has crossed from some distant land and decided, for reasons of its own, to stay. The drifters do not seem to notice. Or perhaps they notice in a way that is not visible — a slight deepening of the water around them, a subtle shift in the currents, a slow chemical exhortation that discourages parasitism and encourages commensalism. They are not hosts. They are not symbionts. They are something closer to substrate — a living geography that happens to move.

No one here has ever tried to mount a drifter. The idea would not occur to them, the way the idea of climbing a river for sport would not occur to a fish. The drifters are not animals to be used. They are conditions to be lived among, the way the water is a condition, the way the warmth is a condition. The settlers build their docks at the basins where drifters are known to pass, and they anchor their rafts to temporary structures that do not damage the animals, and sometimes — on the warmest days, when the haze compresses the world into a soft grey envelope — a drifter will pass close enough to a dock that a child can reach out and touch its encrusted flank, and feel the slow, enormous pulse beneath.

---

The translucent things — and here language falters, because arthropod is a word that carries too much terrestrial expectation — the translucent things move across the surface of the water in colonies that resemble nothing so much as living glass. They are small, each no larger than a finger's width, and their bodies are so clear that you can see the internal structures shifting as they move: the delicate architecture of their limbs, the pulsing of whatever organ serves as a heart. They link together into rafts that stretch across meters of surface, reforming constantly, breaking apart and reassembling in patterns that suggest intelligence and then, upon closer inspection, suggest something simpler: a responsiveness to vibration, to warmth, to the chemical signature of their neighbors.

They are silent. They cast no shadow. On a still day, looking down at them from a dock, they seem less like animals than like a property of the water itself — the water choosing, for a few hours, to have a surface texture. The settlers do not name them individually. They refer to them collectively, with a word that translates roughly as *the skimming*, and they treat them with the mild, tolerant affection one reserves for weather.

The insects — if insects they are — emerge during the warmth-spike. They rise from the water in clouds so dense they alter the quality of the light, a faint amber haze superimposed on the eternal grey. They do not bite. They do not land on skin. They simply rise, swirl in patterns that are neither random nor geometric, and vanish when the warmth recedes. Where they go, no one has ever determined. They leave no nests, no larvae visible in the shallows — only the brief, recurrent spectacle of their ascent and disappearance, as reliable as a clock that no one winds.

---

The squat trees — and tree is, again, an approximation — anchor themselves in the crevices of the basalt ridges with root systems so wide and flat they resemble the foundations of buildings. Their trunks are swollen with stored moisture, pale and faintly translucent when cut, and they produce broad, sparse canopies that spread not upward but outward, catching every photon in the dim air. Their leaves are thick, waxy, and arranged in overlapping patterns that channel water toward the roots. They do not shed seasonally; they shed in response to the water's rhythm, losing leaves when the channels deepen and producing new ones when the water recedes.

Their wood is too waterlogged to burn easily, but it carves well and resists rot, and it is from these swollen trunks that the settlers make their boats. The carving is a communal act — days of soaking and scraping and slow shaping — and the finished canoes are considered almost family. They are named. They are maintained with a care that borders on tenderness. A canoe that has served three generations is not retired. It is repaired, its hull thickened with resin from the stalked growths, its seats replaced one plank at a time until none of the original wood remains and the boat is, by every material measure, new — and yet, indisputably, the same.

The mosses creep in the margins where water thins to film. They are dense, succulent-like, with waxy leaves that store against the periodic deepening of their world. They spread across the exposed basalt in patterns that are neither random nor uniform, and the settlers have learned that certain mosses indicate certain water depths, certain mineral concentrations, certain temperatures. Reading moss is a skill, and the people who possess it are consulted the way, elsewhere, one might consult a surveyor.

And the stalked things — the tall growths that rise from the shallows, not quite kelp, not quite reed, with broad translucent leaves that orient not toward the light but toward warmth and vibration. They lean toward thermal vents. They lean toward the heat of passing bodies. They lean toward buildings, standing at angles that suggest attentiveness. The settlers have long known that if you stand still among them long enough, they will eventually angle toward you, slowly, the way a cat might tilt its head.

No one here thinks of them as plants. Nor do they think of them as animals. They are a third thing, a category that the plateau provides and that the language of the settlers accommodates with a word that carries overtones of *neighbor*.

---

What lives here, in the end, is not separate from the place. The water is alive in a way that is not metaphorical — it responds, it remembers, it moves with intention that is not quite intention. The stone is alive in the way that cooling rock is alive, still settling, still becoming. The creatures are alive in ways that blur the categories that visitors bring with them. And the people are alive in a way that has been shaped so thoroughly by the plateau that the shape of their lives is indistinguishable from the shape of the place itself.

They do not name the strangeness. They live inside it, the way a fish lives inside water, not because they have acclimated but because there is nothing to acclimate to. The water moves things between basins. The stalks lean toward buildings. The algal mats hold the shapes of feet. The music comes from everywhere and nowhere, and it sounds like a memory, and no one asks whose memory, because the question would sound like an answer, and the answer would be something they already know and have chosen, wisely, not to say aloud.

The plateau endures. It is still becoming. And what lives upon it lives the same way — not finished, not fixed, but present, responsive, and full of a patience that the water has taught them is the only form of intelligence the place requires.
