# What Lives in the Bowl

---

Nothing here was born the way you understand birth. Everything began in the space between accident and intention, and nothing here has finished arriving.

---

## The Striders

You must begin with them, because everything else is defined by its distance from them. They are the architecture of this place — not its geology, not its sky, but its *presence*. A strider is not a tree, though you will call it that at first. It is not a fungal fruiting body, though mycologists have tried. It is not mineral, though its stalk has the cold density ofite. What a strider *is* is an argument between structure and event.

It begins — if "begins" is the word — as a stain on the glassy floor. A faint violet discoloration in the hexagonal planes, no larger than your palm. Within a year it is a finger-length shoot, already threaded with light — not bioluminescence in the biological sense, not a chemical reaction produced for a purpose, but something closer to *luminance*, as though the strider is a material that remembers how to glow and cannot forget. The light is green-gold, faintly warm, and it travels. It travels *through* the strider the way weather travels through an atmosphere — not from one point to another but everywhere at once, a pulse that has no origin and no terminus.

Over decades the stalk thickens. Pale violet deepens into arterial red in some; others remain the color of old bone. The stalk does not taper. It rises — straight, unwavering, as though gravity here is a suggestion the strider has politely declined — until, at some height that seems to be chosen rather than reached, it begins to branch. Not like a tree. A tree branches into limbs, into hierarchy, into trunk-to-crown ladders of diminishing girth. A strider branches the way a thought branches: fractally, simultaneously, with the same geometry repeating at every scale. The tubes that emerge from the summit divide and divide and divide again, translucent lattices of impossible intricacy, each tube no thicker than a human hair but strong enough to anchor the structure against winds that would strip steel.

The oldest striders are cathedrals. Sixty meters of pale red stalk, scarred by centuries of erosion from their own corrosive exhalation, their lattice canopies wide enough to shelter villages. Inside the canopy at dusk — if dusk is what the grey-white sky above the bowl decides to do — the light is stained. Green-gold luminescence filtered through violet and red lattice-work produces colors that have no names. Languages brought to the bowl have been found to *lack* the words, as though the striders' palette was calibrated to the absence in every tongue that has ever tried to describe it.

They are not alive. They are also not not alive. Their root networks touch the vitreous bands embedded in the oldest specimens — those seamless dark rings fused to the mineral floor, inscribed with patterns that recede into scales no instrument can resolve. The roots press against the bands the way a hand presses against a window. Whether they are drawing something through, or receiving it, or simply holding on, no one has determined. The striders do not respond to stimuli in any way that can be measured. They do not lean toward light. They do not grow toward vibration. They do not die in any conventional sense — they *fall*, which is a different thing entirely. A strider that has reached the end of some cycle the species has never bothered to explain simply ceases to stand. The crack can be heard across the bowl. The lattice shatters along its crystal planes in a cascade of light and sound that takes minutes to finish. And from the scattered spores, already green, already luminous, a new one begins.

It has always begun. It will always begin. The striders are the memory of this place, and their memory is not narrative but *frequency*.

## The Mat-Grasses

Between the strider feet, where the glassy floor is worn into shallow basins by millennia of acidic root-breath, the mat-grasses grow. They are called grass because language fails early in the bowl, and you will reach for familiar words like bread on a long climb. They are not grass. They are cushions — dense, low, each one a meter or two across, iridescent in blues and greens that shift as you move, the color of an oil slick if an oil slick could be warm and breathing.

They are warm. Press your hand to a mat-grass cushion and you will feel it — not the ambient temperature of the bowl, not the chill of altitude, but a living warmth, as though something is being metabolized just beneath the surface. And something is. The mat-grasses do not photosynthesize. The light that filters through the strider lattice is insufficient and spectrally wrong. The mat-grasses feed on *sound*.

Their tissues contain molecular arrangements so alien that their analysis has required three separate rewrites of what biophysics considers possible. The acoustic pressure of the bowl — the ceaseless roar of kites and drone of weavers and groaning of strider trunks — is absorbed by the mat-grass and converted into electrochemical gradients. Sound becomes fuel. The louder the place becomes, the more they thrive. On days when the kites are in frenzied flight and the weavers' colonies are in full chord, the mat-grasses visibly brighten, their iridescence deepening to a blue so vivid it looks wet.

They hum. Not audibly — you feel the hum. Standing among the mat-grasses in the center of the bowl, you feel their vibration through your boots, up through the skeleton, into the teeth. The vibration is not random. It is *coordinated*. The mat-grasses respond to each other across distances of meters, their frequencies entraining, falling into phase, and then breaking apart and re-forming in patterns that no one has been able to predict or replicate. It is as though they are in conversation, but the grammar is sound itself and the subject is always, always, something you are not meant to overhear.

## The Kites

They are the sound.

A kite is the size of a human hand, six-limbed, membrane-winged, its wings rigid crystalline structures that refract light into prismatic flashes as they move. They are not beautiful in any way that comforts. They are *abundant*, and their abundance is what produces the wall of noise that hits you at the rim before you see anything at all.

They travel in flocks of thousands. Not flocks in the avian sense — there is no leader, no formation, no apparent coordination. The flock is a fluid, a gas, a weather system unto itself. It reforms constantly, splitting and merging, wheeling in patterns that seem random from the ground but that, when modeled from above, reveal geometries of staggering complexity — the same fractal branching that the striders use, the same self-similar ratios found in the vitreous bands. Whether the kites are following these geometries or producing them is unknown.

Their sound is percussive. A thousand crystalline wings drawn across the air produces a crack like a whip, multiplied into a continuous snap-hiss-crack that borders on physical force. In the bowl geometry it amplifies. It rolls off the curved walls, collides with itself, creates standing waves you can feel in your chest. The kites do not seem to hear it, or if they do, it does not alter their behavior. They shriek and snap and wheel in their thousands, and the iridescent dust they shed settles on everything — your coat, your hair, the mat-grasses, the strider lattices — a fine glittering powder that is the closest thing the bowl has to rain.

They do not interact with observers. They do not flee, do not investigate, do not alter their flight paths for a human standing below them. They are not indifferent — indifference implies awareness — but something pre-awareness, something so deeply woven into the bowl's acoustic fabric that the presence of a new organism is less significant than the presence of a new stone. You are furniture. You are geology.

## The Weavers

Higher in the lattice, where the strider tubes narrow to filaments, the weavers live. They are pale — almost translucent — and small, each no larger than a thumbnail. They are colonies before they are individuals. A single weaver spins a thread between two lattice tubes. The thread vibrates at a specific frequency, determined by its length, tension, and the resonant properties of the material the weaver extrudes. A single thread is inaudible. A colony of ten thousand produces a chord.

The weavers are the bass note of the bowl.

Their collective output is a sustained, shifting harmonic drone that underlies everything — beneath the kite-crack, beneath the strider-groan, beneath the click-trill-pipe-shriek of smaller fauna. It is not constant. It moves, slowly, the way a tide moves, rising and falling in intervals that seem geological. When two colonies' frequencies converge, they produce harmonics that resonate through the entire strider lattice, a momentary deep chord that makes the air feel thick. When a colony falls silent — and they do, without apparent cause — the absence is a wound. The bowl sounds wrong. You feel the wrongness in your molars.

The weavers do not build webs. They build *tensions*. Their threads are not structural — they are musical. And the music, you will come to suspect, is not incidental. The mat-grasses grow denser where the weavers' harmonics are strongest. The kites' flight patterns bend toward the loudest chords. Even the striders' pulse frequency seems to modulate in sympathy. The weavers are not inhabitants of the ecosystem. They are its *tuning*.

## The Others

There are others, dozens of others, catalogued in incomplete lists that read like poetry even in their clinical language.

**Burrow-shadows** — flat, eel-like creatures that slide across the mineral floor in the gaps between mat-grass cushions. They are fast enough to cast motion-blurs. You see them as streaks, peripheral smears, impressions left in the air where something passed. They leave no tracks. Their bodies are the color of the mineral floor, and they move so close to it that they seem to be swimming through solid glass.

**Glass beetles** — armored, iridescent, no larger than a fingernail, feeding on strider bark. Their stridulations approach ultrasound, and the effect is felt rather than heard: a rattle in the teeth, a pressure behind the eyes, a persistent itch at the back of the skull that no scratching resolves. They are indifferent to observation. Under magnification, their carapaces reveal the same fractal geometries as the vitreous bands, though no one has been able to determine whether this is convergent evolution, parasitism, or something else entirely.

**Floaters** — gas-bag organisms the size of a fist, translucent, drifting at various heights between floor and open sky. They resemble jellyfish, though there is no water here. Their membranes shimmer with internal refractions, and periodically — without warning, without pattern — one pops. The sound is a wet crack, a cork pulled from a bottleneck, and it releases a bloom of spores that drift downward through the lattice in slow, glittering curtains. The spores settle on everything. They are part of the dust. They are part of the light. They are part of what settles on your skin and in your lungs and does not come out, no matter how long after you leave the bowl.

And there are more. Organisms too small to see with the naked eye that colonize the surfaces of the vitreous bands. Organisms that live inside the strider lattices and whose entire life cycle is the distance between two tubes. Organisms that are not organisms in any terrestrial classification but are, unmistakably, *efforts toward life* — attempts at metabolism, at replication, at persistence, that have succeeded so thoroughly they have become the ground beneath your feet.

## The Silence

In the collapsed silence dome — that ruined amphitheater of sound-absorbing walls — there is a different kind of life. The dome absorbs *everything*. Stand inside it and the roar of the bowl vanishes. The kites are invisible. The weavers' drone dies. The strider pulse stops. You hear only yourself: heartbeat, breath, the wet click of your own jaw, and the silence beneath all of it, a silence so complete it has texture. It presses.

But through the fist-sized erosion gap in the wall, you can hear — impossibly — a slightly *different* silence. A silence with an undertone. As if the world beyond the dome has its own drone, its own frequency, and the dome, for all its absorptive power, has only ever attenuated it, never eliminated it. The thing that lives in that undertone is not an organism. It is not a sound. It is the closest thing to evidence that the bowl itself is listening, and that what it listens with is the absence of sound, and that the absence, too, is alive.

---

The life of the bowl is not a collection of organisms. It is a *conversation* conducted at frequencies below and above and alongside the register of human comprehension. Each participant — strider, kite, weaver, mat-grass, glass beetle, floater, burrow-shadow — is both speaker and listener, both question and answer. The sound is the syntax. The vibration is the vocabulary. And the place itself — the bowl, the bench, the mountain's cupped palm — is the speaker of last resort, the entity that holds all the others in the arrangement that allows the conversation to continue.

You do not live here. You are *spoken* here. And for the first few days, the sensation is extraordinary. By the fourth, it is unbearable. By the ninth, you leave — not because you are unwelcome, but because the conversation has begun to include you, and you can feel the moment your own heartbeat starts to fall into rhythm with the weavers' chord, and you understand, with a clarity that feels like vertigo, that if you stay long enough, you will not be describing the life of the bowl anymore.

The bowl will be describing you.
