## What Lives There

The first thing to understand is that nothing lives *there* — not in the way that word implies, not as tenant inside a building, not as guest at a table. The place is not a container. It is not forest-with-residents. It is closer to say that the place *is* what lives there, and what lives there is still deciding what it wants to be.

You can feel it in the way the creatures refuse to be individuals.

The insects in the bark — those hundreds of carapaces locked together like tessellated teeth — are not insects in any sense you can comfortably hold. They do not come and go. They do not have lives separate from the tree. If you watched long enough (and you cannot watch long enough; your eyes are not built for the patience this demands), you might see one of them shift, a slow telescoping of leg into leg, a jaw moving across a shoulder-blade that isn't a shoulder-blade, and you would realize the mosaic is not a collection. It is a surface. It is skin. The tree has skin made of insects, and the insects are the tree's way of touching the air, and the air is what they breathe in common, and the word *individual* is a kind of blindness you brought with you from somewhere else.

The amphibians are worse. The column you found on the log — the stack of them, pressed together, breathing in unison — they are not cooperating. They are not colonial in the way you've read about, not like siphonophores or coral, each one a specialized limb of a larger body. They are not specialized. They are *identical*, or nearly so, and the near-identical is somehow more disturbing: ten, twenty, thirty smooth-skinned bodies aligned on the log, each one the same slate-dark grey, each one the same size, each one with the same faint seam of bioluminescence along its flanks that you only notice when the amber light shifts. They breathe together because they are breathing the same breath. They are not stacked. They are *folded*. The log is what they become when restlessness is unnecessary, and when restlessness becomes necessary again — and it does, you see it later, a ripple through the column like a nerve impulse, and the topmost one unfolds and drops silently into the mud and is immediately absorbed by the undergrowth — they do not scatter. They redistribute. They become volume. They fill space the way water fills a crack, and the shape of the space determines what shape they take.

The frog at the pool's edge is, you think, the closest thing to what you'd recognize. It is still too large — cantaloupe-sized, heavy-looking, absurd — and its skin mimics the volcanic grit so precisely that you nearly step on it. Its eyes are on the top of its head and they are open and they are not looking at you and they are not looking at anything. They are *receiving*. The frog does not watch. It monitors. Its sides rise and fall on that long ten-second rhythm, and the rhythm is wrong for a frog — too slow, too deep, as if its lungs are backups for something larger, something that breathes in geological time. Its mouth is closed. You never see it open its mouth. You begin to suspect it does not eat, or that eating and what it does are the same act, that it absorbs what it needs directly through that gritty skin, mineral by mineral, warmth by warmth, a living extension of the warm pool it sits beside, which is itself a living extension of the warm ground beneath it. It does not blink. You watch for so long that your eyes water, and when you finally look away, you understand that the frog has been using your attention the way the lichen uses stone: as a surface, as a place to be, as a kind of proof that something solid exists to be inhabited.

Above, the too-many-pointed silhouettes. You never see them clearly. They move in the highest layer of canopy where the branches thin and the false light takes on a quality like being underwater. The wings — if wings — fold in geometries that make your eyes ache. Not insect wings. Not bird wings. Something membranous and jointed, with subsidiary structures that unfold and refold like arguments being made in real time. They call to each other in that tearing-silk sound, and the sound does not echo. It is absorbed so completely that you feel it happen in your chest, a little pinch, a little void. They are not flying so much as *commuting* between positions in a structure you can't perceive — a lattice of routes and perches and anchor-points that exists in the canopy the way capillary networks exist in tissue, visible only if you could see the whole thing at once, which you cannot. You catch one silhouette once, clearly, against a rare gap in the canopy where light drops in a white column. It had too many wings. Four. Six. Some of them vestigial, crumpled, present but not deployed. Its body was long and segmented, not like an insect's segments but like a finger's knuckles, and it hung for a moment on a branch with its weight distributed across points you couldn't count, and then it folded into the green with a sound like closing a book.

There is something else. You feel it more than you see it. A presence in the undergrowth that is not a creature at all but a *behavior* — the way the vegetation parts and closes behind something moving through it, the way the ferns lean away from a pressure that isn't wind, the way a patch of moss dims and then brightens as something passes beneath it. You never see the thing itself. You see only its wake — a corridor of disturbed air, a momentary absence of sound where the pulse falters and then resumes, a faint displacement of water in the pools that spreads outward in rings and then vanishes. It is large. Larger than you. Large enough that the parting of vegetation is not brute force but inevitability, the way a thought parts the softer material of a dream. It has, you become certain, too many legs. But you never count them because counting requires a moment of stillness and attention, and the moment never holds still long enough. It moves at the speed at which the place itself grows — slow, patient, enormous — and it is always just beyond the periphery of your direct vision, at the edge of your flared sight, retreating into the tangle the instant you focus.

And beneath all of this, holding it all, the warmth.

The warmth that rises from the young ground. The warmth that lives inside the trees. The warmth that matches the frog's breath and the pulse in the bark and the rhythm of the sound that never stops. It is not geothermal in any simple sense. It is closer to metabolic. The stone is still cooling, yes, but it is cooling *into* life, the way an ember cools into ash that feeds soil that feeds root that feeds — and here is where the metaphor collapses, because there is no "and then." There is only *and and and*. The warmth is the signature of a process so large and so slow that it is indistinguishable from geology, and the creatures are not living on top of the process or inside the process but *as* the process. They are the process, at the stage of itself where it has become mobile, diversified, self-monitoring, the way a brain is a liver is a lung is all of it at once when you zoom out far enough to see the body whole.

What lives there is a body. That is the only way to say it. Not a body *like* something. A body. One metabolism, one warmth, one breath held and held and held across an expanse of young volcanic grit and ancient wet and green so dense it is structural. The creatures are its cells, its tissues, its organs of perception and exchange. The frog is a kidney. The insect-mosaics are a dermis. The too-many-winged things are dendritic signals carried along the canopy's nervous branchwork. The moving something with the legs is the dream-state, the unconscious, the deep processing that happens in darkness without language or image, just pressure and gradient and the slow turning of nutrients through a system that has no name because it has not yet needed one.

And you — you are not an intruder. You are not even a guest. You are a transient fluctuation. A local increase in awareness that the body registers the way a river registers a stone: briefly, without comment, and then with the stone gone, as though it had never been there at all. The body does not resent you. It does not welcome you. It simply continues, because continuing is all it has ever done, because it began the moment the stone cooled enough to hold water and the first violet lichen touched its surface and said *yes* in a language that predates sound. It will continue after the stone has cooled further and the water has found new channels and the green has thickened into something beyond green, something your eyes will never see because they are built for other places, other contracts with light and duration.

What lives there lives without knowing it lives. It is the most alive place you have ever been, and it does not know the word.
