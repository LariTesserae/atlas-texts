**The Inhabitants**

---

They are not what you expect.

You might imagine — given the stone-age technology, the isolation, the spare and mineral landscape — that the people of Skarn would be hard, angular, pinched by cold into something sharp and withdrawn. They are not. They are, if anything, *soft*: round-shouldered, thick-handed, wide in the chest, with a quality of physical ease that surprises you when you first see them move through the settlement's narrow passages. They have the bodies of people who have never once starved and never once hurried.

Their skin is pale to the point of translucence — not the pale of someone who avoids the sun, but the pale of someone the sun has simply never reached in any meaningful way. The Shelf's light is bright but cold; it washes over them the way it washes over the stone, without warmth. Under it, you can see the faint blue tracery of veins in their temples, the pink geography of their inner eyelids when they blink. Children, particularly, seem almost luminous — as though they are lit from a source just beneath the skin.

They are not tall. Average height, for a Skarnite, is perhaps five feet four inches. But their proportions are dense: short legs, long torsos, broad hands with fingers that taper to blunt, strong tips — the hands of people who knap basalt and weave cordage and dig with their bare fingers into the wet substrate without flinching. Their nails are kept short, filed against stone, and their palms are rough — not calloused in the way a laborer's palms are rough, but permanently *textured*, as though the mineral-rich water and the constant contact with wet stone have simply become part of their skin.

Their hair is dark — nearly black — and worn long by both men and women, pulled back in no particular fashion, often damp. It smells faintly of smoke and the lichen broth that forms the backbone of their diet. There is no adornment convention that you can immediately identify: no piercings, no painted markings, no variation in clothing that suggests status or affiliation. They wear layers — tunics and wraps made from the woven grass-fiber, stiffened by the wet and dried stiff again so many times that the fabric becomes almost like a second skin, creased permanently to the shape of the body beneath it. Colors are limited to the natural tones of the fiber: grey-green, pale brown, occasionally a dull ochre from a root-dye that someone, somewhere, learned to extract.

Their faces are broad, with wide-set eyes that are, almost universally, a pale grey — the color of the Shelf's sky, of the wet calcite, of the condensation film on stone. These are eyes adapted to the bright, cold light: they do not squint. They *receive* light rather than flinch from it, and when you meet the gaze of a Skarnite you have the unsettling sense that they are taking in more of you than you are comfortable with — not with hostility, but with a patience so thorough it feels almost invasive.

They do not smile easily. This is not a culture of easy expressiveness. But when they do smile — and it does happen, usually among children, or between two people who have shared a long silence — it transforms their faces completely: the broad mouths open wide, the pale eyes crinkle, and for a moment the general reserve of the place breaks open into something startlingly warm. You realize, when you see it, that you have been reading their stillness as coldness, and it is not. It is simply *calm* — the calm of people who live in a place that has never needed them to be urgent about anything.

**Daily Life**

The day in Skarn is organized not by clocks or bells — there are none — but by the position of the light and the behavior of the water. The inhabitants know, with a precision that seems almost biological, when the morning condensation will begin and when it will stop. They schedule their activities around it: the early hours, when every surface is wet and dripping, are for indoor work — toolmaking, mending, the preparation of food. The middle hours, when the light is sharpest and the stone has begun to dry (though "dry" on the Shelf means merely *less wet*), are for building, digging, and any task that requires dry hands. The late hours return everyone indoors, to the stone pits and the communal hearths, where the warmth from the ground below rises through the soles of the floors and the families sit together in a silence that is not uncomfortable.

They eat simply. The lichen broth — thin, grey-green, faintly bitter — is the staple, supplemented by the pale hare meat roasted on flat stones over coals, and by a kind of paste made from the fibrous grass roots that the children seem to enjoy and the adults eat out of habit rather than pleasure. Water is drawn from the carved runnels and basins in each building; it is always cold and always faintly mineral, with a taste that newcomers find metallic. The inhabitants drink it without remark, as though it were the most natural thing in the world — which, to them, it is.

They sleep communally, though not promiscuously. Families share rooms — the low, thick-walled cells with their recessed sleeping ledges — and visitors are given space on mats laid on the floor. There is no privacy in the way you understand it, but there is a profound respect for stillness. Even in the shared sleeping spaces, bodies are arranged with geometric care, and the quiet of the clickers vibrating through the walls ensures that sleep comes easily.

Children are everywhere. They are small versions of the adults — round, soft, quiet — but with more energy and less restraint. They play games that involve the crawlers, watching their trails, laying obstacles in their paths, and then — inexplicably — laughing when the crawlers route around them with the same patient, unhurried persistence. They are not shushed when they make noise, but the noise they make is itself quiet: low voices, the sound of stone on stone, the rapid patter of bare feet on wet rock.

**The Relationship to the Place**

This is what strikes you most, if you spend any time among the Skarnites: they do not see themselves as separate from the Shelf. They do not describe the place as their home in any possessive sense. When they speak of it — and they speak of it frequently, in the plain, factual register of their written records — they include themselves as one element among many, as they might describe the movement of water or the growth of moss.

"We are warm," a Skarnite named — there is no surname tradition; names are single, short, given at birth and never changed — told you, by way of explaining why the settlement exists where it does. "The ground is warm. The stone keeps us. We keep the stone by building on it."

There was no metaphor in this. There was no poetry you could detect. And yet — standing there, feeling the cold radiating up through the soles of your boots from the wet basalt beneath, hearing the faint pulse of the clickers in the walls, watching the crawlers trace their silent paths along the seam where wall meets floor — you understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, what she meant. The settlement is not on the Shelf. The settlement is *of* the Shelf. The same processes that raised the stone from the lowlands and threaded it with white veins and kept it perpetually damp and warm from below also produced these people: their bodies, their customs, their quiet, functional language, their Paleolithic tools and their carefully channeled water and their layered, self-burying architecture. They grew here, the way the calcite grows — slowly, in response to pressure and the presence of water, without anyone deciding to.

**The Others**

There are a few things the Skarnites will not show you, or will show you only if you press and then only briefly, with the expression of someone handing you something they are not sure you should hold.

In the deepest level of the settlement — reached by a staircase so old the edges of its steps have been worn into smooth concavities — there is a chamber that the inhabitants call *the low place*. They do not store anything there. They do not gather there. But they maintain it. Someone brings water to it. Someone sweeps its floor. And in it, visible only by the dim light reflected off the wet walls, you can see them: the things that are not crawlers or clickers or pale hares or disc-shaped entities or feathered worms. Shapes that move too slowly to be animals and too irregularly to be plants. Forms along the walls that suggest something *written*, though in no language and in no arrangement that resolves into pattern the longer you look.

The Skarnites look at them the way they look at everything: calmly, and without explanation. When you ask what they are, the answer is always the same word — translated roughly as *background* or *underneath* or *what was already here* — and the conversation ends there, not because they are hiding something, but because they genuinely do not understand what else to say.

They do not fear the *low place*. They do not revere it. It is simply there, the way the water is there, the way the heat from below is there. It is part of the Shelf, and they are part of the Shelf, and the distinction between the living and the not-living, between the settled and the ancient, between the creature and the stone — that distinction, which you brought with you from the lowlands, simply does not hold here the way it does elsewhere.

You will leave Skarn eventually. Everyone does. The settlement receives and releases. Those who stay are those who, for reasons they cannot articulate in any language you share, hear the clicking in the walls at night and find in it not a sound to be startled by but a rhythm to settle into — the way a body settles into warm water, not because it understands the warmth but because it recognizes it.

You will look back from the lowlands, where the cloud has closed over the Shelf again and the place is gone from sight entirely. But you will feel it — faintly, the way you feel a stone you once held still warm in your pocket hours later. A residual heat. A shape pressed into your palm.

Something that was already there, waiting to be touched.
