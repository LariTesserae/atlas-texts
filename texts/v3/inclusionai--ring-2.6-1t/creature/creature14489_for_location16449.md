## What Lives in the Equilibrium Sea

---

**I.**

Begin with the crust.

Before anything with legs or eyes or a mouth, there is the fungal substrate — the thin gray-white iridescence that covers every submerged surface in a film so fine it barely looks alive. Run your hand along a basalt column and you will feel it: a slight resistance, like breath on glass, a coating that is not quite slime and not quite mineral and not quite skin. It has no smell. Or rather, it has no smell that a human nose could separate from the cold mineral scent of the water itself. It is the first colonizer and the last survivor. It was here before the iron was riveted. It will be here after the rivets dissolve.

Under magnification — and the visiting scholars who first magnified it reported a long silence before they wrote anything down — the substrate is not truly fungal, not in any terrestrial sense. Its cells are fungal in shape, in habit, in the threadlike way they penetrate the volcanic glass along micro-fractures, but the chemistry is wrong. It does not consume organic matter. It does not photosynthesize. It eats stone. Specifically, it oxidizes reduced sulfur compounds and chelates iron from the basalt matrix, pulling metal from metal as neatly as a hand pulling a sword from a stone, and it converts this into energy through a metabolic pathway that has no name because no taxonomic framework anticipated it. The scholars called it lithotrophic for want of a better term. But it is not. It is something else. It is the thing that happens when life is given no alternative and no competition, when the only meal available is the planet's own bones and the only instruction is *persist*.

It grows slowly. It grows everywhere. Between these two facts, everything else is possible.

---

**II.**

The grazers are what you notice first in animal terms, because they are the largest moving things and because of the sound.

They are dog-sized but shaped like nothing a dog's ancestry could explain. Low. Wide. Deliberate. Four pairs of jointed legs splayed to either side like the ribs of an overturned basket, each leg ending in a blunt, hooked pad that grips the glass substrate without slipping. Their bodies are enclosed in a segmented shell — not a single carapace but a stack of overlapping rings, maybe twenty of them, each one slightly larger than the one above, giving the animal the silhouette of a pinecone laid on its side. The shell is dark, almost black when wet, and has a matte mineral texture, as though the animal had been sculpted from compressed volcanic ash rather than grown from the inside out.

They move in clusters of five to twelve, usually, though solitary individuals are not uncommon. A cluster moves the way a single organism moves — not in formation exactly, but with a shared tempo, as though all twelve animals are listening to the same metronome. And perhaps they are. The sound they make is part of it: the clicking of their mandibles, a rhythmic, metronomic *tick-tick-tick* produced by two interlocking plates of chitinous mineral that meet at the front of the head and scrape against each other as they feed. Each mandible-click is identical in pitch and duration to every other click, producing a sound that is less biological than mechanical — the sound of a hand-crank, of a cog engaging a gear, of a loom shuttle passing back and forth. When a cluster of grazers works across a submerged ridge, the sound is a wall of tick-ticking that merges with the ambient drone of the place until you cannot tell where biology ends and geology begins.

They are scraping the fungal substrate from the glass. Their mandibles are wide, flat, slightly curved, and they move in lateral sweeps, peeling the mat from the rock with the precision of a squeegee on glass. You can see the cleared patches behind them: bare black basalt, wet and shining, a few centimeters wide, trailing behind the cluster like the wake of a slow boat. They do not eat anything else. They do not need to. The fungal mat regrows within hours. They follow a circuit — ridge to ridge, column to column — and when they arrive at a patch that another cluster has grazed days or weeks before, it has recovered, and they graze it again, and it regrows, and this cycle is as reliable as the turning of anything that could be called a day.

They shed their shells. Not all at once — a ring at a time, the oldest ring at the base loosening and splitting along a pre-formed seam, the animal flexing its body to crack it free and stepping out of it the way you might step out of a boot that has grown too tight. The shed rings lie on the substrate, empty and brittle, and within hours the smaller things arrive.

---

**III.**

The smaller things have no common name in any of the languages the scholars brought, and so they are referred to, in the literature, simply as "scavengers," which is accurate enough and tells you almost nothing about what they are.

They are quick. Dark — so dark they look like holes cut in the water. Multilegged in a way that suggests not an ancestry you could diagram but a *principle* — the number of legs varies by individual, anywhere from eight to fourteen, each leg jointed in two places and tipped with a fine, hairlike seta that senses vibration in the substrate. They move with a skittering, liquid gait that is neither fast nor slow but precisely calibrated to the urgency of the moment, which is usually the urgency of a molted shell just vacated.

A grazer sheds a ring. Within seconds — truly, seconds, the response time is chemical, not conscious — three, four, five scavengers are on it, peeling apart the thin chitin with smaller, finer mandibles, consuming the mineral-rich material, fighting over the softer joints. They are not social but they are not territorial. They tolerate each other at a distance of one body-length, which occasionally results in a brief, violent sparring match resolved when one is flipped onto its back and retreats — scuttling away on its back like a kicked beetle, which it vaguely resembles.

When a filter-feeder dies — and they die, eventually, the sac rupturing in a slow, silent deflation that can take days — the scavengers come for that too. They come in larger numbers then. Dozens. Hundreds. They pick at the collapsed filaments, the desiccated sac walls, the dense mineral precipitate that accumulates in the dead animal's lower body. The feeding clusters at these sites are so dense they look like moving carpets, a single organism made of hundreds of individual bodies, all oriented toward the center, all feeding, all clicking and skittering in a sound that rises above the ambient drone and then, as the carcass is consumed, slowly fades back into it.

There is nothing cruel in this. There is nothing cruel anywhere here. Cruelty requires intention, and intention requires a nervous system complex enough to model the suffering of another. The scavengers do not suffer. The grazers, when their shells are cracked open by scavengers before they have finished with them, do not flinch. The filter-feeders, anchored and blind, do not notice when their dead neighbors are consumed beside them. This is not a world without feeling — the question of feeling here is complicated, unresolved, possibly unresolvable — but it is a world without the particular kind of suffering that comes from knowing you are suffering. It is a world of process, and process does not mourn.

---

**IV.**

The filter-feeders.

You do not see them at first. You see the columns, the hexagonal basalt walls, the glassy surfaces of the calderas flooded to their rims. You see the water. You see the light. And then your eyes adjust, and you see that some of the columns are not columns.

They are anchored to the substrate by a broad, calcified base — a disc of pale, porous material that has fused to the volcanic glass so thoroughly it is indistinguishable from it unless you know what to look for. From this base rises a sac. Enormous. Translucent. Sixty feet tall in the largest specimens, though most are closer to thirty. The sac is oblong, vaguely teardrop-shaped, and its walls are thin enough that you can see the internal architecture through them: a lattice of fibrous tissue, like the framework of a building under construction, with the spaces between filled with a fluid that shifts color as the animal pulses. The fluid is faintly opalescent, a milky blue-white that catches the ambient light and diffuses it, so that a field of filter-feeders at a distance looks like a grove of lanterns lit from within by a cold, sourceless fire.

The pulse is slow. One full expansion and contraction every eight to twelve seconds. On the expansion, the feeding filaments — hundreds of them, each as thin as a human hair, extending twenty or thirty feet outward from the base of the sac — unfurl into the current like the tentacles of a sea anemone made of smoke. They do not move on their own. The current moves them. But they are responsive — they curl away from contact with solid objects, they retract briefly when a grazer's clicking vibration passes through the substrate, and they slowly, continuously reorient toward the densest flow of particulates, which is to say toward the areas where the grazers have been scraping. The grazers and the filter-feeders are not in symbiosis. They are not in competition. They are simply two stages of the same machine, the grazers loosening material from the rock, the filter-feeders capturing it from the water. The machine does not need them to know this.

When a filter-feeder dies — and you know it is dying because the pulse slows, becomes irregular, the sac losing its translucency, going cloudy, then opaque, then gray — the filaments retract. Slowly. Not all at once, but from the outermost tips inward, curling back toward the base like fingers closing around a vanished object. The sac sags. The light inside goes out. And over the course of days, the sac deflates, collapsing in on itself, and what remains is a pale husk draped over the column like a shed skin, which the scavengers will consume over weeks, and then the calcified base will remain, fused to the glass, and on this base — the scholars observed this, documented it, argued about it for years — a new filter-feeder may grow, though nobody has ever observed the growth directly because it takes place underwater, in the cold, in the silence between clicks.

---

**V.**

There are other things.

There are, in the deeper channels between the ridges, things that have been glimpsed but not classified. Long, dark shapes that move against the current with a slow, muscular undulation. They are not filter-feeders. They are not grazers. They are too large and too mobile to be scavengers. The sonar returns from the flooded canyons suggest bodies — if they are bodies — on the order of thirty to fifty feet in length, though this could be the shape of the canyon itself, the acoustic return from a fold in the basalt misread as a living thing. The scholars debated this. Some swore they had seen movement that was too independent, too responsive, too *purposeful* to be geology. Others pointed out that purpose is a lens, not a fact, that the human eye finds intention in anything that moves, that the canyon walls shift in the current and the current shifts with the geothermal vents and the movement is an illusion.

Nobody went down to look. The water at depth is not the knee-deep, clear, light-permeated water of the shallows. In the canyons, it darkens fast. The pressure rises. The temperature fluctuates wildly near the geothermal vents — water hot enough to cook flesh adjacent to water cold enough to stop a heart. The grazers do not go there. The scavengers do not go there. The filter-feeders are anchored above the canyon rims, their filaments dangling at the edges but never descending. Whatever lives in the deep channels, if anything does, lives without competitor, without observer, in absolute equilibrium with conditions that would kill anything that had not spent eons adapting to them.

If you lean over the edge of the watchtower and look down — past the sixty feet of iron platform, past the rusted railing, past the wind-lashed air — into the water at the base of the tower, you will see the fungal crust on the submerged iron, the small grazers working a ridge ten feet below the surface, the filter-feeder anchored to the tower's own base, its filaments brushing the tower's side with a sound like a fingertip dragged along a pane of glass. And below that, the water darkens. And below that, you cannot see. And in that darkness, the drone continues, uninterrupted, indifferent, the mechanical heartbeat of a place that was here before you and will be here after, that does not require your presence to hold its equilibrium, that does not even know you are watching.

The grazer on the ridge looks up. Its mandibles click.

The equilibrium holds.
