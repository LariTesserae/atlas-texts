# The Inhabitants of the Drowned Works

---

They were not planned. Nothing here was.

The first life arrived the way first life always does — not as an arrival at all, but as a permission. A surface organism, larval and adrift, thin as a thought, slipped through a fracture in the upper hull during a storm. It was not built for this. It was built for flux, for light, for the rolling negotiation between tide and air. It died here, of course. But before it died, it shed something — a cyst, a spore, a chemical signature that the warm surfaces of the cooling conduits recognized as an invitation. Within a season, the signature had answered itself back.

What lives here now is not what was put here. It is what the place agreed to let live.

---

**The mineral-crabs** — you will think of them first because you will see them first. They are colonists of the walls, the machines, the skeletal framework of conveyor arms and pressure housings. They move in a state of permanent negotiation with the architecture, gripping corroded steel with their lower chelae the way a hand grips a railing, and testing the water with their upper pair, which are longer, thinner, feathered with sensory filaments that taste the chemistry of the current. They are not individuals in any way you would recognize. They are nodes in a distributed awareness. When one finds a thermal seam — a place where a buried conduit still bleeds warmth — others arrive within hours, not because any of them signals, but because the chemical gradient left behind by the first is a trail the water carries for them. They do not think. They are a thought the water is thinking.

Their shells are calcium-carbonate depositions layered over generations, each crab building its own architecture onto its own body until the boundary between inhabitant and structure dissolves. A crab that dies in the right place becomes substrate. A living crab, nearby, grows over the dead one's shell as if absorbing it. Old crabs are geometric, almost ornamental — spiraled, buttressed, accreted into forms that look less like biology than like the slow sculptures water makes when it is given centuries and something to lean against.

They click constantly. It is not communication. It is the sound of mineral meeting mineral, of a life that has grown teeth to match a world made of teeth.

---

**The mats** are the oldest residents. They were the first to arrive, or the first to succeed — there is a difference. They stretch along the thermal seams in translucent sheets, barely visible unless the light catches them from the side, at which point they glow with a dull amber bioluminescence, the color of a candle seen through fog. They are not plants. They are chemotrophs — they eat the difference in temperature between the warm machine-joints and the cold surrounding water, and they convert that gradient into biomass with a patience that makes a forest look frantic. 

Under your hand, if you were to touch them, they would feel like wet paper. They would not move. They would not respond. They have no organs, no cells in any sense that would correspond to yours. They are colonies of single-celled organisms so deeply interdependent that removing one would unmake the whole. They are, in a real sense, a single creature that happens to cover an area the size of a city block. And it has been eating the same seam of hot iron for longer than your civilization has had writing.

Everything that lives here feeds on the mats, or feeds on what feeds on the mats. They are the root system of a world that has no need for roots.

---

**The veils** — you will not find them for a while. They are midwater, and they are the thing that will make you understand the scale of this place is not emptiness but fullness. They are vast, gelatinous, sheetlike organisms that drift between the machine clusters at depths where the blue light thins. They are nearly transparent, but not quite — they have a faint internal structure, a net of filaments that catches particulate matter and the slow rain of organic debris falling from above. They are filter-feeders. They are the lungs of the place, drawing the water through themselves and leaving it cleaner, leaving behind the small dead things and the smaller living things that sustain everything else.

They move with a deliberateness that is not slow but simply unconcerned with speed. A veil may take a day to shift its body ten meters. It does not need to. The current brings food to it. The thermal gradients orient it. It reproduces by fragmentation — a piece of its trailing edge tears free, and that piece becomes a new veil, and the new veil is genetically identical but does not know the old one, does not need to, because knowledge is not what they trade in. They trade in chemistry and temperature and the long arithmetic of energy.

When you are between them, in the water with their trailing filaments brushing your suit, you will feel what they cannot feel: a presence. A gentleness. Something that has no word for you but has a word for the water, and the water has a word for you, and that word is *new-shape*, *new-shape*, *new-shape*.

---

**The flat hunters** — the largest things here. You will see them as shadows first, then as shapes, then as creatures that defy easy taxonomy. They are broad, dorso-ventrally compressed, like stingrays or like nothing you have ever seen depending on the angle. Their body plan is an ancient one — not unchanged, but deeply conserved. The broad flat body is a thermal gatherer, maximizing surface area against the convective currents. The paired fins articulate with a mechanical precision that is not mechanical, that merely *resembles* machinery because machinery was modeled, once, on the bodies of things that move through water.

They hunt the mineral-crabs. They do so with a patience that borders on geology. They will settle onto a thermal seam and wait — not for minutes but for hours, their metabolism so low that the act of waiting is barely distinguishable from the act of being alive. When a crab cluster grows dense enough along the seam, the flat hunter moves with a suddenness that seems impossible for something so large, sweeping its broad mouth through the cluster and filtering the crabs through baleen-like structures that are, mineralogically, almost identical to the ones your own filters were modeled after. 

They have eyes, but the eyes are vestigial — pale patches beneath opaque skin, structures that once saw light and now register nothing more than the difference between presence and absence of luminescence. They navigate by pressure, by thermal gradient, by the faint electrical signatures of the machines and the creatures living on them. They have lateral line systems so sensitive they can detect the displacement of your body moving through the water three hundred meters away. 

They are not aggressive toward you. You are too large, too diffuse, too thermally inert. You are a wall that moves, and walls do not register as prey. But if you were smaller, if you were a cluster of crabs glowing faintly along a seam — they would come for you with the inevitability of erosion.

---

**The small things.** Always the small things. Nematodes threading through the sediment that has accumulated in the low points of the floor grates. Rotifers cycling through the water in clouds so dense they appear as weather. A species of amphipod — and this one is genuinely beautiful, if beauty is something that can exist without an audience — that has evolved a transparent body filled with symbiotic bioluminescent bacteria, so that when it moves it leaves a brief ghost-trail of green light that fades over seconds, drawing constellations on the walls of the chamber that no one will ever map because they are never the same twice. 

There are things in the sealed tanks, behind the opaqued viewports, that you will not see. You will press your face to the glass — to the calcium — and sometimes you will see a shadow. A shape. Something that was sealed in with the machinery when the air was displaced and that has been living on the interior ecosystem of its own prison for longer than you have been alive. You will not open the tank. You will not know what is inside. It will not need you to.

---

And beneath all of it, beneath the organisms and the ecology and the layered chemical conversations happening in every cubic meter of water, there is the **place itself** — not alive, not dead, but in that third state that only very old systems achieve: *persistent*. The machines still hold heat. The conduits still channel current. The calcium deposits still grow, molecule by molecule, century by century, sealing the human signature under an ever-thickening layer of the place's own making. The crabs wear the metal. The mats eat the warmth. The veils drink the detritus. The hunters harvest the crabs. And all of it, all of it, is powered by the last electrical current running through circuits that were wired before any of this had a name, before the water had language for what it was becoming.

The organisms here do not know they live in a ruin. They do not know what metal is, or industry, or the species that built this place. They know warmth and gradient and pressure and chemistry. They know the taste of the water after a tidal pulse from the ocean above. They know each other — not as individuals, not as species, but as presences, as forces, as weather patterns in each other's lives.

They are the citizens of a world that was never meant to exist.

They are thriving in it anyway.

They will be here — clicking, filtering, feeding, slowly, slowly accruing their mineralite shells and their chemical memories — long after the last signal from the surface fades into the blue.

Long after your suit goes cold.

Long after your name.
