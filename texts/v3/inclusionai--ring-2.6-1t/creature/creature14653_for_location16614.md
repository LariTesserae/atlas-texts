## The Living of the Plateau

---

**The Quiet Engineers**

Begin where the plateau would have you begin — which is to say, begin beneath your notice.

The crusts are the oldest residents. They coat the exposed rock in thin, dark films that look inert until you kneel and see them breathing: lichen communities so ancient they have become part of the stone's surface identity, as though the rock itself had grown a skin. They are not colorful. They are gunmetal and umber and the faintest violet-gray where moisture lingers. They metabolize almost nothing by the standards of faster worlds — a trace of mineral leachate, a film of condensed water at dawn, the occasional dustfall from the haze — and in return they hold the surface together. They are the first colonizers of any fresh exposure, the first thread in a wound's suture. Without them the plateau would be raw, disaggregating, and the wind would have scoured it to nothing long before the pumpjacks arrived.

Alongside them — and sometimes over them, slowly consuming them — are the mats. These are harder to name. They are dark, rubbery, and alive in a way that suggests a lineage that split from something algal very early and then made its peace with the mineral world. They grow in thin, spreading sheets across flat rock surfaces, particularly in the shallow runnels where water passes through during the brief flood events. They are not green. They are nearly black when dry, and when wet they darken further, becoming almost iridescent — oil-slick purples and deep reds that fade within hours of the water's retreat. They feed on the condensation cycle and on whatever dissolved organics the water brings. In turn, they are grazed.

Because something eats them. Look closely at the mat margins in the early dim-light hours and you will see the trails: thin, winding furrows where something has passed over the surface, scraping just the top layer. The grazers are almost too small to resolve — pale, segmented things no longer than a fingernail, with too many legs to count easily and exoskeletons that have the sheen of wet mineral dust. They move in pulses, quick and then still, quick and then still, as though governed by a clockwork that alternates between urgency and patience. They are among the most numerous animals on the plateau and also among the least seen, because their bodies are the exact color of dried calcareous mud and because they freeze when they sense vibration. Step near a mat colony and the furrows go empty in a silence so complete it feels like erasure.

**The Tunnelers**

Below the surface, the plateau thrums.

This is not metaphor. Stand still long enough, boots flat on the rock, and you will feel it — a faint, distributed trembling that rises through the stone. It is not the pulse beneath the dark pools; it is older and less regular than that. It is the aggregate motion of thousands of small bodies moving through a lattice of tunnels so dense and so old that the substrate itself has been restructured by their industry.

The six-limbed burrowers. You have seen their entrances — those small, round holes ringed with pale dust — but you have likely not seen the architects. They are nocturnal in their surface activity, and even then they emerge only briefly: a quick darting motion across bare rock, a shadow that resolves for half a second into something angular and purposeful before vanishing into the nearest crack. They are compact-bodied, with six legs arranged in three pairs that move in an alternating tripod gait, and their limbs are shovel-tipped, adapted for both digging and rapid surface locomotion. Their eyes are small and recessed beneath bony ridges — vision is secondary here, and the plateau has taught them this. They navigate by vibration, by scent carried through the tunnel network, and by a sensitivity to air pressure changes that tells them when a cloudburst is arriving hours before the first drop hits rock.

Their tunnels are not random. **The Ambushers**

If the small burrowers are the plateau's nervous system, then the predators are its slow, deliberate thoughts.

They are legless — or nearly so. Four vestigial limbs near the anterior, too small to bear weight, pressed flat against a body that is all torso: long, low, muscular, built for patient stillness and sudden lateral strikes. Their skin is the color of the basaltic intrusions that seam the plateau — a deep blue-black with an iridescent quality that becomes visible only when the bronze light catches it at the right angle. In the perpetual haze, they are nearly invisible against the rock.

Their heads are blunt, wide, and flat, with the eyes set laterally — two on each side, placed so that the animal has a field of vision that encompasses nearly everything except what is directly behind it. The eyes are large relative to the skull, and they reflect the ambient light with a quality that is not quite luminescence and not quite glare — a focused attention made visible. When one of them looks at you, you feel addressed.

They hunt by vibration. They lie motionless near the productive zones — the water pools, the mat colonies, the burrow clusters — with their bodies pressed flat against the rock and their chins resting on the stone surface. They can feel a six-limbed burrower moving through a tunnel three meters away. They can distinguish the footfall of prey from the footfall of something their own size. They wait. They wait for periods that would be extraordinary by mammalian standards — an hour, two hours, the entire dim-light cycle — and then they move with a speed that seems impossible for something so low to the ground. The strike is lateral, a whipping motion of the anterior third of the body, and the prey is seized in a mouth that extends wider than seems mechanically possible.

They do not eat every day. They do not need to. Their metabolism matches the plateau's tempo — slow, considered, sustained. Between successful hunts they bask on warm rock surfaces, absorbing thermal energy through their dark skin. They are ectothermic, and the plateau's warmth is not incidental to their ecology; it is foundational.

**The Scavengers**

At the margins of the industrial sites, a different life congregates.

Small, social creatures — six-limbed like the burrowers but longer-bodied and more gracile, with elongated snouts adapted for probing into cracks and crevices. They travel in groups of six to twelve, moving with a quick, chattering energy that is the plateau's most openly communicative behavior. They vocalize in short, sharp calls — clicks and trills — that bounce off the pumpjack structures and create a constant, low-level acoustic texture around the extraction sites.

They feed on mineral-rich microbial mats that grow in the warm runoff around pipe junctions, on the small invertebrates attracted to those mats, and on the occasional carcass — their own, prey, mechanical grease, it does not seem to matter. They are omnivorous in the broadest sense, and their adaptability has made them the plateau's most successful colonists of the industrial zone. They nest in the hollows of abandoned pumpjack foundations, and their burrows into the compacted soil around these structures have become, over generations, a kind of secondary tunnel network — shallower and less organized than the burrowers' work, but extensive.

They are not afraid of the machinery. They have never known it to be still. The hum of the generators is their baseline; the rhythmic thumping of the pumpjacks is their metronome. They have been observed pausing in their foraging to press their bodies against the warm housing of a running generator, absorbing heat, their eyes half-closed — a behavior that, if you are willing to read it generously, looks like contentment.

**The Aerial Ones**

Rare. Seldom seen. But present.

Broad-winged — the wingspan is perhaps a meter, proportionally enormous for the body — and dark, so dark they are nearly holes in the bronze sky. They ride the thermals that rise from the sun-warmed rock with minimal effort, wings extended, barely angling. They circle slowly, and their silhouettes pass across the haze like calligraphy — a single stroke, a pause, a curving arc, gone.

What they eat is uncertain. They are sight-hunters, the only animal on the plateau that relies primarily on vision, and their eyes are proportionally the largest of any resident. They have forward-facing gaze — predatory, focused — and they are most active during the brightest hours of the dim bronze day, when the haze is thinnest and the shadows most defined. They have been observed stooping from altitude with a folding of wings and an explosive recovery, but the target of these stoops is never clear. Small burrowers caught in the open, perhaps. Or the smaller scavengers. Or something else — something the haze obscures even from above.

They do not vocalize. The only sound they make is the whistle of air through their primary feathers during a turn, a thin, high note that is gone as soon as it arrives.

**The Ones That Are Not Life**

And then, at the margins of the pools where the mineral crust is thickest, there are the forms.

Crystalline. Regular. Bilateral symmetry and angles that are too precise, too deliberate, for precipitation alone. They grow from the mineral deposits in a way that is technically consistent with the chemistry of the water — the concentration gradients are right, the temperature differentials are right, the dissolved ion ratios are right. But the shapes they produce are not what those parameters should yield. They are more. They are more structured, more intentional, more *composed* than any abiotic process on any other world has been observed to produce.

They are not alive. At least, they satisfy none of the conventional criteria: no metabolism, no reproduction, no response to stimulus. They do not grow in the way that living things grow; they *accrete*, adding mineral layers in slow, regular increments, like the plateau itself adds sediment. They do not move. They do not consume.

But the animals know them.

Every resident of the plateau — the burrowers, the scavengers, the ambushers — gives the formations a margin of distance that is instinctive, unlearned, and absolute. No individual animal has ever been observed approaching within a certain radius of the largest formations. Not because they have been harmed. Not because they have been conditioned. Because something deeper, older, and less articulate than learning has told them: *not here. Not this.*

The pattern is not fear. It is recognition. As though the animals know something about these forms that we have not yet learned to name. As though the formations are not anomalous, and the animals are the ones behaving normally — and it is our expectation of explanation that is the anomaly.

The plateau does not explain.

The plateau processes.
