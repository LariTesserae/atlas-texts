## What Lives in the Khäl

---

### The People of Kaelhof

They are not a single kind of people, and it would be a mistake to call them one thing. They come from somewhere below — the valleys, the lowland cities whose names they sometimes say like prayers or profanities, depending on the season — and they come because the mountain has something that can't be gotten anywhere else. Ore. Energy. A specific mineral seam that runs through the living rock like a vein through a hand, and that the lowlands have learned they can't do without.

But the people who stay — who stay long enough to stop calling the Khäl *this posting* or *the assignment* and start calling it *here* — they change. Not all at once. Not in any way that shows up on a medical scan. They change the way the mountain changes: slowly, in ways that are hard to name after the fact.

A Kaelhofer walks differently from anyone else. You can see it in how they place their feet — not hesitantly, but with a kind of listening attention, the way you'd step through a dark room you've lived in for years. They know where the ground will shift. They feel a micro-tremor in their ankles before their conscious mind registers it. Visitors sometimes say the Kaelhofers move like people walking on a deck at sea, and the Kaelhofers find this very funny, because the mountain moves less predictably than any ship.

Their language has drifted. Not into something unrecognizable — it's still the common tongue of the lowlands, mostly — but it has acquired textures. Long pauses. A specific rising inflection used not for questions but for statements of geological certainty, as if, when you've watched the ridgeline creep upward for ten years, you end your sentences the way a geologist ends a reading: with a quiet *but I could be wrong, the mountain decides*. They use the word *still* the way other people use the word *but*. *The mortar set still the beams are holding.* *The cable hummed still we got up.* It is not broken grammar. It is a grammar of patience, of process that has not concluded.

They are thin. Not unhealthy thin — the altitude and the thin air will do that to anyone — but structurally slight in a way that seems deliberate, as if the Khäl has been winnowing them, selecting for lighter frames, smaller oxygen demands, bodies that heat themselves efficiently against the cold that lives under the cold. Kaelhof children grow up shorter than their lowland relatives. Broader in the chest, if anything, with large lungs that they use with an economy that visitors find almost unsettling. A Kaelhofer does not huff up a slope. They breathe in and they breathe out and they arrive.

They have a relationship with the zones that is not fear and not acceptance but something adjacent to both — a word that might be *courtesy*. No one walks into the Lacuna. Not because anyone has explicitly forbidden it but because everyone knows, the way you know not to touch a hot surface, that the Lacuna is not a place for people. The painted stones are maintained not as a boundary but as a kind of offering — *here is where we acknowledge you, zone, we leave you this small bright thing and you leave us this much space.* Some families take turns painting stones. It is considered a good task for children: precise, quiet, attentive work.

The Kaelhofers live in long rhythms. Shift work at the extraction sites follows the mountain's own cycles — the ore comes easier during certain weeks, and everyone knows this even though no one can explain it. There are what they call *still weeks*, when the stamping plant runs quiet and the mountain holds its breath, and during these weeks the social life of Kaelhof blooms improbably. People gather in the machinery yards around open fires fed by scraps of petrified wood that occasionally surfaces in the newer excavations. They share food — preserved, mostly, tinned and dried, though a few greenhouses carved into the eastern cliff face produce stunted vegetables with an intensity of flavor that makes people cry the first time they taste them. Someone always has an instrument. Not a performance, exactly — more like a group of people remembering, together, that sound can be made for pleasure. The music sounds different here, filtered through the perpetual soft haze, coming from no particular direction, as if the mountain itself were humming along.

There is no government in Kaelhof. There is a council, but it convenes only when something must be decided — where to add the next platform, whether to reroute a cable, how many meters the safety perimeter around the Lacuna should be extended this year. The council's authority rests not on law but on the fact that its members are usually the people who understand the mountain best, and the mountain is the only authority anyone truly respects. Disputes are rare. The work is absorbing. The environment is unforgiving in slow, patient ways that do not produce the kind of sudden crises that generate conflict. Instead, grievances are small, specific, and aired in low voices, and they tend to resolve themselves the way cracks in cooling rock resolve themselves — slowly, through pressure, through time.

Couples pair and unpair with the lowland casualness you might expect from people who live in a place that could kill them at any moment not through violence but through geology. Children are raised communally — not because of ideology but because the practical demands of the settlement make it impossible to do otherwise. A child in Kaelhof has many parents. They learn to read the mountain's moods before they learn to read text. They know which cables sound wrong. They know which patches of moss mean water is close. They grow up in a world where the ground is always slightly alive, always slightly in motion, and they take this not as a threat but as a condition — the way a child raised on a coast takes the tide.

### What Flies

The ravens are the first thing you notice, and the last thing you understand. Their extra wing joint gives them a flight that is unmistakable: a fluidity that seems almost liquid, as if the air itself were thicker for them than it is for everything else. They curve where linear travel would be expected, banking around ridgelines and thermal columns with a grace that looks choreographed. Their calls are lower than lowland ravens — pitched so deep they border on subsonic, so that you sometimes feel a raven communicating before you hear it.

They are watchful. Dangerously watchful. A Kaelhofer will tell you that the ravens know things — not in a mystical way, but in the way that a creature with an extra joint in its wing has simply had more time in the air, more data, more angles on the wind — and if you see a raven sitting motionless on a cable for longer than an hour, something is about to shift in the mountain, and you should probably move to a steadier footing. No one can say whether the ravens actually predict tremors or simply react to stimuli too subtle for human senses. The distinction does not interest the people of Kaelhof greatly. They treat the ravens as fellow residents: present, opinionated, not entirely comprehensible, and not to be trifled with.

The pale-furred dog-things are harder to see and easier to dismiss as feral, until you watch them long enough. They move in packs of four to seven, and their patrol routes cover the scree fields with a thoroughness that suggests purpose beyond foraging. Their tongues are extraordinary — long, dark, precise instruments that move across bare rock faces with the delicacy of someone reading braille. They find mineral deposits invisible to human eyes. They lickite, and quartz, and something iridescent that forms along fresh fractures and that the creatures seem to crave. They are not hostile. They are not tame. They tolerate human proximity at a distance of about fifteen meters — closer, and the nearest individual will turn its head toward you with an attention so focused it feels like gravity. Their eyes are pale, almost colorless, and set at an angle that gives them a permanent look of mild, calm assessment.

They have never been seen to breed. No one has ever found a den, or a pup. They simply exist, in stable numbers, patrolling. Some believe they are a kind of mountain symbiote — organisms that emerged from the Khäl's ongoing construction the way moss emerges from condensation. Others think they are descended from feral dogs brought up by early expedition teams, changed by the environment in ways no one has bothered to study. The Kaelhofers call them *skelters*, a word that may be onomatopoeic — it sounds like the clicking of too many legs on gravel — or may simply be old slang with a lost origin. The skelters ignore the word. They do not appear to respond to any name.

The insects — *clicks*, the Kaelhofers call them, for obvious reasons — swarm near every water seep and condensation point. They are dark, iridescent, and have too many legs: not the comfortable six of common understanding but eight, sometimes ten, arranged in a configuration that makes them look like small mechanical devices assembled by someone who understood motion but not anatomy. Their exoskeletons throw prismatic light in the dimness, little sparks of green and violet against the grey stone. Their clicking is soft, collective, and rhythmic — a thousand tiny percussionists keeping time with a tempo that seems determined by the water's flow rate rather than by any biological imperative.

They are eaten by the skelters, who pick them from rock faces with startling precision, and by the ravens, who drop them from height onto flat stones — not to kill them but, it seems, to crack them open. The clicks do not resist this. They click faster during the fall, and then they stop.

### What Flies Higher

There are shapes above the ravens. Not birds — too large, too slow. They resolve against the dim sky the way a word resolves at the edge of perception: almost there, almost understood, and then not. They appear most often during the dim-phase, when the industrial glow from Kaelhof lights the underbellies of the clouds and the sky is at its most textured, and you might see one — a broad, unhurried silhouette wheeling against the overcast — if you happen to be looking up.

They have been described as catlike, and as manta-shaped, and as wings without bodies, and each description is true to the moment of observation and insufficient in hindsight. No one has ever seen one close. No one has ever seen one on the ground. They are not part of Kaelhof's ecology — or they are part of it in a way that the settlement's pragmatic language cannot yet accommodate.

The Kaelhof children know about them, and call them *dimmers* — not because they dim the light, but because watching them makes the world dim, briefly, the way a daydream does. They are said to be friendly, or at least indifferent. No one has been harmed by a dimmer. No one has been helped by one. They exist in the margins of the Khäl in the same way the zones exist — present, persistent, and operating on principles that are not hostile to human understanding but simply adjacent to it.

### What the Mountain Has Made

And beneath all of this, below the cables and the concrete and the compacted gravel, below the ore seams and the geothermal veins and the deep water channels that taste of dissolved stone — beneath, and within, and around — the mountain is alive.

Not in the way the moss is alive, or the skelters, or the Kaelhofers. Alive in the way a process is alive. The Khäl is still rising. The ridgelines are still finding their final shapes. The Lacuna is still expanding, one meter per year, a slow mouth opening in the eastern rock, and no one can say what it will eventually say, or whether it will say anything at all, or whether *saying* is the right verb for whatever the geometry of that space is doing.

The mountain breathes, in the sense that it moves air through fissures in ways that resemble exhalation. It listens, in the sense that sounds generated at one point in the settlement arrive at another point altered, as if the rock between them has been thinking. It grows, in the sense that inch by inch, year by year, new stone is being forced upward through fractures that did not exist a decade ago, and the survey markers that hang at eye level where they were once bolted at shoulder height are not souvenirs of erosion but evidence of construction.

The mountain is alive, and it is building, and it has not yet decided what it is building, and the people who live inside its body have made a kind of peace with this incompleteness — have built their platforms and strung their cables and painted their stones around an edge that is still forming, in a place where every wall is provisional and every foundation is a negotiation, and where the loudest sound is not the stamping press or the cables or the geothermal stack but the deep, geological murmur of something that has not finished becoming itself and is in no hurry.

The Khäl lives. The Khäl is still learning how.
