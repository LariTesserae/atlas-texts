# Who Lives on the Shelf

---

## The People

You see them first as movement — a shift in the frond-canopy, a shadow that passes across the bioluminescent underglow and does not come again. Then a hand appears, gripping a column edge, and then a face, looking down at you with an expression you cannot read. Not hostile. Not welcoming. The face of someone assessing whether you are part of the place or whether you have come to take something from it.

They are not like you. This is the first thing you must understand, though you may not understand it with your eyes at first — only with your body, after you have been here long enough for the light to change you, for the air to enter you, for the deep hum of the stone to settle into the marrow of your legs.

They are shorter than you expected. Compact. Dense-bodied, with broad shoulders and short, powerful limbs — not the elongated, gracile frame you would expect of people who live in vertical spaces, but something closer to what you might imagine a creature that has been shaped by gravity and pressure and the slow work of millennia would become. Their bones are thick. Their hands are wide, the fingers short and strong, with pads of toughened skin on the palmar surfaces that allow them to gripite andite stone and resin-wrapped frond-matting without tools. They do not need tools for everything. They have kept the things their hands can do, and they have added tools only where the hand must rest.

Their skin is not pale, though they live beneath a sky they never see. It is a warm ochre, almost the color of the amber fronds near the vents, and it is faintly luminescent — not glowing, not to your unaided eye at first, but when the bioluminescent light dims and you think you see a figure standing still against the stone and then realize there is a faint outline, a soft amber-orange edge, like a coal remembering its own fire. You learn later they have ingested the mineral-vegetation since before they could walk. It is in them. Trace compounds ofite anditeite calcium phosphorite and strontium and something you cannot identify accumulate in the deepest layers of their skin and fluoresce faintly under the Shelf's ambient wavelength. They glow the way the Shelf glows — dimly, persistently, from within.

Their eyes are large. You notice this immediately. The pupils are wide even in the ambient light, dark against the faint green-amber wash that fills the column spaces, and the irises — when you see them close — are a pale, almost opalescent grey, like the quartzite veins in the columns. They have a tapetum, or something like one; in the deepest dark of a dead-vent night, when the bioluminescence settles into its lowest ebb and the columns go blue-black around you, you can see their eyes catch what little light exists and return it, briefly, starlike.

They see well in the dim. They see in ways you do not. When you are with them in the mid-canopy — following them along a platform of compressed stone-and-grit that has been widened by generations of feet until it is a broad shelf fastened to the column by embedded anchors of resinite and woven fiber — you notice they do not look where they are going. They look *through* and *past* the structures, at angles, at the faint gradations of the bioluminescent light, at the texture of the frond-surfaces. Their navigation is not visual, exactly, or not visual in the way you understand the word. It is closer to reading — reading the luminous map of the Shelf the way you might read a page.

And they hear. The deep hum of the stone that you noticed only after an hour and forgot after two — they have never forgotten it. It is the baseline of their world, the constant beneath every sound. The children, even the very young, the ones still carried in woven slings against a caregiver's chest, are quiet in a way that is not trained or enforced but simply *of* this place. The Shelf is loud — the crawlers, the keening gliders, the white-noise insects — but beneath all of it is that subsonic pulse, and the people here have learned, unlearned, attuned themselves to it, the way a body attunes itself to the rhythm of a heart against which it has pressed since before birth. They speak softly not because they must but because they can feel the stone's voice beneath their own, and to raise the voice above it would be a kind of shouting over a parent.

## What They Are

You do not ask who they are. Not at first. You are too busy watching the way an elder — a woman, perhaps, though the distinction registers as less meaningful here than it does in the world you have come from — sits on a widened shelf near a vent, her bare feet resting on a stone that shimmers faintly with heat. She is weaving something. Her fingers move slowly — so slowly — drawing fibers from a bundle beside her, plaiting them into a mat that seems to grow under her hands like a living thing. The fibers themselves are mineral-vegetation — the flexible mid-growth fronds, cut at the right stage, when they are still pliant but have begun to calcify, when they are neither too green nor too rigid. She works them into patterns that are not decorative. They are structural. The weave follows geometries you almost recognize — spiral, tessellation, fractal branching — and you realize these are the same geometries as the mineral-vegetation's own growth, as the branching of the root-threads through the stone. She is not weaving a mat. She is extending the architecture of the Shelf.

The people here do not see a boundary between themselves and the living stone-world that surrounds them. Or rather, they see it the way you see the boundary between your breath and the air — present, but porous, continuous, inconsequential. A child crawls across a column surface and pauses to press its palm against the mineral-crust, the pale grey-white calcified filaments that cover the stone like frost, and when the child moves on, the imprint of its hand remains, filled with warmth from the skin, and you watch it slowly close, the filaments knitting together over the shape of the fingers, and for a moment it looks like the column has absorbed the touch, made it part of itself.

This is not metaphor. You watch it happen with your own eyes.

## The Crawlers

They are everywhere. Armored, methodical, patient — the way only things without predators and without need to hurry can be.

The crawlers move in constellations, and the word is not decorative. When you look at a column face from a distance — truly look, past the frond-lattice and the bioluminescent haze — you see the surface *shift* with them, a tide of chitinous bodies flowing in patterns that are not random, not exactly, but that do not resolve into legibility either. They move in response to what you can only describe as a consensus — a chemical signal, a vibration, a shared sense of where the growth is freshest, the mineral-crust thinnest, the grazing best. They communicate the way a murmuration of starlings communicates: not through any single call or signal, but through the aggregate response of each body to the movements of its neighbors, each crawler adjusting its path a fraction of a degree to the motion of the one beside it, and the whole mass resolving into shapes that are emergent, beautiful, and impossible to predict.

They are inedible raw. You know this because you tried, when one of the settlement's hunters — a lean, quiet man who moves through the column-network with the fluid ease of someone who has never known flat ground — handed you a freshly cracked shell and gestured toward the soft interior. It is dense, chalky, faintly metallic on the tongue. The people here eat it with a paste made from ground tuberous growth-matter, bitter and sweet and warm with something that tingles. They watch you chew and say nothing, but there is the faintest softening around their eyes — the look of people who have had visitors before and know that the strangest part is never the food.

The crawlers are the foundation of the food web. Everything feeds on them: the people, the gliders, the six-legged grazers that come down from the upper columns at dusk to graze the mineral-crust where the crawlers have cleared it. Even the mineral-vegetation benefits — the crawlers' grazing stimulates new crust-growth, the way pruning stimulates a plant in a green-leafed world. The crawlers do not know they are tended. But they are.

## The Gliders

At dusk — if dusk is the word for the slow dimming of the upper light as spore-clouds thicken and the bioluminescence shifts from green-amber to a deeper, cooler blue — the gliders leave their roosts.

They do not fly. They *release*. A body drops from a column-top, the membrane-wings catching the faint updraft, and for a long, silent moment the creature hangs in the air between columns, suspended in the vertical twilight, its silhouette a dark kite against the pale glow. Then it banks — shifts the angle of a wrist, a finger-bone, a tension in the membrane — and glides to the next column, and the next, and the next. Their flight paths trace the architecture of the Shelf from above: a map of the column-network seen through a membrane eye that perceives the bioluminescence in gradients you cannot distinguish.

They are social. They roost in clusters, pressed together on the warmest upper ledges, their bodies pressed flank to flank, their keening rising in the evening like smoke. The sound is thin and high, almost painful at close range, but from below, filtered through the column-network and reflected and refracted by the stone, it resolves into something almost harmonic — a chord, sustained, shifting, full of a sadness that is not the sadness of loss but the sadness of a place that has accepted its own limits and made beauty within them.

You will hear them at night. You will wake in the shelter-mat, the woven frond-matting breathing around you, the stone beneath you its steady warmth, and you will hear the gliders keening in the dark, and you will understand, without being able to articulate it, that this sound is the closest thing the Shelf has to a voice. That the place speaks through them.

## The Grazers

They are the strangest, and the quietest.

Six-legged. The size of a large goat, though they do not look like goats — nothing like goats. Their bodies are low-slung and heavy, almost slab-like, built close to the stone as though the vertigo of the upper columns would take them if they stood any taller. Their mouths are broad, flat, and lined with rows of mineral-ite grinding surfaces, and they crop the high fronds and the thin mineral-crust with the slow, deliberate movements of animals that have never known scarcity. Their hides are thick, faintly calcified, and they leave behind them on the stone a faint residue — a thin smear of organic matter and mineral salts that the crawlers will graze in turn, that the crust-filaments will absorb, that will become substrate for new frond-growth.

They are not tamed. The people do not herd them or hunt them — there is too little biomass on the Shelf to waste on predation when the crawlers provide what is needed. Instead, the grazers are treated with a kind of quiet reverence. The hunter who brought you the crawler meat, when you asked about them, looked up at the far columns where a pair of grazers moved in silhouette against the upper light, and he said something in a language you have not yet learned, and the woman beside him touched the nearest column with her open palm, and that was the end of the conversation.

## What It Is to Live Here

The people do not speak of the Shelf the way you might speak of a home. They do not say *I live here* in the way that means *I reside here, I am staying here, I will leave.* They say something that, roughly translated, is closer to *I am in the stone.* Or *the stone holds me.* The verb is intransitive and continuous. There is no object. There is no separation between the living and the place.

A child is born on a platform wedged between columns. The membranes of the shelter are woven by the mother and the mother's mother and the mother's mother's mother, each generation adding fibers, tightening the weave, replacing what has mineralized and gone rigid. The child grows up learning the column paths before they learn to walk — or rather, learning to walk *as* the column paths, the movement of their body shaped by the same vertical grammar that shapes the gliders and the crawlers and the slow mineral-vegetation threading through the basalt.

An elder dies on a warm shelf near a vent. The body is wrapped in frond-matting and placed in a crevice in the stone, where the mineral-crust will close over it — not quickly, not in your lifetime, but in time. In geological time. The calcium will be absorbed. The phosphorite. The trace metals. The body becomes the stone becomes the body. No one speaks of this as death.

This is what you see when you look at the people of the Shelf. You see a civilization — if that is the word — that has settled so deeply into its place that the settlement and the place are no longer distinguishable. You see a people who have traded the sky for the stone, distance for depth, the horizon for the slow green pulse of a bioluminescent canopy. And you see, beneath the strangeness, beneath the unfamiliar geometries and the alien metabolism and the absence of everything you assumed was necessary — you see something that looks, if you are very still and very quiet, like what you have been looking for all along.

A way to belong to a place so completely that the place knows your name.
