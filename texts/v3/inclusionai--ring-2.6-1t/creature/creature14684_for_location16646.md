# Inhabitants of the Rootvault

---

## The Mat-Dwellers

The first life you encounter is not a creature but a condition — the root-mat itself, which is not so much a surface as a medium, something halfway between tissue and terrain. It yields underfoot with the softness of a living tongue. Step into it and it closes behind you, erasing your prints within minutes. But move slowly, kneel, and press your hand flat against the warmth, and the mat will part for you — not willingly, but the way a body might accommodate a splinter: by growing around it.

Within this fused substrate lives a population of organisms so small and so numerous that the mat itself might be considered a single colonial animal. They are translucent, limbless, muscular — the things that slide through the root-tissue like thought through a brain. Each is roughly the length of your finger and the diameter of your thumb, boneless and pale as bruised fruit, contracting and expanding in peristaltic waves that push them forward through channels they themselves secrete. They dissolve the root-mat as they move through it, feeding on its mineral-laden cellulose, and the dissolved tissue hardens behind them into a smoother, denser membrane — a scar that is also a reinforcement. They are the cavern's immune system and its digestive tract simultaneously. When you stand still long enough, you will feel them: dozens, then hundreds, sliding against your boots, tasting you with skin that is also stomach. They do not bite. They do not have mouths. They absorb. If you keep your hand in the mat for five minutes, the tissue will grow gently, imperceptibly, around your fingers — not trapping you, exactly. Cradling you. The way a riverbed holds a stone.

Above them — or rather, in a different register of the same system — live the **pustulators**: small, sessile organisms that look like clusters of pale blisters rising from the surface of the mat or the lower trunks. They are sessile for most of their lives, swelling slowly as they accumulate metabolic gas produced by the mat-dwellers beneath them. When the pressure reaches a certain threshold, they burst — silently, wetly — releasing a fine mist of spores and biological vapor that joins the cavern's atmosphere. This mist is what gives the air its thickness, its tactile quality. The pustulators are the cavern's rain. They bloom in clusters of twenty to sixty, and when a cluster detonates in sequence, the effect is something like a slow-motion cough, a soft percussion that causes the hanging filaments overhead to shiver.

Between the pustulators and the canopy, there exists a stratum of life that has no single name and no single shape. It is a middle world — a network of mats, threads, floating membranes, and small, quick things that live in the space between, in the warm convection currents that rise from the magma channel below and carry nutrients upward like a biological elevator system. This is where the cavern is most densely, most chaotically alive.

## The Climbers

They are the first thing you see when you look at a trunk: methodical, deliberate, armored. The **clickers** — named not for any sound they make with intent, but for the rhythmic percussion of their limbs against the wood, which sounds like a seamstress's shears working through heavy fabric. Each is roughly the size of your hand, oval-bodied, encased in overlapping plates of chitin so dark it borders on black, shot through with faint veins of iridescence that flare blue or copper when the moths' light passes across them. They have six legs, each one tipped with a hooked claw that finds purchase in the wood's crystalline grain, and they move with a patience that is almost unbearable to watch. One clicker, crossing a trunk ten meters in height, will take four hours. A hundred clickers crossing the same trunk will move in perfect coordination without once colliding, adjusting their pace and angle with a sensitivity to their neighbors' movement that feels almost telepathic.

They are not, however, a hive organism. Disrupt one — brush it from the trunk with a stick, and it will fall, curl, remain still for a long moment, and then resume climbing the nearest vertical surface. Its rhythm will be different. Slightly off. A half-beat behind its neighbors. And the neighbors will adjust. Not immediately. Not dramatically. But within minutes, the spacing and tempo of the column will have absorbed the disruption as a river absorbs a stone: the water moves around it, the current is unchanged.

What they eat is unclear. They press their ventral plates against the trunk and remain stationary for long periods, sometimes hours, as though feeding through their shells. The trunks, for their part, seem unbothered — or perhaps welcoming. Observations suggest that the clickers' passage stimulates growth. A trunk frequented by a healthy column of clickers will branch sooner and more vigorously than one left untouched. Whether this is chemistry — secretions from the clickers' plates that act as a growth hormone — or something more systemic, a kind of biological negotiation, is impossible to determine from the outside. What is clear is that the clickers are not parasites. They are participants. They click their way up the trunks the way a heartbeat moves through a chest: not imposed on the body, but generated by it.

## The Moths

You must understand: these are not moths in any terrestrial sense. They are small — the size of a thumbnail — and they are luminous. A cold, blue-white bioluminescence that pulses gently, in no discernible rhythm, each creature its own metronome. Their wings are not membranous but crystalline — thin plates of a chitinous material so clear and so precisely angled that each wing refracts the cavern's ambient amber light into brief, prismatic flashes, so that a single moth in flight becomes a brief, localized rain event, throwing tiny spectrums of color across the trunk and lattice surfaces before folding them away.

In aggregate, they are overwhelming. Thousands. Tens of thousands. They gather in the canopy in clouds so dense that the boundary between organism and atmosphere dissolves — a shimmering, shifting mass that moves as a single body, responding to stimuli no observer can identify. When something disturbs the canopy — a branch falls, a large creature passes through, the distant jaw-boom from the ceiling — the cloud contracts inward like a pupil responding to light, a single contraction of a billion luminous wings, and then, seconds later, expands outward again in a slow, radiant pulse, as though the cavern has inhaled and exhaled light.

They do not land. Or rather, they do, but only briefly — a moth will alight on a filament for a fraction of a second, pressing its proboscis against the tissue, and then lift again. The feeding is constant, collective, and silent at the individual scale. Together, the sound of ten thousand crystalline wings is a faint, high ringing — not quite a tone, more a pressure, the way a plucked string vibrates in other strings nearby without touching them.

Within the moth-clouds, there are individuals that are larger — two or three times the size of a common moth, darker in color, their glow a deeper blue, almost violet. These appear to move with a different intent, cutting through the clouds in straight lines while the common moths swarm around them. Whether they are leaders, mates, or something else entirely is unclear. They do not appear to communicate with the others. They simply move, and the others part.

## The Beetles

Fist-sized. Armored in carapaces of iridescent chitin that shimmer with colors that do not belong to any terrestrial palette — greens that shift to gold, blues that shift to ultraviolet, reds that seem to exist in a wavelength your eye can almost reach but cannot quite hold. They move in columns along the trunk surfaces, dozens or hundreds strong, each beetle spaced precisely two body-lengths from the next, traveling in the same direction at the same pace. The columns move vertically as often as horizontally, ascending trunks in spiraling formations and crossing lattice-bridges in staggered waves.

The coordination is eerie. No beetle appears to lead. No beetle appears to follow. They move like a single organism that has distributed its body across dozens of individuals, and watching a column cross from one trunk to another — flowing across a lattice bridge without hesitation, without collision, without a single beetle breaking stride — is one of the most disturbing and beautiful things in the cavern. It suggests a communication system that operates below the threshold of observation: pheromone trails too faint to smell, substrate vibrations too low to hear, light-patterns too subtle to see. Or something else. Something you do not have the biology to receive.

When a column encounters a dead branch or a patch of damaged wood, it does not detour. It passes through. The beetles at the front of the column begin to feed, dissolving the dead wood with secretions from their mandibles, and the column behind them flows over and around the feeding beetles without pause. The column does not slow. The feeding beetles are simply absorbed back into the flow once they are finished, rejoining the rear ranks as though they had never stopped.

At night — if the cavern has nights, and it does not, exactly, though the magma-channel's glow dims in cycles that might correspond to something — the beetles' carapaces emit a faint glow of their own. Not bioluminescence, but some kind of phosphorescence, a stored light that bleeds out slowly. A column of feeding beetles in the dim phase is a river of cold fire moving silently up a trunk.

## The Large Ones

You see it three times. You are never close enough to see it clearly.

The first time: a shadow against the diffuse light of the upper vault, four limbs extended, wings — no, not wings, *membranes*, wet and dark and heavy — folding and unfolding with a slow deliberateness that makes no aerodynamic sense. Too large to fly. Not flying. *Pouring.* The word returns to you unbidden. The creature is pouring itself through the air the way liquid pours through a gap — the space it occupies simply becoming its body, and then being left behind. It passes between two trunk-lattices fifty meters above you and does not touch either.

The second time: closer. You see the undersurface of one membrane-wing and it is veined, translucent, faintly bioluminescent — a dark purple glow that pulses in a rhythm you recognize, belatedly, as a heartbeat. The limbs are long and multi-jointed, folded against the body during glides but extending with a sudden, boneless grace during turns, splaying outward like the ribs of an opening umbrella. The head — you see it for less than a second — is featureless. Smooth. Rounded. No eyes, no mouth that you can discern. Just a blank, pale surface, like a stone worn by water. It is the most disturbing thing about the creature: the face of something that has no need to look back at you.

The third time: it is descending. Not gliding, not pouring, but descending, slowly, deliberately, as though choosing to land. You hear the wet snapping of its wings and it is unmistakably organic, unmistakably *large*, like canvas being torn by something very strong and very patient. It passes behind a lattice of trunks and you lose it. You wait. It does not return.

You hear it again, later — a shriek, or something adjacent to a shriek, a sound below language, a frequency that you feel in your sternum and your teeth and the fluid of your inner ear. It is not a roar. It is not a call. It is a vibration that the cavern's architecture has been shaped, over time, to contain and amplify, and it resonates through everything — the trunks, the lattices, the filaments, the root-mat beneath your feet — and for a full five seconds, the entire cavern is the creature's voice.

Then it stops. Then everything resumes.

## The Jaw

In the upper vault — thirty, forty, fifty meters above the highest trunks — the root-mat thickens and knits into a tangle of tissue and tendon that hangs from the stone ribs like an enormous, upside-down throat. It does not move. It is always open, in the sense that it is always *capable* of opening: a seam in the tissue that is ordinarily held shut by the pressure of surrounding growth, but which periodically, without warning or pattern, splits along its full length — perhaps two meters, perhaps five — and reveals, for half a second, a wet red darkness and rows of mineral-white structures that are too large and too evenly spaced to be bone and too curved to be wood.

They might be teeth. They might be something the cavern grew for a purpose you cannot guess. When the seam closes, the sound is enormous — a percussive boom that, unlike the large creature's shriek, is physical. It moves through the air, through the root-mat, through the trunks, through your body. Your ribs vibrate. Your teeth chatter. The moths scatter. The clickers freeze. The beetle columns halt. For five to ten seconds, the entire cavern holds its breath — and then the filaments tremble, the moths regroup, the clickers resume their patient ascent, and the rhythm rebuilds, as though the boom were not an interruption but a downbeat.

You never see it eat. You never see anything enter it. But the cavern is older than your understanding, and the jaw is older than the light that reaches it, and you do not presume to know what it is for. The cavern has swallowed the builders' arch and the builders' walls and the builders' channels. It has swallowed light and sound and time. Perhaps it eats something you lack the word for.

## The Ghosts of the Builders

They are not here. They left — or were consumed, or became substrate, or evolved into the clicker-columns, or simply dissolved into the biological economy of the vault in a way that makes "gone" and "present" meaningless in this lightless, warm, breathing architecture.

But their work speaks. The low wall with its parallel grooves — tooled with precision, patient, deliberate. Someone shaped this stone with something harder than stone, and the grooves are still clean after uncountable seasons of biological growth. The channel cut into the floor runs in a perfect straight line for meters before vanishing beneath root-mass. It is not a river channel. There has never been water here. It may have been a conduit for something else — gas, heat, electrical charge, something the builders understood about this mountain that you do not. The arch, with its keystone cracked and held together by the tree that grew through it, is a handshake between two kinds of engineering: one that builds against entropy, and one that builds by consuming it.

You find no bones. No tools. No inscriptions, no symbols, no graves. Only these small, quiet, stubborn marks — the last words of a civilization that built a home inside a mountain and then, gradually, beautifully, became its compost. The clickers move over the wall with their patient click-click-click, and their chitin plates catch in the grooves as though the grooves were made for them. Perhaps they were. Perhaps the builders designed the grooves the way the cavern designed the clickers: not with intent, but with shape. Not as architects, but as ancestors.

None of this answers the question of what lived here before. None of this tells you what the builders looked like, or spoke, or called themselves. But the groove in the stone fits your thumb. The arch, braced from within by living wood, still stands. And if you press your palm flat against the root-mat and hold very still, you can feel beneath it a faint, rhythmic warmth — not geological, not biological, but something adjacent to both — as though the mountain itself were remembering, in its slow and mineral way, a language it once spoke fluently and has since translated into growth.

The Rootvault does not need you. It did not need the builders, either.

It is building itself.

It has always been building itself.

And when you finally leave — climbing back through the narrowing passages, the air thinning, the hum fading through the soles of your boots — you will carry with you the sound: that single, continuous, all-encompassing roar of ten billion organisms performing the work of a world in the space where a ceiling used to be.

You will hear it in your chest for days afterward.

You will not be able to tell where it ends and where you begin.
