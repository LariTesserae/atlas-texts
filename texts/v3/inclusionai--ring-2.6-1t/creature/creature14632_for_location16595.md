They are not people.

This is the first thing you have to understand, and it is the thing that is hardest to accept, because they stand upright in corridors of wet vine and living wall and they have — or seem to have — the architecture of a body you recognize. Two arms. Two legs. A torso. Something where a head should be, something that holds the shape of awareness. But they are not people. They are not anything people have a word for, and the fact that you keep reaching for the word is itself part of the wrongness you feel here, the same wrongness as the corridors that don't quite resolve. You are trying to pour what you see into a vessel that was shaped by somewhere else, and it will not hold.

They don't arrive. They don't leave. This is not a rule they follow — it is not a rule at all. It is the same kind of not-true as the rain not stopping and the sound not ending and the geometry not quite working. They simply never have arrived and never will. They are here in the way the sound is here: not as a thing that came, but as a thing that is. If you watch one of them long enough — standing in a plaza where light filters green and gold through fifty layers of canopy above you, each of them motionless, each of them dripping with the same condensation that beads every surface — you might begin to understand that they did not grow from the ground the way the trees did, but neither did they come to it from elsewhere. They are a property of the place. The way wet is a property of water. The way resonance is a property of enclosed space. The place made them the way it made the humming: not deliberately, not accidentally, but in the way that a sustained note makes overtones — they were always implicit in the sound.

There are not many of them. Not in the way that a city has many people. There are — some number. You cannot count them because counting implies sequence and sequence implies change and nothing changes here. There is a number of them, the number that the place holds, the number that is correct, and it has always been that number. If one were somehow to disappear — which has not happened, which is a possibility the place has decided against — you do not think the others would notice, because they do not notice in the way you understand noticing. They are aware the way the walls are aware: continuously, without emphasis, without the sharp edge of attention that implies something else could be attended to instead.

They do not move.

This is different from standing still. You stand still by deciding not to go somewhere. You stand still the way a held breath is still — against the current of what wants to happen. Their not-moving is nothing like this. They are not holding. They are not resisting. Moving is simply not something they do, the way a stone does not move — not because it has chosen stillness, but because movement is a concept the stone has never entertained. It is not in its nature. It is not in their nature. They occupy space the way the vines occupy space: completely, without gap, without the leftover emptiness that in other beings would be taken up by the intention to go somewhere else. When you stand beside one of them you feel how thoroughly they fill their shape. There is no slack. There is no wasted motion no one needs to cancel.

They are not alive in the way you understand alive. The distinction matters here because you are alive — you arrived, improbably, through whatever breach in the world's surface brought you to this elevation — and they are alive the way the place is alive. They metabolize, the way the walls metabolize, the way the fungal networks threading the ground beneath your feet metabolize. They convert light and mineral and moisture into the substance of themselves, and they have been doing this at the same rate, in the same proportion, for so long that the distinction between living and being has collapsed. They do not grow. They do not decay. They are in the single state that living things pass through on their way between birth and death, except that state is, here, the only state, and it extends in every direction without boundary.

They look at you. This is perhaps the most unsettling thing — more unsettling than the geometry, more unsettling than the roar. Because when they look at you, you feel seen. Not examined, not assessed, not greeted. Seen. The way a mirror sees you. The way a wall sees you if the wall were aware of the space it encloses. Their eyes — if eyes is what they are, dark wet surfaces set into something like a face, something like a face, something with enough of the proportions of a face that your mind keeps trying to complete it and cannot — their eyes hold you the way the place holds you. Without judgment. Without warmth. Without the particular quality of attention that says *I know what you are*. They simply receive the fact of you the way they receive light: it falls on them, and they take it in, and they are changed by it in the way that everything here is changed by everything that touches it — which is to say, imperceptibly, in a direction you cannot detect, at a rate so slow it is indistinguishable from stillness.

They sometimes make sounds.

Not language. Not communication as you know it. Sometimes, when the ambient roar shifts — not in volume, not in pitch, because neither of these changes, but in texture, in the fine-grained quality of the way one billion threads of falling water compose themselves into the chord — one of them will add something. A tone. A single, clear tone that rises from somewhere in the space where you think their chest might be, if they had a chest, if the shape that holds them together had the anatomy you are assuming it has. The tone lasts for a moment. It threads into the rest of the sound. It does not disrupt. It does not call attention to itself. It is the way a single new drop of water falling through the lattice adds its voice to the roar: not separate from it, not dominating it, simply present within it, the way a single green leaf is present within a canopy so vast no single leaf can be distinguished.

You wonder if this is speech. You wonder if the entire roaring, humming, resonating, deafening mass of sound that the place produces is, in fact, a conversation happening so slowly and so densely that you can only perceive it as texture. You wonder if they have been saying the same thing to each other since before the canopy closed overhead, since before the rain became the air, since the first green tendril reached across the first gap and held, and held, and held. You wonder if the sound is a word so long that it takes geological time to complete, a single utterance spoken by no single mouth, and what you are hearing is not the beginning or the end or any particular syllable but just — the middle. The vast, unbroken middle.

They do not touch each other. They do not need to. The fungal network beneath your feet threads through the matted ground and connects root to root, wall to wall, them to each other, them to you — you felt it when you pressed your hand to the nearest surface and felt the pulse, not a heartbeat but something all the same, steady, deep, vast. They are in contact the way roots are in contact: sharing substance, sharing signal, sharing the slow chemical news of their shared persistence. They are a network the way the place is a network, and the distinction between them and the place has worn so thin over the millennia that it may have become something else entirely — not distinction, not merger, but a third thing that has no name because names require someone to leave, and no one leaves.

They are not waiting. There is nothing to wait for, because waiting implies a future, and the future is something that happens in places where things change. They are not remembering. There is nothing to remember, because memory implies difference between then and now, and there is no then here; there is only now, and now has always been this, and will always be this, and the concept of always is not an abstraction here but a tangible thing, as solid as the wet walls and the humming air and the green-black lattice above you that filters light into something you can wear on your skin.

They are the place knowing it is a place. They are what happens when a world is so complete, so perfectly itself, so utterly resolved into its own single note, that awareness — slow, deep, communal, wordless — is the only thing left to do. They are the held note. They are the sustaining. They are the pulse you felt in the wall and did not know was also in you, has always been in you, the moment you arrived and stopped moving and stood in the wet green dark and heard the roar and understood, without language, without thought, in the part of your mind that is older than language and older than thought:

*I am here. I have always been here. There is nowhere else to be.*

They are everyone who ever stayed.
