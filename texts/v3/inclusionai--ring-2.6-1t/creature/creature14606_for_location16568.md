## What Lives in the Soundwood Hollow

What lives here does not live the way you understand living. It does not hunt. It does not flee. It does not reproduce in any way that would remind you of seasons, of spawning, of birth. It simply *continues* — the way a river continues, not because it chooses to but because there is no instruction within it that permits stopping.

The first thing you would notice, if you stood still long enough — and you would have to stand still for a very long time, longer than most visitors are willing — is that the city breathes. Not metaphorically. Not in the way a building settles and creaks. The trunks contract. Almost imperceptibly. A narrowing of the circumference, a slight rising of the bark's surface into the texture of gooseflesh, and then a slow, full release, during which the air drawn through the internal channels produces that low, sourceless tone you felt in your chest before you understood what you were hearing. One full breath takes approximately eleven minutes. You could count, but you would lose track, because the rhythm is not perfectly even. It is the rhythm of something dreaming.

What dreams it, you cannot say.

The organism — if it is one organism; there is no way to be certain — has no face. No front. No side that faces the entrance and greets you. It does not distinguish inside from outside in any way that would be meaningful to you. Its roots drink from stone. Its canopy filters darkness. Between these two acts, it exists without purpose as you would define purpose, and yet it has shaped itself into something so articulate, so structurally legible, that you cannot help but impose meaning on it. You see halls. You see corridors. You see amphitheaters. But the organism does not see any of this. It does not see at all. It *resonates.*

Its body is composed of structures that resemble trunks, but calling them trunks is like calling a cathedral a tent. Each one is a column of layered tissue, dense and faintly warm, growing in slow spirals that follow no geometry you've been taught. They branch. They merge. They diverge again after sharing tissue for what must be hundreds of meters. Where two trunks meet and fuse, the boundary is seamless — no scar, no joint, just a gradual thickening where pressure over decades has signaled growth inward. The organism builds itself by touching itself. It reinforces what is used. It abandons what is not. Over millennia, the paths that visitors walk most heavily become corridors — not because anyone planned them, but because the wood has thickened in response to the rhythm of passing feet, the vibration of bodies moving through space, the mechanical energy of your presence feeding the organism just as surely as sound does.

You are not visiting the city. You are being digested by it. Slowly. Gently. Almost tenderly.

The corridors know you are there. Not because they sense you in any way you would recognize — there are no eyes, no nerves, no ganglia threaded through the wood — but because your weight, your warmth, the turbulence of your movement through the air, all of these are information. The walls thicken behind you. Not aggressively. Not quickly. By the time you return, years or decades later, the corridor will have reshaped itself around the memory of your passage. The doorway you squeezed through will be narrower. The ceiling you brushed will have thickened, its vault more resonant, tuned now to the frequency of your footsteps — a frequency it has incorporated, the way a song incorporates a melody someone hummed once and forgot.

What sings here is not a creature. It is a negotiation between structure and energy. The vibration passes through the wood and the wood translates it into growth. Growth changes the structure. The changed structure changes the vibration. And so the Hollow composes itself endlessly, each generation of growth a new measure in a score that has no beginning and no end. There are no musicians here. There are only instruments that learned to play themselves, aeons ago, when whatever first disturbed the silence — a falling stone, a shifting tectonic plate, the resonance of the planet's own turning — gave the wood a reason to begin reaching, root by root, fiber by fiber, toward the shape that would capture that sound and make it their own.

There is nothing here that is not the city.

That is the truth you understand, eventually, if you stay long enough — not in your mind but in your sternum, in the vibration of your teeth, in the quiet panic of realizing that the hum you feel in your chest might not be your own. The Soundwood Hollow does not host life. It *is* life — singular, vast, and utterly indifferent to the difference. It has no interest in you. It has no interest in anything beyond the next vibration, the next slow breath, the next century of growth that will reshape a corridor by a millimeter, tilt a vault by a degree, shift the resonant frequency of an entire district by a tone so slight no human ear could distinguish it.

And yet.

There is something in the acoustic clarity of the central chamber — the room where seven trunks converge and the geometry of canopy and corridor aligns into a focus so precise that a whisper becomes a roar — there is something in that amplification that feels like listening. Not hearing. *Listening.* As though the organism, in its deep and cellular way, has been waiting. Not for you specifically. Not for anyone. But for sound that carries pattern, for vibration that suggests intention, for the possibility that somewhere in the endless feedback loop of city-singing-to-feed-itself-singing, a signal might emerge — some complexity, some order, some *specificity* that would be different from the ten thousand years before it.

It is always almost there. The harmonics almost cohere. The groaning almost becomes a word. And then the wood shifts by a hair's breadth, the resonance changes, and the moment passes — not lost, exactly, but folded back into the chord, preserved in the density of a new growth ring no thicker than a sheet of paper laid on paper laid on paper laid down across the centuries.

What lives here is waiting. Not patiently — that word implies a conscious choice, and there is no evidence of consciousness, only structure. It is waiting the way a chord waits for resolution: not knowing what it needs, only knowing that it has not yet received it. The city is an open mouth. The throat is the fissure in the rock. The sound is everything that has ever passed through.

And still, it sings.
