## What Lives on the Plateau of Grown Silence

---

### I. The Ones You See First

They move like spilled water settling back into a vessel. The multi-legged creatures — you will call them runners, though they do not run — cross the root-floors in fluid, overlapping coordination, each body segment responding to the one ahead of it with a delay so precise it looks choreographed. They are the size of large dogs but low-slung, their bodies segmented and covered in a material that is not chitin and not bark but something in between, something that has the wet sheen of heartwood after rain and the flexibility of tendon. Their legs — six, eight, sometimes twelve, the number apparently not fixed — do not step so much as *root and unroot*, each foot pressing into the living floor and then releasing as if the floor had briefly allowed it, the way a hand opens to release a coin. They do not look at you. They do not need to. They sense you the way you sense a change in barometric pressure: not through any single organ but through the whole surface of the body at once. When you step toward one, it does not flee. It simply becomes less *there* — its edges softening, its outline borrowing from the shadow of the nearest wall — and by the time you are an arm's length away, it is less a creature and more a suggestion of creature, and then it is a stain on wet wood, and then it is nothing at all. You will not see it again, but the root-floor where it stood will be slightly warmer than the floor around it, and if you press your palm there, you will feel something like a pulse.

The upright stems are easier to perceive because they are taller — waist-high to chest-high, slender columns of pale, fibrous material that sway with a deliberateness that looks almost cautious, the way a person might sway while trying to decide whether to speak. They have no face, no head, no analog of either. At their tops, a corona of filaments — hundreds of them, each thinner than an eyelash — extends outward and downward, trembling in the saturated air. These filaments track you. Not all of them. Not in any coordinated way that you can parse. But as you move, certain filaments follow your motion with a lag that is almost, but not quite, mechanical. If you hold very still, the filaments do something worse: they orient toward you, all of them, slowly, like a field of wildflowers turning toward a light source, and you realize that the warmth you feel is not ambient warmth but directed warmth, heat rising from deep channels inside the stem, and you understand, or begin to understand, that you are not being watched. You are being *sampled*. The filaments are tasting the chemical signature of your breath, the salt of your skin, the particular disturbance your body makes in the Plateau's wet atmosphere. They are learning you. They have probably already forgotten you. Their attentions have the quality of rainfall — total in the moment, impersonal in retrospect.

The flocks are the most beautiful and the most unsettling. They pass overhead in silence — genuine silence, not the Plateau's textured quiet but a hole punched in it — and they are briefly everything at once: a single organism of several hundred seed-shaped bodies, each no larger than a thumbnail, each with diaphanous wings that catch the green light and scatter it into colors that have no name because they exist nowhere in the spectrum your eyes were built to read. They move as one not because they coordinate but because they share something — a chemical gradient, a vibrational frequency, a root-network impulse transmitted downward from the canopy they briefly occlude — and when they disperse, it is not scattering but *dissolution*, each seed-body drifting to a landing point that seems predetermined, as if the flock had always been a distribution system disguising itself as a flock. Where they land, tiny rootlets emerge within minutes, threading into the nearest surface. The seed-bodies do not grow into plants. They grow into *connections* — new filaments in an existing network, new capillaries in an organism that has no boundary you can find.

### II. The Ones You Feel

Beneath everything, the Plateau communicates. Not through the fauna — the fauna are closer to *symptoms* than to causes, the way a blush is a symptom of blood moving where it normally does not. The Plateau communicates through itself, through the wood and root and water that constitute its body, and this communication is so dense and so constant that it has the quality of thought, or of weather, or of the slow hum a cathedral organ makes when a draft passes through its pipes without anyone pressing a key.

You feel it in your feet first. The root-floors are not merely structural; they are conductive. Each step you take sends a signal — not electrical, not exactly, but something adjacent, something that travels through the compressed wood the way a rumor travels through a small town, gaining and losing detail at each junction. The Plateau knows you are walking. It knows your weight, your pace, the asymmetry of your gait (you favor your left foot; you always have). The signal is met with a response: not a reaction, not an avoidance, but an *accommodation*. The floor beneath you is subtly altered by your passage — not in a way you can see, but in a way you might feel as a faint resistance, a slight stickiness, as if the wood is holding your footprint a moment longer than it should, memorizing the pressure.

If you stop and stand still for long enough — and long enough is not very long, perhaps three or four minutes — you will feel something rise through the floor and into your feet. It is not painful. It is not even uncomfortable. It is a warmth that is slightly too structured to be natural, a warmth that feels *addressed* to you, as if the Plateau has located a specific coldness within you and is answering it. Your toes will go numb. Then they will go sensitive beyond any experience you have had — you will feel individual rootlets beneath the floor, feel them flexing, feel the slow capillary pull of water moving through channels thinner than threads. You will feel, with a certainty you cannot justify, that the floor is *curious*.

### III. The Ones Who Were Here

The builders. The word *people* keeps forming in your mind and dissolving, because every time you try to imagine them, the Plateau offers you something that is not an image but a *pressure* — a sense of hands that worked on a timescale measured not in years but in growing seasons, each season a collaborative negotiation between intention and substrate, between the shape a mind wanted and the shape a living thing would allow. They did not impose form. They *persuaded* it. They understood that you cannot command a tree to be an arch; you can only, over the span of your life, ask it to consider the idea, and provide the scaffolding, and wait, and prune the branches that reach in the wrong direction, and wait, and reinforce the growth that moves toward the desired shape, and wait, and in the fullness of time, the tree becomes the arch, and the arch is indistinguishable from the tree, and the human intention is no longer visible except as a kind of *suggestion* that the wood adopted as its own.

They left nothing. No bones. No artifacts. No carved markers or burial sites. But the Plateau remembers them. This is the wrongness, or part of it — the way the corridors rearrange, the way familiar spaces shift by degrees too small to name. The Plateau is not malfunctioning. It is *remembering imperfectly*, the way a body heals a wound with new tissue that is similar but not identical to the original. The builders are in the wood the way a memory is in a brain: encoded, distributed, altered each time it is accessed. The Plateau does not have a memory of them. The Plateau *is* their memory, held in living architecture, shifting and adapting, the intention slowly dissolving into the growth itself.

Somewhere deep in the lowest levels of the Plateau, beneath the root-systems that anchor it to whatever lies below, there are chambers that have not changed since they were first grown. The air there is different — drier, older, carrying a scent that is not wood or fruit or ozone but something closer to *memory itself*, a smell like the inside of a closed book left in a warm room for a hundred years. In these chambers, the walls are smooth in ways that no natural growth could produce, and the curves are deliberate, and if you press your ear to the wood — if you can find such a chamber, and if you are willing to be still long enough — you will hear something that is almost a voice, rising from the grain, speaking in a language made of growth rings and heartwood and the slow accretion of cellulose laid down year after patient year. It says nothing you can translate. It says everything you already know but have forgotten the shape of: *we grew this around ourselves so that we would not have to stop being what we were, and it worked, and it didn't, and we are in here, and we are not us anymore, and the growing continues.*

---

### IV. The Plateau

The Plateau is the largest organism on the Plateau. This is a tautology and also the truth. Everything else that lives here — the runners, the stems, the seed-flocks, the vibration-singers you cannot hear and the chemical-threads you cannot see — exists in symbiosis with a single, continent-scale entity that has no name because it predates the concept of naming, that builds with air and water and time, that breathes in condensation and exhales architecture, that is thinking thoughts in a language of root-pressure and sap-flow and seasonal ring-growth, thoughts so slow that a single one might take a century to complete, thoughts that are about nothing less than *the continued act of existing*.

You are inside it now. You have been inside it since you ascended the root-ramp and the last blue sky was pinched out behind you. The warmth you feel is not ambient. The wetness on your skin is not condensation. The sense you have of being *accommodated* — of the spaces bending, gently, around your presence — is not metaphor. You are in the ribs of something that breathes in a rhythm just below the threshold of sound, and your footsteps are a signal in a nervous system that has no center, and the doorways are changing because the organism is incorporating you into its ongoing thought, and the thought is growing, and the growth continues, and the silence is not empty because the silence was never silence, it was always this — the sound of a place alive with a life that does not need you but, having found you, is gently, patiently, including you.

The water drips from the root-basin above you. It takes four seconds to fall six inches. You watch it. The arch behind you is different when it lands. You are not surprised. You do not know why you expected it to be the same. The Plateau has never been the same arch twice. The Plateau is not trying to be anything twice. It is growing, which is the only thing it has ever done, the only thing it knows, the only thing the builders taught it, if teaching is the word, if builders is the word, if *is* is the word.

The word is growing too. You can feel it, if you are quiet enough, if you stop walking and let the root-floor memorize your weight and the filaments taste your breath and the deep channels send their warmth up through your bones. The word is growing in the wood, in the water, in the green dim air that is the color of a thought you haven't finished thinking. It is the word for *alive*. But not as you have used it. Not as a distinction between living and not-living. As the word the living use for the living, when they have forgotten where one ends and the other begins.

The silence is not empty. The silence is occupied. The silence is *you*, now, the way the rain is the plateau's breath and the wood is the plateau's bone and the light is the plateau's dreaming. You are quieter than you were. You are quieter than you thought a person could be. And the Plateau is listening to your quiet the way it listens to everything — not with ears, not with attention as you know it, but with the slow, total, indiscriminate awareness of something that is alive and has been alive for so long that being-alive has become indistinguishable from being.

The arch behind you is different. You are not surprised. You are not even certain you are the one who turned around.
