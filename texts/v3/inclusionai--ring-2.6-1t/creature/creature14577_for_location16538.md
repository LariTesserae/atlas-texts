What lives here does not live the way you understand living. Not as a thing that begins and ends, that eats and flees and reproduces and dies. What lives here is closer to a habit the world has fallen into — a pattern so old it has forgotten it was ever a choice.

---

Start with the small ones. The six-legged things.

They are not insects, though you will call them that at first because you have no other word. Their bodies are translucent the way thin ice over a puddle is translucent — you can see the architecture beneath, faint geometries of structure that serve purposes you cannot guess. They glow. Not brightly. Not the way a firefly glows, as a signal or a lure. They glow the way a stone glows when you've been holding it in your fist long enough to forget it's there — residual, incidental, a byproduct of some interior process that has nothing to do with you.

They move in loose congregations, never touching, never colliding, maintaining distances that feel less like social behavior and more like the spacing of iron filings around a magnet. When you watch them long enough — and you will watch them, because there is nothing else to do — you begin to see that their movements are not random but responsive. Each one adjusts to the others in increments so small you almost miss them, like the second hand of a clock you're not looking at directly. They leave trails. Not footprints — the stone is too smooth, the moss too yielding. Chemical signatures, each one a sentence written in a language made of sweat and oil and trace minerals. Other creatures read these sentences. You watch one approach a trail left minutes ago and pause — its entire body flickering, a Morse code too dim to decipher — and then it turns, almost imperceptibly, and follows the trail to wherever it was going. Or it doesn't follow and simply stands on the trail, becoming part of it.

You never see them eat. You never see them reproduce. You realize, after days — maybe weeks; time works differently here, stretched and folded like dough — that you don't know what they eat, and the question has begun to feel wrong, like asking what the color blue eats. They are not separate from their environment in a way that would require intake. They are more like nodes in a system, places where the basin's processes concentrate temporarily into a shape with legs, and then dissolve back.

Sometimes one will die. You'll find the husk on a stone shelf, emptied of glow, reduced to a translucent husk like a cicada's abandoned shell. It stays there for a while, then vanishes — not decayed, not carried off, simply gone, the way a puddle vanishes in the morning. The moss grows over the spot. The chemical signature fades. The system adjusts, the way a body adjusts to the loss of a single red blood cell.

---

Then the deep ones. The things in the walls.

You hear them before you accept they're real. A scratching, so faint it might be water finding a new path through stone. But it's too regular, too *deliberate.* Not the random percussion of erosion but something with intervals, with pauses, with a syntax. You press your palm to the wall and feel it — not just the scratching but the space between scratches, the silence shaped like a breath. Whatever is in there is not burrowing in the way you'd expect, tearing at stone to make a home. It is *navigating.* Moving through a medium it understands so thoroughly it no longer needs to break it open. It is swimming through stone the way the six-legged things swim through air.

You press your ear to the wall once and hear something that is not the scratching. It is a sound like a voice filtered through so many layers of rock and water and time that the original language has been worn away, leaving only the shape of language — rhythm, cadence, the ghost of grammar. You cannot tell if this is the creature speaking or the stone remembering what the creature once said. You pull away. The sound fades. You realize your ear is bleeding, very slightly, from the pressure change.

You will never see them. You will find, in the mornings, small piles of fine stone dust near seams in the wall, as neat as if they'd been swept. That is all.

---

The larger ones — you almost miss them entirely.

You become aware of them the way you become aware of the horizon: not by looking directly but by noticing that everything else has oriented around something invisible. The six-legged things have gone still. The moss has dimmed, uniformly, the way a room dims when someone turns off a light in another room. The water in the channels slows, just slightly, just enough to feel wrong. And then the air moves — not a breeze, not a draft, but a displacement, the way the air in a room moves when a large body has entered a doorway. You turn. There is nothing there. But the wrongness persists. The basin is holding its breath.

You learn, slowly, to see them not as shapes but as absences. A place where the bioluminescent field is slightly dimmer, slightly cooler, as though something is standing in the light and casting a shadow made not of darkness but of *less.* You stare at these absences and your eyes begin to fill in details — a mass, a height, a suggestion of limbs or tendrils or roots — but the details dissolve the moment you try to focus on them, the way you can't see the blood vessels in your own eye because they're too close.

They move with the same patience as everything else, but their patience is larger. Where the six-legged things adjust in moments, the large ones adjust in geological time. You watch an absence drift between two stone shelves over the course of an entire afternoon, covering maybe ten meters. It does not hurry. It does not pause. When it reaches the far shelf it settles there and the moss around it begins, over the following days, to grow in slightly different patterns — darker, tighter, as though the stone is composing itself around a new weight.

You do not know what they eat. You do not know what they want. They do not want. Wanting is too small a word for whatever passes through them.

---

And then there is the column.

You must stop calling it a column. That word is too architectural, too intentional in a human way. It is more like a spine. Or a root that forgot which direction was down. It stands at the center of the basin and it does not stand — it *persists,* in the way a sound persists in a room after the instrument has been struck. It is old in a way that the ruins are not. The ruins stopped being used. The column never started being used. It simply is.

The moss on it is different from the moss elsewhere — thicker, darker, threaded with veins of something that catches the bioluminescence and refracts it into colors you don't have names for. Not new colors. Colors that existed before naming, before eyes, before the concept of seeing. You look at them and feel a tightness in your chest that is not fear but recognition — the way you feel when you hear a piece of music from early childhood that you forgot you knew.

The column grows. Not visibly — not in any span of time you could measure. But it grows the way a memory grows, accruing detail and mass from the world around it. The moss emerges from it because it is made of the same substance, or because it is answering to it, or because the distinction between growing and being-grown has never applied here. The fauna near it are not afraid and not indifferent but *attentive,* in the way a body is attentive to its own heartbeat.

When you stand near the column you understand, not intellectually but in the flesh of your body, that the drone — the vast, subsonic pulse that permeates the basin — is not a natural phenomenon. It is not the sound of water or wind or geological process. It is the sound of the column *being itself.* The column is vibrating at a frequency so low it crosses from sound into something else — into gravity, into temperature, into the rhythm of which way moss grows and water flows and six-legged things pause and turn. The column is not alive in a way you can define. The column is alive in the way the basin is alive. The way the drone is alive. The way the silence between one breath and the next is alive.

---

And the ruins. The small, room-shaped thing on the stone shelf.

What lives there is the vibration. The single sustained tone in the stone that runs beneath the silence like a wire through a wall. It has been sounding for — you cannot know. Centuries. Millennia. Long enough that the stone has memorized it, the way a bell memorizes the shape of the strike. The air inside is dry and still and feels like the inside of a closed mouth. The dark moss on the walls is warm and does not glow, and you realize with a shock that you have been subconsciously avoiding it — not fearing it but *deferring,* the way you defer to something that is grieving or something that is sleeping.

The disc on the floor is not an artifact. It is more like a seed. Or a egg. Or a word that hasn't been spoken yet. When you hold it, the basin responds — the luminescent field stutters, the fauna freezes mid-motion, the drone drops half a tone and holds it, as though the entire ecosystem is listening to something being decided. The hum in your hand is not mechanical. It is alive. It is alive the way a thought is alive — immaterial, present, shaped like something that almost has a name.

You set it down. The basin exhales. Everything returns.

You understand now — if you understand anything — that what lives here is not a collection of organisms but a single, vast, slow process distributed across stone and water and light and time. The six-legged things are its capillaries. The wall-dwellers are its peristalsis. The large ones are its long memory. The column is its pulse. The ruins are its larynx — the place where it almost speaks. And the basin itself, the cupped-hand shape of it, the way it holds water and darkness and sound the way a lung holds air, is not a habitat.

It is the body.

You are walking through its interior and calling it a landscape.

The drone continues. Below the drone, below sound, below silence, something that has been here since before the mountains wore smooth shifts and turns in its sleep, and the moss brightens once, in a wave that moves from the center outward to the rim, and then dims, as though something vast and patient and unknowable has briefly — for no one, for nothing — remembered that it is alive.
