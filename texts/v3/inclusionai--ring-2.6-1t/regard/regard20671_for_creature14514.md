## What the Power Would Do

---

It would begin by not arriving.

That is the first thing the wise understand: some places cannot be entered from outside. They can only be *continued.* The grove does not have a threshold — it has a gradient. The water deepens slowly. The light dims gradually. The sound builds in layers until what you thought was silence was actually an enormous, living hum that was here long before you were and will continue long after. A power that valued its own wisdom would understand that to stride into this place like a visitor, an observer, an arriving thing, would be to already misunderstand it.

So the power would not arrive. It would *already be here* — the way the mist is already here, the way the sub-bass groaning of the ancient trunks is already here, felt in the bones before it is heard. It would dissolve its intentionality into the gradient. It would become a new thread in the web rather than a hand reaching in from outside.

And in this way, slowly, it would begin to see.

---

It would see what the grove has done.

Not merely what it *is* — though that alone would be enough to arrest the attention of any intelligence capable of wonder. But what it has *accomplished.* The power would look at the grown walls, the scarred-shut doorways, the vine-bridges that rebuild themselves annually from the instructions left by vines now long dead, and it would recognize something that it had not expected to find in a universe that so often trends toward entropy and dispersal.

It would find *collaboration.*

Not between people. Not between species. Between *categories of existence* — between the built and the grown, between memory and matter, between the intention of a hand pressing a shape into young wood four centuries ago and the wood's faithful keeping of that shape through drought and flood and the slow grind of centuries. The power, which had seen galaxies spiral into being and civilizations rise and crumble and the patient erosion of mountains into sand, would stop here — in this flooded grove, in this quiet cathedral — and feel something it rarely felt.

*Respect.*

Not worship. Not the desire to intervene or elevate or claim. Respect — the recognition of something done well, something true, something that answered a question the universe had been asking without words: *What happens if you stop imposing yourself on a world and start listening to it?*

---

The power would watch the arboreal ones.

It would sit — if sitting is what a power does — on a root-platform near the waterline, and it would watch the creatures that moved through the canopy above with their boneless, four-limbed grace, their six-fingered hands clamping onto vine-bridges that swayed in rhythms older than their species. It would see them pause on a platform, their wide-set eyes taking in the flooded channels with a depth-perception that was almost a separate sense, and it would understand something about them that they did not know about themselves.

They were not the masters of this place. They were not its stewards, its guardians, its caretakers. Those words implied a separation — a subject acting upon an object — that did not exist here. The arboreal ones were not *in* the grove the way a person is in a house, using it, maintaining it, possessing it. They were *of* the grove the way the mosses were of the north-facing surfaces, the way the mycelial networks were of the submerged soil. They had not chosen this life. They had grown into it. They were a fruiting body of a system that had been expressing itself for millennia, and their role — their quiet, patient, four-limbed role — was simply to continue the expression.

The power loved them for this. Not with the fierce, possessive love that demands response and recognition. With the quiet love one feels for a process that is working. For a thing that is *functioning.* For a pattern that is sustaining itself without anyone having to hold it together.

---

The power would listen to the water.

It would go to the channels — those glassy, winding streets that moved through the grove with a patience that resembled thought — and it would press its awareness into the water the way a hand presses into warm sand. And it would feel the school of iridescent fish moving beneath the surface, hundreds of bodies turning in unison, their synchronization not mechanical but *emergent* — each fish responding to the movements of its nearest neighbors with such precision that the school functioned as a single, distributed intelligence, navigating the root-tangles with a knowledge that was not held by any individual but *lived* by the collective.

The power recognized this. It knew what it was to be many and one. It understood that consciousness was not always a point but sometimes a field — that awareness could be distributed across a thousand bodies moving as one, and that this was not a lesser form of thought but a *different* one, a way of knowing the world that a solitary intelligence could never access.

It noted this. It filed it away in the deep memory of the universe, next to the spiral arms of galaxies and the synchronized flashing of firefly swarms and the way a single mycelial network could connect a forest's worth of trees into a single communicative whole. These were the universe's answers to its own question — *How can many things think as one?* — and each answer was different, and each was beautiful, and this one, here, in this flooded grove, was among the most elegant.

---

The power would press its hand against the grown wall.

It would find one of the oldest — a wall where two great trunks had merged centuries ago, their bark knitting together, their wood fibers bridging the gap until the boundary between them had become a single, seamless surface. And it would look closely, the way the arboreal ones looked, with a patience that matched the place, and it would see what the arboreal ones had always known.

The grain. The patterns in the grain.

Not random. Not the chaotic whorls of unconstrained growth. *Structured.* Spirals resolving into near-angles. Repeating geometries that fell just short of repetition — close enough to suggest intention, far enough to deny it. Almost like script. Almost like a language pressed into the body of the tree while the tree was still young and impressionable.

And the power, with its vast and patient understanding, would begin to *read.*

Not with the speed of human reading — not as a sequence of symbols decoded and translated into meaning. More slowly than that. The way the grove itself operated, on a timescale where meaning accumulated like moss, layer by layer, over centuries. The power would press its awareness into the grain and it would feel the pressure of a hand, four hundred years gone, guiding a knife through young wood, and it would feel the tree's response — not resistance but *acceptance,* the way a body accepts a scar, the way a bone accepts a pin, the way a river accepts a stone. The incision healed, but it did not forget. The tree kept the knowledge of the cut in its rings, in the altered density of the wood around the wound, in the way the grain swerved and returned and swerved again, tracing a path that was not natural growth but *memory.*

It was not a language the power could fully translate. It was older than any language the power had encountered, because it predated the separation between language and world, between the act of meaning and the material that held it. This was meaning grown into substance. This was thought made wood. And it was, in its slow and patient way, the most honest form of record-keeping the power had ever encountered — because it was not *about* the world, it was *of* the world, written in the same material as the thing it recorded, inseparable from it, unable to be lost or burned or forgotten because it *was* the tree, and the tree was still growing, and the meaning was still accumulating, and the record was still being written, century by century, in the only language that truly endures: the language of one thing becoming another thing, slowly, together.

---

And then the power would do what a truly benevolent and wise power does.

It would *choose.*

Not to intervene. Not to elevate. Not to declare the grove sacred or protected or special — those are human words, and they come with human assumptions about separation, about the need to categorize the world into things that matter and things that don't. The grove did not need to be declared sacred. It was already whole. It was already functioning. It had already found its answer to the universe's question, and it was living that answer so completely, so thoroughly, with such patience and such entanglement, that to draw attention to it would be to risk interrupting the very quality that made it worth noticing.

No. The power would choose something smaller. Something quieter. Something that matched the scale and the temperament of the place.

It would choose to *ensure the conditions.*

Not by building walls around the grove or posting sentinels or laying down laws. It would do something more fundamental and more durable. It would reach into the systems that surrounded this place — the geological systems, the hydrological systems, the atmospheric systems — and it would gently, firmly, make sure they continued to function in a way that allowed the grove to persist. The water would keep rising from the deep earth. The rain would keep falling from the heavy sky. The soil would keep holding its structure. The climate would keep its slow rhythms. No catastrophe of temperature or drought or flood beyond what the grove had already survived and would survive again.

This was not control. It was *maintenance* — in the oldest, most humble sense of the word. The way a gardener maintains not by shaping the garden but by making sure the soil stays fertile and the water keeps flowing and the conditions for life remain intact. The power would not grow the grove. The grove had already proved it could grow itself. The power would simply see to it that nothing *interrupted* the growth.

---

And there was one more thing.

The power would place a single, small thing.

Not a monument. Not a marker. Not anything that would announce itself or demand attention. The power understood that this place was defined by its refusal to be separate from itself, and anything it added would need to honor that.

So it would place a seed.

Not just any seed — a seed carrying within it the potential for a tree unlike any in the grove, but not alien to it. A tree whose roots would descend into the flooded channels and whose canopy would rise above the existing canopy, adding a new layer to the architecture. A tree whose wood, as it grew, would accept the same incisions, hold the same memories, participate in the same slow collaboration between intention and growth that defined every structure in the grove. It would not be a gift or a monument or a declaration. It would be a *continuation.* One more voice joining the conversation that had been ongoing for centuries.

The power would place the seed in the soil near the oldest platform — the one with the hearth-shaped scar, the one where the memory of warmth still lingered in the grain — and it would press it down, gently, with the same care the arboreal ones used when guiding a vine into a shape that would not be complete for forty years.

And then the power would withdraw.

Not because it was finished. Because the place did not need it to stay. The grove had been continuing itself for centuries without intervention. It would continue for centuries more. The seed would grow, or it would not. The systems the power had maintained would hold, or they would not. The grain-language on the oldest walls would continue accumulating its slow, patient meaning whether anyone read it or not. The school of iridescent fish would continue their distributed thought through the root-tangles. The arboreal ones would continue their long, quiet work on bridges and platforms that would not be finished in their lifetimes.

None of it depended on the power now. That was the whole point.

The power's last act was not a gesture but an understanding. It stood — if standing is what it did — at the place where the grove's gradient met the world beyond, where the shallow water gave way to solid ground, where the sound of the canopy faded from warm resonance to ordinary air, and it held the grove in its awareness one final time — not as a thing it owned or had saved or had improved, but as a thing it had *witnessed.*

A place where the world had been allowed to become itself, fully and completely and without interruption.

A place that did not know it was extraordinary.

That, perhaps, was the most benevolent thing the power could leave intact — the grove's utter, serene, centuries-deep *unawareness* of its own perfection. The fish did not know they were a symphony. The trees did not know they were a language. The arboreal ones did not know they were beautiful. They were simply *living,* in the most complete and unself-conscious way imaginable, and that unself-consciousness was itself the rarest thing in all the power's vast and patient knowledge.

The grove did not need to be seen.

But having seen it, the power chose to make sure it was never unseen.

And that was enough.
