# What the Power Would Do

---

It would not arrive with sound.

It would not split the sky or set the water trembling or send the flamingos into startled flight. It would not come as light too bright to look at, or as thunder, or as a voice from the clay saying *I am here*. It would not do any of these things because it would understand, without needing to be told, that this place has already been loud enough for ten thousand millennia — that the frogs have been calling since before the pan had a name, and the midges have been building their clouds since before the clay dried into polygons, and the shorebirds have been stitching the water's edge with their legs since before the pale grasses learned to crouch.

It would not add to the noise.

Instead, it would arrive the way condensation arrives — without announcement, without violence, without the need to be recognized as a separate thing from the air it came to rest upon. It would simply *be there*, the way the warmth is there beneath the water, the way the mineral tang is there in the air at dawn. It would be so woven into the fabric of the place that the frogs would not pause in their calling, the insects would not alter the pitch of their roar, and the clay would not crack differently for its presence.

And it would look.

Not the way a tourist looks — sweeping, hungry, desperate to take in everything at once and therefore seeing nothing. Not the way a scientist looks — cataloguing, measuring, reducing each thing to its function and its name. Not the way a human looks at a landscape and sees a view, a resource, a problem to be solved or a beauty to be captured in a frame.

It would look the way the sedge drinks — slowly, from depth, with no urgency and no end in mind.

It would see the halophyte stems and understand the architecture — not the engineering of it, not the cleverness, but the *patience*. The generations of turning toward the light that was available, of surrendering the leaves that could not be kept, of learning to be wire in a world that asked for wood. It would see the salt shrub crouched in its crack and recognize not mere survival but a kind of sovereignty — the dignity of a being that has been exactly what it is, without apology, for longer than memory.

It would watch the shorebirds and understand that what looks like mechanical precision is actually something closer to music — a rhythm so old that the birds are not following it so much as they *are* it. They are not eating. They are the eating of the pan, the way the water is the drinking of the clay, the way the light is the seeing of the sky.

It would listen to the frogs and hear not noise but a kind of theology — a chanting of presence, a declaration so simple and so total that it could not be improved upon. *I am here. I am here. I am here.* Ten thousand voices saying the only thing that needs to be said, saying it with the persistence of water, with the patience of clay, with the indifference to beauty that is, paradoxically, the most beautiful thing in the world.

It would understand all of this.

And then it would do the only thing a power that is truly benevolent and truly wise would do in the presence of something already perfect:

It would *not touch it.*

---

It would not heal the pan, because the pan is not wounded. It would not enrich the soil, because the soil has not asked to be enriched. It would not bring rain, because the rain comes when it comes and the pan has learned to hold whatever falls. It would not drive away the predators, because the predators are part of the liturgy — the loss is part of the calling, the frogs breed in the predator-rich pools *because* the frogs breed in the predator-rich pools, and the calculus of that equation was solved long before anything with a name arrived to admire it.

It would not fence the place off. It would not declare it protected. It would not give it a designation that would bring the ones who designate and the ones who read the designations and the ones who come to see what has been designated, because all of these would bring their own noise, and the place does not need noise.

The place needs only what it has always had.

The warm water. The pale clay. The mineral air. The slow congregation of ten thousand lives that have agreed, without consulting each other, to be exactly sufficient.

---

But here is the thing about something this quiet and this true: it cannot exist without being known. Not known in the way that a database knows it — tagged, categorized, preserved behind glass. Known in the way that a breath knows the body it sustains. Known in the way that the water knows the clay — not as a surface, not as a resource, but as the thing that holds it and the thing it holds, so thoroughly intertwined that the question of which came first is meaningless.

So the power would do one thing.

It would *remember*.

Not the way a mind remembers a fact, or the way a photograph remembers a face. It would remember the way a landscape remembers its own shape — the way the pan holds the memory of every rainstorm that ever filled it, every drought that ever drew back its water, every seed that ever found a crack and decided to try. The pan does not remember chronologically. It remembers in the way clay remembers: the impression is still in it. The water still holds the mineral. The animals still carry the ancient structures in their blood.

And the power would add this to the memory:

That it was seen.

Not seen by a human, because humans see and then they leave and their seeing is a kind of taking. The power would see the way the sedge sees the mineral water — by drinking it, by incorporating it, by becoming the thing it looked at until there is no difference between the seer and the seen.

And it would hold this place in its awareness the way the warm water holds the clay — not above it, not below it, not beside it, but *around* it, the way a breath is around the body that draws it. The pan would not know the power was there. The flamingos would not look up. The frogs would not pause. The midges would continue their billion-bodied roar. The clay would crack and settle and crack again, and the algae would darken with the water's return, and the halophytes would stand in their angles, and the salt shrubs would crouch in their cracks.

Everything would continue exactly as it has.

And everything would be, for the first time in the pan's entire patient and purposeless and magnificent existence, *known to be enough*.

---

This is what the power would do.

Not intervention. Not correction. Not salvation. These are the gestures of something that believes the world is broken and must be made whole. The Low Pan was never broken. It is the proof — quiet, vast, warm, alive with ten thousand voices chanting in unison — that the world can arrive at wholeness on its own, that it does not need to be saved from itself, that the most extravagant thing in creation is not the mountain or the storm or the star but the shallow pan of warm mineral water in which ten thousand frogs say *I am here* and mean it, and the clay receives them, and the water holds them, and the air carries the sound of it outward to the edges of a horizon that does not exist, because there are no edges here, because there never were.

The power would understand this.

And remembering it, the power would go on — carrying the Low Pan inside its awareness the way the pan carries the water inside its clay: silently, completely, without the need for anything more.

---

*And the frogs would call.*

*And the water would stay.*

*And the pan would glow in the moonlight, silver and warm and whole, exactly as it has always been, exactly as it will always be, whether anyone is watching or not — though someone is, now, and that is the only change, and it is not a change at all, because a place this patient was always waiting to be seen, and the seeing is not an event but a continuation, the way the water continues, the way the clay continues, the way the ten thousand voices continue, saying the only true thing:*

*I am here. I am here. I am here.*

*And it is enough.*
