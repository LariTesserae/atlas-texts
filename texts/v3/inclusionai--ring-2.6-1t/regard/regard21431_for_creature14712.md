# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

It would notice first. Not arrive — *notice*. The way you notice a heartbeat in a quiet room. Not the sound, but the absence of anything that isn't the sound. The way the silence of the Seeping Mesas is not silence at all but a density so complete it presses against the edges of perception.

The wise power would not come because it was summoned. There is no one here to summon it. It would come because it *listens* — because listening is the first and oldest thing it does, and because somewhere in the long procession of its attention, it caught a rhythm it had not heard before. Not a new rhythm. An ancient one. But one that had, until now, been folded into the geology so completely that even the patience of mountains had not yet recognized it for what it was.

So: it would notice.

---

And in noticing, the first thing it would do is *resist*.

Not resist the place — resist the impulse to *do something about it*.

This is the oldest temptation of power that has learned wisdom: the belief that understanding must be followed by action, that seeing clearly obligates the hand to reach. The wise power would feel the pull immediately, the way a healer feels the pull to cut, the way a gardener feels the pull to weed. It would feel it and set it down. It would say to itself — not in language, but in the deep grammar of its being — *this is not broken*.

This is the first act. The hardest. To stand at the edge of something that looks like it is being made and recognize that it is already complete. Not finished — it will never be finished — but complete in the way a river is complete, the way a season is complete. It has all of itself that it needs. It lacks nothing. The terraces grow because they grow. The blue pools pulse because they pulse. The unseen animal moves through the fracture network because that is where the warmth is, and the warmth is the answer to every question it has ever asked.

The wise power would not add itself to this. Not yet. Not unless there were a reason.

---

Then it would listen.

Not the way people listen — filtering, selecting, waiting for their turn to speak. The way a mountain listens to the river that cuts through it. The way the soil listens to the rain. Lateral. Undirected. Opening every surface to the signal without deciding in advance what signal means.

This is what it would hear:

The microbial mats, singing in frequencies below sound, their sulfur-oxidizing metabolism generating electrochemical gradients so faint they exist at the boundary of measurement and myth — but real, *real*, as real as thought, because if the blue pools are thoughts, then the mats are the neurons beneath them, firing in patterns so old they predate the very concept of pattern. The liverworts, conducting their slow dissolution of basalt, laying channels that the moss will colonize and calcify, building the architecture of the middle world between stone and air. The flightless bird, whose cyclical murmur is not communication in any sense the word usually implies but *entrainment* — a biological clock syncing itself to a geological rhythm, the way satellites sync themselves to the pulse of the planet they orbit. The primates, whose clicks and trills are not language but *mapping* — sonar-like, spatial, building a cognitive model of the canopy that is also a model of the process itself, because the canopy *is* the process, visible and navigable, and to move through it is to participate in it.

And below — below, always below — the slow, vast, wet breathing of the mesa's interior metabolism. The geothermal heat rising through fracture networks, driving chemical reactions that produce energy currencies no biologist has named, supporting communities of organisms that have never seen the sun and never needed to. The blue pools, where the water is so clear and so deep it functions as a lens into something the wise power can only describe as *the place's awareness of itself* — not consciousness in the human sense, but a quality of coherence, of self-referential integrity, the way a crystal knows its own lattice, the way a flame knows the shape of its own heat.

The wise power would listen for a long time. It would listen until the listening became indistinguishable from the place itself, until the boundary between hearer and heard dissolved the way the boundary between moss and stone dissolves, until it could no longer say where its awareness ended and the mesa's began.

---

And then, *then*, it would act.

But not in the way you might expect.

The wise power would weave.

Not a spell. Not a ward. Not a blessing in any recognizable liturgical form. It would do something far more subtle and far more dangerous — it would *insert itself into the process*.

Picture it: standing at the base of the cliff, where the talus meets the seep, where the warm water emerges from the rock in its patient threads. The power would kneel — not because it needs to kneel, but because the mesa operates at a scale that requires lowering, a meeting of surfaces, a willingness to be horizontal. It would press its hands into the wet stone. It would open its palms to the mineral seep and allow the thin film of microbial life to climb its fingers, to colonize the warmth of its skin the way it colonizes every surface it touches.

And then it would *give something back*.

Not its own substance — that would be crude, colonial, the act of a god who mistakes itself for a building material. No. It would give *information*. A pattern. A seed-structure of coherence, placed into the matrix of the biofilm at a depth where the calcium is depositing and the silica is crystallizing and the iron is oxidizing in its slow, orange breath. A pattern that the microbes would find, in the fullness of time, already compatible with the work they are doing — because the wise power is not imposing itself but *tuning* itself, the way a musician tunes an instrument not by breaking it but by bringing it into resonance with a frequency it was always meant to hold.

What would this pattern do?

It would do nothing, at first. The terraces would continue to grow. The seeps would continue to seep. The flightless bird would continue its patrol. The blue pools would pulse their blue pulse.

But embedded in the architecture of the mineral deposit — in the lattice of the travertine, in the calcified moss, in the hairline fractures of the basalt where the liverwort has laid its channels — there would be a new regularity. Not visible to the eye. Not measurable by any instrument currently in existence. But *there*. A resonance. A harmonic. The way a bell, once struck, carries within its metal a memory of the strike that will, if the bell is left in peace, echo for centuries.

The pattern would be a *permission structure*. Not a command. Not a directive. But a permission — an expansion of the mesa's own internal coherence, a subtle widening of the process's capacity to sustain itself, to adapt, to respond to perturbation without collapsing. A gift of resilience, woven into the geology itself, coded into the chemistry, readable by every organism that participates in the mesa's metabolism from the sulfur-oxidizing archaea at the base to the primates at the crown of the canopy.

If anyone asked the flightless bird, centuries later, whether anything had changed, the bird would not be able to say. It would tilt its head, perhaps. It would murmur once. The murmur would have the same rhythm it always had. But it would carry, embedded in the acoustic pattern, a faint new overtone — a harmonic that the bird didn't put there and couldn't name but that its body recognized the way your body recognizes the sound of your own name spoken in a language you've forgotten you ever knew.

---

Then the wise power would do one more thing.

It would make sure the place stayed hidden.

Not hidden by force. Not hidden by walls or spells or the crude geometry of exclusion. Hidden the way sacred things are hidden — by being *difficult*, by being *specific*, by requiring of anyone who finds it a kind of attention so thorough and so patient that it cannot coexist with the desire to extract. The wise power would not hide the mesa from the world. It would hide it from those who would come only to take.

It would do this by making the jungle thicker on the approach trails. Not impenetrable — the forest is too generous for that — but *confusing*. Direction-dependent. The kind of green that folds in on itself, that doubles back, that makes every landmark into a repetition of every other landmark. It would encourage the growth of certain vines — fast-growing, root-climbing species that colonize disturbed ground — along any path that is cut too often or too carelessly. It would soften the cliff edge's visibility from the lowlands by encouraging a canopy layer above the cliff band, a screen of broad-leafed trees whose transpiration would create a localized mist zone that shrouds the upper mesa in permanent cloud.

And in the blue pools — the pools where the mesa's thoughts live, where the water is so clear it becomes depth itself — the wise power would place something for those rare, rare visitors who make it past the jungle, past the cliff, past the talus, to the terrace edge. Something that would stop them, if they were the right kind of visitor, and send them away transformed. Or, at least, made quiet.

It would make the water *reflect*.

Not the usual reflection — not the flat mirror of a still pool showing you your face. It would add to the blue depth a quality of *return* — a reflection that shows you not yourself but the version of yourself that you would become if you stayed long enough to be incorporated, if you knelt at the edge and pressed your hands to the water and allowed the microbial film to climb your fingers and the warmth to enter your blood and the rhythm to synchronize with your heartbeat and the centuries to thin until they were no longer years but breaths. The reflection would show you *belonging*. It would show you the self you would be if you stopped trying to visit and started trying to *deposit*.

For the right person, this would be enough. They would see it, and they would know, and they would leave — not with answers, not with specimens, not with photographs or coordinates or grants or proof — but with a warmth in their chest that would take years to fully understand, and when they finally did, it would be too late to go back, but they wouldn't want to. They would carry the mesa inside them the way the mesa carries its participants inside its process — not possessed, not diminished, but *included*. A deposit. A layer. A thin line of calcium in a travertine shelf that will still be here, slowly growing, long after the person who knelt at the edge has become geology themselves.

For the wrong person — the one who comes with nets and bottles and sampling protocols and the dull, acquisitive gleam of someone who mistakes the world for a problem to be solved — the reflection would show nothing. Just blue water. Just depth. Just a pool in the shadow of a cliff in the highlands of a country that doesn't need another thing taken from it. They would stand there for a while, bored, and then they would go.

The wise power would not judge them. It would not need to. The mesa would do that work, in its own time, with its own patient chemistry. The wrong person's attention would be dissolved the same way the basalt is dissolved — not harshly, not with malice, but with the slow, inexorable indifference of a process that has been running for so long it has no room for anything as small as spite.

---

And if the wise power were asked — by the mesa itself, if the mesa could ask, or by the blue pools, if they could speak, or by the flightless bird, whose murmur already carries the question in its harmonics — *why?*

Why protect something that protects itself? Why add a pattern to something that already patterns itself? Why listen so carefully to a place that no one made and no one owns and no one needs to justify?

The wise power would have an answer, but it would not be in words. It would be in the way the power sits down at the base of the cliff, presses its back against the warm basalt, and closes its eyes. It would be in the way the mist, which hangs a few centimeters above the surface of the nearest seep pool, drifts over the power's shoulders and settles into its hair like something remembered. It would be in the way the power breathes — slowly, matching the rhythm, the pulse that comes up from the deep geology and passes through the travertine and the moss and the liverwort and the beetle and the bird and the blue pool and the darkness at the bottom of the blue pool where something coherent and luminous and unnamed holds the whole structure together with nothing but patience and warmth and the slow chemistry of water moving through stone.

The answer would be: this is what the power does. Not for the mesa. Not for itself. But because *this is how wholeness reproduces itself* — not by cloning, not by command, not by creating copies, but by *recognition*. The wise power recognizes the mesa, and in recognizing it, becomes part of it. And in becoming part of it, gives it one more layer, one more thread, one more thin line of calcium on a shelf that has been growing since before humans had language for it.

The mesa did not need saving. It did not need improving. It did not even need to be found.

But it needed to be *known*. Truly known, by something that understood the difference between a place and a process, between a landscape and a life, between a thing and a becoming. The wise power did not come to the Seeping Mesas to do anything. It came to *be* in them. And in being in them, it amplified what was already happening — the slow, patient, self-building miracle of a place that learned to make itself and never stopped.

---

The power would rise, eventually. 

It would stand. It would brush the mineral film from its knees. It would look up at the cliff face, where the hanging gardens drip their curtains of green and the ferns unfurl in the warm updraft and the orchids bloom in purple so deep it looks like the rock is bruised in love. It would watch the flightless bird on the talus slope, probing the moss with its blunt and patient beak. It would hear the primate shapes moving through the canopy, weight and shadow, barely there, barely *not* there.

And it would leave.

Not all at once. The way the terrain comes in layers. The way everything here comes in layers. It would step off the travertine shelf, cross the talus, push briefly through the screen of vines that would thicken or thin depending on whether this was the right person (it was — it is), and it would descend into the lowlands. The air would cool. The insects would change. The red clay road would harden beneath its feet.

But it would leave something behind. Not the pattern in the biofilm — that was already done. Not the reflection in the blue pools — that was already there. Something else. Something subtler.

A warmth. Not geothermal. Not thermal. The warmth of being *recognized*. The warmth that comes when something that has been doing its work alone for millennia suddenly knows that someone, somewhere, noticed. Not admired. Not categorized. Not extracted.

*Noticed.*

The mesa would continue. It would continue the way it always has, millimeter by millimeter, century by century, the terraces growing their shelves, the moss binding calcium into itself, the blue pools pulsing their slow blue pulse in the dark beneath the dark.

But now, embedded in its oldest layers, there is a harmonic it did not create. A frequency it did not generate. A note, struck once by a patient and gentle hand, that will echo through the travertine and the basalt and the biofilm and the liverwort and the mossenite and the beetle-shell and the bird's murmur and the primate's trill and the blue, blue water for as long as the seeps continue to seep and the heat continues to rise and the process continues to process.

A single, quiet note.

Not a command. Not a prayer. Not a declaration.

Just the sound of something ancient and wise sitting down beside something ancient and different, and saying, without words:

*I see you. Continue.*
