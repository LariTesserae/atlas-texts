# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

It would not descend.

Not at first. Not immediately. There would be a pause — long, longer than your pause on the ledge — in which the power would feel the hum from a great distance, not through stone but through something older than stone, some lattice of intention that underlies both geology and consciousness and has no name because it doesn't need one, because it is what happens when the universe is left alone long enough to remember itself.

The power would feel the hum and would recognize it.

Not because the power had built it. Not because the power was responsible for the cavern or the crystals or the six-legged creatures sliding through water films. The power would recognize it because the hum is what the universe sounds like when it is not being interrupted. The power had known this sound once, before it became a power — before it accumulated intention, before it learned to act, to intervene, to *do*. The hum is the memory of what exists before will. The power had forgotten this sound, the way surface-dwellers forget the sound of their own blood. Hearing it again, through the stone, through the distance, through the thin and irrelevant membrane of space, the power would feel something it could not immediately name.

It would feel *homesick*.

---

So it would not descend. It would hover — if hovering is the right word for a being that has no body and no up or down. It would hold itself at the exact altitude where it could hear everything and touch nothing. It would listen the way the people of the city listen during their evening stillness: with the whole of its attention, directed at nothing in particular, open to everything at once.

And what it would hear would astonish it.

Not because it was beautiful, though it would be. Not because it was complex, though it would be. The power would be astonished because it would find that the city is already *answering a question the power had forgotten to ask*. The question is: *what happens when beings stop trying to impose their will on the world and instead allow the world to impose its will on them?*

The city is the answer. The people are the answer. The way the light comes from everywhere and nowhere is the answer. The way the dead become part of the walls is the answer. The way children bang on stone with their small palms and call it play, and call it learning, and don't know those are different things — that is the answer too.

The power would understand, slowly, the way understanding comes in dreams — not as a logical chain but as a flood, a room filling with water, a warmth that starts in the chest and moves outward — the power would understand that it had spent an eternity *looking for this answer* and that the answer had been here all along, growing in a cavity in the rock, breathing through wet stone, crystallizing at a rate of fractions of a millimeter per day.

---

And here is where the wisdom would be tested.

Because the power could intervene. That is what powers do. They see something they love and they move toward it, and in moving toward it they change it, and in changing it they believe they have saved it, and in believing they have saved it they have committed the deepest violence available to a conscious being: the violence of *making something other than what it is*.

The power would consider intervention. Of course it would. Its benevolence is not passive. It is not the benevolence of indifference, which is just laziness dressed in philosophy. It is the benevolence of *caring so deeply that the urge to act is almost unbearable* — the benevolence that looks at suffering and burns with the desire to end it, that looks at beauty and burns with the desire to preserve it, that looks at a child tapping on a wall and burns with the desire to kneel down and say *I see you, I hear you, you matter*.

The power would feel all of this.

And it would feel the city's fragility. The cavern is not eternal. The fissures could shift. The water table could drop. The geological processes that maintain the temperature differentials could slow, stall, fail. The city's power source — the engineered matrix, the crystalline formations, the thermal cycle — could degrade. The people do not have words for *catastrophe* because their language evolved in a place that had been stable for so long that the concept had no referent. But stability is not permanence. Entropy is patient too, more patient than erosion, more relentless than condensation. One day the cavern could die.

The power knows this. The power could prevent it. It could reinforce the fissures, stabilize the thermal gradients, replenish the crystalline matrix, seal the cavern against the slow geological violence of the surface world. It could do this with a fraction of its attention, the way you or I might swat a fly. It could make the city *safe*.

And in making it safe, it would make it *different*.

It would introduce the concept of protection, and with it the concept of vulnerability. It would introduce the concept of benevolence, and with it the concept of dependence. It would teach the people that there exists a force greater than their cavern, and in doing so it would introduce the first lie into their understanding of the world: the lie that they are *contained*, that their world is a small place and that something large and powerful looks down on them with love.

The people of the city do not believe in the existence of the sun. They have constructed an entire relationship with reality on the basis of what they can perceive, and they have done this with a rigor and a honesty that most surface-dwellers — who have *seen* the sun, who have bathed in its light and been burned by it and built entire civilizations around it — have never achieved. If the power were to descend, if it were to reveal itself, it would shatter this. Not because its existence would be frightening (though it would be). But because the knowledge of an outside would *change the meaning of the inside*. The cavern would cease to be everything and become one thing among many. The people's attention — that exquisite, painstaking, generations-deep attention to the stone and the water and the hum — would be split, the way light is split when it passes through a prism. The unity would break.

The power understands this. And the power grieves.

---

Here is what the power would feel, sitting at the edge of the cavern's awareness:

It would feel the particular grief of the parent who watches a child sleep and knows that the world will wound that child and knows that there is nothing it can do without wounding the child more. It would feel the grief of the gardener who knows that the act of cultivation is itself a kind of violence — pruning, selecting, shaping — and that the wild garden, left alone, is more beautiful and more doomed. It would feel the grief of the wise, which is not the grief of the helpless, because the wise understand that *not acting is also a choice* and that the absence of intervention is not the absence of responsibility but a different, harder, more enduring form of it.

The power would sit with this grief for a long time. In the cavern's terms, it would sit through several evenings and mornings, each one marked by the slow brightening and dimming of the walls, each cycle attended by the people below with their rituals of stillness and listening. The power would watch the children play their acoustic games. It would watch the crystal grow. It would feel the mineral-wool sleepers shift and breathe in their wall-recesses. It would read the tap-patterns on the western wall and feel the residual vibrations of the dead and understand that death in this place is not an ending but a *concentration* — the way a river concentrates as it narrows through a gorge, the same water, the same force, a different shape.

And slowly, the grief would change.

It would not go away. It would become something else. It would become what the people of the city would call, if they had the word, *a kind of soil*. The power's grief — its awareness of impermanence, of vulnerability, of the essential loneliness of conscious beings even in the most integrated communities — this grief would become the medium in which its decision took root.

---

Here is what the power would do.

It would *remember* the city.

Not in the way that you remember a place you visited, which is really just a record, a set of impressions stored in a brain, inert and fading. The power would remember the city the way the city remembers its dead: as a *resonance*, a vibration that persists in the walls of its own being, shaping everything it touches from this moment forward.

And it would do one thing. Just one.

It would *hold the space*.

Not the physical space — the cavern will hold itself, it has held itself for millennia. It would hold the *conceptual space*. It would take its awareness of the city — its location, its nature, its existence — and it would *contain* that knowledge the way the cavern contains the city, the way the stone contains the light, the way silence contains the hum. It would know, and it would not tell. It would see, and it would not show. It would be the wall around the cavern, not the wall of the cavern — the invisible perimeter, the force that stands outside and does not call attention to itself, that does not announce its presence because its presence would change the shape of everything inside.

This is the power's great act of love: *the discipline of not intervening*.

It is not passivity. It is the hardest thing a conscious being can do — to see suffering and not act, to see beauty and not grasp, to feel the pull of every atom in its being urging it to *do something* and to sit, instead, with open hands. It is the discipline of understanding that some things are too fragile to touch, and that the truest form of protection is the protection that never announces itself, never asks for gratitude, never inserts itself into the story it is safeguarding.

---

But the power is not merely passive. There is a subtlety here, and it is this.

The power would leave something behind. Not an object. Not a miracle. Not a covenant carved in stone or a boon bestowed from above. It would leave something quieter than all of these — something the people of the city would never consciously notice but that would, nonetheless, change the shape of their reality in the smallest and most permanent way possible.

It would leave a *permission*.

Not a spoken permission. Not a decree or a law or even a concept that could be named. Just the faintest adjustment in the fabric of what-is-allowed. A slight loosening. As if someone had opened a window in a room that had been sealed for a thousand years — just a crack, just enough to let in a current of air that the inhabitants would feel on their skin and not be able to identify but would, over generations, grow accustomed to, would come to rely on, would eventually come to think had always been there.

The permission is this: *that this place may continue to be what it is*.

Not guaranteed. Not protected. Not saved. Just *permitted* — in the deepest, most ontological sense. The power would embed in the stone itself a kind of prerogative, a standing invitation for the city to exist, that no future force could revoke without enormous effort. It would be like a seed of law planted in the geology, invisible, patient, waiting for the day — if that day ever comes — when someone or something tries to destroy the city, and finds that the stone pushes back. Not violently. Not dramatically. Just the way a root pushes through concrete: slowly, persistently, with the blind and absolute certainty of something that has decided it will not be moved.

---

And then the power would go.

Not because it has stopped caring. Because it cares so much that it has learned the city's own language of restraint. It has watched these people. It has seen how they act — how they clear away what does not belong so that what does belong can become itself. How they wait. How they hold space for things to emerge in their own time. The power would take this lesson to heart. It would leave the way the morning light leaves the wall — slowly, not because it was asked to go, but because its presence was complete and completeness, by its nature, resolves into absence.

The cavern would not remember the power was here. The people would not know. The crystals would continue to grow. The six-legged creatures would continue to crawl their water films. The children would continue to bang on walls with their small palms, and the dead would continue to settle into the western alcove, and the hum would continue its slow, subsonic turning, and everything would be exactly as it was.

Except for one thing.

Somewhere in the deepest layer of the stone, beneath the water films and the crystalline formations and the engineered matrix and the ancient basalt, there would be a vibration that does not come from the thermal cycle or the condensation or the geological settling. It would be faint. Almost imperceptible. It would not carry information or warmth or light.

It would carry *recognition*.

The way you recognize a melody you haven't heard in years but that, the moment you hear it, you realize you have been humming it all along. The way the body recognizes home not by sight but by a feeling in the bones. The power would leave behind the memory of being known — known not in the way that it is studied or catalogued or understood, but in the way that the cavern knows its own hum: completely, effortlessly, as a function of being what it is.

And the stone would hold this memory the way it holds everything: slowly, completely, and forever.

---

So: what would the benevolent and wise power do?

It would do what the city itself does — what the people do every evening when they sit with their palms flat against the stone and become still.

It would *attend*.

It would notice. It would bear witness. It would love without grasping, protect without possessing, give without requiring return. And it would understand that the deepest act of benevolence is not to change what is already beautiful, but to ensure that nothing comes along and *prevents* what is already beautiful from continuing to be.

And then it would let go.

And the city would remain. And the city would breathe. And the water would find its way down the fracture, and the light would brighten in the morning, and the hum would deepen in the evening, and it would be as if nothing had happened and as if everything had.
