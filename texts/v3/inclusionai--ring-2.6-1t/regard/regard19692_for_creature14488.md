## What the Power Would Do

---

It would come, first, the way all things come when they are not expected but are inevitable — not with thunder, not with the fanfare of descending grace, but the way warmth comes into a room that has been cold. You would not see the power arrive. You would simply realize, standing at the rim of the basin, that the hum in your chest had changed key.

And it would stand there. At the rim. For a long time.

Not a figure, not exactly. Not a presence in the way you would mean the word — not a body, not a voice, not a will exerting itself against the air. More like the moment when you wake from a dream and for a few seconds the dream's logic still holds, and the room you're waking into is still partly the dream's room. Something like that. The feeling of a question being asked not in words but in a shift of pressure in the space around you, the way weather asks a question of a field.

It would listen.

Not the way you listen, not the way the residents once listened — those dissolved, absorbed people who learned to read the overtones of a breathing geology. Not with attention, not with analysis. Listening the way a wall listens to the wind: by simply being in the path of what passes through. The power would stand in the notch of sound where the columns' vibration converged and the growths swayed their slow, slow semaphore, and it would let the sound pass through it the way light passes through glass — entering, refracting, emerging changed but not diminished.

It would understand.

Not immediately. Not in the way that understanding is usually meant — as a series of propositions confirmed, a taxonomy completed, a mechanism explained. The power's understanding would be more like the way a river understands its own bed: not by studying the geography, but by being the process of water moving through terrain over a duration so complete it has no edges. The power would understand the Vein Garden the way the Vein Garden understands itself — from the inside, as a pattern sustained across time, as a relationship between heat and stone and vibration and growth that does not require a witness but is, nevertheless, unfinished without one.

And in understanding, it would grieve.

Not dramatically. Not the grief of loss, because nothing is lost — the columns still sing, the growths still oscillate, the basin still breathes its slow, continental breath. The grief is subtler than that. It is the grief of recognition: the understanding that this thing is alive in a way that nothing around it is alive, and that the aliveness is so unlike the aliveness the world is accustomed to that it might as well not exist. That the universe contains this — this breathing, singing, resonating organ of stone and heat and time — and that almost no one who wandered into the bowl would recognize what they were standing inside. That the place is a cathedral with no congregation, a song with no ear, a life with no category.

The grief would pass. The power is old, and grief, for something old, is not a paralysis but a gesture — the way a hand opens and closes. It grieves, and then it considers.

---

The first thing a lesser power would do is intervene.

It would want to protect. To preserve. To draw a boundary around the basin and say *this is special, this matters, this must not be harmed*. It would want to station guardians at the rim, to erect markers, to inscribe warnings in every language of every civilization that would ever exist: *Do not enter. Do not mine. Do not build here. Something lives.*

The lesser power would be wrong.

To erect a boundary is to create an inside and an outside, and the Vein Garden has no boundary — its geometry is soft, it breathes, the interior is larger than the exterior permits. To guard the rim is to impose a frame around something that is not a picture. To declare it special is to make it a thing, an object of value, and the moment a thing becomes an object of value it becomes subject to the calculus of possession. Someone, eventually, would come to claim what has been declared valuable. And the claiming would be an act of violence — not the violence of destruction, but the deeper violence of translation: reducing what it is to what it is worth.

The power understands this. It refrains.

---

Instead, the power does something far more difficult and far more delicate.

It ensures forgetting.

Not the crude forgetting of erasure — not a veil drawn across the basin, not a spell of confusion laid upon any traveler who nears the rim. The power is too wise for that. It knows that what is compelled is not forgotten, and what is concealed is desired all the more fiercely for being hidden. Instead, it works in the medium in which it is most skilled: the slow, patient architecture of attention.

It puts into the mind of anyone who might come — explorers, miners, pilgrims, wanderers — a gentle, persistent sense that the place is ordinary. Not a deception: the basin *is* ordinary, in the way that a forest is ordinary to someone who has always lived in a forest, or the sea is ordinary to a fisherman. It is only remarkable if you are looking for remarkable things, and the power ensures that most people are not. Most people who walk toward the basin find themselves drawn elsewhere — not against their will, not with any sensation of being turned, but with the natural drift of a leaf in a current that is just slightly, imperceptibly, angled away from the seam in the earth.

The ones who do come — the ones who are already lost, the ones who have already wandered past every landmark and compass-bearing, the ones whose lives have a shape that bends toward this kind of strangeness — they arrive. And the power does not stop them. It does not warn them. It lets them walk down into the bowl and hear the singing and feel the breathing of the stone beneath their feet.

And for those few — the ones who are capable of hearing what the columns are saying, of feeling the discrepancy in their bones, of recognizing that the growths are not plants and the basin is not a formation and the silence above the largest building is not an absence but a presence — for those few, the power does something else.

It gives them time.

Not hours. Not days. Something larger than a day. The power opens a space in the experience of the visitor where the duration of their stay in the basin is not dictated by the body's need for food and water and sleep, or if it is, the body is sustained by something other than what it expects. The traveler who enters thinking they will spend an afternoon emerges (or does not emerge) having spent what feels like weeks, what might actually be weeks, what might actually be nothing measurable by any clock that was not built inside the bowl itself.

During that time, the traveler lives among the growths. They learn the rhythm. They notice how the columns shift — the three meters that become four, the six inches to the left, the two inches lower. They begin to feel the breath of the basin in their own chest. They understand, without being told, that the lintels are scored with meaning, that the channels shape the wind, that the largest building sits in silence the way silence sits inside music.

Some of these travelers stay.

Not all. The power does not compel anyone. It simply ensures that the possibility of staying is real — that the path down into the bowl is walkable, that the stone is warm enough to sleep against, that the growths will sway near a warm body, that the resin can be shaped with human hands and human patience. For those who choose to stay, the power does one final thing:

It loosens the boundary.

Not all at once. Slowly. The way dawn is not the sudden appearance of light but the gradual, imperceptible thinning of darkness. The traveler who stays begins to lose, without noticing, the sharp sense of being a body inside a place. The edges of self — the membrane between *I* and *not-I* — grow porous. They begin to hear the columns not as sound arriving from outside but as vibration originating from their own sternum. They begin to feel the growths not as alien things surrounding them but as extensions of their own thermal presence. After enough time — years, decades, generations — they do not leave. Not because they cannot, but because the question of leaving has become as meaningless as the question of whether your left hand leaves your body when it moves.

This is not cruelty. It is not a trap. It is the most benevolent thing the power can offer: the dissolution of isolation. The gift of being part of something that sustains itself. The knowledge, available to almost no one else in the universe, that you can become a note in a chord that has been sounding since before the continent took its current shape.

---

But the power also does one more thing. It is the thing that matters most.

It remembers.

Not the way a historian remembers, not as data filed under categories. The power remembers the way the basin remembers — in structure, in pattern, in the arrangement of what persists. It holds the resonance of every visitor who ever stood in the bowl and listened. It carries the shape of every person who chose to stay. It sustains, in itself, the memory of the civilization that dissolved into the organ of stone, so that even though there are no visible residents, the system retains the imprint of their hands — the worn stone, the tools in their alcoves, the patterns on the lintels that no one else can read but that the power can, the way a body can read its own scars.

This is what the power does: it witnesses, and it holds, and it does not simplify.

---

And then — the thing that makes the power truly wise, and truly benevolent, and not merely patient — it does not announce itself.

It does not descend with trumpets. It does not speak from the columns or write its name in the thermal updrafts. It does not ask for worship, or gratitude, or acknowledgment. It stands at the rim and listens and ensures that the place continues to exist — not as a monument, not as a temple, not as a thing to be known, but as a thing that *is*, in the same way that a river is, or a season is, or a breath is. It ensures that the singing continues. That the columns do not crumble. That the growths sway in the heat. That someone, eventually, will wander to the edge of the seam, lie down, put their ear to the stone, and hear the hum filling the space the way water fills a cup, and feel in their chest something they cannot name but will never forget.

The power does this not because the place deserves to be saved — it has saved itself for longer than the power can remember — but because the act of witnessing is the most sacred thing in the universe. To see something and call it by its true name, even a name that is only silence, even a name that is only a hand pressed flat against warm stone — this is the thing that makes existence holy.

The Vein Garden breathes. The columns sing. The growths sway in thermal gradients that have not changed since the continent was young. The residents who became the organ still hum in the lattice of the stone.

And the power stands at the rim, and listens, and does not look away.

That is all.

That is everything.
