# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

## I. Listening

It would not arrive with thunder.

It would arrive the way the fog arrives — without announcement, without the permission of anyone below, and without violence. It would step through the low overcast at the upper edge of the Tablelands, where the basalt breaks off into nothing and the air tastes of iron and distance, and it would stand there for a long time. Not hours. Longer.

It would listen.

This is the first thing a truly wise power understands: that the first act of benevolence is restraint. That to *do* anything before you have heard what a place is saying is to impose yourself upon it, and imposition — even kind imposition — is a kind of harm. The Tablelands of Qerreth have been speaking for two hundred million years. The basalt remembers cooling. The grooves in the stone remember walking. The lakes remember skies that no longer exist. The fog remembers a time before there was fog.

So the power would listen. It would press its awareness — vast, but deliberately narrowed, the way a wise person dims their own brightness so they can see what is already shining — down into the stone, into the water, into the slow pulse that the miners feel through their boots without naming.

And what it would hear would take a long time to parse, because the Tablelands do not speak in words. They speak in erosion. In the migration of mineral-laden water through fractures in the basalt. In the slow chemical conversation betweenite and moss, between condensate and stone, between the three-toed presence that walks the river-channels at night and the ground that receives its weight. It speaks in rhythms so long that a human lifetime is a single breath of it.

But the power would not be impatient. That is what makes it wise.

---

## II. Seeing

Having listened, it would look — not at the landscape, which is only the surface of the conversation, but *into* the structure of what is happening. It would see what the geologists have only theorized and what the old-timers in the Flatline know without being able to articulate:

That the Tablelands are not a place. They are a *gestation.*

The basalt is a shell. Beneath it, in sub-plateau strata that no drill has reached and no instrument has mapped, something is forming. Theite is not a mineral in the conventional sense — it is a byproduct, a shed skin, the calcareous residue of a process that the planet undertook a very long time ago and has not yet completed. The seams that the miners extract from are not veins in a body. They are *wake trails* — the residual warmth left behind by something that moved through the deep geology of the world the way a warm current moves through an ocean, and the stone hardened around it, and the heat crystallized intoite, and the basalt table above it was planed flat not by erosion but by the sheer even pressure of whatever passed beneath.

The three-toed prints are the same walker. Not the walker that made the grooves two hundred million years ago — that one finished its journey long before the fog began — but a successor. An inheritor. Something that rose from the gestation of the deep geology and now walks the surface in the dark, checking the progress, the way a gardener walks the rows at night, touching the soil, reading what has grown.

The fog is the Tablelands breathing. The lakes are its eyes, seeing forward and backward simultaneously because time, in a place that is still becoming, does not flow in a single direction. The moss and the ferns and the unnamed red creepers are not vegetation in any biological sense the botanists would recognize — they are *exudations.* The landscape is sweating its interior life outward, expressing through organic forms what it cannot yet express through stone.

And Kethrine — the town, the people, the drills and pipes and steam and narrow-gauge rail — sits on the surface of this process like a child sitting on the back of a sleeping animal, unaware of the animal, pulling material from its hide to fuel a distant city that the child has never seen.

---

## III. Mourning

The wise power would grieve.

Not for the people — though there is cause enough — but for the walker. The three-toed presence that moves through the river-channels at night, leaving prints that vanish by morning, trailing the scent of wet stone and metallic tang. The power would see what no human has seen: that the walker is *losing coherence.* With every seam ofite that is extracted, a small portion of the gestating process is undone. The walker depends on the heat-residue the way a plant depends on soil. When the miners take theite, they are taking the Walker's footing. Its prints are growing fainter. Its circuits around the plateau are growing longer, more erratic. In another three generations of extraction, it will have dissolved entirely — not died, for it was never precisely alive, but *dispersed,* the way a smell disperses in an open room, until there is nothing left to trace.

The power would also grieve for the people, because they do not know what they are doing. They are good people. The miners are careful, the engineers are conscientious, the families are kind to each other in the narrow, steam-hissing alleys. But they have been shaped by the extraction economy into instruments of dissolution. They wake and they drill and they haul and they eat their thick stew and they sleep and they wake, and the Walker beneath them grows weaker, and they do not know, and no one has told them, and the city below drinks theite-fired power and does not ask where it comes from.

This is the particular grief of a wise power: to see a harm that is not anyone's fault, that is in fact the aggregate of a thousand small reasonable decisions made by reasonable people at different times, none of whom could have seen the whole.

---

## IV. What It Does

The power would not descend to the town. It would not manifest in the streets of Kethrine like a prophet or a punishment. It would not split the sky or raise its voice or alter the shape of a single stone.

Instead, it would do the following things, in this order, because the order matters:

**First**, it would *heal the Walker.*

Not by restoring what has been taken — the power is wise, not omnipotent, and time does not run backward. But it would do what a gardener does when a plant is losing its roots: it would redirect nourishment. Deep beneath the Tablelands, in the sub-strata where no drill has reached, the power would touch the gestating process and accelerate it — gently, the way you might cup your hands around a candle flame in the wind, not to make it larger but to ensure it survives. The Walker's heat-residue would begin to regenerate at a rate that outpaces extraction. Theite would still be mined. But the Walker would no longer depend on the existing seams, because new ones would form, slowly, in deeper strata, like a tree sending out new roots when the old ones are cut.

This would take a thousand years. The power would begin it and would not see its completion, and this would be fine, because a wise power does not require completion to justify beginning.

**Second**, it would *change the fog.*

Only slightly. The residents of Kethrine have always known that the fog has texture, that it clings or passes through, that it seems to carry whispered shapes. The power would make this real, in a way that was safe. On certain nights — not many, perhaps once a season — the fog would thicken in the alleys and streets of Kethrine, and if you stood still within it, you would feel, with absolute certainty, that you were being *accompanied.* Not watched. Accompanying. As though the fog were a medium through which something vast and old and slow was walking beside you, matching your pace, carrying a portion of your weight.

The children would feel it first. They would stop in the middle of the street and tilt their heads, and the fog would press gently against their cheeks, and they would smile without knowing why. The old-timers would nod, because they would recognize it — they had always felt it, but now it would have substance.

No one would be frightened. That is important. The power would ensure that what was felt was felt as comfort, not as dread.

**Third**, it would *leave a single mark.*

In the schoolroom where the teacher reads from the battered atlas, a crack would appear in the basalt wall — not a flaw, but a deliberate fissure, thin as a thread, running from floor to ceiling. Through it, the fog would enter. It would not dissipate inside, as fog normally does in warm rooms. It would persist. It would pool on the floor in a layer no deeper than a breath and would remain, night and day, season after season.

The children would notice it first, of course. They would put their hands in it. They would describe it. One of them — a girl, perhaps, with dirt under her fingernails and a talent for noticing things — would describe it as *warm* and *like walking on a sound.* The teacher would not believe her. But the teacher would keep the crack open, because the schoolroom would become the warmest room in Kethrine, and the children who learned there would learn faster and dream more clearly than any generation before them.

Over time — decades, generations — the children of that schoolroom would begin to *understand* the Tablelands. Not scientifically, not at first, but in the way that you understand a parent's mood: through a thousand small observations that accumulate into something like language. They would sense the Walker's route. They would know, without being told, which seams ofite were safe to extract and which were not. They would develop a vocabulary for the stages of the fog. They would write, eventually, a new kind of geology — one that treated the stone not as a resource but as a living process, and the mining not as extraction but as a conversation.

**Fourth**, which is really the first and the last: the power would *ask a question.*

Not aloud. Not to anyone in particular. The question would enter the fog one night, riding the condensation that formed on the windows of the Flatline at three in the morning, and it would exist there for exactly one breath before it dissolved. But the question would be structured so that the Tablelands themselves — basalt and water and moss and Walker — could answer it, in their own time, in their own language of erosion and exudation and slow becoming.

The question would be this:

*What do you want to become?*

And the answer, which would take ten thousand years to arrive, which would come not as words but as a shift in the orientation of the grooves, a new color in the moss, a third note in the hum that the miners hear through their boots — that answer would be the beginning of the next conversation.

---

## V. What Does Not Change

The road would still climb for hours along the scar in the basalt. The switchbacks would still end abruptly. The Tablelands would still stretch out like the palm of an enormous hand, flat against the sky, flattering nothing. The fog would still creep through Kethrine's alleys, and the conveyor belts would still clank, and the children would still run through the narrow streets, and the thick stew would still taste more of fuel than flavor.

Theite would still be mined. The city below would still drink its light. The three-toed prints would still appear in the wet mud after heavy condensation nights, and they would still vanish by morning.

What the power would add was not a revelation. It was an *attention.* A sustained, gentle, unblinking attention to the fact that this place — this vast, flat, fog-draped, slowly-becoming place — was not merely a setting for human industry but a process in its own right, with its own ambitions, its own timeline, its own quiet desire to become what it was going to become.

And the wisdom of the power was this: that the best thing you can do for a world that is still becoming is not to stop its becoming, nor to accelerate it, nor to protect it from itself, but to *witness it faithfully* — and to leave behind just enough of yourself that the world knows, in whatever dim and mineral way it knows anything, that it has been seen.

That is the gift. Not power. Not knowledge. 

*Witness.*

The fog presses against your coat as you walk alone. It feels like company. It is company. It has always been company. Welcome to the Tablelands of Qerreth. Sit down. The stone is still speaking, and it does not mind if you listen.
