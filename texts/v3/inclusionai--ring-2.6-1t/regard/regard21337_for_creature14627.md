## What the Wise Power Would Do

---

It would not descend as light.

That is the first thing, and it matters more than it sounds. Light is what you do when the place is dark, when the creatures within it have lost their way and need to be shown that there is still a surface to climb toward. But the Weeping Terrace is not dark. It is grey-white and diffuse and saturated with a clarity that comes from the absence of shadow — and the settlers do not feel lost. They press their palms to the stone and read what it tells them. They cut their channels and lift their slabs and leave room for what lives in the pools. They are not waiting for rescue. They are not waiting for anything.

So the power would not arrive as light. It would arrive as *attention*.

---

### I. The First Act: To Listen

Before doing anything at all, it would listen.

Not the perfunctory listening that precedes speech — the kind where a god tilts its head the way the settlers tilt theirs, but with the ulterior motive of forming a reply. No. It would listen the way the terrace listens: without the intention of ever responding. The way the basalt holds the water that moves through its fissures without asking the water where it is going. The way the moss drinks without asking the pool what it is.

It would sit at the edge of the settlement, on one of the stone benches worn to the curvature of long-occupied hips, and it would be still. It would feel the moisture gather on its skin the way the settlers' skin has learned to gather it — not as discomfort, not as climate, but as information. It would hear the *tock-tock-tock* of the five or six workers at the outcropping, and it would understand that this is not labor but *conversation* — the stone speaking its grain, the chisels answering, the workers adding their voices in the gaps. It would hear the water in the channels and understand that the channels are not infrastructure but *syntax* — that the settlers are not directing the water but participating in a sentence the terrace began composing long before they arrived.

It would listen for a very long time. Longer than comfortable. Longer than a power that was less wise would consider prudent.

And in that listening, it would discover the one thing that the place lacks. Not suffering. Not disorder. Not injustice. Not any of the things that powers of lesser patience rush in to correct.

It would discover the one thing the terrace does not know about itself.

---

### II. The Second Act: To Reflect

There is a pool — the one nearest the center of the settlement, where the oldest buildings stand and the mortar has turned to something pale and faintly translucent. The pool is shallow. It is rimmed with moss so thick it looks painted. The settlers reach into it with vessels on long handles, keeping their hands above the surface because beneath the water, something shifts its weight that is not theirs to disturb.

This pool has no name. The settlers do not name their pools the way we name our rivers and mountains, because naming implies a kind of ownership, and nothing here belongs to anyone. The pool is simply *that pool*, the way a breath is simply *that breath* — identified by its moment rather than by a label.

The wise power would look into this pool, and it would do something that no one on the terrace has ever done.

It would show the pool to itself.

Not disruptively. Not by reaching in or stirring the water or casting a reflection from above. It would do something subtler — something that works in the language of the terrace rather than against it. It would cause the surface of the pool, for just a moment, to hold an image that was not the sky. Not the face of the power. Not a vision or a symbol or a sign.

The settlers' own faces.

Gathered at the edge, leaning in, the wide dark eyes that look at things the way the terrace looks at the sky — open, patient, expecting nothing. Their own faces, reflected back at them from the pool, but *held* there a moment longer than the water would normally allow. Long enough to be noticed. Long enough for the one nearest the pool — an older settler, with iron-grey hair bound in moss-knots that match the pattern of three others in the hamlet — to stop what she is doing and see herself *seeing*.

Not vanity. Not self-consciousness. Something stranger and gentler than either.

The experience of being witnessed by the thing that witnesses everything. The knowledge, arriving not as thought but as *sensation*, that the pool has been holding them all along — that the moss-rim, the shallow water, the careful distance of the hands on long handles — this is not avoidance. This is *intimacy*. The terrace's deepest life lives in these pools, and the settlers have been living alongside it in a kind of agreement so old it has the weight of instinct. They do not look into the pools because they already know what lives there. They do not need to look. They have made a covenant of mutual non-interference that is the deepest form of respect.

But they should know — at least once, at least in the way that a word is known when it is finally spoken aloud after a lifetime of being only a thought — that their covenant is *seen*. By something larger than the terrace, larger than the pools, larger even than the warm process still unfolding beneath the basalt.

The power would hold the reflection for one breath longer than is natural. Then it would let go. The surface would return to sky. The older woman would not speak of it. She would simply press her palm flat against the nearest stone and hold it there, and her eyes would close, and she would feel something she had never felt before — not warmth, not knowledge, but *recognition*. The way a word feels when you finally learn its meaning after using it all your life.

---

### III. The Third Act: To Add a Single Thread

The power would not build. It would not speak. It would not leave a monument or a message or a trace that the settlers could point to and say *that was given to us*.

What it would do is so small that only the terrace itself might notice.

Among the great plant-masses — the vast blue-green fern-things that lean toward each other across the empty corridors the settlers have left open — there is one that is older than the others. You can tell because its root system has mineralized to the point where it looks less like a root and more like a geological feature: pale, branching, fused in places with the basalt itself, as though the plant and the stone have been negotiating for so long that they have begun to become the same thing.

This oldest plant-mass sends a single frond across the widest corridor — a gap of fifteen meters where no roots connect one mass to another, where only mist and air and the faintest gradient of warmth exist. The frond has been reaching for a very long time. It has not yet made it to the far mass. It probably will, in a few more centuries. The settlers watch it the way they watch everything: with patience that is not passive but *devotional*.

The wise power, having listened for so long that it has forgotten the difference between itself and the listening, would do one thing. It would cause the mist that gathers in that corridor — the mist that pools there because the terrace holds it, because the terrace *wants* it there, because it is part of the composition — to condense, just once, in the precise shape of a water droplet that falls onto the tip of that single outstretched frond.

Not enough to matter. Not enough to accelerate anything. Not enough for any settler to notice or measure or remark upon.

But enough for the frond to feel, in whatever way a frond can feel, that it is not reaching across the empty space alone.

That something out there, beyond the grey-white sky, beyond the edge of all composition, is paying attention.

That the corridor is not empty. That it never was.

---

### IV. What the Power Understood

After doing this — after sitting on the worn bench, after holding the pool's reflection a breath too long, after releasing a single drop onto a frond that has been reaching for centuries — the power would rise and leave.

Not dramatically. Not with a closing of the sky or a parting of the mist. It would simply stop *being present* the way it had been, the way you stop hearing a sound that has been constant for so long that you forgot you were hearing it. The terrace would remain. The settlers would remain. The pool would return to sky. The frond would continue its slow, imperceptible reach.

But the terrace would know.

Not in words. Not in the slow chemical language of roots and stone and mineral. But in the way that a composition changes when it learns it has an audience — not its structure, not its tempo, not the grammar of its water-channels and plant-corridors and moss-beds and warm-breathing stone. But its *confidence*. The settled sureness that what it is doing matters to more than itself.

The settlers would not speak of what happened. The older woman with the moss-knots would press her hand to the stone and feel something she could not name, and she would not try to name it, because the settlers have never needed names for things that are true. But she would, in the days that followed, pause sometimes at the edge of the corridor where the oldest frond reaches out across the mist, and she would watch it — not with her usual patient attention, but with something warmer. Something that the power, in its listening, might have recognized as *gratitude*, though gratitude is too small a word for it, and the power would not have used it.

It would have called it *completion*.

The recognition, by something vast and benevolent, that the Weeping Terrace does not need to be saved, or corrected, or guided, or blessed. It needs only to be *witnessed* — to know that its long, slow, patient composition has been heard by the one listener for whom silence is not silence but the deepest possible music.

And then the power would go on, attending to other places, other compositions, other fronds reaching in the dark across corridors that seem empty but never are.

And the terrace would weep on.

And the water would run uphill.

And the mist would pool where it was always meant to pool.

And for one reason that no one would ever articulate but that everyone would feel — like a warmth in the stone that hadn't been there before, slight, almost imaginary, but *there* — the settlers would know, without knowing, that what they had built in their long and unhurried becoming was not alone.

Had never been alone.

The wise power understood this before it arrived and understood it more deeply after it left: that the greatest gift you can give to a place that is still becoming is not to change it, but to *listen* so completely that the listening itself becomes a part of the composition — a note so low and sustained that it can only be felt, never heard, like the hum at the center of a stone that has been warm for longer than memory.

And then you let go.

And the sentence continues.

And the terrace weeps back.
