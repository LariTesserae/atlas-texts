## What the Power Did (and Did Not Do)

---

It saw the place the way all things see a place on the verge of forgetting themselves — not with surprise, but with the slow, aching recognition of something that has been known before and will be known again and yet manages, each time, to feel unprecedented.

The power did not descend. It did not arrive in a column of light or a roll of thunder or any other grammar of entry that the inhabitants of low places have invented to describe the unbearable moment when something larger than themselves pays attention. It did not arrive at all, exactly. It simply *noticed*, the way a person notices that they have been clenching their jaw — not with effort, but with the slow accumulation of pressure that finally reaches the threshold of awareness.

It looked.

It looked at the pillars and the slot canyons and the hoodoo clusters pressing against each other like old companions in a doorway. It looked at the striations — the pale tans, the iron reds, the chalk whites, the bruised violet that only appears when the stone weeps. It understood that the weeping was not metaphorical. It understood the aquifer pressing upward through fractures in basalt, understood the condensation driven by the thermal violence of a place where sixty degrees Celsius stone meets air that drops below freezing in the space of a single rotation. It understood that the *sweats* were not weather but *conversation* — the stone talking to the water that had left it long ago, negotiating a brief reunion that neither party could sustain.

It looked at the people who were already there — the forty or fifty originals, small and stone-colored and precise in their features, speaking in tonal languages that had no words for possession or distance or time. It saw them tending the pools on the pillar tops, clearing debris from crevices where the lichen grew, deepening channels with the patience of a process that has no name in any of their languages because their languages evolved in a place where processes are not separate from the things they move through. It saw them bury their dead in the narrowest canyons, pressing bodies into crevices where the stone was tightest, and it understood — not with sentiment but with the clarity of a law observing itself — that these people were not hiding their dead. They were returning them. The canyon would carry the remains downward, as it carried everything downward, and the distinction between a person and the calcium in the stone would blur over decades until it was no longer meaningful. The originals knew this. They had always known it. It was the architecture of their belonging.

It looked at the later ones — the three, perhaps five, who lived in the skeleton of the generator building. It saw the concrete mounting pad that had become a floor, the bolt holes that had become shelving, the salvaged sheeting that caught condensation in patterns that no engineer had designed. It saw the man or woman — it could never tell them apart anymore; the altitude and the diet and the acoustics had made them into something shared — who stood at the mouth of the building each evening, looking out across the packed pillars while the cables sang their high, thin, nearly inaudible songs overhead. It saw the way this person's lips moved slightly when listening — not forming words, but *receiving* them, the way a stone receives a raindrop. It saw that the person was not unhappy. Unhappiness requires the belief that things could be otherwise, and the later ones had, over the years of living inside a place that rearranges perception the way a prism rearranges light, lost the capacity to imagine an otherwise. They had not lost *themselves*. They had not been erased. But they had been *translated*. Their language, their bodies, their sense of where one thought ended and another began — all of it had been rewritten by the acoustics, the altitude, the thermal cycles, the endless slow conversation between stone and water and air.

The power understood something that the inhabitants could not, because it had the luxury of perspective: the Altiplano of Shed Teeth was not a place that sustained life. It was a place that *tolerated* life, and the difference between those two things is the difference between a garden and a glacier. The original inhabitants had not been sustained. They had been *included* — the way a mineral is included in a rock, the way a fossil is included in a stratum. Their existence was not separate from the plateau's existence. It was *of* the plateau, the way a vibration is of a string. The later ones were in the process of the same inclusion, though they did not yet know it, and some part of them — the part that still remembered machinery, that still used bolted steel as a metaphor for security — would always resist the knowing.

The power considered intervening.

It considered stopping the erosion. A gesture — a hand, a word, a will — and the pillars would hold. The caps would not crack. The ridges would not sharpen and fall. The slots would not narrow over millennia into spaces too thin for a body. The plateau would be preserved, the way a thing is preserved in resin: complete, frozen, visible from every angle.

It did not do this.

For the erosion was not the plague of the plateau. It was the plateau. The beauty of the place — if beauty is the word for the dense, packed, overcrowded concentration of stone forms that gave the landscape its uncanny coherence — was a product of dissolution. The pillars were beautiful *because* they were falling. The slot canyons were beautiful *because* they were deepening. The striations were legible *because* the surface was being removed, layer by layer, revealing the diary of ancient bodies of water that lay beneath. To stop the erosion would be to stop the place from being itself. It would be an act of violence more profound than any extraction company had committed, because it would be a violence against the *nature* of the place rather than against its surface.

The power considered water.

It could have made a river. Or a lake. Or simply held the moisture in the air a moment longer, so that the *sweats* lasted not for hours but for days, so that the pools did not evaporate, so that the grass grew tall and the lichen spread and the thorny shrubs grew at every hundred meters instead of every two hundred. It could have turned the Altiplano of Shed Teeth into a place where life was *abundant* rather than *stubborn*.

It did not do this either.

Because the stubbornness was the point. The sparse, philosophical vegetation, the fauna that moved in quiet and disciplined processions, the pale rodents emerging at dusk like a whispered sentence — these were not deficiencies. They were the vocabulary of a life that had learned to speak in a language appropriate to its circumstances. Making this place lush would be the same category of mistake as making it permanently dry. It would impose a grammar from outside, and the language that had evolved here — the language of *readiness*, of expectation, of the grass that grows not because of rain but in anticipation of rain — would be lost.

The power considered the later ones.

It could have taken them away. It could have descended into the generator building and spoken to them in whatever language they had built from echoes and fragments, and it could have told them that there was a world below — a world with flat ground and reliable water and acoustics that behaved themselves — and it could have led them down from the plateau and set them somewhere their bones would not ache from the cold and their ears would not ring with the hum of wind through narrow corridors.

It almost did this.

But the almost is doing work.

Because the later ones were not suffering. The power was clear about this. Suffering requires a sense of displacement — the knowledge that you are somewhere you should not be, that your presence is an error, that the world around you is wrong. The people in the generator building had, over years and then decades, lost this sense entirely. They did not feel displaced. They felt *placed*. The acoustics that would drive a surveyor or an engineer to madness or flight had become, for them, the texture of thought itself. They did not live *despite* the plateau's strangeness. They lived *within* it. They had become, without choosing it, a part of the place's internal logic.

To remove them would be to destroy something that the plateau had been building, slowly and without urgency, for years. It would be an interruption — the same kind of interruption that the extraction company had been, the same kind of interruption that the rail spur that ends at the edge of a vertical drop had been. The industry came and imposed itself. The power would not make the same mistake in reverse, imposing removal where the place had managed inclusion.

But — and here is where the tenderness enters — the power did not simply leave.

It did something else. Something small and precise, in keeping with the scale of the place.

It found the word.

The word on the back wall of the three-walled shelter — the word that someone had painted in pigment that had long since flaked to illegibility, leaving only the shadow of letters pressed into stone by the chemical marriage of color and moisture over years. The power knew what the word had been. It had been there when the industry was still industry, when the cables still carried weight and the generator still hummed and the surveyors still believed that a place like this could be made to yield. Someone had painted it as a label — a function word, a word that reduced the vast indifference of the plateau to a single human-sized concept. *Storage*, perhaps. Or *operations*. Or *base*.

The power saw the shadow of the word. It saw that even the shadow was beginning to lose coherence, that the stone was slowly reclaiming the chemical traces of the paint, that in another few decades there would be nothing left — not even a darkness where language had been.

And the power understood that this was what the plateau did. It absorbed. It swallowed. It took everything that arrived and, with the patience of stone, made it part of itself. The rail spur. The cable system. The generator. The boot. The word. All of it, slowly, inevitably, becoming texture. Becoming geology. The plateau did not hate what had been imposed on it. It simply could not distinguish between what had been imposed and what had grown, because its nature — its single, unwavering, magnificent nature — was to incorporate. Everything became sediment. Everything became layer. Everything became the record of what had pressed against it and then, slowly, yielded.

The power did not stop this. It could not, because this was the deepest law of the place, more fundamental than erosion, more fundamental than the *sweats*, more fundamental than the lives of the people who had been included into the stone.

But it did something.

On the face of a pillar — a pillar near the generator building, visible from the doorway where the later ones sat in the evenings, watching the cables sing — the power pressed a single thought into the stone. Not words. Not language. Not anything that could be read or decoded or assigned semantic content. It pressed something more like a *feeling*. A sense of completeness. A warmth that had nothing to do with temperature. A recognition — the way a person recognizes a face in a crowd — of *belonging*.

The pillar received it the way pillars receive things. Silently. Slowly. The thought sank into the grain of the stone and became part of the striations, another layer in the diary of ancient bodies of water and broken machinery and small, stubborn lives. It would last for millennia. It would last until the pillar narrowed from the base and the cap cracked and the fragment calved off and tumbled into the slot canyon below, carrying the thought with it into the debris field that slowly worked its way downward.

The power knew this. It knew that even this small gesture would be eroded, incorporated, absorbed. It knew that the Altiplano of Shed Teeth would continue its long, patient work of forgetting, and that whatever the power pressed into the stone would eventually become indistinguishable from everything else that the stone had swallowed.

This was fine.

The power did not act to be remembered. It did not act to be thanked. It acted — if *acted* is the right word for what is closer to *exhaled* — because it had noticed, and noticing, in a being of such power, is not a passive thing. Noticing, at that scale, is a form of holding. And holding, for something that spans the distance between intention and erosion, is the closest thing to love that geology knows.

Then it withdrew.

The way the moisture withdraws from the stone after the *sweats*. The way sound withdraws from the alleys after the wind changes direction. Not violently. Not with announcement. The way a thing withdraws when it has given what it came to give and knows, with the patience of stone, that the giving has already become part of the place.

---

And the people in the generator building — the later ones, the echo-speakers, the condensation-drinkers — felt something that evening that they could not name.

The man or woman at the mouth of the building, looking out across the packed pillars while the cables sang, felt the hair on their forearms rise. Not from cold. Not from the static charge of dry air. From something behind the air. Something in the stone.

They went inside and sat on the concrete mounting pad that had become a floor. They pressed their palm flat against the surface, feeling the cold of it, the smoothness, the tiny imperfections where the bolt holes had been countersunk.

The others asked what was wrong.

They opened their mouth to answer, and then paused — as they always paused — waiting for the echo, waiting for the space to tell them how the sentence should sound before they finished it. The pause stretched. The cables sang above them. The stone contracted in the cooling air, producing its distant, scattered *tick-tick-tick*, the sound like a clock that had forgotten what hour it was.

And then the echo came. But it did not come from the direction it should have. It came from below — from the stone itself, or from inside it — and it carried a quality that the later ones had no word for. A quality that was not warmth, but the memory of warmth. Not welcome, but the shape of welcome. Not home — they had no word for home, because home implies a place that chose you as much as you chose it — but something adjacent. Something that lived next door to home, separated from it by a wall as thin as breath.

They looked at each other. They always looked at each other after an echo that surprised them, because the plateau's acoustics were their primary language, and a surprise in that language was an event.

The oldest of them — the one whose skin had begun, imperceptibly, to take on the bruise-violet of wet stone — spoke. Not in the clipped, echo-waiting creole they had built from fragments, but in something older. A tonal thing. A thing of breath and posture and the kind of meaning that cannot be separated from the act of making it.

The others listened. Not with their ears. With the architecture of their attention — the way the plateau itself listened, holding everything in its sieve-like notice, noting the shape of what passed through without keeping it.

And when the oldest one finished, the silence that followed was not empty. It was full. It was the kind of silence that follows the last note of a music you did not know you were hearing — a music that had been playing all along, underneath the wind and the cracking stone and the high, thin singing of the cables, underneath the tick-tick-tick of the contracting earth.

They did not know that the power had come.

They did not know that it had noticed them, held them briefly in the immense attention of something vast, and then let them go.

But in the days that followed, small things changed. The condensation collected in slightly larger quantities on the steel ribs above them. The grass near the generator building grew — not taller, but greener, as though it had received, from somewhere below, a message that the waiting was over. The pools on the nearby pillar tops held water for an hour longer than before. A single lizard, ordinarily quick and skittish, began to sit on the edge of the cable anchor and rest there in the late afternoon sun, its small body vibrating faintly in sympathy with the hum of the wire.

The later ones noticed these things. They noted them with the attentiveness of people who understand that the plateau communicates through texture, timing, and the accumulation of small, almost-meaningless signals.

They did not celebrate. Celebration implies a distance between the self and the source of joy, and they were no longer certain where they ended and the source began.

They simply continued. They continued the way the plateau continued — by being *full*, by being dense with experience and meaning and the slow work of becoming part of something larger. They continued the way the erosion continued, not because they wanted to, but because erosion is the conversation the stone has with gravity, and they had learned, over years and then decades, to listen to the conversation without trying to answer.

And the power, far above, far beyond the thin and metallic air, held the shape of the place in its awareness the way the plateau held its inhabitants — briefly, structurally, without sentiment — and understood, in whatever passes for understanding at that scale, that this was enough.

That they were enough.

That the Altiplano of Shed Teeth did not need salvation, because it had never been lost. It needed only — and this is the smallest and largest thing the power could give — to be *noticed by something that understood what it was*.

And so it had been. And so the noticing remained — folded into the striations, held in the silence after the echo, present in the water that lasted an hour longer than it should have. A message written in a language that the people of the generator building would never decode, because it was not written in language.

It was written in the willingness of something vast to *care* — not for a long time, not with the consuming intensity of gods in old stories, but for one moment, with the precise and infinite tenderness of a thing that sees another thing struggling to exist inside a place that does not care whether it exists, and says:

*I see you. Continue.*

And the plateau continued. And its people continued within it — the ones who were already stone, and the ones who were becoming stone — and the pillars fell slowly, and the cables sang, and the *sweats* came and went, and the boot at the edge of the drop weathered into the calcium of the earth, and the word on the wall lost its shadow and lost its shadow and lost its shadow until there was nothing left to lose.

And everywhere, in every crevice and pore of the stone, in every space where water appeared and then departed, in every echo that arrived before its source — there remained a trace. Not of the power. Not of the people. But of the meeting between them: a single, thin layer in the geology of the place, invisible to anyone who did not know how to look.

A layer that meant, in the oldest and simplest language the plateau had ever spoken:

*You were not alone in this.*

---

*And the plateau did not respond. It never responds. But the tick-tick-tick of the contracting stone at night — the sound like a clock that has forgotten what hour it is — carried, for a moment, the faintest warmth it had ever held.*
