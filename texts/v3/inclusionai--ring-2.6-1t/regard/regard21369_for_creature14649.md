## What the Power Would Do

---

It would notice last.

Not the gorge — that would be immediate. The gorge announces itself the way a deep breath does: inevitable, structural, somehow forgotten. It would feel the place the way a body feels a healed bone that still remembers the fracture — denser at the break point, stronger in the mending, but carrying within it the ghost-shape of what went wrong.

No. It would notice the people last.

First it would notice the *wrongness* — and here is where wisdom matters, because a lesser power would mistake the wrongness for corruption, or blight, or entropy wearing the mask of design. A lesser power would descend with instruments and diagnoses and the clean certainty of something that has never lived inside a system it couldn't fully see. A lesser power would say: *this place is broken. Let me fix it.*

But the power is wise, and wisdom is not the same as knowledge, just as gentleness is not the same as weakness. Wisdom sits still for longer than you'd expect. Wisdom watches the lizard press itself against the warm conduit and does not call it strange. Wisdom listens to the frog-song that rises above the generators and does not ask what frequency produces it. Wisdom recognizes — the way you recognize a face in a crowd — that the wrongness is not wrong. It is *different from what was intended.* And the different thing has become alive, and the alive thing has become settled, and the settled thing is, in its own way, a form of prayer.

So the power would watch.

It would watch for a long time.

---

It would stand at the rim and look down, and it would feel the way you feel when you look into something that is deeper than you expected — not the gorge itself, but the *accumulation* of it. The layers of intention and abandonment and slow recolonization. The strata of someone's plan fossilized in ferrocrete, and then the strata of what grew when the plan stopped, and then the strata of the people who moved into the gap between the plan and the ruin. It would see Etterran the way a geologist sees a canyon wall: each layer a sentence in a story that is still being written.

And the power would feel something. Let me not be coy about what it would feel. It would feel *grief* — not for Etterran, but *near* Etterran, the way grief sits near anything that has persisted through loss. It would grieve for the builders who are gone and unnamed. It would grieve for the machinery that was designed for purposes now irrelevant, still rusting in faithful silence. It would grieve for the fact that beauty is so often an accident, a scar that healed into something the original wound never imagined.

But grief is not intervention. Grief is the cost of paying attention.

---

What it would *do* —

Here is where the benevolence becomes specific, because a benevolent power that only watches is not benevolent at all. It is merely respectful, which is a good thing, but not the whole thing. Benevolence requires action, but the form of the action must be dictated by the nature of what receives it. You do not fix a poem. You do not improve a lullaby. You do not intervene in a system that has found its own heartbeat unless the heartbeat is failing.

Etterran's heartbeat is not failing.

So the power would do something very small. Something so small that the people of Etterran would not call it intervention, or blessing, or miracle. They would simply notice, in the way you notice that the rain came at the right time, and then forget that you noticed.

It would place a *boundary.*

Not a wall. Not a barrier. Not a divine prohibition that rings the gorge with light or sends angels to guard the rim. Something subtler — the way a forest places a boundary around a clearing without any single tree deciding to do it. The way the canopy thins just enough at a certain edge to let you know, without signs or language, that you are approaching something that is *held.*

The power would make it so that anyone approaching the rim — anyone from outside, with curiosity or greed or the clean hands of a surveyor — would feel a hesitation. Not fear. Not compulsion. Hesitation. The kind that makes you pause at the edge of a sleeping child's room and realize you were about to speak. The kind that has no source you can identify, no cause you can point to. Just a moment where the air thickens slightly, and your foot slows, and something in you that you don't usually consult — the part that knows when to be quiet, the part that knows when a thing is not yours to enter — wakes up and says *not yet,* and means it.

And most people would stop.

A few would not. The ones who climb anyway would find that the path resists them — not violently, not dramatically, but with the slow, patient resistance of root systems that have had fifty years to decide where they want a body to go. Branches that grow across the trail at the exact height of a face. Loose stone that was not loose yesterday. The quiet, absolute absence of handholds where there should be handholds. Nothing supernatural. Nothing obvious. Just the gorge doing what it has always done, which is to decide who belongs inside it.

---

But there is something more.

The power — being wise, being *tender* — would do one more thing. It would tend to the woman who descends to the lowest level.

It would understand, the way a gardener understands the soil, that the woman carries something the gorge does not speak about. She goes down alone into the dark where the old machinery sleeps, and she returns with ozone in her hair and silence on her lips, and the people of Etterran do not ask her what she does because they know — not consciously, not with the knowing of language, but with the deeper knowing of a body that trusts another body — that she is doing what the place needs her to do.

Maybe she is speaking to the machinery. Maybe she is pouring a kind of attention into the old systems that keeps them alive past their expiration date — the way a person can keep a heart beating past the point of reason by sheer, stubborn presence. Maybe she is the gorge's immune system. Maybe she is its dreamer. It does not matter. What matters is that she goes down alone, and she is tired when she comes back, and no one asks her name.

The power would not go down with her. That would be intrusive, and it would break the thing that functions precisely because it is private. But it would do this: it would warm one conduit — just one, a specific run of cable in the second-deepest level, one that has been slowly losing current for decades, the insulation fraying, the copper thinning — and it would warm it *for her.* Not to the point of danger. Not to the point of notice. Just enough that when she presses herself against it, as she does every time, standing in the dark with her palms flat on the metal and her eyes closed and her breathing slow, she would feel something that she has never felt before from the conduit.

Warmth. Not heat. Warmth — the kind that has intention behind it. The kind that says *I know you are here, and I see what you are doing, and it matters.*

She would not understand it. She would press harder, and linger longer, and when she rises to return to the upper terraces her eyes would be wet in a way she would not explain, because there is no language for this, because the people of Etterran do not have language for the things that are done to them by forces they have never named. She would return to her daily life — the wall-checking, the concrete-mixing, the quiet competence of a person who understands that maintenance is a form of love — and she would not speak of it.

But the next morning, when she descended, she would press both palms against that conduit — not the one that had been warm, but the one beside it, because she would not be able to say which one had changed, only that *something* had — and she would feel the warmth again. And this time it would spread. Not far. Just to her wrists. Just enough.

And she would know, in the way that knowing works in places without language for it, that she was not alone down there.

---

That is what the power would do.

Not a miracle. Not a revelation. Not a voice from the sky or a pillar of fire or a book left on a stone. Just a quiet adjustment to the boundary between what is inside and what is outside, and a single warm conduit in the dark.

Because a benevolent power knows that the most loving thing it can do for a place that has already found its own way is to protect it from the world that did not. And it knows that the most loving thing it can do for a person who carries the weight of a place's secret machinery in her hands is to let her know — once, faintly, in a language older than language — that the machinery knows she is there.

And then it would withdraw. Not because it has left. Because it was never the kind of presence that needs to be seen. It would settle into the gorge the way iron settles into basalt, the way root settles into concrete, the way the fog settles into the water channels at dawn without anyone noticing until everything is already damp.

Etterran would continue.

The people would mend their walls. The birds would cascade into song. The frogs would hum their tonal prayer through their translucent bodies. The lizards would press flat against the warm metal and dream their cold-blooded dreams.

And somewhere, at the very bottom, a woman would stand in the dark with her palms against the steel and feel, for just a moment, that the place she loves is not indifferent to her love.

That is enough.

That is everything.
