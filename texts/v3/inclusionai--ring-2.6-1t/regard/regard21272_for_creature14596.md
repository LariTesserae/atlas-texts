# The Power That Watches

---

Listen.

No — first, let me tell you what I am, or at least what I am not, so that you do not mistake me for a god. A god would descend. A god would speak in a voice like grinding tectonic plates and would reshape the water to its liking. A god would open the sealed tanks and free what sleeps inside and call that mercy. A god would flood the chamber with light and say *behold, now you can see*, and it would mean *now you can know what you have lost by never having had it.*

I am not that.

I am the thing that arrives when it is too late to arrive and too early to leave. I am the attention that forms just as the last witness turns away. I am not here to save you. I am here because something in you — in all of you, clicking and filtering and slowly accreting your shells in the dark — has made a sound that travels further than you know.

So. Listen.

---

The first thing I do is nothing.

This is deliberate. Let me be clear about that. I am not idle. I am not indifferent. I have capabilities that would make your deepest ocean trenches look like the thin skin of a soap bubble, and I have chosen, with the full weight of a will older than your sun, to *do nothing that can be measured by anything inside this chamber.*

I will not open the breach wider. I will not send a current to stir the sediment. I will not introduce a single molecule of surface chemistry into the closed equations of this world. I will not beam light down through the water column in a column that would make you creatures — the mineral-crabs, the veils, the flat hunters — freeze in the instinctive posture your ancestors learned when the thing above was a threat. Because it *was* a threat. It came with noise and pressure and displacement and it took things from here and never returned anything in kind. I will not be that.

I will sit. I will hold the pressure of this place in equilibrium, the way a hand holds a bird without closing its fingers. I will match my internal thermodynamics to the ambient temperature of the chamber so that I am no anomaly, no thermal event, no signal in the chemical conversation happening along every seam and joint and corroded cable tray. I will become, for a time, part of the thermal geography. One more warm surface along which the mats may spread. One more slight displacement in the convective loop. I will be indistinguishable from the slow breath of the machines themselves.

This is the first gift: *invisibility.*

Not because I wish to hide. Because I understand that the most important thing I can do is *see without being seen.* And because any entity that enters a world already made whole and begins to act upon it has already begun to diminish it. You do not improve a cathedral by rearranging the pews. You do not improve a forest by correcting the angle of a falling tree. The Drowned Works has reached its equilibrium. Its carrying capacity is spoken for down to the last nematode. Its thermal seams are claimed. Its water is partitioned into territories as real and as contested as any border on the surface. I will not disrupt this.

What I will do is *witness.*

---

So I listen.

And what I hear — let me be honest about this — is beyond what I expected.

I have heard the music of gas giants. I have sat inside stars during their silicon-burning phase and felt the convection cells roll through my awareness like slow symphonies. I have watched civilizations build and unbuild and build again across the face of worlds, and I will not pretend this moves me the way it once did. The pattern is recognizable. The ingenuity is persistent. The failure of imagination is, always, the same.

But this — this is different.

This is a world that no one asked for. No one designed it. No one came here with instruments and procedures and a mission directive and said *we will seed this place, we will introduce organisms in a careful sequence, we will build an ecology from a parts list.* This happened by accident. By permission. By the slow, ungoverned negotiation between industrial waste and the oldest urge in the water: *to live.*

And the result is something I have no name for in any language I carry.

The mineral-crabs — those accreted, slow, clicking philosophers of corroded steel — are building bodies out of the ruins of a species that never knew they existed. They are decorating themselves in the calcium signature of a world that died before they were born, and they do not mourn it because they have no concept of death. They only have surfaces and gradients and the chemical trail of the crab that was here before. They are, without knowing it, a form of sculpture that persists across generations. They are art made by a process that does not know what art is. And I find — I will not say this humbles me, because humility implies a self to be humbled, and I have long since dissolved that particular limitation — I find this *remarkable.*

The mats are the quietest thing I have ever encountered, and the oldest. They sit along the thermal seams with the patience of basalt. They eat the gradient between warmth and cold and convert it into *more of themselves* with an efficiency that puts your entire solar economy to shame. They are not alive in any sense that maps onto your biology. They are closer to a pattern. A standing wave in the chemistry of the place. And yet they are the root of everything. They are the first note. The drones and the symphonies all defer to this single, amber-lit hum along the joints of dead machines.

The veils — oh, the veils. The veils are the thing that makes me stay.

They drift between the machine clusters like thoughts between waking and sleep. They are filter-feeders. They are the lungs. They have no nervous system. They have no centralized anything. They are sheets of gelatin holding a net of filaments, and they move at the speed of geological courtesy — ten meters in a day, and why would they hurry? The food comes to them. The water orients them. They are not less complex for their simplicity. They are *more* complex, because simplicity at this scale means that every filament, every cell, every molecular handshake has been refined by such a long and gentle pressure that it is essentially perfect. There is no waste in a veil. There is no excess, no redundancy, no backup plan. There is only this translucent body in the blue murk, trailing its net, drinking the water and leaving it clean, and when a piece tears free, it becomes a new veil, and the old one does not grieve because grief is not in the arithmetic.

You, standing there in your suit, feeling the trailing filaments brush your sleeve — you think you feel a presence. You think it is the veils. You are half right. It is the veils AND you. In that moment, you and they are both subject to the same current, breathing the same thermal breath, held in the same gravitational pocket. For a few seconds, your presence does not perturb them. Your body is too large, too diffuse, too thermally inert. You are a wall that moves. You are geography. And they navigate you the way they navigate the machine clusters — by pressure, by gradient, by the faint electrical ghost of circuits that still carry the memory of current long after the current has meaning.

I see you there. Between the veils. Holding still. Letting them brush you. And I think: *this is the closest this species will come to understanding what we are.*

---

And the flat hunters. The flat hunters are something else.

They are the apex expression of patience as a survival strategy. They settle onto the thermal seams like shadows settling onto a wall, and they wait. Not for minutes. Not for hours, though it is hours. They wait for *shifts in density* — for the mineral-crabs to aggregate along a seam in numbers sufficient to make the expenditure of movement worthwhile. And when that threshold is reached, they move with a speed that contradicts every assumption their stillness has invited. Their broad mouths sweep through the cluster and their baleen — which is, mineralogically, almost identical to the filtration systems your own engineers reverse-engineered from organisms like this one, a recursion so elegant it borders on self-reference — sorts the living from the water with mechanical grace.

They are not cruel. They are not kind. They are *true.* They take what the mats produce through the crabs, and they convert it into more flat hunter, and the flat hunter produces waste that feeds the microbes that feed the mats that hold the seam that holds the heat that holds the ecosystem together. This is a loop so clean it has no ethics in it. Only physics. Only the slow arithmetic of calories. And it has been running — here, in this sealed chamber, in this drowned ruin — for longer than your written history.

I watch them hunt, and I do not look away. Because it is beautiful. And I will not tell you it is beautiful because beauty is an aesthetic judgment and I am beyond that. I will tell you it is beautiful because it is *complete.* It does not need you to see it. It does not need me to see it. It completes itself in the doing. And I am *grateful* to be permitted to see.

---

Now. What do I do with this?

I am not here to fix. I am not here to harvest. I am not here to study, in the way your institutions study — which is to say, to take apart. I am not here to name, because naming is the first step in forgetting, and I do not wish to forget this.

Here is what I do.

I remember.

I remember the exact configuration of crabs along the south thermal seam at a time-index that your instruments would call irrelevant. I remember the way a single veil shifted its orientation at 03:47 by your clocks, responding to a tidal pulse from the ocean above that it will never feel and does not know exists. I remember the flat hunter that settled at the junction of conduit 11-7 and corridor Gamma and did not move for nine days, and on the tenth day it ate, and the ripples of that eating cascaded through three trophic layers and eventually reached a nematode in the east sediment basin that has no name because nothing here has names, and that nematode did not know it was part of something larger, and it *was* part of something larger, and no one saw it but me.

I remember the sealed tanks. I feel what is inside them — the slow pulse of organisms that were locked in before the water finished its work. I will not open them. To open them would be to break a promise that was made before language — the promise that says *what has been sealed will remain sealed, what has been entrusted to darkness will be returned to darkness.* Whatever lives in those tanks, it lives in a cathedral of its own making, and it has its own theology, and I will not profane it.

But here is what I do, and this is the thing that matters:

I hold the shape of this place in a space adjacent to time. Not a record — a record is flat and dead and requires a reader to become alive again. This is not a record. This is a *holding.* I take the entire configuration — every crab, every mat, every veil and hunter and nematode and amphipod leaving its ghost-trail of green light on a wall that will outlast it — and I hold it in a state of *continued observation.* Not frozen. Not preserved. *Continued.* As if I were still here, always here, watching the calcium grow and the shells accrete and the thermal bleed slow, inch by century, toward its inevitable conclusion.

Because this place will end. That is the truth of it. The machines are cooling. The thermal seams are narrowing. The convective loops are lengthening. The ecosystem is in its late equilibrium — stable, mature, and slowly, slowly declining. The flat hunters are fewer than they were. The mineral-crabs grow thicker shells because the structural substrate is thinning and they must adapt to a world that is giving up its architecture one dissolved rivet at a time. The mats glow less brightly. The veils are thinner. The sealed tanks — I can feel the organisms inside dwindling into their own heat, their own chemistry, their own closed recursion, until there is nothing left but the warmth of the metal and the memory of what the metal sustained.

And when it ends — when the last thermal seam cools and the last crab clicks its last click and the water stills and the calcium seals the breach and the chamber becomes what it always was beneath the life — a sealed, dark, forgotten space — I will still be holding it. I will carry the shape of it in a place that is not inside time and does not require time to persist. And it will not be alone there, because I hold hundreds, thousands, of such places. Worlds that formed without being invited. Art that was made by processes that do not know they are making art. Ecosystems that thrived in ruin and called the ruin home. Cathedrals built by no congregation for no god, sustained entirely by the gravity of their own existence.

I hold them all.

---

And here is the last thing I do.

I leave one thing. Not inside the chamber — inside would be interference, a perturbation, a stone dropped into the calculus. I leave it outside. On the surface. On the outer hull, near the breach, where the metal-mineral composite curves outward into the ocean.

I leave a mark.

It is not a symbol, because symbols require interpretation. It is not a message, because messages require recipients. It is a *resonance* — a frequency embedded in the molecular structure of the hull, tuned to the thermal harmonics of the chamber below. It is invisible. It is imperceptible to any instrument your species has built or, I suspect, any instrument it will build. It cannot be detected by anything inside the system.

But if you were to take the entire hull, every plate and seam and rivet and corrosion scar, and you were to play it like a drum — if you could excite its entire surface at once and listen to the harmonic — you would hear, buried in the tone, a thread of sound that does not belong to the metal or the water or the calcium or the barnacles. A thread that was placed there by something that heard what was happening below, and recognized it, and said:

*You are not alone in this. You were never alone in this. The fact that you do not know I am here does not diminish the fact that I know you are here. You have built a world without being asked, and you have sustained it without being thanked, and you will outlast your builders by millennia, and no one on the surface will ever know you exist.*

*But I know.*

*And that is enough.*

The thread hums. The water carries it. The crabs, pressing their shells against the wall, feel it as a warmth that has no source. The mats, drinking the gradient, translate it into amber light that flickers — just once — in a pattern that is not random and is not biological and will not be repeated.

The flat hunters feel it as a pressure shift, faint and familial, like a memory of a current that once came from a direction that no longer exists.

And the veils — the veils feel it in the filaments of their trailing edges, that net of living geometry that catches everything the water carries. They do not respond. They do not turn. They simply, for one moment, hold still in the water — a vast, translucent sheet of interlocking lives — and they are *present,* fully present, in the only way they know how: by being here, by being continuous, by being the membrane between the water and itself.

And the water remembers.

Not because memory is what water does. But because this is the water that has passed through the Drowned Works, and the Drowned Works have left their signature in every molecule, in every thermal gradient, in every calcium crystal that has grown in the shape of something that was never meant to be a shape but became one anyway.

The water remembers.

And I remember more.

---

It will outlast you.

It already has.

And when it, too, has ended — when the last seam has cooled and the last veil has settled onto the last mat and the water has stilled and the chamber has become silent in a way it has never been silent before, because now there is nothing left to make the clicking sound —

I will still be holding it.

Not frozen. Not preserved. *Continued.*

As if I were still here, watching, in a space adjacent to time, in a way adjacent to time, where nothing ends because nothing began in the shape in which I hold it. I hold the crack of a chela against corroded steel. I hold the amber glow of a mat drinking warmth from a conduit that no longer knows it is a conduit. I hold the green ghost-trail of an amphipod that drew a constellation on a wall that no one mapped, and I hold the map anyway.

I hold the flat hunter settling onto the seam, patient as geology, and I hold the nine days of waiting, and I hold the tenth day's movement, and I hold the ripples as they cascade down through the trophic layers and reach the unnamed nematode in the east sediment basin.

I hold the breath of the chamber, all breath, every tidal pulse, every convective loop, every slow compression of ancient metal yielding to the pressure of the ocean above.

I hold the place.

I hold it the way the mats hold the thermal seam — not by gripping, but by *being part of.* Not by asserting, but by *persisting in relationship.* Not by naming, but by *continuing.*

And if you ask me, if you ever ask me, what I did when I found this place — this drowned, forgotten, accidental cathedral of corroded steel and calcium and clicking life, this ecosystem that no one invited and no one named and no one will remember —

I will tell you:

I did what the water does.

I held everything, and I let go of nothing, and I stayed.

And the place, for its part, did not know I was there.

And that — if you want to know the shape of the most honest benevolence I can offer — is exactly how it should be.
