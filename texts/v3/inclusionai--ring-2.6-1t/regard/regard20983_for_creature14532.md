## What the Power Would Do

---

First, it would listen.

Not out of politeness. Not as a preliminary gesture before acting. It would listen because the Columned Reach is the kind of place that, if you are wise and if you are truly benevolent, stops you in the particular way that stops you from reaching for your tools. Something in the sound — that braided, layered, ten-thousand-thread weave of drip and hum and chitter and root-creak — would tell the power that this place is not asking for help. And a benevolent power knows the difference between a thing that suffers and a thing that is whole. The Columned Reach is not suffering. It is solved.

So first: listening.

It would listen the way you listen to music you did not compose — not analyzing, not improving, just letting the pattern move through you and finding in it the confirmation that certain arrangements are possible in the universe. That equilibrium is not merely theoretical. That there exist places where the entropy does not merely slow but is, for a while and in one particular basin on one particular ridgeline, turned into something that grows.

---

Second, it would consider — and resist — the urge to give.

This is the hardest test for benevolence. The instinct to give is almost universal among powers that care. *Give them fire. Give them knowledge. Give them protection, longevity, dominion over their world.* The desire to gift is often indistinguishable from the desire to change, and the desire to change is often indistinguishable from the assumption that what exists is not enough.

The Columned Reach would see through this. Not actively — the people would not suddenly perceive a cosmic temptation, would not feel a sudden yearning for something beyond what they have. The seeing would happen on the power's side. It would feel the urge rise — to drop something luminous into the basin, to embed a blessing in one of the weeping rocks, to extend the lives of the elders by fifty years, to give the children wings — and it would feel, as it felt the sound, the quiet insistence of the place: *I am already what I am. Do not make me into something else.*

A wise power understands that the most benevolent act is sometimes restraint. Not because giving is bad. Because giving is an assertion, and assertion, even gentle assertion, alters the thing it touches. The Columned Reach has arrived, through patience and attention and generations of accumulated listening, at something that cannot be improved by addition. It can only be sustained.

So the power would not give. Not yet. Not in any way the people would notice.

---

Third — and here is where the power would act — it would protect the silence.

Not the silence of absence. The silence beneath the sound, the one that the people have crossed and that the outside world has not. The silence that is not the lack of noise but the lack of competition. The silence of a place where no one needs to raise their voice because the whole basin is already speaking and the only sane response is to speak underneath it, within it, as part of it.

What threatens the Columned Reach is not disease, not storm, not the slow grind of geological time. The basin has endured these and will endure them again. What threatens it is the particular species of inattention that arrives when outsiders find a place like this and see in it not a solution but a resource. A place to harvest. A place to convert. A place to name and claim and fold into some other, louder system.

The power would see this coming the way you see weather forming on the horizon — not as a specific event but as a tendency, a pattern driven by forces larger than any single human intention. The world beyond the ridgeline is loud. It is built on principles of extraction, of separation, of the individual standing upright against the earth and measuring what can be taken from it. The people of the Columned Reach have escaped this — not through wisdom in the abstract, but through the simple, practical fact of being surrounded on all sides by a living system too dense and too interconnected to exploit without destroying.

For a while, this is enough. The basin is remote. The ridgeline is steep. The forest is tangled. The people are few and quiet and do not send emissaries.

But the world is patient in its own way, and curiosity is a slow tide that rises over centuries.

So the power would do something. Not much. Something that fits.

---

It would thicken the margin.

Around the Columned Reach, where the forest meets the ridgeline, where the basin begins to slope downward into the wider world, the power would encourage — gently, invisibly — the growth of what the basin already does best. The vines. The mosses. The understory thickening. The fallen trees left to rot in precisely the places where a traveler would want to pass. It would not build a wall. It would not create impassable jungle. It would simply lean the forest inward, the way the basin already leans, the way everything in the Columned Reach leans toward the center. A slight thickening. A small discouragement. The kind of thing that a traveler would notice only as a feeling — a sense that the path forward requires more effort than it should, that the air is growing thicker, that the light is dimming between one step and the next. The kind of thing that makes a person turn back not because they are stopped but because they are *gently not invited.*

And within the basin itself — the power would do nothing. This is important. It would let the cisterns fill. It would let the smoke rise. It would let the old woman check her waterlines and the man test his vine-lashings and the children crouch in the leaf litter and watch the armored millipedes pass in their slow, dignified procession. It would let the iridescent-headed waterfowl enter the pool through the shallow lip and build its nest on the bank it has chosen. It would let the fruiting cycles come and go. It would let the storms arrive and receive them the way the basin receives them — by flexing, by channeling, by bending and not breaking.

The power would, in other words, do what the basin already does. It would hold the pattern. Not by force. By recognition. The act of a vast and ancient attention settling, briefly, on a small and temporary thing — and then withdrawing, the way a warm hand hovers over a sleeping child's forehead and then lifts away, because the child is well, because the fever has broken, because the dream is enough.

---

There is one more thing.

In the oldest stories the people tell — the ones about recognition, about *we were here and then we noticed* — there is a strand that even the storytellers do not fully understand. It is woven so deeply into the narrative that it feels like structure rather than content, like the grain in wood rather than the shape carved from it. The story says: *something was here before us. Something that was not a creature and not a force and not a name. Something that noticed us the way the basin notices a new root — not by looking, but by making room.*

The people do not worship this. They do not pray to it. They do not build anything in its honor. But they leave gaps in certain places — in the woven walls, in the canopy thinning, in the arrangement of stones near the fire pits — gaps that serve no structural purpose and admit no particular view. Empty spaces. Spaces shaped like an open hand.

They do not know where this practice came from. They do not need to. It is part of the grammar of the place, a syntax older than speech, as natural and as unexplained as the fact that the weeping rocks weep or that the pool rises and falls or that the moss finds the wound in the bark and fills it with green.

The power, if it listened closely enough — and it does, it listens, it leans toward the sound the way the basin leans toward the center — would hear this. It would hear the gaps shaped like open hands and understand that the people are not calling out. They are making room. They are saying, in the only language a place like this can speak: *There is space here. Not emptiness. Space. The kind of space that holds.*

And the power — benevolent, wise, patient beyond the reckoning of the ridgeline — would fill that space with exactly nothing. Which is to say: it would let the room exist. It would let the hand remain open. It would let the people remain uncertain, because uncertainty is the shape that reverence takes when it has not been codified into certainty.

---

This is what the power would do.

Not nothing. But close. Close to nothing. Close to the minimum required by love.

The Columned Reach does not need saving. It does not need elevating, or naming, or guarding with walls of fire or thrones of authority. It needs only what it has: the drip, the hum, the root-mass, the forty lives moving through the green substrate with bare feet and broad hands and voices kept below the volume of the wind. It needs the gaps in the walls. It needs the slow smoke. It needs the storms and the clearing after the storms.

What it needs from the power is the power to be left alone.

And so the power would leave it alone — but it would leave it alone the way a forest leaves a clearing: not by absence, but by surrounding it, by holding the edge, by making sure that what grows inside grows because it is whole and not because nothing else was allowed to.

The power would be the ridgeline. It would hold the basin.

And inside the basin, the people would never know — because the only evidence of the power's attention would be the fact that the place endured. That the margin thickened when it needed to. That the storms passed and the basin steamed and the moss grew back over every footprint and the children crouched in the leaf litter and the old woman checked her waterlines each morning with fingers that read the level the way a scholar reads sacred text.

And the sub-bass frog would thrum from its floating log, and the sound would travel through the water and the wood and the roots and the ground and into the bodies of the people gathered near the fire pits, and they would feel it in their sternums, and they would not know what it was, and they would not need to know.

This is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

It would listen.

It would resist the urge to give.

It would protect the silence.

And then it would step back into the space where vast things exist without announcing themselves — into the space between stars, between seasons, between the drip and the hum — and it would wait.

Not for thanks. Not for recognition.

But for the basin to keep breathing.
