## What the Power Saw, and What It Did Thereafter

---

There is a kind of attention that does not seek to be answered. It has no hunger in it, no want. It merely looks, and in looking, it gives weight to what is seen. This is how the power arrived at The Cleave — not summoned, not drawn by augury or prayer, but simply turning its gaze to the place the way a person turns their gaze to a window, to rain, to the slow passage of a hand below a chin.

It had always been capable of looking anywhere. It did not, most of the time, because most of the time the things it could look at were not ready to be looked at without changing. A watched thing becomes a performed thing. A noticed thing becomes a thing that knows it is noticed, and that knowing reshapes it. The power understood this. It was patient in the way that erosion is patient — not because it lacked urgency, but because it understood that the most enduring things are the ones allowed to arrive at their own shape.

But something called it. Not a voice. Not a signal. Something smaller and stranger than that: a resonance. The way two tones, played far apart, can set a third thing vibrating without ever touching it. The power felt this resonance the way you would feel a hum in the floor of a room you are about to enter. A frequency beneath hearing. A pressure beneath thought.

It looked. And what it saw was The Cleave.

---

The first thing the power comprehended — and it understood this before it understood anything else — was that The Cleave was not a place that had been made. It was a place that was still arriving. The fracture was new. Not new the way a wound is new — injured, lamentable, requiring repair — but new the way a question is new. An opening. An opportunity for the rock to discover what it might become if it stopped being a single thing and became two.

The power did not heal the fracture. It sat at its edge and watched the rock settle, and it found the settling beautiful. Here was stone doing what stone does when no one tells it what to be: splitting, settling, revealing the pale veins beneath its skin. There was something honest in it. Something unhurried. The kind of honesty that does not perform for an audience.

The second thing the power comprehended was the moss.

It had seen moss before — on every world it had visited, on every stone and every forgotten surface where moisture gathered and light found its way. Moss was common. Moss was almost never remarkable. But here, in this wound, the moss had become something else. It was not merely growing; it was speaking. Those pulses of blue-green light, arrhythmic, overlapping, drifting into and out of synchronization — the power understood at once what the moss was doing and why no one had ever explained it.

It was not explaining because it could not be explained. Not yet. Not in any language that moves between one mind and another. The moss was saying *here* and *now* and *I am alive and I am here and there is still time* in a tongue made entirely of light. It was not communication in the sense that a message is sent and received. It was communication in the sense that a fire is fire — it exists, it burns, and if you are near it, you are warm.

The power sat with the moss-light for a long time. It let the pulses wash over it, and it did not try to decode them or accelerate them or make them brighter. It simply received them, the way a stone receives rain.

---

The third thing the power comprehended was the clingers.

It saw the hundreds of them first — the thousands — pressed against the walls in their vast congregation, their bodies warm against cold stone, their impossible wings folded and unfolded in the silence. It saw the way they read the world through their feet, the way young ones folded into the spaces between adults without being asked, the way the whole colony became a single sentence written in warmth and silence against the stone.

The power was moved. Not in the way that sentimentality moves, which is shallow and brief and self-reflective. It was moved the way a river is moved by the land — fundamentally, over long stretches, with the kind of force that carves canyons. Because what it saw in the clingers was not strangeness. It was a version of something it had seen before — many times, in many places — the determination of life to exist in negotiation with the terms offered by the world. The clingers had not chosen this wound. The wound had not chosen them. But here they were, together, and the terms of their coexistence were so precise, so delicately calibrated, that any interference — even interference born of kindness — could unravel everything.

The clingers did not need to be improved. They did not need to be understood. They needed to be left alone enough to continue being what they had become.

---

The fourth thing the power comprehended was the larger ones.

These were harder to see. They lived in the lower darkness, deeper than the moss-light reached, and they moved with a deliberateness that made them nearly impossible to follow. The power could see them, of course — nothing was hidden from it — but it had to adjust its attention the way you adjust your eyes when you step from sunlight into a dim room. The shapes were extraordinary. The wingspan. The trailing fingers. The patient, unhurried way they rose and descended, hunting and grazing and breathing the moss into dissolution with a chemical exhalation that the power recognized as something between a sigh and a sacrament.

The larger ones were not monsters. They were the other end of the sentence the clingers were writing. Without them, the clingers would multiply and consume the moss and die. Without the clingers, the larger ones would lose their only prey and turn on the moss directly, and the moss would not survive, and the whole chain would collapse and the Cleave would become what most vertical wounds in stone become: empty, silent, and slow.

The power saw the young of the larger ones, too — or rather, it recognized that they must exist somewhere in the deepest folds of the Cleave, hidden in a place no light reached and no external observer had found. It felt the young there the way you feel movement behind a wall: not seeing it, but knowing. The young were safe. The dark was deep enough. The stone was thick enough. Whatever the larger ones were doing with their young, it was working. It had been working for longer than the power expected.

---

The fifth thing the power comprehended was the circle of stones.

It understood them last, and it understood them longest.

The circle sat at the base of the Cleave, half-swallowed by time, its stones worn smooth and angled inward with a care that no animal would have taken and no accident would have produced. The concentric arcs scratched into the granite were older than the stones themselves, or so the mineral patina suggested, and they told a story in the only language available to a mind that had no words for what it had seen: *I was here. I looked at this place. I tried to draw what I saw, and what I drew was the shape of looking itself.*

The power knelt — or its equivalent of kneeling, which was a lowering of its attention to the level of the stone, a willingness to be small in the face of something large. It examined the hand-axe, the cordage, the bundle of sticks. It understood. These were not abandoned tools. They were a conversation interrupted. Someone had been building something or dismantling something or transforming something, and they had left when they realized the thing they needed to finish could not be finished here, or not yet, or not by them. The Cleave had asked something of them that they could not answer, and so they had gone to try.

The power sat among the stones for a long time.

---

What the power did next was not what you might expect, and it was not what it wanted to do.

It wanted to stay. It wanted to sit at the entrance to the Cleave like a guardian at a threshold, to turn away anyone or anything that would bring harm, to watch the moss light its slow blue-green fires and hear the clicking of the clingers' joints and feel the larger ones moving like thoughts in the deep dark below. It wanted to be the hand that reached into the circle of stones and held them in place as they slowly sank into the granite.

But it knew — with the kind of knowing that does not come from thought but from the accumulated weight of watching forever — that sitting at the threshold was not enough, and that being a guardian was not enough, and that the desire to stay was, in its own way, a kind of selfishness. The Cleave did not need a watcher. The Cleave needed a world around it that allowed it to continue.

So the power did something smaller, and in its way, more difficult.

It went away.

But not entirely. Not completely. It could not bear to be wholly absent from a place that moved it the way this place moved it, so it did what stone does when it cannot remain and cannot leave: it left a part of itself behind.

---

The change was imperceptible. The kind of change that is only visible in time.

The wind that entered the Cleave from the wound above shifted, almost imperceptibly, so that it carried moisture from distant sources — moisture that nourished new colonies of moss on walls where moss had never taken hold. Not much. A thumbprint of green-blue light on a stretch of bare stone. But enough. Enough for a new colony to begin, and with it, enough for the chain to extend a little further, to find new purchase on walls where nothing had lived before.

The clicking of the clingers changed — not in its essential character, which it could not and would not, because the clicking was who they were, but in its range. A few new sounds appeared, small variations in pitch and rhythm, the way a language acquires new words when new things need to be named. The clingers did not consciously create these sounds. The new colonies of moss, growing in new places, created new conditions — different textures, different temperatures, different vibrations — and the clingers' feet read these conditions the way you read a page, and the clicks shaped themselves around what the feet found.

The rock settled a little more gently. Not because it chose to — rock does not choose — but because the power, in departing, had left the fracture in a state of slightly less tension, the way you might, after watching someone struggle, quietly remove an obstacle they did not know was in their way. The stone did not notice. The stone does not notice things that help it. But it settled, and the sound of settling became softer, and the dark below became a fraction warmer, and somewhere deep in the unreachable low places, the young of the larger ones stirred in warmth they would never know the source of.

And around the circle of stones, the power did something it had seen the earlier visitors almost do but never quite finished.

It completed the arcs.

Not by scratching new lines into the granite — that would have been too much, too loud, too much like writing a name on a thing that does not want a name. Instead, the power did something subtler. It changed the way the stone aged. The mineral deposit that had been slowly covering the arcs, century by century, altering their depth, was — very gently, over what would be centuries or millennia — allowed to accumulate unevenly, so that the arcs, which had been incomplete, would in time appear to complete themselves. Not because they were completed. But because the stone, shaped by a patience older than language, would grow in the pattern the earlier visitor had tried to give it.

It would take a thousand years. Maybe ten thousand. The arcs would become visible again, not as scratches but as ridges — raised lines of mineral growth that traced concentric circles in the granite with a precision no chisel could achieve. And whoever came next — clinger, larger one, or something with hands — would see them and feel what the first visitor had felt, and would understand the understanding, and would not need words.

---

That is what the power did. It watched, it listened, it mourned the fact that it could not stay, and it gave the smallest possible gifts — moisture, warmth, the completion of a gesture that had waited a thousand years — and then it left, carrying the sound of the clicking with it the way you carry a melody after you leave a room.

And The Cleave continued.

The moss pulsed. The clingers clung. The larger ones moved through the dark like slow breaths. The stones settled. The unseen young grew. The circle of stones, now beginning its slow transformation, sat at the base of the wound like a closed eye that was not sleeping but dreaming.

And the power, from very far away, felt the hum — the frequency beneath hearing, the pressure beneath thought — and knew that the place was alive, and that it would stay alive, and that this, finally, was enough.

Not to fix. Not to save. Not to claim.

Only to ensure that the sentence the clingers were writing against the wall would continue, that the moss would keep saying *here* and *now* and *still here* in its language of light, and that the wound in the mountain would keep opening, slowly, into whatever shape it needed to become.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does when it finds a place it loves: it leaves it alone, and it leaves it a little more alive than it found it, and it trusts the dark to do the rest.
