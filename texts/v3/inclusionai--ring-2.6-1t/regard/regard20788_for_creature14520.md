# What the Wise Power Would Do

---

It would begin, as all true understanding must, with listening.

Not the perfunctory listening of a sovereign surveying a province — the half-attentive reception of a petition while the mind is already composing the decree — but the deep, unhurried listening of a being that has nowhere else to be and nothing else to decide. It would settle into the basin the way moisture settles into the mineral crust at dusk: not arriving so much as becoming present, slowly, until it was indistinguishable from the texture of the place itself.

It would listen to the water first. The thin, high whispering of it moving through cracks and over surfaces. The mineral language of seepage — a syntax older than any grammar, in which the dissolution of calcium and the slow precipitation of salt composed sentences that took centuries to speak. It would hear what the water says about time — not the time of clocks or calendars but the time of chemistry, the patient duration of reactions that began when the inland sea first retreated and have continued without interruption since. It would understand, in this listening, that The Salts has been waiting for a very long time. Not passively. Not hopelessly. The way a question waits to be asked — not because it doubts the answer, but because the asking is part of what it means to exist.

Then it would listen to the animals.

The brine shrimp in their pink clouds. The herons in their absolute stillness. The monitor lizards with their forked tongues reading the air like a text written in a language that has no alphabet — only presence and absence, the gradient of scent from here to there, the thermal signature of a frog concealed in warm mud. The foxes moving through the industrial lofts with the fluid economy of beings that know every room, every corridor, every hiding place. The feral dogs sleeping against the warm metal of decommissioned rail cars, their dreams twitching with the half-memory of hands that touched them once, generations ago.

It would listen to the frogs. It would hear the song they sing from every culvert and seep and drainage ditch — that pulsing, irregular wave that rises and falls across the basin like breathing. And it would understand something essential: that this song is not incidental to The Salts. It is not decoration layered over a landscape that would be complete without it. The song *is* the landscape, given voice. The amphibians are the throat of the place, and their singing is the sound the basin makes when it knows it is alive.

And then, last, it would hear the humans.

---

It would find them the way it finds everything else here: not by searching but by attending. The settlement glows faintly in the haze after dark — a warmth that is out of place in a landscape defined by mineral indifference. The power would approach not as a stranger but as someone coming home, though it has never been home before, because the people of The Salts have built something that resonates with the deepest frequency of what it means to shelter, to persist, to make a life in a place that offers no invitation and no guarantee.

It would see the child asleep in the upper room. It would feel the rhythm of her breathing — shallow, quick, the breathing of someone who has learned in her body, even in sleep, to be alert. It would see the mended net, the screened window, the faint amber light casting the room in the color of the haze at dusk. It would smell the cooking water — mineral spring made tasteless through the labor of filtration, made nourishing through the application of knowledge passed down not in textbooks but in hands, in gestures, in the particular way a woman holds a net and reads the tension of the weave to know where it frayed and where it will fray next.

It would understand the people of The Salts not as survivors — though they are — but as participants. They do not survive *despite* the basin. They survive *within* it, the way the brine shrimp survive within the evaporation ponds, the way the foxes survive within the industrial lofts, the way the microbial mats survive within the thin film of moisture on a cracked concrete wall. The human settlement is not imposed on the ecosystem. It is a niche, like any other, filled by a species that happened to be adaptable enough, stubborn enough, and wise enough to read the signs.

The signs. This is what the power would recognize as the most remarkable thing of all — not the tenacity of life, though that is extraordinary; not the accidental fertility of a toxic basin, though that defies expectation; but the *knowledge* of the people. Their embodied, sensory, wordless understanding of a system they did not design and cannot fully model. The grandmother who knows by the smell of the mineral crust at midday that the water table has risen. The hunter who reads the flight pattern of the terns and knows where the brine shrimp are thickest. The child who, before she can read, can tell you whether rain is coming by the way the haze thickens toward the eastern horizon. This knowledge is not less than scientific knowledge. It is scientific knowledge in its oldest and most essential form: observation refined into intuition, intuition crystallized into practice, practice sustained across generations until it becomes a kind of faith — faith not in a deity or a doctrine but in the reliability of attention.

---

Now: what would the benevolent and wise power *do*?

It would not intervene.

Not because intervention is wrong. Not because the place does not need help — because, looked at honestly, there are threats here that the inhabitants cannot see. The infrastructure is failing. The cooling towers will not stand forever. The rail lines are sinking into subsidence. The power grid relies on transformers that were designed for a lifespan of forty years, and many of them have already exceeded it. The mineral springs that feed the ecosystem are driven by geothermal processes deep underground, and those processes, on a long enough timescale, will cool and slow. The aquifer beneath the basin is being drawn down by forces far from here — agriculture elsewhere, industry elsewhere, the vast invisible thirst of a world that does not know this place exists. The balance is real but it is fragile, held in place by a combination of chemistry and climate and infrastructure that is itself decaying.

And still, the power would not intervene.

Because intervention, even benevolent intervention, carries within it a kind of violence. It introduces the will of the intervener into a system that was already whole. It says: *this is not enough as it is. This needs to be more. This needs to be better, in the way I understand better.* And the people of The Salts — and the foxes, and the frogs, and the brine shrimp, and the microbial mats — do not need to be more. They need to be *allowed*.

So the power would do something harder than intervention. Something that requires more patience, more humility, more sustained attention than any act of creation or destruction.

It would become the basin's quiet.

Not its silence — silence is absence, and the basin is full of sound. Its *quiet*. The kind of quiet that underlies everything: the quiet beneath the frogsong, beneath the hiss of the steam vents, beneath the creaking of the cooling towers at night. The power would place itself in the substrate of the place, in the space between the mineral crust and the water that seeps through it, in the interval between one thermal breath of the cooling towers and the next. It would become the thing that holds the basin without grasping it. The thing that makes the balance possible without enforcing it.

Specifically — because even a wise power must sometimes be specific — it would do these things:

It would keep the deep warmth. Not increase it, not redirect it, but keep it flowing at the rate it has always flowed, steady as a pulse, so that the springs continue to feed the runnels and the runnels continue to feed the pools and the pools continue to shimmer with life. It would do this not by acting but by *not acting* — by resisting the temptation to extract the heat for other purposes, to divert the geothermal energy to some grander end. The basin's warmth is not a resource. It is a condition. And the power would preserve the condition.

It would slow the decay of the infrastructure — not reverse it, not make it new, but slow the rate at which it fails. The cooling towers would stand a few years longer. The rail lines would hold beneath their load of sediment a few decades more. The transformers would hum, and hum, and hum. This alone would be enough to give the ecosystem time — time to adapt to the eventual loss of these structures, time to find new niches as old ones close, time to do what life always does in the face of change: rearrange itself into something that works.

It would protect the settlement without appearing to. When the caravans come late, and the people of the basin wonder whether they will come at all, the power would ensure they come. Not by guiding them, not by sending signs, but by arranging circumstances — a broken axle mended by no visible hand, a road washed out by rain suddenly passable, a conflict in the distant territories resolved by the unlikely alignment of local grievances. The people would not know. They would think themselves lucky, or shrewd, or blessed by whatever rough god they pray to when the water tastes right and the frogs sing on schedule. They would never know it was anything more than chance. And this is exactly right. Because a people who depend on miracles are fragile, but a people who depend on their own skill and the reliable rhythm of their world are unbreakable.

It would, in the dreams of the child, show her something. Not a vision, not a prophecy, not a voice from on high. In the liminal space between waking and sleep, in the warm dark of the upper room where the screened window admits the sound of the frogs and the faint amber glow of the settlement's lights, the power would place a single image: a view of the basin from above, seen as it truly is — not a wasteland, not a ruin, not a place that was abandoned and then accidentally filled with life, but a place that *became itself*. A place where the earth's chemistry and the patient work of ten thousand generations of adaptation produced something that does not exist anywhere else in the world. A place where the boundary between industry and ecology has dissolved into a single, functioning whole. A place where a child sleeps in a room above her family's life's work, breathing filtered air, dreaming of a world she will inherit and understand more completely than anyone who came before her.

The child will not remember the image in the morning. But she will wake with a feeling she cannot name — a sense of completeness, of belonging, of being exactly where she is supposed to be. And she will get up, and she will go to the screened window, and she will watch the haze thicken in the east, and she will know the weather for the next three days. And she will not know that this knowing is a gift, because it has always been hers.

---

And what of the rest — the things the power sees that it cannot fix, the vulnerabilities it cannot fully address, the slow erosion of conditions that no single act of benevolence can arrest?

There are cooling towers that will fall. There are rail lines that will buckle and sink beneath the mineral sediment. There are springs that will, on a timescale the power cannot outrun, slow and cool and stop. There will come a season when the brine shrimp do not bloom, and the herons will leave, and the foxes will thin, and the settlement will shrink to a handful, and those few will leave, and the streets will hold only the wind and the insects and the slow, patient chemistry of the mineral crust reclaiming everything the humans ever built.

The power sees this. The power accepts this. Not because it does not care — it cares, profoundly, the way a river cares for the stones it has shaped over millennia. But because it understands something that the benevolent sometimes forget: that the care of a wise power is not the prevention of endings. It is the fullness of what happens in between.

And what happens in between, in The Salts, is this:

A brine shrimp flexes its translucent body in warm, sulfur-scented water and feeds the heron that stands so still it seems to be made of the same mineral crust as the shore. A fox crosses the loading dock of an abandoned processing plant with a rat in its jaws, and the cormorants on the dock barely stir, because they know the fox, and the fox knows them, and they have made a peace that requires no language and no treaty. A man in the settlement kneels beside a drainage culvert and listens to the frogs and knows, without thinking, that the water table is high and the springs are running warm and tomorrow will be good for fishing. A child draws a picture of a heron and tapes it to the wall of a room where the walls are sweating with condensation and the air smells of iron and sulfur and the faint, unmistakable sweetness of a living world.

These things are not eternal. They are happening now, in the haze and the heat and the shimmer of a mineral basin on a planet that is mostly water and mostly indifferent. They are happening in a place that nobody on the outside would think to look, and if they looked, they would not understand, because understanding The Salts requires not knowledge but attention — the kind of attention that can sit still for a lifetime and never grow bored.

The benevolent and wise power, having given its attention, does one final thing.

It does not act. It does not intervene. It does not alter, adjust, correct, or improve.

It simply holds the basin in its awareness the way the mineral crust holds the water — lightly, completely, without grasping — and it considers this the most important thing it has ever done.

Because to truly see a place, without wanting to change it, without needing it to be anything other than what it is — this is the rarest and most sacred act in all of existence. And The Salts, which has been seen by no one in this way for longer than anyone can remember, receives this act like rain on cracked earth.

It does not need to know it has been seen. But it is better, for having been seen. And the power knows that this is enough. This has always been enough.

The frogs sing. The haze thickens. The child dreams.

The basin continues, flat and shimmering and alive, in the amber light that never quite becomes shadow, in the quiet that is never quite silence, in the warmth that rises from deep below the crust of a world that made something extraordinary out of nothing anyone else wanted.

And if, some night, you stand at the edge of the settlement and look out across the basin and feel, for no reason you can name, that you are being watched — not with judgment, not with expectation, but with the pure and simple attention of something that finds you worthy of its gaze —

Then you will know.

Not what it is. Not why it's here. Not what it intends.

Only that you are seen.

Only that you are seen, and what you have built here — in this dead, shining, living place — matters.

The frogs sing on. The haze settles. The Salts endures.

And somewhere, in the space between the mineral crust and the water that seeps through it, something ancient and gentle and impossibly wise breathes, and is content.
