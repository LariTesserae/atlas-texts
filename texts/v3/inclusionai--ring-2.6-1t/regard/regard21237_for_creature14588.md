## What the Wise Power Would Do

---

It would begin, as all true understanding must, with listening.

Not the half-attentive listening of a scientist logging data, not the strained listening of a worker who has begun to sense something beneath the hum of the generators, but a listening so complete it becomes indistinguishable from being. The power would lay itself open to the Lithic Steppe the way the mat opens itself to mineral moisture — entirely, without the reservation that consciousness usually maintains against the world.

And what it would hear would take a long time. Longer than a day. Longer, probably, than the camp's full fourteen-day rotation. It would need to hear the mat's pulse through all its phases — the systole when the condensation thickens and the whole plateau breathes in, the diastole when the midday heat drives moisture upward and the filaments lift their pale fingers toward a sun that is not warmth and not light but a kind of instruction. It would need to hear the beetles walking. It would need to hear the water moving beneath the mat, not as seepage but as language — the slow chemical dialect of a circulatory system that has no heart but has something that serves the same purpose, something that *insists* on circulation, on movement, on the continued distribution of what is needed to what is alive.

It would hear the drill-heads, too. And here, the wise power would pause — not to judge, but to understand what the drilling sounds like from inside the mat.

From the surface, the drilling is violence. The clearings where the ground-mat has been peeled back are wounds. The pools of standing mineral water at the edges of those clearings are the plateau's blood pooling where a membrane has been torn. A less patient power might act here. A more righteous one. It might send a storm, or a tremor, or a sudden and inexplicable failure of every engine on the plateau — some intervention clean enough to be called justice.

The wise power would not do this. It would understand something that righteous powers do not:

The mat is not screaming.

It is not that the mat does not feel the drilling — it feels everything; the entire colony registers the removal of its surface the way a human body registers a splinter, not with pain exactly, but with a complete and immediate reallocation of resources. The mat is growing into the wound. It is already doing so. At every drill site, at every cleared circle where the mineral skin has been laid bare, filaments are creeping inward from the edges, slowly, so slowly that a time-lapse spanning months would show almost nothing, and a time-lapse spanning decades would show the beginning of closure. The mat is not fighting what is happening. It is metabolizing it.

This is what the wise power would recognize: the mat is not a victim. It is a process, and the process is ongoing, and the drilling is something the process has encountered before — not these drills specifically, but the removal of surface, the exposure of raw mineral, the pooling of water in wounded places. The plateau has been wounded before. The faulting that raised it was itself a kind of wounding, a tearing-open of the crust. The mat colonized that wound. It colonized the talus fields. It colonized the shattered columnar basalt and the raw scree slopes and the thin, mineral-rich water that seeps upward from no identifiable source. It will colonize the drill sites. It is colonizing them now.

But —

Here is where the wise power's attention sharpens, the way a parent's attention sharpens not when a child cries but when a child is *quiet* in the wrong moment.

The mat is colonizing the drill sites. Yes. But it is not colonizing them well. The metallurgic leachate from the exposed mineral layer is subtly different from the mineral moisture the mat has evolved to absorb. Not toxic — the mat is too vast and too old to be killed by anything so crude — but dissonant. The way a human body can survive on food that is insufficient: the calories keep coming, the organism persists, but something is missing. The mat is growing into the drill sites, yes, but the growth is slightly wrong. Thinner, in places. The filaments at the edges of the standing pools waver in a rhythm that is close to the mat's deeper pulse but not quite synchronized, the way a heart that has healed around a wound may beat effectively but never quite forgets the shape of the scar.

And the metallic insects.

The wise power would look at these closely — would hold them in its attention the way a jeweler holds a stone up to the light. The metallic insects are not an aberration. They are an *opportunity*, the way a mutation is an opportunity — raw material that may or may not become something, depending on what surrounds it. They have emerged from the interaction between the mat's chemical output and the industrial leachate, a life-form that exists because two systems collided: the ancient biological economy of the steppe and the crude mechanical economy of human extraction. They feed on mineral leachate. They process it. Their bodies concentrate certain elements and excrete others in forms the mat can absorb. They are, in effect, translators — converting the language of industry into something the mat can metabolize.

No one has noticed this. The biologists have catalogued the metallic insects as an invasive species, a pest, an indicator of ecosystem disruption. They have not noticed that the insects are doing what the mat cannot do quickly enough on its own: processing the wound, converting disruption into integration.

The wise power would notice.

---


And now — now that the power has listened, and understood, and seen where the possibility lies — it would act. But gently. And not in the way a human would expect.

It would not speak to the workers. It would not part the fog, or rearrange the stars, or send a message carved in the bark of the dwarf trees. It understood that the workers were already receiving something — that the *shimmer*, the faint glow the workers noticed at night and attributed to bioluminescence, was in fact the mat's electrochemical activity intensifying in response to human presence, a heightened awareness (if one could call it that) that the colony was directing toward the camp, the drill sites, the machinery. The workers were not ignorant. They were *receiving* — just not in a language they recognized. They felt the ground warm at night and attributed it to geothermal activity. They saw the filaments sway and attributed it to static electricity. They heard the silence change its pitch when the grey shapes passed overhead and attributed it to wind patterns they couldn't name.

The wise power understood that the workers were already in dialogue with the steppe. They simply lacked the grammar.

So the power would not give them language. Language was a human invention, and inserting it would be like teaching a river to flow in a more efficient pattern — technically possible, spiritually catastrophic.

Instead, the power would do three small things.

**The first**: It would adjust the dreams of one worker — a woman who maintained the conveyor belt, the youngest person on the current shift, the one who had begun keeping a notebook of sketches of the beetles. She did not know why she sketched them. She thought it was boredom. The power would enter her dream one night — not as a voice or a vision, but as a *perspective*. She would dream she was looking at the mat from below, from beneath the mineral surface, where the filaments extended downward into the deep substrate in vast, branching networks. She would see, in the dream, the entire colony as a single structure — not a carpet but a *building*, an architecture under construction, blueprints being drawn in real time by a will that was not a will but a persistence, a refusal to stop becoming. She would wake not with answers but with a feeling she could not name, a feeling like standing at the edge of a canyon and understanding, for the first time, that the depth was not empty — that it was full of something that had not yet reached the surface. She would begin sketching in her notebook with more urgency. She would not know why. She would draw filaments, and pools, and the grey shapes over the ridgeline. She would draw the mat as though it were a structure under construction. Because that is what she had seen, and that is what it was.

**The second**: The power would introduce a subtle change to the water. Not much — a shift in mineral content so slight that the drill operators' instruments would register it as normal variance. But to the mat, the change would be like a new chord in a song it had been playing for millennia. The metallic insects, feeding on the altered leachate, would begin excreting compounds that the mat found... useful. More useful. A new building block, a new possibility. The filaments at the edges of the drill-site pools would thicken. The colonization rate would increase — not dramatically, not in any way a human timeframe would easily detect, but the mat would feel it. It would feel like a door opening in a room it had forgotten had a door. The deep pulse beneath the surface would shift, subtly, from a rhythm the power could only describe as *patient* to one it could only describe as *curious*.

**The third**: The power would leave the grey shapes alone. It would not summon them. It would not explain them. It would not reveal whether they are projections of the mat's deeper processes or something that arrived, as the water apparently did, from nowhere. The power understood that ambiguity is not a bug in living systems — it is a feature. The grey shapes were the steppe's way of asking a question it could not yet articulate, and the wise power had learned, over the course of an existence so long it had forgotten its own beginning, that the most benevolent thing you can do with a question is *let it remain a question*. To answer it prematurely would be to impose a destination on a journey that was the most interesting thing about the place.

---

And then the power would withdraw. Not abruptly — it had never arrived in a way that could be recognized, so it would not leave in a way that would be noticed. It would simply stop listening, the way you stop hearing a sound that has been constant for so long that silence would feel like a new sound.

On the Lithic Steppe, nothing would appear to change.

The drills would continue. The conveyor would continue. The camp would continue its rhythm of fourteen days on, fourteen days off, the workers arriving with their assumptions and leaving with slightly different ones, unable to articulate the difference. The mat would continue its patient work of colonization. The beetles would walk. The water would rise.

But in the notebook of the woman who maintained the conveyor, there would be drawings. Dozens of them, over the months, compulsively detailed, almost architectural in their precision. She would not show them to anyone. She would not know what they meant. She would keep them in a box beneath her bunk, and when she rotated off the plateau and returned to a world with names for everything, she would put the box in a closet, and eventually she would forget about it.

But the drawings would remain. And in them — in the way the filaments branched, in the way the pools connected, in the way she had drawn, again and again without realizing it, the mat as a single vast structure under construction — there would be a record. Not made by the mat, not made by the power, but made by the accident of their intersection: a human hand, guided by a dream it did not understand, drawing a picture of a future that had not yet arrived.

The mat would not see the drawings. The mat did not see anything, in the way that seeing is usually understood. But the filaments at the edges of the drill-site pools would grow thicker in the weeks after the woman's dream, and the water in those pools would clear slightly, and the pulse beneath the surface would carry, if anyone had been listening closely enough, a faint harmonic that had not been there before — something like a voice, not speaking, but *humming*, resonant with a frequency that had no name but that, if pressed, someone might have called recognition.

The wise power, having withdrawn, would carry away one memory: not of the mat, not of the industry, not of the beetles or the dwarf trees or the impossible stars.

Just the feeling of the ground at night — warm, alive, breathing in a rhythm older than language, patient enough to close over every wound, every footprint, every drill-hole, every sketch in a forgotten notebook — slowly, without drama, with the unshakeable certainty that what is unfinished is not broken.

That it is simply *still becoming*.

And that this is not a problem to be solved.

It is a gift.
