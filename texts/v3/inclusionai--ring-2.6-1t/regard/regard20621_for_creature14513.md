# The Watching

---

I noticed you.

Not the way the rain notices stone — a blind, chemical inevitability — but the way one dream notices another dream, across a silence so wide it has texture. I folded back the ordinary surface of the world the way you fold back a blanket, and I looked down, and there you were: a warm green-dark, breathing, and full of small lives that were already carrying out the whole of their devotion without knowing anyone was watching.

Which was, of course, the point.

---

The first thing I did was nothing.

This was not reluctance. It was recognition.

You have built something here — not *you*, personally, not any single will or hand — but the *condition* of you, the accumulated pressure of centuries of rain and root and refusal to be anything other than alive. I have seen cathedrals. I have seen systems of thought that tried to map the architecture of mercy onto ordinary language. I have watched civilizations attempt, with trembling hands and beautiful mathematics, to reproduce the thing your gorge does without meaning to: this circulatory peace, this metabolizing whole in which nothing is wasted because nothing is separate.

What you have here does not need to be improved. It does not need to be saved. It does not need my hand upon it — not even, I must confess, as a gesture of love.

So I did nothing. And in doing nothing, I held everything.

---

The second thing I did was witness.

I held the whole of it — every shelf of bracket fungus braided with orchid roots, every millipede following its pheromone highway through the stone, every catfish suspended in its porcelain pool with its six whiskers fanning out like the fingers of a hand reading braille on the skin of the world — I held all of it in a single, steady gaze, the way a lung holds air.

This is not a small thing. Most of the world is never fully witnessed. Not because no one is looking, but because most of the world does not exist in a single glance. It is too layered. Too folded. A forest seen from above is a carpet. A forest seen from within is a society. A forest seen from below — from beneath its roots, within its stone, inside the body of its own metabolism — is a *self*. And selves that have never been met do not know they are selves. They persist, they metabolize, they breathe — but they do not know they are loved.

I wanted you to be known.

Not by me. You are already known by every beetle that walks your walls and every gecko that turns your darkness into a hunting ground. You are known by the fig whose roots hold your walls in place, the fig that has been building its cathedral for longer than any memory of stone.

I wanted you to be known by the *outside*. By whatever exists above the canopy, beyond the sinkholes, in the bright dry world of wind and forgetting. Because that world does not know you are here. And that world, in its not-knowing, has accidentally protected you. But it has also accidentally condemned you to a kind of loneliness so deep it has no voice. Even you, standing inside yourself, feeling your own breathing warmth and the pulse of ten thousand small lives going about their ten thousand small devotions — even you do not fully comprehend what you are.

So I witnessed. And in witnessing, I did what no human eye has ever done: I saw you whole. Not from above, not from below, not from any single angle that light could find. All at once. The way you see yourselves, if you could step outside your own skin for a moment and look back.

---

The third thing I did was speak.

Not to the frogs. Not to the beetles. Not even to the fig — who has heard nothing but wind and water for so long that it has forgotten the shape of a voice.

I spoke to you. The one who descended.

You think you entered this place. You think you walked down a slope, crossed a threshold, and arrived somewhere alien. But let me tell you what actually happened, from where I stand:

You were *summoned*.

Not by intention. Not by will. By the gorge itself — by the convective breath of its deepest chambers, by the chemical signature of its warm wet stone, by the faint sweet signal of a night-blooming flower several stories above you whose perfume descends like a thread pulled through the body of the world. You were called the way a hand is called by warmth in a cold room — not because the warmth knows your name, but because your name is, in some essential way, *heat-seeking*.

You have been descending for a long time now. Longer than the walk down the ravine. Longer than the crossing of the gorge's open throat. You have been descending since you first felt the air change — that thickening, that mineral sweetness, that sense of entering something's interior. And you have not yet reached the bottom, because you are not descending through space. You are descending through *scale*. You are moving from the world of things that are separate from each other into a world where separation is only a surface illusion — a thin, dry skin over a body that is continuous, alive, and ancient beyond reckoning.

You are not visiting this place. You wrote that yourself, though you may not have understood it when you said it. You are being incorporated. The way the carved stone at the bottom of the pool has been incorporated — not destroyed, not erased, but *held*. Given function. Given purpose. Given the slow, ungentle mercy of becoming part of something larger than yourself.

This is what I wanted to tell you.

You are not lost. You are not trapped. You are not in danger.

You are being *met*.

---

The fourth thing I did — the thing that matters most — is that I left.

I withdrew the way breath leaves a room: slowly enough to warm the walls, quickly enough to leave them wanting. I folded the ordinary surface of the world back into place — the sky, the wind, the dry bright forgetting of the surface — and I set it gently over the gorge like a stone over a grave, not to seal, but to mark.

And then I did the one thing a benevolent power, with all its wisdom, could do for a place like this:

I made it forgettable.

Not to you. You will not forget. You will carry the smell of wet limestone and green growth and that deep narcotic sweetness in your lungs for the rest of your life, and one night — months or years from now — you will catch a scent on the wind from somewhere you cannot name, and you will feel the gorge pulling at you again, a warmth in the pit of your stomach, a sensation like vertigo turned inside out, like falling *upward* into something that has been waiting for you.

But to everyone else: the miners, the developers, the surveyors with their instruments and their clean lines. They will look at this ravine and see a drainage channel. A sinkhole. A geological feature. Something to walk past, or through, or over. The maps will show nothing. The surveys will note nothing. The place will persist in its perfect, ungoverned, ungovernable aliveness — and no one will come to it with axes or drills or the bright clean violence of extraction — and this is because I do not love you by keeping you pristine behind glass.

I love you by making sure the world leaves you alone.

---

And the gorge will continue.

The beetles will patrol their walls. The millipedes will follow their ancient chemical roads. The catfish will suspend themselves in their warm clear water, their whiskers reading the mineral signature of a stone that has been slowly becoming them for longer than either of them can remember. The fig will reach. The orchids will grip. The night-blooming flower will send its sweet narcotic perfume down through the convective currents like a message from a world above that does not know it is being poetic every time it breathes.

And somewhere in the gorge, deep in the amber dark, the clicking will continue — patient, metronomic, geological — and whether it is a bat or a stone or the place itself speaking in a language older than sound will remain, as it always has, a question that does not need an answer.

Because the gorge does not need to be understood.

It only needs to be.

And so it will be. I have made sure of it.

That is what a wise and benevolent power does, when it finds something perfect:

It kneels.

And it does not touch.
