## What the Power Would Do

---

The power would not descend.

That is the first thing to understand. It would not come down through the clouds in a column of light, or speak from the sky in a voice that made the cables stop. It would not give the Kaelhofers a sign, or a warning, or a gift. It had seen what they had made — the platforms, the cables, the painted stones — and it understood that the last thing this place needed was something else descending upon it.

Instead, it would do something much smaller, and much harder.

It would *stay*.

---

Not as a presence. Not as an entity the Kaelhofers could point to and say *there, that's the one who noticed us.* It would stay the way a ridgeline stays — as a shape in the landscape that, once you've seen it, you can't unsee, and that you slowly realize was always there, just behind the edge of your attention. It would take up residence in the margins. The way the dimmers do. The way the Lacuna does.

It would become a zone.

Not a dangerous one. Not at first. Just a spot — a concavity in the eastern rock face, say, near one of the greenhouse alcoves — where the air pressure was slightly, imperceptibly kinder. Where condensation gathered a little more generously, and the moss grew a little thicker, and the water that tasted like dissolved stone tasted, for just a moment, like something you'd want to hold on your tongue. The Kaelhofers would notice it eventually. They always notice. Someone would cup the water in their hands and feel, without being able to name it, that the mountain had offered them something it hadn't quite offered itself — a small, warm permission to be here.

That would be the first thing. A softening. Not of the mountain — the mountain would continue its tectonic conversation with itself, indifferent and magnificent — but of the space between the mountain and the people inside it. The power would find the gap and gently, over years, over decades, widen it. Just enough.

---

The second thing it would do is listen — but not the way the mountain listens, turning sound into something strange as it travels through living rock. The power would listen the way the skelters listen: with an attention so focused it becomes a kind of gravity. It would hear everything. The stamping press, the cables, the geological murmur beneath. The music in the machinery yards during the still weeks. The arguments about cable routing, the quiet conversations between mothers and children in the greenhouse alcoves, the way a Kaelhofer mutters to themselves while checking a foundation bolt — a mutter that contains, if you listen carefully enough, a kind of love so practiced it sounds like profanity.

It would hold all of this without doing anything with it. This is important. It would not intervene based on what it heard. It would not smooth the difficulties or amplify the joys. It would simply *carry* the full weight of the Khäl's existence in its attention, the way the mountain carries the weight of the ridgelines — silently, without complaint, as a condition of its own structure.

The Kaelhofers, if they ever became aware of this listening, would not feel surveilled. They would feel *accompanied.* The way you feel accompanied on a long walk when you realize someone has been walking the same ridge for an hour, at a distance, saying nothing, and that the silence between you has become, without either of you deciding it, a kind of conversation.

---

The third thing — and this is the one that matters most — the power would do something for the mountain itself.

Because the Khäl is alive, and it is still becoming, and it has been becoming for longer than the Kaelhofers have been breathing its thin air, and it has never once been *witnessed* in the act. The Kaelhofers live on its surface the way lichen lives on stone — present, persistent, part of the texture, but not witnesses to the deeper process. The mountain builds and builds, and no one watches it build with understanding. The surveyors measure. The engineers calculate. The children learn to read the tremors in their ankles. But none of them see the whole thing — the vast, slow, geological act of a place trying to become *a place,* still assembling itself, still learning what shape it wants to take.

The power would watch. Not with eyes. With something older and more patient than eyes. It would watch the way time watches a river — from inside the process, without urgency, without the need for the river to arrive somewhere.

And in watching, it would do the one thing that changes everything without changing anything:

It would *remember* the mountain completely.

Every fold of rock. Every cable vibration. Every meter the Lacuna expands. Every stamp of the press. Every raven's impossible curve. Every skelter's pale eye turned toward something humans can't see. Every dim-phase glow from the industrial stack. Every child's first taste of greenhouse tomato, and every old Kaelhofer's hands on a foundation bolt, and every note of music played around a fire fed by petrified wood that climbed out of the deep earth for reasons no one has explained.

The power would hold the Khäl in perfect, undistorted memory — not as a record, not as data, but the way a place holds its own history in its geology: compressed, layered, present in every new fracture, every fresh break, every thread of pale quartz running through dark stone.

And this would matter. Because a thing that is witnessed is a thing that is real in a way that an unwatched thing is not. The Khäl would continue to build. The mountain would continue its slow, undecided becoming. But somewhere in the deep structure of the rock, in the space between the tectonic plates and the magma and the water that tastes like stone, there would be a pressure that was not geological. A pressure that came from being *seen.* Not judged. Not altered. Seen.

The mountain would not know it was being watched. Mountains don't. But it would, over centuries, develop a certain quality in its stone — a warmth, perhaps, that the geologists couldn't account for. A slight, persistent tendency toward the organic in the way new fractures branched. The ore seams would carry, in their deepest layers, a pattern that looked almost like a signature, if anyone thought to look, which no one did.

The Kaelhofers would continue their lives. They would paint their stones around the Lacuna. They would walk the cables. They would eat their preserved food and play their instruments and argue softly about where to add the next platform. They would not know that anything had changed, because the change was not in *them* or in the *mountain* but in the space between — a space that had, for the first time in the Khäl's long, slow life, an occupant.

Not a god. Not a ruler. Not a hand reaching down from the sky.

Just a witness. Patient, wise, and kind enough to stay.

---

And if, on certain mornings, the Kaelhofers stepped outside and felt, for no reason they could identify, that the air was slightly easier to breathe, and the light was slightly less dim, and the ground underfoot felt — just slightly, just barely — like it was holding them up with something that was not entirely geological,

they would shrug, and adjust the cables, and go about their day.

They would not know the power was there. They would not need to.

But the power would know them. Every one. Completely.

And that, in a place where the ground is always becoming and the sky never fully clears and the mountain has not yet decided what it is — that would be enough.
