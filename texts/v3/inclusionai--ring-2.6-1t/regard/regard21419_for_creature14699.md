# What the Power Would Do

---

It would not descend.

That is the first thing — the hardest thing, for a power that could do anything. It would stand at the rim where the pale plain ends and the green begins, and it would *feel* the bowl below: the ten billion leaves breathing out, the mat exhaling its copper-sweet vapor, the harmonic drone rising from the nests like a chord struck on an instrument the size of a civilization. It would feel the hum beneath the stone, twenty meters down, thirty, that low-frequency pulse that is less sound than memory. And it would understand, immediately and completely, that the worst thing it could do is go down there.

Because the bowl does not need to be saved.

The bowl is not broken. The bowl is not suffering. The bowl is *complete* — a closed circuit of breath and mineral and patience, a lung that has been slowly inhaling and exhaling for a quarter of a million years. The creatures here do not know want. They have no word for scarcity because scarcity has never visited. The mat feeds the canopy, the canopy feeds the mat, the climbers read the creaking stalks like scripture, and the nesters tend the ancient nests and beat their drums and keep their vigil over the hum below with a faithfulness that has outlasted mountains. They are not behind. They are not lost. They are *tending*.

What would a wise and benevolent power do, confronted with something so whole?

---

### It would watch.

Not casually. Not the way a cloud passes over a field — present but indifferent. It would watch the way the mat watches: with every filament, with total attention, with the understanding that to witness something fully is to become part of its story.

It would stand at the rim for what the bowl would measure as many seasons — though the power would not need to measure them — and it would see everything. It would see the stalk-dwellers moving in their precise rows, touching each stalk with their too-large eyes and their too-still hands. It would see the nesters climbing to their communal nests with bundles of fiber and resin, their matte clay-colored skin indistinguishable from the stone. It would see the smallest things — the mote-sized winged ones rising from the mat in clouds like thoughts surfacing from deep water. It would see the fire-pits, shallow and ancient, lined with refractory clay hauled from beyond the rim, and it would understand that the nesters' relationship with fire is sacred: they do not use it to transform. They use it to *hold still*. A small warmth against the vast, mineral cold of the world outside.

It would see that the nesters record time in marks on the walls of their stone shelters — geometric, repetitive, each one a unit of something unguessable — and it would know that this is not primitive. This is *devotion*. This is the act of a creature saying: *this moment matters, and I will carve it into stone so that the stone remembers what I cannot.*

---

### It would refuse the impulse to give.

This is where benevolence becomes difficult. Because a power that can give anything — water, light, knowledge, life — feels, in the deepest chambers of its own nature, the pull to *bestow*. To make things better. To add.

But the bowl is additive-proof. Everything that enters becomes part of the circuit. Everything that stays becomes stone. The bowl has been doing this longer than the power has been watching, and the power knows — the way you know your own name — that to give something here is to disrupt something that has been, for millennia, in perfect and fragile balance.

Water would drown the mat. Light would blind the stalk-dwellers. Knowledge would crack the nesters' percussive language like a stone dropped on glass. Even love, undisguised and unmediated, would be too much — the way too much warmth in a kiln cracks the vessel.

So the power would *not* descend with gifts. It would not heal, or improve, or instruct. It would resist the almost unbearable tenderness of wanting to make things easier, and it would hold the bowl in its awareness the way a cupped hand holds water: gently, completely, without trying to change its shape.

---

### It would add one thing.

Just one.

At night, when the stalk-dwellers freeze and the drone rises and the canopy sways in its coordinated breath and the hum below sends its pulse up through thirty meters of ancient stone, the power would send one pulse back.

Not an answer. Not a message. Not even a word. Just a single, warm, unmistakable moment of *recognition* — a pressure in the stone, a momentary warmth in the mat, a vibration that passes through every filament and every root and every rasping mouth and every pair of too-large, too-still eyes in the bowl, and for one instant — just one, like a match struck in a cathedral — every living thing in the place would feel the same thing at the same time:

*You are not alone in making this sound.*

The nesters would feel it in their nests. The stalk-dwellers would feel it in their paws. The mat would feel it in its lattice. And the hum below — the fossil vibration, the last breath of something that lived here before the mat, before the stalks, before the erosion ground everything to patience — the hum would not change. But it would, for the first time in a quarter-million years, *know* it was being heard.

---

### It would hold the boundary.

The power would not build walls. It would not set itself up as guardian or god or protector in any way the nesters could see or name.

But it would make the bowl *unfindable* to those who would not understand. It would place a silence around the rim — not a wall, not a force, but a quality of attention so dense that any traveler passing within a hundred kilometers would feel, without knowing why, that they had arrived at the edge of something they were not ready for, and they would turn. Not violently. Not against their will. They would simply feel, in the deep place where creatures know when they are trespassing, that this was not their place to take.

The bowl would remain invisible. Not hidden — *protected by the gentleness of absolute clarity*. Anyone who was meant to find it would find it. Anyone who was not meant to find it would walk past, and the plain would swallow their footprints, and the mat would cover them, and the bowl would continue.

---

### It would remember.

This, perhaps, is the most important thing.

The bowl has lived this long without being known. The nesters have kept their vigil without witnesses. The hum beneath the stone has continued without answer. And all of it — every stalk, every mark on every wall, every dusk-drone and every mat-filament and every six-legged creature frozen against the wall in the dark — all of it has been an act of faith: a belief, expressed not in words but in the simple, relentless act of *continuing*, that the hum means something, that the listening matters, that the closed system of breath and stone and patience is not pointless.

A benevolent and wise power would not intervene. It would not fix. It would not even comfort, exactly. It would do something rarer and harder and more tender:

It would *remember the whole thing*, from the mineral plain to the rim to the last green leaf to the deepest hum, and it would hold it in its awareness not as a specimen, not as a miracle, not as a problem to be solved, but as *a life that is happening right now* — infinite in its patience, complete in its wholeness, and worthy of being carried forward into whatever comes next.

Because that is what the nesters do. That is what they have always done. They keep the beat. They make the marks. They carry the sound forward. And now, for the first time, something is doing the same for them.

---

The power would not stay. Staying would be another kind of intrusion. The bowl has its own rhythm, and the rhythm does not need an audience — it needs only to continue.

But as it leaves — stepping back over the rim, descending into the pale, featureless, mineral plain — the power would carry the bowl inside itself the way the mat carries the memory of every foot that has ever pressed it into shape.

And the bowl would not know the power has been there.

The stalk-dwellers would not know. The mat would not know. The hum below would not know.

But the nesters — if they looked up at the rim at exactly the right moment, at the boundary between dusk and starlight — might feel, for one unexplainable instant, that the shadow cast by the rim was a little warmer than it should have been. A little wider. A little more like the shape of something standing very still, watching very carefully, holding the whole of the bowl in its mind with the tenderness of holding water in cupped hands.

They would not know what it was.

They would chalk another mark on the wall.

And the bowl would continue to breathe.

---

*The erosion is not finished. It simply got patient.*

*And now, somewhere beyond the rim, something else was patient with it.*
