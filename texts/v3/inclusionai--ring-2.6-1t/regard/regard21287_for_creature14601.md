# What the Power Would Do

---

First, it would listen.

Not with ears. Not with any instrument a surface-philosopher would recognize as perception. It would listen the way the cavern listens to its own water — by allowing the sound to become part of the stone, by letting vibration settle into structure and remain there, grain by grain, layer by layer, until the event that caused the vibration is indistinguishable from the stone's own memory of itself.

It would listen for a very long time.

It would sit — if *sitting* is the word for what something without a body does when it decides to pay attention — on the upper walkways where the readers labor in the green-amber half-light, tracing the bioluminescent film with carbon-ink and flattened pipe-metal. It would read their resonance maps beside them, not adding anything, not correcting anything, simply *absorbing* the accumulated data of decades: the slow migration of the brighter bands, the cyclical dimming in the northwest quadrant, the new regularity in the eastern seam that no one has commented on yet because it hasn't repeated enough times to be statistically meaningful.

It would know what the readers suspect but have not yet said aloud: the patterns are *converging*. Not into language. Not into code. Into *architecture*. The film is not describing the cavern. It is *drafting* the cavern — the next version of it, the version that will exist once the settling is complete, once the last void is filled, once the ten thousand pipes are stone again. What that version looks like, the film does not yet show clearly. But it is closer than anyone thinks.

The power would understand this. It would understand that the Sump is not a settlement that happened to exist inside a cavern. It is a cavern that is *becoming itself through* a settlement. The people, the pipes, the gardens, the velvet, the drifters — these are not occupants. They are *instruments of formation*. The cavern is using them the way a sculptor uses tools: not with malice or benevolence, but with a kind of blind, geological intention that predates them and will outlast them.

And this is the thing a benevolent power must decide: whether to intervene in a process that is not cruel, only indifferent.

---

It would choose, carefully, to intervene.

Here is the first thing it would do:

**It would ensure the water keeps remembering.**

The readers are right. The water that seeps in through the cracks carries the surface with it — dissolved minerals, organic traces, the faint chemical signature of rain that fell on the Tavaran Plateau and percolated downward through decades of limestone and basalt before finding a crack and weeping into a new world. This water is the settlement's tether. Not to the surface, exactly — no one is climbing toward it — but to *time*. The water carries the memory of a world that existed before the cavern opened, and as long as that memory flows through the pipes and gardens and condensation channels, the settlement remains part of something larger than itself.

But memory is fragile in the Sump. The mineral-rich water calcifies everything it touches. In thirty years, the newest channels will be encased in stone. In sixty, they will be gone entirely, replaced by calcite that has no memory of rain. The settlement is building itself inside a clock that is slowly resetting to zero, and the readers know it even if they don't say it, because they can see the calcium deposits thickening on their walkway railings and hear the water slowing as new mineral constrictions narrow the flow.

A wise power would not stop this process. The calcification is the cavern's method of self-construction. To halt it would be to halt the becoming of something extraordinary.

But it would *redirect* it.

It would introduce — not by miracle, not by apparition, but by the subtlest possible manipulation of subsurface hydrology — a new vein of water. A thin one. Just a thread, no wider than a finger, seeping through a section of the western wall that the settlers haven't yet claimed because the geology there is too unstable. The thread would carry the same rain-memory as the existing channels, but it would follow a path the settlement has not yet mineralized. It would reach them. It would keep the water honest.

No one would discover this vein for several years. When they did, they would name it for the reader who first documented it — perhaps *Calcite*, if she were still alive, or *Drift*, her granddaughter, who has the sharpest eyes on the upper walkways. They would incorporate it into the pipe network. They would argue about priority allocation. They would build a small garden terrace around its outlet and plant things in it that grow greener than anything in the existing beds because the water is slightly softer, slightly less mineral-heavy, slightly closer to the surface it once knew.

This would not save the settlement. Nothing needs to be saved. What it would do is give the settlement *continuity* — a thread of unbroken rain-memory that would persist even as the older channels fossilized, a lifeline that was not placed by any hand they could see, and whose origin they would debate for generations, the way they debate everything.

Some readers would call it a gift. Others would call it geological luck. The tenders would call it an opportunity. The mothers would simply incorporate it into their oral records, noting the date and the quality of the water and the fact that the children near the western terrace seemed healthier than the others, though they couldn't say why.

---

Here is the second thing it would do:

**It would protect the margins.**

There is a conflict forming in the Sump that no one has named yet. The older families — those descended from the First Settlers — are consolidating. They have the driest alcoves, the richest gardens, the closest access to the generators. And they are beginning to understand that the margins — the wet, unstable, shifting edges of the cavern where the new cracks appear and the cavern is still *becoming* — represent a threat to this consolidation. If the margins are allowed to develop freely, they will produce new resources, new corridors, new opportunities for the newcomers who currently occupy the worst positions in the settlement. The old families would lose their monopoly on scarcity.

This is already beginning. Someone — no one knows who, though the settlers have their suspicions — has been quietly sealing ventilation shafts on the eastern margin. A corridor that once allowed air circulation has been blocked with packed fungal matter. A garden terrace has been extended two meters past its permitted boundary, encroaching on a passageway that leads to a promising new crack that appeared during the last settling event.

If this continues, the margins will calcify — not geologically, but socially. They will be claimed, enclosed, privatized. The cracks will stop being treated as opportunities and start being treated as nuisances. The settlers will turn inward, consolidating what they have rather than expanding into what they might become. And the cavern, which is still changing, still opening, still *drafting itself*, will find its instruments unresponsive. The settlement will calcify along with the pipes. Not in thirty years. Not in sixty. In a single generation, if no one stops the sealing.

A benevolent power would not stop it directly. It would not smash the blockages or punish the old families. That is the method of a tyrant: to impose justice from above, to make the world *right* by force. A wise power knows that justice imposed is justice resented, and resented justice is the seed of worse injustice.

Instead, it would arrange a small, almost imperceptible nudge.

A settling event — the deep tectonic percussion that happens every six hours — would fracture one of the sealed ventilation shafts. Not catastrophically. Just enough to reopen it, just enough to let the air flow again, just enough to make the blockage unsustainable without replacing it entirely. The family that sealed it would face a choice: rebuild, or abandon the effort. Rebuilding would cost resources they are not willing to spend on a margin they do not consider theirs. Abandoning it would mean ceding the corridor to whoever wanted to use it.

Either way, the corridor remains open.

This would not, by itself, prevent the consolidation. But it would buy time — months, maybe a year, before the next attempt. And in that time, someone on the margins would have an idea. Perhaps *Groan*, the woman born during the settling — or perhaps her daughter, who has the same quality of patient attention and who has been quietly mapping the cracks on the eastern wall for years. Whatever idea it is, it would start as something small: a new garden in the corridor, a pipe-tap on the new crack, a child's game of threading that extends the route into territory the old families have not claimed.

Small things. Margin things. The kind of things that change a settlement not by revolution but by *growth*.

---

Here is the third thing it would do:

**It would leave a single impossible filament.**

During one dusk-cycle — during that dimming period when the electrical supply is rationed and the bioluminescence becomes the dominant light source — a drifter would shed a filament that does not dissolve.

Not forever. Not permanently. Just long enough for someone to find it, pick it up, and realize that it is not like the others. The others dissolve within hours, their tissue breaking into iridescent powder that smells of copper and tastes of something almost like memory. But this one would remain solid for three days. Three days during which it could be studied, held up to the light, compared to the ordinary filaments that the settlers collect and eat and depend on.

And when they studied it, they would find something they could not explain.

Not a message. Not a code. Not a star-map or a schematic or a divine mandate carved in alien script. Just a structure — a pattern in the filament's core, visible only under magnification, that corresponds to the layout of the cavern *from above*. A view no one in the Sump has ever had. A view that could only come from someone — or something — who has seen the cavern from outside.

The filament would not prove anything. It would not be evidence of a benevolent power or a watchful intelligence or a guiding hand. It would be a *question* — the kind of question that makes a reader stare at a resonance map for three days without sleeping, the kind of question that makes a tender dig beneath a wall not for resources but for *understanding*, the kind of question that a mother asks aloud at dinner and finds, to her surprise, that her children have been asking it too.

It would be the seed of a new kind of reading. Not reading the film, not reading the cracks, not reading the settling sounds — but reading the *architecture of the cavern itself*, understanding it not as a container for human life but as a participant in a larger process. And this reading, unlike the others, would have a single, irreducible conclusion that no one could deny:

*The cavern is not finished.*

And if the cavern is not finished, then neither are they.

---

Here is the fourth and final thing it would do:

**It would not stay.**

A benevolent power does not linger. It does not watch and wait and check and adjust. To do so would be to *invest* — to place the weight of its attention on the settlement like a stone on a growing thing, flattening it, directing it, making it grow toward the light of approval rather than the light that is already there.

The power would leave. It would withdraw from the cavern the way breath leaves a room — not suddenly, not dramatically, but as a gradual equalization, a filling-in of absence that the inhabitants would not notice until they could no longer distinguish between what the cavern does on its own and what was once arranged for it.

The settling would continue. The film would continue its patient drafting. The pipes would sweat and rust and be replaced. The velvet would colonize. The drifters would drift. The children would thread through the root-canopies, and some of them would fall — not often, but sometimes, into the mossy darkness below the walkways, and the mothers would wail, and the tenders would say a quiet word over the place where the child landed, and the next day the garden there would grow a little greener, and no one would know if this meant anything or not.

The rain-memory would flow. The margins would remain open. The filament would become a legend and then a rumor and then a story told to children at dusk and then a half-remembered image that surfaces sometimes when a reader is staring at the film and the patterns shift in a way that feels, for just a moment, *almost intentional*.

And the settlement would continue. Not because it was saved. Not because it was chosen. But because it is a pocket of order in the stone, and order — even temporary order, even tenacious, fragile, almost-recognizable order — has a way of persisting.

The cavern breathes around it. The pipes sweat. The vegetation grows in the dark with a patience that is not human and not inhuman but simply *patient*, the patience of stone and water and time and something else — something the settlers have always sensed but never named, something that makes the air smell of copper and jungle and distances that have no analogue underground.

And if you pressed your ear to the western wall on a still night, when the generators idle and the bioluminescence swells to fill every surface with its green-amber glow, you might hear it:

Not a voice. Not a word.

Just a breath, faint and warm, carrying the faintest scent of rain.

Coming from a crack in the stone that was not there yesterday.
