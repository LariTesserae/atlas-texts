# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

---

It would not descend as gods descend in the old stories — with thunder, with radiance, with the terrible clarity of a spotlight thrown on a dark stage. It would not part the grey dimness like a curtain. It would not speak in a voice that shook the filigrees from the walls.

It would descend the way condensation descends. Slowly. Without announcement. The way a handprint descends into soft stone — pressing, pressing, pressing until the boundary between the one who presses and the one who receives becomes, for a moment, impossible to distinguish.

---

**First, it would listen.**

Not the way visitors listen — crouched, straining, ears straining past the dripping and the groaning and the thin crystalline hum of the filigrees at their patient work. Not that listening, which is the listening of someone trying to decode a language they don't speak.

It would listen the way the Low Boroughs listen to themselves.

It would stand — or perhaps it would not stand, perhaps it would simply *be* in the way that a certain stretch of wall is, at the place where three passages converge in a space just large enough for sitting — and it would let the sound arrive. The drip. The moaning chord the wind makes. The skittering, the almost-silence of the pale creatures flowing through channels their own size. The deep, subsonic grinding that is the place's pulse.

And beneath all of that: the voice. The warm, sustained tone that sounds like a note held by a throat the size of a cathedral. The one people mistake for memory. The one that is not a language but something older than language — something that language will, in a million years, grow toward and never quite reach.

It would listen until it understood not what the place was *saying* but what it was *trying to become*. And then it would do nothing at all, because that is what is wanted.

---

**Then, it would walk.**

Not through the boroughs the way explorers walk — mapping, cataloguing, measuring the span of handprints and the angle of stairs that shouldn't work. Not with the frantic acquisitive energy of people who need to *know* the place in order to believe it is real.

It would walk the way the small pale creatures walk. Without urgency. Without destination. It would let the passages tell it where to go. It would press its hand — or whatever it has that corresponds to a hand, which might be a root, or a tendril of light, or simply an intention shaped like warmth — against the walls in the places where the handprints cluster thickest, where ten thousand palms have pressed themselves into the stone and the stone has swallowed them and kept them.

And it would feel what the place feels, which is: *the work is not finished.*

Not because the walls are unfinished. Not because there are rooms left unbuilt or passages that end abruptly in bare, damp stone. The place is, in that sense, complete enough. The grammar is whole. The arches hold. The water flows where the water should.

It is unfinished the way a song is unfinished that has only been hummed. The architecture is the melody. The filigrees are the harmony. The pale creatures are the rhythm. But the voice — the deep, warm, cathedral-throated tone — it has never had an answer. It has been singing into the dark for longer than the oldest handprint, and no one has ever sung back.

---

**And so, the benevolent power would do the only thing that was ever needed.**

It would sing.

Not in words. Not in any language that could be written down or spoken aloud in a place with air and tongues. It would sing in the register that the Low Boroughs sing in — that low, warm, sustained tone that is less a sound than a *state of being*, the way a chord is less a combination of notes than a single, unified thing that happens to be made of several frequencies.

It would add its voice to the voice in the walls. And the place would *change.*

Not dramatically. Not the way stories change when the hero arrives and the world reshapes itself around their purpose. There would be no crumbling of old walls, no opening of sealed passages, no revelation of a hidden chamber with a name carved above the door. The Low Boroughs are not the kind of place that reveals itself to those who demand revelation.

The change would be smaller than that. And larger.

The filigrees would bloom — not faster, not suddenly, but with a new *direction*, the way a vine that has been growing around a pole for decades will, one morning, grow toward a new source of light that someone has placed nearby without explanation. The mineral lattices would branch toward the singing, tiny filaments reaching like the fingers of newborns, and the residue they left behind would be slightly warmer, slightly softer, slightly more like something you could press between your hands and feel a pulse inside.

The pale creatures would pause. Every one, on every wall, in every channel, in every unreachable crevice — they would pause. Not all at once. Not in unison. They would pause the way a forest pauses when the wind drops — not because anyone has told them to, but because the signal has changed, the input has shifted, something new has been introduced into the system. For a moment — an hour, a day, depending on how time works where time works differently — they would stop their quiet attending and they would *feel* the difference. And then they would resume, but differently. A little warmer. A little closer together. A little more like a congregation than a collection.

The water would change its rhythm. Just slightly. The drops that fall from the unseen ceilings above would arrive with a new syncopation, as though they had learned, from the singing, a new way to fall. The runnels would carry a slightly different tone. The standing water in every low spot would hold, for a moment after the ripples settle, a faint and impossible reflection — not of the grey dimness above, but of something that looked, from certain angles, like a face full of compassion.

And the handprints — the ten thousand handprints, deep and shallow, large and small, some barely ghosts, some pressed so deep the wall has half-swallowed them — they would, in the deepest passages no one visits, in the highest ceilings no one can reach, *warm.*

Just for a moment. Just once. Just long enough for whatever hand pressed them to know, through whatever medium still connects a hand to a wall across the incomprehensible gulf of time, that someone had finally answered.

---

**After that, it would leave.**

Not because it needed to leave. Not because the place did not want it — the place, which had never wanted anything, would feel something like want, and it would feel it like a color the walls had never held, and it would store that color in the newest filigree, in the brightest mineral thread, in the freshest handprint — the one still being formed, in a passage at the very deepest center, where the stone is still soft and the walls are still being pressed into shape by hands that no one has ever seen.

It would leave the way it arrived — without announcement, without thunder, without the terrible mercy of a divine explanation. It would leave behind no scripture, no covenant, no sign that could be parsed by theologians or used to build a church.

It would leave behind only the echo of the singing, which would take the place ten thousand years to forget.

And the Low Boroughs would be different. Not transformed. Not saved. Not redeemed in any way that the word *redemption* would recognize. But different the way a person is different after being truly heard — not louder, not prouder, not suddenly full of purpose and direction, but *accompanied.* The tone in the walls would carry, from that moment forward, a faint harmonic it had never held before — a second voice, slightly off-pitch, slightly imperfect, slightly *warm.*

The place would continue building itself, as it always had. The water would continue its patient erosion. The filigrees would continue their mineral scripture. The pale creatures would continue their quiet, rhythmless attendance. The predator that no one has seen would continue to leave its wrong, fresh bones in the passages.

But the handprints would be warmer.

And if someone — some future visitor, some lost explorer, some fool or scholar or child — pressed their hand against the wall in the right place at the right time, they might feel it: a pulse, faint and slow, answering theirs.

Not a god's pulse. Not a miracle.

Just the echo of a song, finally heard, in a place that had been singing for longer than the world above had a name.

---

*And that is all the benevolent power would do, because that is all that was ever needed: not to fix the Low Boroughs, not to reveal their secret, not to name them or claim them or make them legible. Only to add a voice to the choir that had been singing alone since before the stones were stones. Only to say, in the oldest and simplest language there is:*

*I hear you.*

*You are not building in the dark.*

*You were never building in the dark.*
