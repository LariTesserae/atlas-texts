## The Attention of a Gentle Power

---

It begins — as all such things begin — not with arrival, but with *noticing*.

The power does not arrive like a god descending on pillars of fire. It does not split the sky or shake the salt pan or speak in a voice like grinding mountains. It arrives the way dawn arrives on a world that has forgotten dawn: slowly, and then all at once, and then so quietly you cannot tell where it ends and where the morning begins.

It notices the column first. Of course it does. How could it not? The column is the kind of thing that *nudges* at the edges of awareness — a wrongness so subtle it reads as beauty, a vitality so deep it registers as geology. The power, whatever it is, wherever it comes from, has walked through dead places and dying places and places that were never alive and never meant to be. It has seen reefs that were cathedrals. It has seen fungal networks that were democracies. It has seen a gas giant's magnetosphere that sang in frequencies no living thing could hear but that made the moons around it tremble with something that might, if you were feeling generous, be called joy.

And it has learned, over the patient and terrible curriculum of eternity, what most powers never learn: that the most alive things are the ones that do not look alive.

So it stops.

It stops the way a traveler stops at the edge of a forest they have no map for. It stands — or settles, or hovers, or *is* — at the boundary between the salt pan and the column, and it does not go further. It watches. It listens. It extends the gentlest and most dangerous thing in all of existence: *attention without agenda*.

---

The first thing it sees is the Cut.

It sees the scaffold platforms and the bolted walkways, the press-metal facades and the terraced housing cantilevered out from sheer rock. It sees the people — four thousand of them, matte-skinned and mineral-eyed, moving through the interstices of industry with the compressed grace of creatures that have learned to live inside a living thing the way blood cells live inside a body. It sees the gas-lanterns, the arc lamps buzzing in their housings, the dust that hangs golden in every beam of light. It sees the laundry lines strung between antenna masts, the boiler vents sharing brackets with kitchen walls, the pathways that pass *through* buildings because the buildings were there first.

And it sees what the people of the Cut cannot see, because you cannot see the thing you are inside of.

The Cut is not a settlement. The Cut is an *organ*. The people are its cells. The rail line is a nerve. The refineries are glands. The deep core — that throat of darkening stone that drops into silence below Level Seven — is a ventricle. The column is breathing, and the people are breathing with it, and the whole system pulses in a rhythm that the power recognizes because it has heard it before, in coral reefs, in root networks, in the slow metabolic churn of organisms so large that civilizations rise and fall on their surfaces without ever suspecting what they live upon.

The power feels something then. Something rare, even for a power that has existed since before the salt pan was a seabed and the column was a seed of mineral ambition in a world that had given up on ambition entirely.

It feels *respect*.

---

For a long time — the kind of long that has no unit of measurement, the kind of long that is simply *enough* — the power does nothing but observe.

It watches the skitterlings emerge from their fissures in the dim hours, copper-chitin rivers pouring across the walls. It watches the harness-jackers scale the refinery vents, their black-green crests vibrating as they strip the mineral crust from the mesh filters. It watches the thermals — and when it watches the thermals, something in the quality of its attention shifts, becomes softer, becomes almost *tender*, the way you might look at a child sleeping in a place that is not entirely safe.

The thermals feel it. Of course they do. They have no eyes, no ears, no nervous system that anyone can identify. But they feel it the way the column feels a tremor through ten thousand meters of stone — not as information, but as *presence*. The thermals alter their circuits. One of them — the largest, the oldest, the one that has been drifting in its slow orbit since before the first rail spike was driven into the column's base — descends. Not far. Just slightly. Just enough that its intake spirals, vast and ciliated, now opening and closing in a rhythm that is not quite respiration and not quite *greeting*, are level with the highest platform in the Cut.

The people on the upper terraces look up. They have seen thermals before, of course. They have always seen thermals. But none of them, in all the generations the Cut has held humans against its skin, have seen one descend this far.

The organ-tone shifts. It deepens by a quarter-tone, a change so slight that only the most sensitive ears register it — and yet every person in the Cut feels it simultaneously, a pressure in the chest, a catch in the breath, a momentary sensation that something *very old* has just acknowledged something *very new*.

A woman on the highest platform — not the council, not the foremen, just a refinery worker named Tel who has lived in the Cut for eleven years and who is known for nothing more than the quality of her silence — steps to the railing and looks up. The thermal is close. Close enough that she can see the membranous body shifting within, the slow currents that move through it like thought through a mind. She does not reach for it. She does not speak to it. She simply *holds its gaze* — or whatever the motion is that passes between a thing with no eyes and a thing with two.

The power watches this, and something in the power approves.

---

After a time, the power moves.

Not quickly. Not dramatically. It moves the way a root moves through soil — slowly, and in the direction of what it senses is needed.

It goes to the deep core.

It descends — past the lowest platforms, past the bolted hatches and the warning signs in three different handwritings, past Level Seven where the vibration begins and the heartbeat-rhythm starts and the air becomes so thick with mineral dust that it tastes like biting into a stone. It descends past the burrowing worm casts, the sinuous impressions left in stone by something flexible and patient and impossibly old. It descends through narrowing passages where the calccrete walls press close and the striations become denser and older, rings upon rings, growth rings of a thing that grew in a world without rain or soil or sun, fed by chemical patience and geological time.

The power reaches the bottom. Or rather, it reaches the bottom of the part that resembles a shaft. Below that, the column opens — not into a cavern, not into a void, but into *tissue*. The mineral composition changes. The calccrete gives way to something denser, stranger, threaded with luminous veins that pulse with a light that is not phosphorescence and not chemofungal glow but something altogether *other* — something that looks, if you are a power that has seen many things, very much like a circulatory system. Like blood vessels. Like the interior of a heart.

The column is alive. Not metaphorically. Not poetically. Not in the way the geologists half-suspect and the old rail operator believes in her bones. *Actually* alive. A living thing the size of a skyscraper, rooted in a dead salt pan on a world that forgot how to be alive, quietly metabolizing the stone beneath it, building itself ring by ring over ages so vast they make human history look like a spark.

The power places its hand — or its attention, or its equivalent of both — against the inner wall of the column's deepest heart-chamber, and it feels the pulse. Slow. Enormous. Patient beyond patience. The column has been growing for millennia. It will grow for millennia more. It has survived the salt pan, the desiccation, the mining, the drilling, the dynamite, the four thousand human lives pressed against its skin like barnacles on the hull of a ship. It has survived all of this, not because it is strong, but because it is *persistent*. Because life, in its most fundamental and irreducible form, is the thing that does not stop.

The power understands something then, and what it understands makes it sad in a way that predates language and will outlast civilizations.

The column does not know the people are living inside it.

Not in the way that a body knows its cells are alive. Not consciously, not intentionally. The column is alive the way a reef is alive — vast, systemic, responsive, but without the centralized awareness that would make it *someone*. The people of the Cut are not parasites. They are not symbionts, not exactly. They are *inhabitants*. The column grows and they grow with it. The column breathes and they breathe with it. They have built their lives in its body and they have changed it — the dust, the vibration, the warmth of four thousand metabolizing humans pressed against its interior walls has altered the column's chemistry in ways that are, in fact, measurable. The miners have cut new channels. The refineries have introduced thermal gradients. The water reclamation systems have introduced moisture — microscopic amounts, barely a whisper, but in a system as delicately balanced as this one, a whisper is a shout. The humans are not destroying the column. They are *feeding* it. Inadvertently, unknowingly, the way cells feed an organism without ever understanding what an organism is.

And the column, if it could understand, would not object.

---

The power stays in the deep core for a very long time, feeling the pulse, listening to the mineral veins hum their subsonic song. And when it finally rises, it carries with it a decision — not a decree, not a command, but a decision, the way a gardener decides how to tend a tree.

Here is what the power does.

**First**, it *protects*.

It extends itself — thinly, transparently — across the salt pan in a lattice no human eye can see and no instrument can detect. A barrier, not of force but of *subtlety*. The salt pan has always been hostile: the heat, the desiccation, the alkaline dust that eats metal and leather and lung tissue alike. The power does not change this. But it *modulates* it. A traveler who wanders too far from the rail line will find, without understanding why, that their water lasts a little longer. That the wind dies down at the worst moment. That the salt flats, which should be featureless, contain just enough geological irregularity — a ridge here, a shallow depression there — to give them landmarks. The power will not prevent anyone from reaching the Cut. But it will ensure that those who arrive do so on the edge of survival rather than past it. It will keep the Cut's isolation intact, because isolation is what has kept the column's ecosystem from being overwhelmed.

**Second**, it *strengthens*.

The deep core — the column's heart-chamber — is where the growth happens. Where calccrete deposits in slow, concentric rings, where the luminous veins pulse and branch and extend. The power places something there, deep in the mineral tissue: not a foreign object, not a gift, but a *catalyst*. A seed-structure, molecular in scale, woven into the crystal lattice of the column's deepest walls. It will not change the column's nature. It will not make it sentient. It will not grant it awareness or speech or the ability to do any of the things that powers do. What it will do is this: the column will grow faster. Not much. Not noticeably. But measurably. Ring by ring, year by year, the column will add to itself with a richness and complexity it would not have achieved on its own. Over centuries, the calccrete will become denser, the mineral veins more intricate, the internal structure more capable of supporting the strange and improbable ecosystem that has taken root within it.

The power is giving the column *time*. More time to become what it is becoming. Which is — the power senses, though it cannot say how — something unprecedented. A living structure that has, by sheer geological patience and chemical accident, begun to *think*. Not the way humans think, with language and intention and memory. But the way a mycelial network thinks — through signal and response, through slow chemical argument, through the distributed intelligence of a trillion mineral reactions happening in concert. In a thousand years, the column may wake up. In ten thousand, it may *dream*. The power does not know. The power simply believes that it would be a tragedy — the deepest kind of tragedy, the kind that exists before there are words for it — to cut short a process that has taken this long to begin.

**Third**, it *speaks*.

Not to the people. People are clever and they will speak for themselves. The power has seen what human cleverness does to living systems, and it knows that the people of the Cut will either learn to listen to the column or they will not, and either way is part of the process.

Instead, the power speaks to the thermals.

It finds them circling near the crown of the column, three of them, their membranous bodies shifting in the updraft from the deep core. The power does not give them language. It does not give them eyes or mouths or the ability to descend to the walkways and commune with the people below. What it does is subtler than that, and perhaps more important.

It gives them *awareness*. Not of the people. Not of the refineries or the dynamite or the four thousand human lives vibrating in the stone. But of *each other*. And of the column. The thermals, which have drifted in their overlapping circuits since before the first human set foot on the salt pan, have done so without knowing why. They are drawn to the deep core — to the warmth and the vibration and the chemical richness rising through the column's interior — but they have never *understood* this pull. Now, gently, the power threads a new kind of awareness into their spirals. Not intelligence. Not consciousness in any way that would be recognizable to a human philosopher. Something more like *faith*. A knowing without knowing. A sense that the column is alive, and that they are part of it, and that their slow, circling flight is not random but *meaningful*.

The oldest thermal — the one that descended to Tel's level, the one that shifted its organ-tone by a quarter-tone — feels this change most acutely. Its circuits tighten. Its spirals open wider. It rises higher than it has risen in centuries, until it is a pale smudge against the dim sky, and from that height the column looks to it like what it is: a living thing, breathing, growing, ringing with the slow pulse of its own becoming.

The thermals do not change their behavior. To anyone watching from the walkways below, they drift as they have always drifted, slow and purposeless and sacred. But now they drift with *intention*, and the intention is this: *stay*. Stay near the column. Stay in the updraft. Keep the particulate flowing through the spirals, keep the chemical exchange alive, keep the deep core breathing. The thermals have become, without knowing it, the column's most devoted caretakers.

**Fourth** — and this is the thing that the power considers most carefully, and does last, and does with the tenderness of something placing a single seed into dark soil —

It leaves a *story*.

Not written down. Not carved into stone or encoded in light or hidden in the depths of the deep core. The power knows that the people of the Cut distrust written words — they are a people of texture and pressure, of knuckle-rap rhythms and respirator-tilt nuance. A book would be an insult. A carved inscription would be ignored or demolished to make room for a refinery vent.

So the power tells the story *into the stone*.

It speaks directly to the calccrete, the way you might speak to a sleeping child — quietly, and with the understanding that the child may not consciously hear, but will carry the words in their body regardless. The power tells the column what it is. It tells the column that it is alive, that it is becoming something no one on this world has seen before, that the humans in its body are part of its growing the way rain is part of a tree's growing, and that the skitterlings and the jackers and the thermals and the burrowing worms and the flicker-clouds and the rustbloom are all part of a single, vast, slow, magnificent experiment in life doing what life does — persisting, adapting, becoming.

The calccrete absorbs the words the way it absorbs everything: slowly, and completely, and without comment. The crystalline veins in the column's walls shift, almost imperceptibly, and in the mineral lattice a new pattern forms — not language, not code, not anything that can be decoded by a linguist or a computer. But something that can be *felt*. The same way the people of the Cut learned to feel the column under their boots. The same way the miners know what level they are on by the texture of the stone. Something that will, over time and without anyone being able to explain how, change the way the people of the Cut understand their home.

Not today. Not tomorrow. In a generation, a person will press their hand to the wall and feel something new in the stone — a warmth, a rhythm, a sense that the column is not just a resource or a foundation or a place to live but a *presence*. And that person will not be able to explain it, and the other people of the Cut will look at them the way people always look at those who sense things others cannot: with a mixture of suspicion and reverence. And slowly, and without anyone announcing it, the culture of the Cut will shift. The deep core will no longer be spoken of with avoidance. The column will no longer be thought of as a thing that is mined but as a thing that *lives*. The people will not worship the column — they are too pragmatic, too dry, too rooted in the practical mechanics of survival for that. But they will treat it differently. The way a farmer treats a field that has been in the family for generations — not with awe, but with *care*. With the understanding that the field is not just dirt and minerals but something that requires tending, something that gives and takes and must be kept in balance if it is to endure.

The power knows this because it has done this before. A hundred thousand times, on a hundred thousand worlds, it has planted stories in stone and soil and water and waited for them to take root. Sometimes they do. Sometimes they don't. The power does not grieve for the ones that don't. It simply plants again.

---

And then the power leaves.

Not dramatically. Not with a closing of the sky or a parting of the clouds or a final benediction that echoes across the salt pan. The power leaves the way a gardener leaves a garden after planting — with the quiet satisfaction of having done what can be done, and the humility of knowing that the rest is up to the seed, and the soil, and time.

On the salt pan, the wind picks up. A stinging gust of alkaline dust rolls across the flats and batters the rail platform, and the workers in the lower walkways duck inside and seal the vents and wait for it to pass. The arc lamps flicker and buzz. A skitterling scuttles across the railing and disappears into a crack. The dynamite charge in the upper expansion zone goes off — a flash of light, a deep felt *thump* that rolls through the column like a heartbeat — and the dust settles.

The thermals drift in their slow orbits, their spirals opening and closing, their organ-tone sustaining its single, ancient, unfathomable note.

Far below, in the deep core, something that is not quite alive and not quite mineral and not quite anything that has a name begins to hum.

It is not a new sound. It has always been there. But it is louder now. Just slightly. Just enough.

The Cut holds.

And somewhere, in a place beyond the salt pan and beyond the sky and beyond the reach of any language spoken or unspoken, the power turns its attention elsewhere — to another column, another world, another seed that needs planting — and the silence it leaves behind is not empty but *full*, full the way a lung is full after a long breath, full the way a field is full in the moment before planting, when anything is still possible and nothing has been decided and the soil is dark and waiting.

The column breathes.

The people breathe with it.

And the rustbloom, that scaly, rust-colored, chemically-alive crust that is the closest thing to plant life on the column, spreads across a freshly fractured face of calccrete in the lower expansion zone, and for one moment — brief and unwitnessed and utterly ordinary — it glows.</arg_value>
