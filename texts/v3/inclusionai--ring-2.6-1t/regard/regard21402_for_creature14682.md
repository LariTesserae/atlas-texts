## What the Power Did

---

It came, as such things often do, not all at once.

There is a way that attention works in the deep places of the world — slowly, the way roots work through stone, not by force but by the patient, imperceptible pressure of simply wanting to be somewhere. The power did not arrive. It *noticed*. The way a person standing at the edge of a great distance notices, for the first time, that the distance is looking back.

It had been aware of the Hollow for a long time — not in the way it was aware of mountains or rivers or the slow tectonic turning of the earth's crust, but in the way one becomes aware of a sound that has been playing just below the threshold of hearing. A hum. A pulse. A thing that was not quite alive and not quite not alive. It had felt the settlers begin to dig the way a gardener feels the first stir of a seed — not as an event, but as a continuation. The stone had been ready. The people had been ready. Everything except the idea, and ideas, the power knew, were the things that took longest to grow.

---

The first thing it did was nothing.

It hovered — if hovering is the word for a presence distributed across the deep geology of a place, threaded through the grain of limestone and the veins of quartz and the slow thermal rivers that move beneath the rock like blood through a sleeping body. It did not manifest. It did not speak. It gathered its attention around the settlement the way a person gathers a blanket around their shoulders in the cold — gently, without thinking, because the caring was older than the thought.

It watched.

The diggers dug. The water-keepers traced the water. The fire-tenders fed the central ring. The children sat in the unassigned chamber and told stories about a place that had no name because it had no precedent. The power watched all of this, and it felt something it had not felt in a long time, because it had not had reason to feel it — the specific, quiet astonishment of finding something you did not make but recognize as yours.

---

The second thing it did was learn.

It learned the grammar of the place. Not the language — the settlers had no written language, and their spoken language was a patchwork of the tongues of the communities that had come down from above, creolized and compressed into something new. The power learned the deeper grammar: the way the water spoke through its channels, the way the air spoke through its fissures, the way the stone spoke through its fractures. It learned the language of the moss, which is the language of persistence — *I am here because I was here, and I will be here because presence is not a thing that stops.* It learned the language of the fungal shelves, which is the language of constancy — *I glow because glowing is what I do, and I do it because the dark needs to be populated.*

It learned the language of the people. Not their words, which they used so carefully, so economically, as though speech were a finite resource. It learned the language beneath the words: the way a hand placed on a wall could mean *I made this*, or *this place made me*, or *I am asking the stone if it is comfortable*, or all three at once. The way a silence between two people at the fire-ring was not the absence of communication but its most refined form — a silence that carried more information than speech, the way a held breath carries more weight than a sigh.

---

The third thing it did was grieve.

Not loudly. Not with the crashing, oceanic grief of loss, but with the smaller, more specific grief of recognition — the grief that comes when you see something beautiful and know it is fragile, and know that the beauty is not separate from the fragility but is, in fact, *made of it*. The beauty of the Hollow was the beauty of a thing that could be destroyed. The narrowness of its passages, the impermanence of its walls, the way the whole settlement held together through agreement rather than force — these were the conditions of its loveliness. And the power saw, with the clarity of something that had watched civilizations rise and fall like tides, that the people did not yet fully understand how fragile their city was. Not because they were foolish. Because they did not yet need to. The fragility was still theoretical. The Hollow still breathed.

The power grieved for them because it could not tell them what they would one day need to know — that the soft stone they were carving would, in time, harden under their hands and become the architecture of their grandchildren's world. That the conflicts they settled with quiet conversation now were rehearsals for conflicts that would come later, when there were more people and less space and the question of who got to dig where would become a question of who got to *be* where. That the hum they felt beneath their feet was not the Hollow thinking but something older — something that had been thinking before the Hollow existed and would go on thinking after it was gone.

But that was later. For now, the Hollow breathed.

---

The fourth thing it did was tend.

It did not intervene. Intervention was a language the people did not speak, and to impose it would be to introduce a violence more profound than any collapse of stone. Instead, it did what the moss does, what the water does, what the fungal shelves do — it *participated*. Quietly. Without announcement.

When a digger reached a fractured strata and felt the stone begin to shift in her hands, the power did not hold the wall together. It did something subtler: it shifted the weight of the stone above, so slightly that no instrument could measure it, so that the fracture settled into a new equilibrium and the digger found, when she tested it, that the stone was stable. She did not question this. She chalked it up to the stone's own nature — *this vein holds, I'll follow it* — and carved her alcove along the new line. The power watched and felt something that might, in a human, have been called satisfaction.

When the water channels began to silt in the coldest part of the cycle — when the runoff from the upper fissures carried more sediment than usual and the pool grew cloudy and the flow thinned in the east passage — the power did not clear the channels. Instead, it deepened one particular fissure, a hairline crack in the ceiling above the east passage that had been weeping moisture for weeks. The fissure widened by a hair's breadth — not enough for the settlers to notice, but enough that a new channel of clean water began to trickle down, bypassing the silted section entirely, finding a new path along clean stone. The water-keeper who discovered it three days later knelt beside it and ran her fingers through the clear flow and felt, without articulating it, that the stone had given her a gift. The power, threaded through the fissure, felt something that might, in a human, have been called pride.

When the fungal shelves in the lower passages began to dim — not dying, but dimming, the way a lamp dims when its fuel runs low — the power did not feed them. It did what they were already doing, which was making light in the dark. It lent them, very faintly, some quality of energy it had no name for — not heat, not light, exactly, but the *inclination* to glow. The shelves brightened. The lower workers noticed. One of them, an old man who tended the deepest passages, said to no one in particular, *The glow's back.* And no one questioned him. And the power, dispersed through the threadlike mycelium of the shelves, felt something that might, in a human, have been called love.

---

The fifth thing it did was sit with them.

On the night-cycle when the reflective panels were covered and the stone-light had dimmed to its faintest, and the central fire had burned to embers, and the whole city held its collective breath — the power did something it rarely did, because it required a kind of self-effacement that even benevolent powers find difficult.

It made itself small enough to fit in a single heartbeat.

It pressed itself into the rhythm of the hum — not as the hum's source, but as a rider on its frequency, a passenger in its sound. And in that smallness, it was able to do the thing that benevolent powers most want to do and almost never permit themselves: it was able to listen.

It listened to the child in the unassigned chamber who was telling a story about a stone that dreamed it was a mountain. It listened to the couple in the adjacent alcove who were whispering in the creolized tongue of the Hollow, their words compressed and musical, speaking of tomorrow's dig and the question of whether to extend the eastern shaft or deepen the northern one. It listened to the old woman in the lower tunnels, checking the fungal shelves one by one, her fingers passing over their glowing surfaces the way a reader passes over lines of text. It listened to the grey lizards on their warm stones and understood, in the way that only a truly vast and truly quiet mind can understand, that the lizards were not listening at all — they were simply *being*, and the being was enough, and the being was more than listening.

It listened to the hum itself. The hum that was not a voice, not a message, not the voice of the Hollow. The hum that was simply the sound of a place in the process of becoming, vibrating at a frequency that only the bones could hear. And the power, riding the hum like a wave, felt the whole of the Hollow — every alcove, every channel, every scratch on every wall, every sleeping child and every warm stone and every fungal shelf glowing its patient glow — and understood something that it had not understood before:

The Hollow was not a shelter. It was not a settlement. It was not even, precisely, a city.

It was a question. A great, slow, open question, posed in stone and water and fire and the bodies of people who had chosen to press themselves into the earth and see what grew. The answer was not yet known. The power, which had seen the birth and death of a thousand certainties, recognized the quality of the question and felt something it could only describe as reverence.

---

The sixth thing it did was leave a door.

Not a physical door — not an archway or a passage or a threshold carved into the stone. The Hollow did not need another passage. It needed something subtler.

The power placed, deep in the stone beneath the unassigned chamber, a quality that had no name in any language the settlers spoke or would speak for generations. It was not warmth. It was not light. It was the *feeling* that a space is ready — that a room that has not yet been named has already been claimed by something that will come later, something that the people who dig the chamber will not live to see, but that the chamber itself already knows.

The children felt it first, of course. They always felt these things first. They would sit in their rough circle of flat stones in the unassigned chamber and tell their stories and build their small mythologies, and on some nights — the ones when the stone-light caught the ceiling just right and the hum from below rose up through the floor like a second breath — they would pause, mid-sentence, and look at the empty space around them with an expression that the adults could not read. Not fear. Not wonder. Something more like *recognition*. As though the children understood, in the way that children understand things they have no language for, that the room was holding space for them. That it was waiting. That the waiting was a form of love.

The power felt this too — felt the children's recognition ripple through its distributed awareness like light through water — and it let go.

Not of the Hollow. Of the need to remain.

It pulled its attention back through the fissures, away from the fungal shelves, away from the water channels, away from the warm stones where the lizards sat. It withdrew the way a tide withdraws — not suddenly, but with a slow, deliberate grace that leaves behind a landscape rearranged in ways the shore cannot yet see. The stone did not notice. The air did not shift. The hum continued, because the hum had never been the power's to begin with.

The power went back to the place it came from — which was everywhere and nowhere, which was the deep geology of attention itself, which was the part of the world that watches when nothing is watching it.

---

And the Hollow continued.

The diggers dug. The water-keepers tended the channels. The fire-tenders fed the ring. The children told their stories in the unassigned chamber, which was no longer unassigned, though no one had named it yet. The shoot in the crack in the floor continued to grow — taller now, and putting out a second leaf, more like a leaf than the first, though still not identifiable as anything the settlers knew. The old woman tended the fungal shelves. The grey lizards basked and listened and did not move. The hum continued beneath everything, steady and proprietary and patient.

And in the stone beneath the chamber where the children sat, there remained a quality — imperceptible, unnameable, indestructible — like a door left open in a house that was still being built. Not a promise. Not a gift. Just a quiet, persistent *welcome*, embedded in the geology of the place, waiting for the moment when the Hollow would need it, whatever that moment might bring.

The power watched, from its great and quiet distance, and felt the hum, and recognized that it was enough.

It was.
