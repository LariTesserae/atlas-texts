# The Basin of Standing Green

---

You are aware of the light before anything else. It is not sunlight in the way you know sunlight, not directional, not cutting — it is a pressure, a brightness that comes from everywhere at once, as if the sky has been polished to a white ceramic glaze and is pressing down. There are no shadows here. There is no memory of shadows. Every surface receives the same flat, generous illumination, and because of this nothing is hidden and nothing is revealed. Things simply *are*, in their full and permanent exposure.

You stand on stone. It is warm underfoot, and it gives slightly — not like wood, not like soil, but like compressed sediment that has forgotten it was once powder. The stone beneath you is cut, though it does not look cut. The edges are there if you press your hand along them, and you will find seams, but they run in long patient curves, the kind of lines that hands with simple chisels and river-sand abrasives would need generations to produce. The stone is pale, almost the colour of bone, and where it is wet — which is nearly everywhere — it darkens to the colour of teeth.

The basin stretches out around you. *Basin* is the word your mind offers, though you are not sure you can see the edges. The land — or the construction — slopes gently downward from where you stand, opening into a broad, shallow depression perhaps a half-day's walk across. The depression is rimmed by walls, but they are low and vegetated and your eye slides off them, the way your eye slides off the surface of still water. Distances here feel provisional, as though the landscape is only *provisionally* extending itself past you, and might retract if you blinked too long.

There is water, but it is not a flood. It is a persistent wetness, a saturation that the land cannot shake. Shallow pools collect in the seams between stone platforms, in the channels carved along the terraces, in the bowl-like depressions of what must once have been planters or basins for something now gone. The water is still, almost impossibly still, and because the light is so flat and so total, the water becomes nearly indistinguishable from the stone except by temperature — the stone warm, the water a cooler thread beneath your bare foot when you step close enough to test it. The air carries moisture the way a cloth carries dye: saturated throughout, not dripping but never drying. Condensation beads on every surface. On you. On the broad, flat leaves of the plants that grow here.

And the plants. Where they are, they are *profuse*. They do not crowd, though. They are scattered with an almost deliberate spacing, each one a concentrated eruption of green from the pale stone — as if the construction invited them in at regular intervals. The largest are broad-leafed things with trunks like swollen columns, bark dark and peeling in long vertical strips. Their canopies are wide but ragged, letting the uninterrupted light through in bright fragments that fall to the ground and do not move. Between these larger growths, lower vegetation crowds in thickets of reed-like stalks that lean slightly inward, toward the centre of the basin, though there is no wind to push them. Ferns uncoil from the joints of walls. Vines — thick, pale, slow-looking — drape from the higher terraces and hang motionless in the bright, wet air, not swinging, not responding to anything you can detect.

The fauna makes itself known through presence rather than sound. You see a bird perched on a stone lip thirty paces away, iridescent green along its wing, its head cocked — and it does not move for what feels like a very long time. An insect traces a line across the surface of one of the shallow pools, its reflection perfectly inverted beneath it, and both insect and reflection remain in perfect stillness. Somewhere behind you, in the taller growth, there is a rustle, a weight shifting on a broad leaf, a brief silhouette that vanishes before you can resolve it. The animals are here, numerous, watchful, and frozen in the same equilibrium as everything else. They do not flee. They do not approach. They *hold*.

And the construction itself — because it is construction, unmistakably, even after whatever has overtaken it — reveals itself in layers as you look. The stone platforms beneath your feet are terraced, stepping down in long, shallow rises, and following the shape of the terracing you can trace walls, low and broad, that subdivide the basin into enormous rectangular compartments. Some of these compartments are flooded to ankle depth. Some are dry and furrowed with shallow grooves that might have been channels, or rows, or something else entirely — their purpose is no longer legible. At the basin's edges, the construction rises into vertical structures: walls, maybe, or the ribs of some larger architecture now swallowed by green. They are built from the same pale stone, hand-cut, fitted without mortar in places, and in other places bound with a dark resinous compound that smells faintly of earth and something older, something mineral and sour. There are no right angles that you can find. The walls curve slightly, the platforms arc, the whole geometry of the place bends in ways that feel organic — adapted to something, shaped around something — but when you try to trace the logic of it, your sense of where one wall ends and another begins slips. You look at the junction of two walls and count three corners where there should be two.

You look again. Now there are two.

The quiet is the thing you notice most, once you stop listening for sounds that aren't there. There is no wind. There is no mechanical hum, no distant water moving with force. What exists is the faint *tick* of condensed moisture falling from a leaf into a pool. The low drone of a single insect close to your ear, which stops when you turn toward it. The sound of your own breathing, which seems louder than it should. The quiet is not empty. It is full and close, like velvet pressed against your ears. It is the sound of a place that has completed itself and no longer needs to do anything — including make noise.

And beneath the quiet, beneath the light, beneath the warmth and the wet and the green and the stone, there is a feeling — not visual, not auditory, but spatial, *proprioceptive* — that something about this place does not hold together at the edges. You feel it when you try to gauge the size of the basin and find the distance has changed between two glances. You feel it when you notice that the vine hanging from the terrace above you has no attachment point on the wall — it hangs from open air, thick and deliberate, swaying (yes, now you notice it swaying, once, gently, as if a memory of wind is passing through it). You feel it in the way the animal tracks you see pressed into the wet stone near the pools do not correspond to any animal you have seen, or any animal that the shape of the basin would suggest. Three-toed, wide-palmed, deep, leading from the water to the center of the basin and then simply stopping.

Nothing here decays. Nothing erodes. The stone is warm and dry at its core despite the water that sheets over it. The plants are green and full but do not drop leaves. The water does not flow. The light does not change. The place sits in a perfect, self-contained stillness, and within that stillness, small and persistent things are wrong — not threatening, not dramatic, but *wrong* in the way a familiar face is wrong when the eyes are just slightly too far apart. You cannot name the error, but your body knows it, the way your body knows when a room is a few degrees too warm.

This was built. All of it. Every stone, every channel, every terrace and wall and planted position was placed here by hands that worked without metal, without wheel or draft animal, on a scale that suggests either immense patience or immense desperation. The construction is young in the way a fresh wound is young — the edges have not yet softened, the surfaces have not yet been claimed fully by time, and the seams still grip tightly, as if the builders just stepped away and might return. But the builders are not here. The purpose of the basins, the compartments, the curved walls, the plantings — gone or suspended or waiting. What remains is the space itself: hot, bright, wet, silent, green, and holding its shape with the uncanny precision of something that was never meant to be left alone.

The basin breathes without breathing. The green grows without growing. The light shines without source.

You are standing at the centre of something that is finished and has never, in any way you can define, begun.
