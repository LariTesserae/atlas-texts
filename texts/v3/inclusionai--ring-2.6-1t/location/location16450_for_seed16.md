# The Vein Garden

You would not find it if you weren't already lost.

There is a seam in the earth — not a canyon, not a gorge, not a sinkhole, though you might use all those words before you realized they were wrong. It is a long, shallow bowl pressed into the underplate of the continent as if a thumb were slowly, over millennia, pushed into the bottom of a cooling sheet of glass. The walls are not steep. And then you're in it.

The first thing you notice is the heat, but the second is the silence — no, not silence. The opposite. There is a sound here that fills the space the way water would fill a cup. It is everywhere and it comes from nowhere. The lithophone effect: the standing columns ofite mineral growth that rise from the basin floor catch the thermal wind — and there is always wind here, convective, pulled upward from the heated stone like breath from a lung — and they sing. Not melodically. Not tunefully. A deep, oscillating hum, some of it below the range you'd call sound and more in the chest, a vibration you feel in the roots of your teeth before you hear it with your ears. The columns are of different heights and different densities and they produce different tones and the wind does not blow steadily and so the sound never resolves. It drones. It shifts. It swells and recedes like the breathing of something enormous that is asleep but not quite.

The source of the columns is the walls. Looking up — and you must look up, because the bowl is shallow but it is wide, and the sky is a pale copper disc overhead, too bright to look at directly but too dim in character to be called a sun; it is a disc of white-gold heat haze, featureless, without texture — looking up past the rim you can see the strata, the layered bands ofite mineral deposits in ochre, umber, vermillion, and a black so dark it is almost purple. The columns grow from these strata, extruded over ages the way a stone building grows from scaffoldings: slowly, and not all at once, and with the logic of materials under pressure rather than the logic of a builder's plan. They are roughly cylindrical, sometimes bifurcating near the top into two or three thinner spires, and their surfaces are not smooth — they are latticed with fine fractures that catch the light and throw it back as a dull, mineral shimmer, the only source of illumination in the bowl once the disc of sky is no longer visible between the column field.

And among them: the growths.

Call them plants and you will be understood, but you will be wrong. There is nothing green here. There is nothing wet here. There has never been anything wet here. The growths are silicate-based, rooted in the calcite substrate through networks ofite filament the thickness of a human hair, and they feed on thermal differentials — the heat rising from the basin floor, the relative cool of the upper air, the vibration of the columns themselves. They resemble, if you had to say, a cross between a sea anemone and a chandelier, though neither of those things is what they are. Their structures are fractal: branching, subdividing, each arm splitting into thinner arms splitting into thinner arms, until at the extremities there are structures so fine they are nearly translucent, and these catch the thermal updraft and sway — slowly, slowly, on a timescale you can almost perceive, the way a sleeping animal's flank rises and falls. Some of them are tall, six or seven meters from root-tip to highest filament. Others spread wide and low along the ground, their branches skimming the red dust, and these are the ones you trip over if you aren't watching where you step, because they are the same color as the ground until they move, and they move so slowly that the motion is a function of subtraction: you look away, you look back, and what you thought was a rock is now three feet to the left of where you thought it was.

The vegetation is lush. That is the only word for it. It islushlushlush. But it is lush the way a desert is vast — the richness is apparent only over time, over distance, as you begin to register the density of variation: the thicknesses of the branching, the differences in opacity, the way some specimens pulse with a faint internal phosphorescence (a blue-white, barely there, like the memory of lightning) while others are dark and absorbent. There are what you might call thickets — small clusters of the tall columns growing close together so that their filaments interweave into a canopy — and there are clearings where nothing grows and the ground is bare and cracked and radiating heat so that the air above it wavers. The clearings are the worst places to be, incidentally. They are the hottest spots in the bowl. The vegetation, for all its alienness, provides shade.

There are no animals. There is no scurrying. There is no wing-beat or footfall or vocalization from a throat. The soundscape is entirely mineral and atmospheric: the singing columns, the faint creak and shudder of thermal expansion in the stone underfoot, the whisper of filament moving through air, and occasionally — every few minutes, irregularly — a sharp crack as one of the columns fractures a fraction under its own weight and the sound bounces off the basin walls and returns to you as an echo that seems to come from a direction slightly different from the original, as if the bowl were not quite the shape you think it is.

Which it isn't. But we'll come to that.

The settlement is on the eastern rim, where the gradient is shallowest and the columns are smallest.

It would be generous to call it a village. It is forty structures, perhaps, made from the same material as everything else here — the mineral substrate, cut and stacked and mortared with a paste made from ground calciumiteite and thermal resin harvested from the bases of the larger growths. The resin is viscous, amber-dark, and it smells like nothing you can name, and it hardens in the heat toite somethingite something approachingite stone. The builders — whoever they are, and they are here, you can see that from the structures, though you have not yet seen them — they use no metal. No mortar in the conventional sense. Stone on stone, resin between, hand-shaped with tools ofite shapedite basiteite basite basalt anditeite bonite. Simple. Functional. The structures are low and rounded, hugging the curve of the rim, and they are built into the existing formations rather than replacing them: a wall that incorporates a standing column as a support, a roof that uses an overhang of natural stone as its outermost edge. The settlement does not impose itself on the landscape. It grows out of it, the way the growths themselves grow out of the calcite floor.

There are wide, shallow channels cut into the ground between the structures — irrigation, perhaps, though there is nothing to irrigate in any sense you'd recognize. The channels are carefully graded, following the natural slope of the rim, and they concentrate the thermal updrafts into specific corridors. The residents, if that is what they are, have learned to shape the wind. You can feel it as you walk through the settlement: the channels take the singing of the columns and focus it, amplify it, create pockets of near-silence and pockets of overwhelming resonance. One structure — the largest, set at the highest point on the rim — sits in a dead zone where no sound reaches it at all, and it is eerie to stand near that building, to feel the vibration stop so abruptly, like stepping off a cliff.

There is evidence of long habitation. The stone underfoot inside the structures is worn smooth. The hand-tools left behind — you find them in alcoves, in small caches — are of a craftsmanship that suggests generations of refinement. Chisels. Awls. Scrapers with edges ofite so fine they could slice filament. A single hammerstone, palm-sized, worn into a shape that exactly fits a human grip. No metal. No wheel. No writing, unless the patterns scored into the lintels of the largest buildings constitute a script, which you cannot confirm because they predate any written language you know and follow no logic you can parse.

Whoever built this place knew the columns. They built around them with the care of people who understand that the singing is load-bearing — not acoustically, but structurally. The vibration keeps the mineral lattice of the columns from fracturing further. Remove the sound, and the columns would crumble. The settlement is built inside a living system and the residents are — were — part of it.

Now: the weirdness.

You will not notice it at first. The landscape is strange enough that the weirdness hides within the general strangeness, the way a figure in a crowd is invisible until it moves.

The geometry is soft.

Walk from the rim to the center of the basin and count your steps. You will need exactly forty-seven. Walk back. You will count forty-nine. The difference is small. The distance is the same — you can verify this by measuring, by laying out a line, by any empirical method you choose. The number of steps will not agree. It is not that you are walking faster or slower. It is that the space itself is not quite consistent with itself. The basin is wider than it has any right to be given the size of its rim as seen from outside. You can see this if you climb the wall — and the wall is climbable, theite stoneite crumbling and soft enough to get hand- and foot-holds — and look down. The columns are farther apart than they appear from the floor. The growths are taller. The scale of the interior does not match the scale of the exterior. You are inside something that is larger on the inside than the outside permits.

The residents knew. The patterns on the lintels encode the discrepancy — not explaining it, not resolving it, but acknowledging it, the way a mapmaker notes that true north and magnetic north diverge. They built with extra space in mind. The rooms are slightly too large for the number of people they could reasonably house. The corridors are slightly too wide. The channels are slightly too deep. They are compensating. They have always been compensating.

And sometimes — rarely, unpredictably — the discrepancy shifts. A column that was three meters tall is now four. A cluster of growths that was dense is now sparse. The stones underfoot have moved, not dramatically, not enough to notice in a single step, but if you mark a spot and return to it after an hour, it is six inches to the left and two inches lower than it was. The bowl is breathing. Slowly. On a timescale that is not geological and not human but something in between.

The sound changes with it. When the bowl inhales, the columns lean very slightly inward and the pitch drops, all of them, simultaneously, a descent so gradual it is only noticeable in contrast with the silence that follows the exhale. The residents must have lived with their whole lives organized around this rhythm. You begin to feel it before you can hear it — your body knows before your ears do, a pressure change in the chest, a shift in the weight of the air.

You are standing in the lung of the earth and it is breathing and it isoldoldold and it will continue long after you have climbed out of the bowl and walked back across the calcite flats toward whatever you came here from, and the last thing you will remember — the thing that will surface in your mind at odd moments in the weeks after, in the shower, while falling asleep, in the space between one thought and the next — is the sound of the columns, deep and unresolved and full, filling the space the way water fills a cup, and how for a moment, standing in the dead zone above the largest structure, you heard nothing at all, and the silence was so complete it had texture, was a material, was a presence, was a thing leaning close and listening to you the way you were listening to it.
