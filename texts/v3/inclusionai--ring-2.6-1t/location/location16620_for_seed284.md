## The Column of Calcara

You see it before you feel it — which is strange, because it is enormous.

A single column of pale mineral rock, roughly hexagonal in cross-section, rising from a flat and featureless salt pan that stretches to the horizon in every direction. The salt pan is not white; it is the color of old bone, and it is utterly, punishingly dry. There is no water here. There has never been water here, or if there was, it left so long ago that even the memory of it has mineralized — deposited in thin, crystalline veins that run through the column's lower strata like the ghost-rivers of a world that briefly, foolishly, wept.

The column is perhaps two hundred meters tall. Its sides are sheer, freshly fractured — the erosion number is young, and it shows. The faces are not weathered smooth but raw, broken, splintering inward at ledges and shelves where the stone has split along crystal planes. The edges are sharp enough to cut. The top is a ragged plateau, slightly concave, as if the column were a stump — and in a sense, it is. Geologists who have studied it (and there are many, because Calcara is one of only seven such columns in the known salt basin) disagree about what it is a stump *of*. Some say igneous extrusion. Others say it is biological — the petrified trunk of something that tried to grow in a world that had forgotten how to grow things. The column is laced with a mineral structure that resembles vascular tissue when cut in cross-section, though no one has been able to prove it carries anything. It is unusual. Not impossible. Just wrong enough to make the back of your neck tighten.

The temperature is cool — not cold, but the kind of cool that sits on your skin like a damp cloth that isn't damp. Around nine to eleven degrees, steady, regardless of the time of cycle. The air is thin and tastes ofite — of powdered stone and static charge. There is a faint metallic sweetness at the back of the throat that newcomers find disorienting and old residents find comforting, the way anyone finds the smell of home comforting.

---

You arrive at the base by rail — a single-track industrial line that runs across the salt pan on elevated girders, itself a recent addition. The train is electric, powered by a generator complex three columns to the west, and it shudders to a halt at a platform bolted directly into the rock face at the column's foot. The platform is a brutal thing: riveted steel decking, bolted timber planking already warping from the dry heat, and a railing made of pipe that has been painted yellow so many times that the paint is now geological itself — layer upon layer, flaking in the mineral wind.

From the platform, you look up.

The settlement is called **Hive-Calcara**, though no one who lives there calls it that. They call it **the Cut**, because the town was not built on top of the column so much as *into* it. The residents — miners, engineers, refinery workers, tool-makers, and the vast population of people who exist to serve them — have carved, cut, bolted, welded, and nailed their lives onto every available surface of the column. The lower third is a vertical city of scaffold platforms, suspended walkways, hoist cages, and industrial shelving. The middle third is where the real construction begins — proper buildings, pressed-metal facades bolted to drilled anchors, terraced housing cantilevered out from the rock face, narrow balconies held up by nothing more than engineering confidence and the fact that the column's stone is dense enough to hold. The upper third — near the top, where the column begins to narrow into its concave crown — is quieter, newer, less built-up. It is where the expansion is happening now. Where the dynamite echoes come from.

The settlement is **dense** in every sense. Structures are layered, stacked, and interleaved. A boiler vent from a mid-level refinery shares a mounting bracket with someone's kitchen wall. A laundry line strung between two antenna masts crosses a ventilation shaft. Walkways pass through the interiors of buildings because no one could agree on whether the path or the building came first, and so both simply exist, and people move through the walls the way water moves through gravel — finding the path of least resistance without comment.

There are perhaps four thousand people living in the Cut. It feels like more. It feels like everyone is *in* something — tucked into crevices, wedged into gaps, living in the interstices of industry the way lichen lives in the cracks of a sidewalk, if lichen were loud and smoked dried chemofungus and argued about ore grades.

---

There is no vegetation. None. The concept does not exist here in any form that would be recognizable to someone from a wet world. What grows — and the word "grows" is generous — is chemotrophic mineralweed, a crusty, rust-colored fungal-analog that spreads across exposed rock surfaces in thin, papery sheets. It is not alive in the way plants are alive. It is a mineral reaction — a slow crystallization process that propagates across surfaces whenite temperature and mineral saturation reach certain thresholds. The people of the Cut call it **rustbloom**, and they scrape it off their walls with putty knives when it gets thick, because it is mildly corrosive and smells like vinegar made from nails. It is the closest thing to plant life on the column, and it is barely that.

The fauna, though — the fauna is another matter entirely.

There are **skitterlings**. Small, many-legged creatures with exoskeletons like hammered copper, six to twelve centimeters long, that live in the cracks of the column's stone and emerge at dim-light hours to feed on rustbloom and on each other. They are fast and they are everywhere. When you walk the lower walkways at night, they scatter from your boots in shimmering waves, their legs clicking against the stone like tiny castanets. They are the base of whatever passes for a food chain here, though "chain" is too linear a word for what actually happens — it is more of a web, a tangle, a knot.

Above the skitterlings: **harness-jackers**, mid-sized arthropodal creatures with wide, flat bodies and four grasping arms, each ending in a hooked claw. They are the size of a large cat. They live on the upper walkways and the scaffolding, and they hunt skitterlings, but they also — and this is the part that makes the Cut's residents tolerate them — they eat the mineral deposits that clog the refinery vents. The refinery operators have learned to leave out offerings of crushedite crystal on the rooftops, and in return, the jackers keep the vents clear. It is not domestication. It is a negotiation.

And above the jackers: **the thermals**. You feel them before you see them — a displacement of air, a sudden warm updraft from the column's heated interior. They are enormous, pale, membranous creatures that ride the thermal currents rising through the column's hollow core. They have no eyes. They have no mouths, as far as anyone can tell. They are filter-feeders of some kind, drawing inite particulate through vast, ciliated intake spirals on their undersides. They drift in slow, stately circles near the column's peak, occasionally emitting a sound like a church organ sustaining a single, low note. The people of the Cut consider them sacred, or at least portentous. No one harms a thermal. No one even agrees on whether they are fauna or weather.

There are others. Smaller things. Things in the walls. Things in the vents. A species of burrowing worm that vibrates at a frequency just below hearing, so that you feel it in your molars. Flocks of something — not birds, nothing so familiar — that move in tight, coordinated clouds through the column's upper chambers, their bodies flickering with bioluminescence, appearing and disappearing like a signal you can't quite decode. The fauna is abundant, and most of it is still being catalogued, and some of it has not been catalogued at all because it bites back.

---

The **light** is dim and it comes from everywhere and nowhere. The column's stone has a faint natural phosphorescence — a mineral property, perhaps, or perhaps the rustbloom's chemofungal metabolism leaching energy from theite bonds and releasing it as a dull, amber-green glow. This light is barely enough to see by, but it is enough, and it gives the whole interior of the Cut a permanent twilight quality, as if the place is always dusk, always becoming night.

Supplementing this: industrial lighting. Gas-lanterns bolted to walls, their mantles glowing a sickly yellow-white. Electric arc lamps strung along the major walkways, buzzing faintly, throwing hard shadows. The light is not consistent. It flickers, dims, surges. The generator complex to the west sends power through cables strung along the column's exterior, and the power is unreliable, and the residents have learned to live in strobing half-darkness the way you learn to live beside a railroad — you stop noticing the shaking after a while.

There is dust in the light. Always dust. Fine mineral particulate that hangs in the air and catches every beam in a pale, golden haze. It settles on everything. It gets in your lungs. The Cut smells of it — dryite, hot metal, chemofungal acid, and the sharp, clean scent of freshly fractured rock.

---

The **sound** is the strangest part.

The Cut should be loud. Four thousand people, dozens of refineries, engines, hoists, dynamite charges in the upper expansion zones — it should roar. And it does roar, sometimes, in bursts. But the baseline, the constant state, is **quiet**. Oppressively, strangely quiet. The column's stone absorbs sound. The mineral composition of theite — whatever it is — dampens vibration in a way that acousticians have studied and cannot fully explain. Voices carry strangely here: a shout from a mid-level walkway will arrive at your ear sounding as if it came from inside your own skull. The dynamite in the upper reaches comes not as a bang but as a deep, felt *thump* in your chest, seconds after the flash.

The dominant sounds are mechanical and low-frequency: the hum of generators transmitted through the rock itself, felt in your boots rather than heard. The grinding of ore processors. The rhythmic clang of the stamp mills, which the residents have learned to sleep through. The clicking of skitterling legs in the walls. The distant, organ-sound of the thermals overhead. And beneath all of it, almost below perception, a vibration — a deep, geological tone, as if the column itself were resonating at a frequency just below the threshold of hearing. Old residents say it's the column breathing. Geologists say it's thermal expansion. No one is sure. Everyone hears it.

---

The **industry** is the reason anyone comes here, and the reason no one leaves.

The column is made of something called **calccrete** — aiteite superdense mineral composite laced with rare metallic isotopes that are extraordinarily valuable for energy storage, propulsion systems, and certain types of industrial catalyst. Calccrete is, in a word, priceless. And it is everywhere in the column. The Cut exists to extract it, refine it, and ship it out on that single-track rail line to the manufactory cities on the basin's edge.

The refineries are built into the column's face at every level, bolted-on structures of pressed metal andite alloy, vented through the rock itself. They process calccrete ore in a multi-stage chemical-reduction process that produces immense heat, toxic fumes, and a fine metallic dust that coats everything in a permanent grey film. The workers — and there are many, mostly contract laborers from the basin settlements — wear respirators, goggles, and thick-soled boots. They develop a particular walk: flat-footed, careful, always testing the surface. After enough time in the Cut, you can feel the texture of the stone through your boot soles. The column becomes a language your feet learn to read.

The dynamite charges in the upper expansion zones shake the whole structure. Metal sings in the upper walkways. Plaster dust falls from ceilings that were freshly plastered yesterday. Buildings constructed six months ago are already showing stress fractures. The Cut is always being rebuilt. The column is always being cut. It is a place in the act of becoming itself — if it ever settles into being something, that day has not come.

---

There are **rules** in the Cut, though they are not written down.

You do not take more calccrete than your claim allows. You do not block a ventilation shaft. You do not harm a thermal. You do not go below the lowest platforms without a partner and a line. You pay your water-reclamation fee — ironic, given that there is no water, but the reclamation systems recycle the body's own moisture, condensation from breath, the humidity of living, and it is this thin cycle that keeps people alive. You do not speak of the deep core — the hollow center of the column that drops into darkness below the lowest reachable level. No one knows how deep it goes. No one who has gone to find out has come back with anything but a change in the way they look at walls.

There is a **council**, sort of. It is made of refinery foremen, rail operators, and the three families who own the surface-rights to the column. They meet in a room carved out of the stone at mid-level, a space that is deliberately left unadorned, bare calccrete walls, a single arc lamp, a long table bolted to the floor. Their decisions are simple and their enforcement is physical: you lose your claim, you lose your rail pass, you lose your place in the Cut. There is no jail. There is no need. The salt pan below is sixty degrees and an hour's walk in every direction. The air is dry enough to crack your lips in minutes. The sun, when it is visible through the dim mineral haze, is a white disc with no warmth in it. You do not leave the Cut by choice. You leave it in a crate on the ore train, and no one asks questions, and everyone knows.

---

Stand at the rail platform at dusk — though dusk here is just the dimming of an already dim phosphorescence — and look up. The column rises above you, dark and mineral-pale, its face glittering faintly with rustbloom and the sparks of refinery vents. The walkways crawl with skitterlings, moving in rivers of copper chitin. A harness-jacker perches on a balcony three stories above you, its claws hooked into the railing, watching you with faceted eyes that reflect the arc-lamp light like wet stones. The hum of the generators rises through the soles of your boots. Somewhere above, a dynamite charge goes off — you feel it before you hear it, and when the sound arrives it is a low, rolling crack, more felt than heard, absorbed by the vast thickness of calccrete above and below and around you.

A thermals drifts past the top of the column. You see it silhouetted against the dim, milky sky — pale, vast, membranous, its intake spirals opening and closing like slow breathing. It makes that sound again. The organ-note. The deep, resonant tone that lives in the space below hearing, in your chest, in the stone beneath your feet.

No one here talks about what the column is. Everyone here knows. It doesn't matter. The ore is good. The rail runs on schedule. The Cut holds.

The Cut always holds.
