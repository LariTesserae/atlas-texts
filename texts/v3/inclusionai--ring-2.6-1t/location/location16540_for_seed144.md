**The Altiplano of Shed Teeth**

You feel the altitude before you see anything. The air is thin enough to taste — metallic, dry, carrying just enough moisture to coat your lips and then betray you by evaporating before it hydrates. The sun here is not the sun of lower places. It is closer in proportion, harsher, less filtered. It does not warm you so much as *press* against you, a flat thermal weight on every exposed surface of skin. The sky is so blue it looks manufactured, the kind of blue that doesn't occur in pigment — a blue that has no softness in it, only saturation.

The ground, seen from the edge, looks like a mistake.

It is not a mesa. It is not a canyon. It is something in between that has no agreed-upon name. The plateau extends for what might be forty or fifty kilometers in every direction — a high tableland at roughly three thousand meters, give or take, its surface not flat but *sectioned*. The erosion that has worked here for millennia has produced a landscape of such dense, packed features that there is almost nowhere you can set a flat foot. Towering sandstone pillars — some no wider than a human torso, others as broad as a house — stand shoulder to shoulder like a congregation that has been mid-sentence for a million years. Ridges of compressed sediment run in parallel, knife-edge to hip-width, separated by shallow clefts and slots that descend sometimes two meters, sometimes twenty. Hoodoo clusters crowd against each other, their mushroom-cap tops nearly touching, forming narrow alleys that the wind uses as corridors. There are no open flats. There are only the gaps between things.

The stone is layered in striations that read like a diary of ancient bodies of water — pale tans, iron reds, chalk whites, a bruised violet that appears only when the rock is wet. And the rock *is* wet, intermittently. That is the dampness. It arrives as a weather pattern that the locals — the few who came here, and whatever was here before — call the *sweats*. Not rain, exactly. The moisture rises from somewhere — perhaps a deep aquifer finding thin points in the basalt substrate beneath the sandstone, perhaps condensation driven by the extreme diurnal temperature swing — and the rock faces weep. Thin rivulets appear in the slot canyons. Water beads on the ceilings of overhangs and drips in a rhythm so slow it seems geological rather than meteorological. Pools form in depressions on pillar tops, shallow and warm, alive with a faint mineral iridescence. They last for hours. Sometimes a day. Then they are gone, leaving only a dark watermark and a faint smell of sulfur and calcium.

There is almost nothing growing. But *almost* is doing work here. In the crevices where the rock fractures into narrow enough gaps, a pale lichen clings — not green but a washed-out grey-gold, so thin it looks like mineral deposit itself. Tufts of wiry grass, no taller than a finger, sprout in sheltered concavities where the *sweats* are most reliable. A single species of thorny shrub — knee-high, grey-leafed, with roots that must penetrate deep into fractures to find purchase — appears at roughly two-hundred-meter intervals across the plateau as though planted by an algorithm with no sense of aesthetics. That is essentially all. The vegetation is sparse to the point of being philosophical. Life here is not abundant; it is *stubborn*, and it wears its stubbornness quietly.

But the fauna — the fauna is a different story, or at least a different volume.

The place is full of animals. The packed terrain, with its thousands of micro-crevices, overhangs, slot caves, and pillar-shadowed ground, offers an almost infinite number of denning sites, and they are nearly all occupied. Lizards — small, quick, the color of the stone they live on — are everywhere, and they are the loudest creatures here, which is to say they occasionally make a soft clicking sound with their mouths. Insects move through the warm, damp crevices in clouds so fine they look like mineral dust suspended in light. Rodents — pale, long-spined, large-eared — inhabit the deeper slot canyons and emerge at dusk in a quiet, disciplined procession. Raptors — a species of wide-winged hawk with feathers the color of dry clay — circle overhead with barely a sound, riding thermals that rise from the sun-heated stone with a smoothness that seems engineered. They hunt the rodents. They hunt without calling. Everything here hunts without calling.

The quiet is the sound. Not silence — there is no true silence on a plateau with wind and fauna — but *near*-silence. The dominant sound is the low, continuous whisper of air moving through the narrow alleys between pillars, a sound so constant it becomes the texture of your hearing, like white noise shaped by geology. Occasionally, a rock falls — a fragment the size of a fist calving off a pillar edge — and the sound it makes is startling precisely because of how much quiet preceded it. A crack. A small percussion. Then the whisper resumes. At night, when the temperature drops and the contraction of the stone produces a sound — a faint, widespread *tick-tick-tick*, like the landscape is slowly adjusting its posture in sleep — the plateau sounds like an enormous clock that has forgotten what hour it is.

There is something wrong with the acoustics. This is the unusual part — the weirdness that is not spectacular, just *persistent*. Sound does not travel here the way it should. The density of the formations, their spacing, their angles, creates a condition in which noises arrive from unexpected directions. You hear your own footsteps behind and above you. Voices carry from one slot canyon to another at oblique angles, arriving distorted, half a second late, as though spoken by a ghost with a lisp. The wind produces, at certain corridors, a low sustained tone — not a howl, more a *hum*, a few notes below the threshold of melody, a note you feel in your sternum before you hear it in your ears. Some of the deeper pillar alleys produce echoes that return sounds *before* you finish making them, a perceptual glitch that is deeply, instinctively unsettling, even after you understand the geometry.

Industry came to this place, and it left behind small, scattered evidence of itself — not ruins, exactly, but artifacts of intent that are now in the process of being reabsorbed.

There is a building — "a building" overstates it; it is three walls and a partial roof of corrugated metal, the fourth wall having collapsed outward into a scatter of flat stones and rusted sheeting. It sits in a widened section of the plateau, nestled against a natural stone wall like a shelter someone built hastily and then abandoned mid-thought. Inside: a concrete pad where machinery once stood. Bolt holes. A drain channel, now clogged with fine red dust and the desiccated remains of a lizard. On the back wall, someone painted a word — a function word, a label — but the paint has flaked to the point of illegibility; only the shadow of the letters remains, pressed into the stone by the chemical interaction of pigment and moisture over years.

Elsewhere: a rail spur. It arrives from the south — you can trace its path as a faint raised line in the hard-packed earth, barely distinguishable from a natural stone ridge — and terminates abruptly at the edge of a vertical drop. No bridge. No loading platform. Just the end of intention, a few meters of exposed rail curving slightly inward as though it wanted to continue but was told there was nothing to continue toward. The rails themselves are gone — pulled up, scavenged — but the wooden ties remain, blackened by decades of sun and frost-cycling into a state of near-petrification.

A cable system runs up one of the taller pillar formations — two parallel steel cables anchored at the top with rusted clamps, descending to a point about twenty meters up where they simply stop, mid-air, suggesting something was once lifted or lowered along this route. The cables sing faintly in the wind, a sound so high-pitched it borders on the inaudible, a vibration more felt than heard.

There is a generator building, or what was one. It is smaller than the first structure — a single room, poured concrete, with a heavy steel door that hangs open at an angle that is not quite a swing and not quite a fall, held by one hinge that has stretched under its own fatigue. Inside: a concrete mounting pad the size of a small car, bolt holes arranged in a precise pattern. Someone bolted something industrial and heavy to this pad, and someone else — or time, or both — unbolted it. The pad is clean. Everything around it is dusty. The difference in cleanliness tells a story, but the story has no words.

There are scattered artifacts beyond these structures: a ceramic insulator from a high-voltage line, sitting in the dust of a pillar ledge with the delicacy of a bone; a section of pipe, partially buried, corroded to the orange of the lichen nearby; a single work boot, leather hardened to the consistency of wood, placed at the edge of a drop as though someone took it off and set it down with care before stepping into the air.

The industry here is not a memory so much as a *phase*. Something was extracted. The extraction ended. The landscape has begun the long, patient work of forgetting the extraction, but the forgetting is incomplete. The infrastructure is too robust to vanish quickly, too scattered to feel monumental. It exists in the state of a sentence interrupted — you can feel its grammar, but you will never hear its end.

The rules of this place:

Water does not stay. It visits. It condenses, it seeps, it flows briefly, it is gone. The entire hydrology of the plateau is an event, not a condition. Life here is organized around the *expectation* of water rather than its presence. The grass does not grow *because* of rain; it grows *in readiness for* rain. The fauna drinks from the ephemeral pools, but the ecosystem subsists on vapor, condensation, and the psychological promise of moisture.

Gravity is the primary architect. Everything that can fall, is falling — slowly, on the timescale of stone, but inevitably. Pillars narrow from the base. Caps crack and shed fragments. Ridges sharpen and eventually become too thin to survive, collapsing into the slot canyons and becoming part of the debris field that slowly works its way downward. The landscape is in the process of *becoming lower*, and everything about it — its verticality, its density, its packed grandeur — is the spectacle of a thing mid-collapse that has not yet finished.

Heat is an aggressor. At this altitude, with this little atmospheric insulation, the daytime temperature is punishing — the stone surface can exceed sixty degrees Celsius — but the nights are cold enough to produce frost in the shaded crevices. This thermal violence is the engine of the erosion. It cracks the stone. It drives the contraction-expansion cycles that flake surfaces into the fine dust that covers everything. It is why the landscape is simultaneously *packed* and *dissolving*. The density you experience is real, but it is a snapshot of a process that is, frame by frame, reducing everything to sediment.

And the weirdness — the acoustic anomalies, the strange placement of moisture, the way the shadows fall across the pillar alleys in patterns that feel too geometric to be accidental — the weirdness is perhaps just what it looks like when a high-altitude desert with a dense, eroded surface meets industrial infrastructure that has begun to decay and a fauna population that has adapted to every strange consequence of that combination. The landscape does not feel designed, but it *feels*, and that feeling is the weirdness. It is not haunted. It is not alien. It is simply a place that has existed long enough, and compactly enough, to have developed a quality that human perception registers as *uncanny* — the feeling of a place that is more coherent than it should be, that follows its own internal logic too well, that gives you the sense you are being *read* as you look at it.

You are not being read. The pillars are not watching. The cables are not transmitting. The boot was not left as a message.

But you can feel the plateau *noticing* you, in the way that a very old, very dense place can feel like it is holding you in its attention simply by existing with such concentration that your presence is absorbed into its texture and noted, not consciously, but structurally — the way a sieve notes the shape of the water that passes through it.

That is the Altiplano of Shed Teeth. It does not welcome you. It does not reject you. It is simply *full*, and you fit into it the way a new note fits into a chord that was already playing before you arrived and will continue after you leave — a small addition, slightly dissonant, absorbed into the whole.
