You arrive into it through a throat.

A passage no wider than two shoulders, cut into something that is not quite stone — harder thanite, closer toite, butite. The walls are slick and dark where water beads and trails downward in paths so old they've annealed into the surface, leaving channels you could trace with a fingertip, whole river-systems in miniature descending toward a floor that is perpetually, quietly wet. Not flooded. Not pooled, exactly. Just — damp in the way a body is damp. The footing is always giving slightly. There is a smell of mineral stillness and something older underneath it, something warm and vegetal and faintly sour, like a cellar that has been breathing for centuries.

The passage opens.

That is the first thing to understand: it *opens*. The space beyond is enormous and it is enclosed, and the enclosure is the architecture. This is not a cavern. A cavern would have roughness, accident, geological character. This has geometry. The ceiling soars into dimness — you cannot see it, only sense it by the way sound returns from it and by the faint wash of grey light that comes from somewhere high and diffused, as though the sky had been poured through a colander and only its pallor remained. The walls are vertical. The floor is level. The proportions are almost right and that is what makes them wrong. A room forty paces across, perhaps sixty long, with columns at intervals that are too slender for the span they bear and yet bear it without visible strain. They are made of the same dark wet material as the passage walls, and they rise with a slight inward lean, like trees, like ribs, like the stance of something listening.

Nothing is natural here. You understand that immediately and without being told. The stone is too uniform. The curves are too deliberate. The water that runs in thin rivulets along the floor grooves — grooves that were carved, obviously, with purpose — has the look of something that has been *expected*. The whole place was built and everything in it, including the water, is behaving as instructed by a set of instructions no one living remembers.

It is nearly empty of objects. The density of *stuff* is astonishingly low. The room's volume is a cathedral of air, and most of that air is doing nothing but holding sound. But the life — the life is thick.

There are creatures everywhere.

Not animals you would know. Not exactly. The nearest comparison would be frogs, if frogs were the size of your forearm and had translucent skin that caught the dim light and turned it into a faint internal glow, a blue-green smudge beneath the surface of their bodies. They are *everywhere* — on the columns, on the floor, on the walls, clinging in clusters where moisture gathers. They do not move quickly. They breathe in visible contractions, their throats expanding like small lanterns. When one jumps — they jump with a sound like a snapped twig, a sharp percussion that rings off the stone and multiplies — you see the webbing of their feet, too wide, too articulated, more like fingers than pads.

There is something else. Higher. A movement in the dim upper air — a flutter, a scudding, a skeletal silhouette crossing the light-source far above and gone before you can orient your eyes. It comes again. And again. They are not birds. They are flat-shaped and angular, like kites made of black paper, and they move with a jerk-and-glide rhythm that suggests joints, not wings. They do not land. They do not descend. They exist only in the upper volume of the space, where the light barely reaches, and their calls are the loudest thing here: a thin, reedy keen, almost tonal, almost musical, rising and falling in intervals that feel like speech you once almost understood.

The sound in this place is layered and constant. The water — it is always present, a low hiss and trickle that comes from everywhere and nowhere specific, the aggregate of a thousand tiny channels feeding some hidden drain at the lowest point of the floor. The creatures — their breathing, their clicking, the occasional territorial throat-bulge that produces a sound like a wet hand slapped against a hollow log. The angular things above — their keening. And beneath it all, a hum. Faint. Structural. The way a glass vibrates before the note is played. The building itself is resonant, you realize, and something in its daily cycle — temperature shift, moisture change, settling — produces a tone so low it is more felt than heard. It sits in your sternum.

There is a second room through an opening on the far side. The transition is abrupt: one step you are in the damp grey light, the next you are in near-dark. This chamber is smaller — hamlet-small, room-small, intimate. The ceiling is lower. The walls here are different. They are carved with patterns. Not decorative, not symbolic in any way you can intuit — they are more like circuitry, or river-deltas, or root-systems, rendered in shallow channels that have been worn smooth by water. The water here is deeper, an inch or two over the floor, and it moves *through* the carvings, flowing along the channels as though the walls were maps of plumbing, or as though the room itself were a body and the carvings were its veins. The water is warmer here. You can feel the difference on your ankles.

In this room there are nests. Not made by the frog-things — something larger. Something that drags material into the corners and arranges it with a fastidiousness that borders on compulsion: shed skins, small stones, lengths of something fibrous that might be plant matter but might also be woven — thread, twine, pulled-apart cloth. The tech-level of who or what built this is primitive in the way that a bird's nest is primitive: perfect adequacy, zero abstraction. Whoever made these nests understood materials and gravity and water-flow, and that was enough.

There is a doorway out — an archway, plain, leading to another passage, and you can hear through it: running water, louder now, and a sound that might be wind but that has too rhythmic a pulse to be wind. Beyond the archway, the light shifts. Not brighter, but *different* — a reddish amber, the color of late afternoon filtered through something resinous and old. A different aperture. A different sky, perhaps, if this place has skies.

The erosion tells the story. Everything here is in balance. Nothing is crumbling — it is past crumbling. The forms have rounded into themselves. Edges have become suggestions. The stone has become more likeite than theite,ite than it was at whatever moment it was placed. The water has polished channels into channels. The nests have been abandoned and reoccupied and abandoned again, layered like archaeological strata, each generation of builders working with the leavings of the last. This place is not dying. It is not thriving. It is *settled*. It has achieved the equilibrium of a reef, a mangrove, a gut — a system that is not pleasant and not hostile, just functional, just *ongoing*.

And the strangeness — the real strangeness, the 1.7 out of 3 strangeness — is not in any single feature. It is in the combination. It is in the fact that every aspect of this place works flawlessly by a logic you can follow step by step, and yet the destination of the logic is unintelligible. The channels drain somewhere. The light comes from somewhere. The warmth rises from somewhere. The frog-things with their finger-feet know exactly what to do here, and the angular kites above know exactly when to call. They have made this place into an ecosystem without any participation from the intelligence that built it, and the building itself seems not to mind. It holds. It resonates. It waits with the patience of something that learned patience so long ago it forgot it was learning.

You stand in the ankle-deep warm water and you listen to the keening above and the clicking below and the hum in the walls and you understand, without knowing how, that this is not a ruin. This is a place that was always meant to become exactly this — empty enough to be full of life, dim enough for strange things to glow, loud enough to feel like silence if you stay long enough.
