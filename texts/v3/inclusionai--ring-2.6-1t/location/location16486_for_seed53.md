## The White Basin

You find it without meaning to — the ground simply stops cooperating with the idea of going up, and the walls around you close inward, and suddenly you are not on a mountain anymore but inside one.

The basin is not large. You could throw a stone from one side to the other, though you would not want to; the stone would ring off something unexpected — a shelf ofite protruding from the wall, a pillar of fusedite, a ridge ofite — the place is made ofite, and the word "stone" feels too soft, too uniform, the way "tree" would feel if you used it for every kind of thing that had ever grown. The walls rise steeply on three sides, curving overhead into a partial dome that is not quite a dome — it is the remnant of forces that arrived from below and met forces that arrived from above and neither won, and what remains is a bowl with a ragged lip, open on one side to a view of nothing but distant peaks so bright in the high thin air that they look like exposed bone.

It is very bright here. Not warm-bright. The kind of bright that finds you before the warmth does, that sits on every surface and makes you squint and feel, paradoxically, that you are being looked at. The sun, when it is visible above the rim, does not pour into the basin so much as it *presses* — flat, white, relentless, catching every mica flake and calcite crust and throwing the light back at you from a dozen angles at once. On overcast days (which come more often than you would expect at this altitude, thin veils of ice-crystal cirrus dragging across the sky like breath on glass), the brightness does not leave. It merely redistributes, becomes diffuse and sourceless, and the whole basin glows with a kind of internal luminosity as though the mineral crust itself has absorbed the idea of light and is slowly metabolizing it.

The walls are old. You can tell by the way they have settled into their own shape, the way a face settles into its own lines. The erosion here is not dramatic — no fresh scars, no raw fractures (those exist higher up, on the ridgeline, where the wind has not yet had time to soften things). Here the rock has been worked and reworked by frost and melt and wind and time until it has acquired a kind of patience. Layers are visible in cross-section wherever a shelf or overhang has formed: dark bands, pale bands, the occasional vein of something that is not quite white and not quite grey and seems to shift between the two depending on the angle of your gaze. Theite. You don't know the proper term. You run your gloved hand along a face and feel the layering like braille — sediment from an era when there may have been a sea here, or may have been ashfall, or may have been something that doesn't have a name in the language you were taught.

No plants. Nothing green. Nothing that grows in the way you were taught growing works. There are things that *deposit* — mineral crusts that form in slow, branching patterns across the stone, especially near the seeps. The seeps are the water. It comes as condensation first, you think — the temperature differential between the cold basin floor and the marginally less cold air above the rim pulls moisture out of the atmosphere, and it collects on the north-facing surfaces as a fine rime that thickens overnight and thins by midday. Sometimes it comes as sleet, driven sideways by wind that finds the gap in the rim and funnels down with a thin, reedy whine. Sometimes — rarely — the sun warms a particular face enough to melt a trickle that runs for an hour or two before freezing again or evaporating or simply disappearing into a crack.

This is the dampness the place was promised. Intermittent. Unreliable. Present more as a *possibility* than a fact. The basin is always on the verge of being wet without quite committing.

And the mineral crusts that form where the moisture reaches are the closest thing to life here. They spread in fractal branching patterns along the lower walls and across the floor in the places where water pools and drains and pools again. Calcite, mostly. Maybeite. Travertine-like deposits that build up in thin, concentric rings around points where water emerges and evaporates, leaving behind a pale residue that crunches underfoot like dried sugar. The patterns are unsettling in their precision — not random, not quite geometric, but occupying that uncomfortable middle ground where you cannot tell if you are looking at chemistry or intention. You crouch beside one and study it: a central point from which branches radiate outward, each branch splitting into smaller branches, each smaller branch splitting again, the whole thing no larger than your hand. It is beautiful. It is not alive. You are almost certain it is not alive.

The space is dense with form. Not crowded — not in the way a forest is crowded — but every volume of air is bounded, claimed, textured. Pillars ofite stand like the ribs of some enormous ribcage, curving up toward the dome overhead. Shelves protrude from walls at varying heights, some wide enough to sit on, some so narrow they seem to exist only as the memory of a wider ledge that has partially collapsed. The floor is uneven — not chaotic, but folded: low ridges alternating with shallow depressions, as though the stone once had a different shape and has since been compressed into a new one, and the old shape is still faintly legible beneath the new, like a word written on top of a word.

You notice the built things slowly. They do not announce themselves. The first is a cairn — a small, waist-high pile of stones at the edge of a low ridge, loosely stacked, slightly askew. It is the kind of cairn a person builds without thinking, a marker, a "I was here." The stones are dark — darker than the surrounding mineral, and you crouch and pick one up. It is dense, heavier than it should be, with a grain that feels almost crystalline under your thumb. You set it down. You look at the cairn again. Most of the stones are the local paleite. Two or three are this dark material, placed with no obvious pattern — or with a pattern you cannot read.

Further in, against the lee wall of the basin, the partial remains of a structure. Low walls of dry-stacked stone —ite, mostly, though a few darker stones are worked in here as well, as if they were chosen deliberately. The construction is simple: flat stones laid in courses, no mortar, no shaped blocks. The technique is ancient in its logic. Someone who knew stone but did not yet know how to shape stone with precision. The structure is no larger than a garden shed — three walls rising to about your chest, the fourth side open to the light. The roof has partially collapsed. You can see the remnants: flat slabs that once spanned the top, now tilted and fallen, half-buried in mineral dust. Inside, the floor has been cleared smooth. There is a shallow depression in the center, deliberate, maybe a basin for water, maybe a hearth, maybe nothing at all. A few notches are chipped into the interior wall — hand-holds? Decorative? Functional in a way that has been forgotten?

There are marks elsewhere. On a broad, flat face of the north wall, a series of horizontal lines scratched into the stone — not carved with any tool you can identify, but gouged, repeatedly, with something hard and pointed. The lines are evenly spaced, dozens of them, running across six or seven feet of stone. Counting them is a compulsion you don't act on. Near the base of the east wall, half-buried in mineral crust, what might be a tool: a length ofite knapped into a rough wedge shape, with a grip worn smooth by use. You pick it up. It fits your hand. This bothers you more than it should.

No animals. No insects. No scuttle or flutter or call. The fauna of this place is absent — not extinct here, not driven out, but simply *not present*, the way a number is absent from an equation. The silence is not empty but *full* — full of the absence of the sounds you expected. This is the kind of quiet that sits on your chest a little.

But it is not total. The wind moves through a gap in the rim with a thin, variable whine that rises and falls without clear rhythm — the air mass above the basin is not stable, and thermals shift in slow cycles driven by the sun's arc across the rim, drawing air in on one side and expelling it on the other. The sound is mournful in a way you were not expecting, almost tonal, almost like singing but not quite. Now and then a shard of frost or a small plate ofice peels away from an upper wall and falls with a sound like a whispered word. The mineral seeps make their own sounds if you are close enough: a faint clicking, the sound of mineral-laden water depositing and hardening, thousands of tiny events per second collapsing into a white noise you can only hear when you hold very still.

The place is active. Not alive — *active*. The frost-heave that slowly, seasonally, pushes up a section of floor. The mineral deposition that, over decades, thickens a crust by a millimeter. The wind that redistributes dust and reshapes the lowest drifts. The occasional ice fall from the upper lip. The cycle of condensation and evaporation that marks and remarks the walls. You feel, standing in the center of the basin, that you are inside something that is in the process of becoming what it will be, that you have arrived at an intermediate moment in a long, slow event. The place might look different in a year. It will certainly look different in a thousand years. But it will look much the same tomorrow.

The cold is not severe. It is the kind of cold that announces itself as absence rather than presence — the absence of warmth, the absence of the sun's full strength filtered through a thick atmosphere. At this elevation, the air is thin and dry and holds heat poorly. Your breath makes clouds that linger longer than they should. The stone is always cool to the touch, even in direct sun, and in the shaded crevices along the north wall, frost persists through most of the daylight hours. There is no deep freeze here — the temperature oscillates around a point just below the boundary between discomfort and danger, and the dampness makes it worse, because wet cold is a different animal than dry cold, and the condensation that forms on your outer layers overnight does not fully dry during the day.

And the weirdness. It is quiet, the weirdness. It sits in the back of the basin like a presence that has not yet decided what it is. The patterns in the mineral crusts. The dark stones in the cairn. The tool that fits your hand too well. The scored lines on the wall, evenly spaced, evenly deep, made by something or someone with a patience and precision that does not match the crudeness of the shelter. The way the light lingers in the basin after the sun has moved behind the rim, as though the stone is reluctant to release it. The sense, difficult to articulate but persistent, that this place was arranged — not built, not constructed, but *arranged* — and that the arrangement is not finished.

You step carefully over a low ridge of mineral deposit and feel, beneath the crunch ofite, a faint vibration. It might be the wind in the upper walls. It might be settling. It might be something else.

You stand at the edge of the basin and look back in. The structure with its fallen roof. The cairn with its dark stones. The scored lines. The branching calcite patterns that, from this distance, look almost like rivers on a map of a place that doesn't exist. The light presses down. The wind sings its thin note through the gap.

The place has been here longer than the stones suggest. You are not sure how you know this. You step out onto the ridge, and the wind hits your face, and you do not look back.
