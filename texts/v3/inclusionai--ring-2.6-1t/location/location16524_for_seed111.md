# The Cleave

You hear it before you see anything at all.

A low, wet shuffling — hundreds of it — rising from below like breath drawn through a throat the size of a valley. Then the clicking: not insect clicking, not chitin on chitin, but something harder, mineral, like small stones being tapped together in rhythm. And beneath both of those, a sound like tearing linen, slow and continuous, which is the sound of rock still settling, still finding its final shape. This place is still being born.

The Cleave opens at altitude, somewhere above the treeline where the air tastes thin and metallic on the tongue, at the height where clouds used to pass but now pass no longer. It is not a cave exactly, nor a canyon. It is a wound. The mountain splits along a vertical fault — two sheer faces of dark stone separated by a gap no wider than a chapel nave, plunging downward three or four hundred feet into blue-black shadow. The walls are nearly parallel, almost unnervingly so, as though something pressed upward from below and parted the rock like a hand drawing a curtain. The fracture is recent — geologically recent, which is to say yesterday, or last year, or a thousand years ago, which in the life of stone is less than a breath. The surfaces are raw. No lichen has taken hold yet on the lower walls. The rock face is a palette of grays and iron-blacks, striated with veins of something paler — quartz, maybe, or calcium, or something without a name that catches the faintest ambient light and throws it back in a color your eyes struggle to register.

There is no sky visible from the bottom. Only a thin wound of grey-white far above, a crack in the ceiling of the world through which no meaningful light descends. The darkness here is not the darkness of night — night has texture, gradation, the soft fade of horizon. This is a darkness that *presences*. It has density. It pools in the lower third of the Cleave like standing water. And yet it is not uniform. Things glow in it. Faintly. Patches of bioluminescent moss — the only vegetation, and it is sparse — cling to the walls in colonies no larger than a hand, pulsing in slow, arrhythmic waves of blue-green. The light they give off is barely enough to silhouette the nearest rock face. It makes the walls look like the inside of a ribcage.

The Cleave is not wide. You could throw a stone from one wall to the other, if you were strong and the stone were true, and it would clatter off the opposite face and tumble into the dark below. But it is tall, and layered, and this is where the density begins.

Every surface that is not vertical is occupied.

The ledges — narrow, freshly fractured, barely wide enough to hold a body — are packed wing-to-wing and shoulder-to-shoulder with animals. They cling, they nest, they press their bodies flat against the stone or into the crevices between. They are not birds, not exactly. They have four limbs and a membrane — patagia stretched between elongated fingers, yes, like a bat, but the proportions are wrong. The forelimbs are too long. The fingers too many. Some of them have six digits, and the membrane is ribbed with fine cartilaginous struts that click and flex as they breathe. Their faces are broad, flat, almost noseless, with wide mouths that they open frequently, revealing rows of small, blunt teeth. They do not echolocate — not in any way you would recognize. Instead they orient by vibration, their clawed feet pressed flat against the rock, reading the tremors of the stone and of each other. They are silent when still. When they move, the clicking comes from the joints of their wings — a dry, mineral sound, like flint struck against flint.

There are hundreds of them. Thousands, maybe. On every ledge, in every fissure. They layer atop each other. A ledge meant for two holds seven. A crevice wide enough for one contains four, pressed warm against warm, their membranes draped over each other like damp cloth. The young are everywhere — not in nests, for there are no nests, nothing constructed. The young simply cling to the adults, folded into the gaps, their oversized wing-fingers wrapped tight. When an adult shifts, the young shift with it, a single organism made of many.

Below — and this is where the sound comes from — there are larger ones. You catch glimpses. A shape detaching from the darkness and rising a few feet on a slow, lazy downstroke of those impossible wings before banking and descending again. Bigger. Much bigger. The proportions more extreme. The forelimbs longer still, the fingers so elongated they trail below the body like the legs of some deep-sea thing. These ones move slowly, patient, and when they pass through one of the bioluminescent patches the moss dims — not from the wind of their passage but from something else, some contact, some chemical exhaled through the mouth — and then recovers seconds later, the blue-green glow returning like a tide coming back in.

They eat the moss. That's what the sparse vegetation supports. A single slow metabolism, a single unbroken chain: rock, mineral vein, moss, small clinging grazers, the larger shapes below that feed on the grazers and on the moss both. The whole system hangs from a thread of damp stone.

It is cool here. A steady, ambient cool — not the biting cold of wind and exposure, but the still, radiating cool of deep earth. Condensation beads on the lower portions of the walls, forming tiny runnels that trace slow paths downward, merging, splitting, finding each other. The air smells of wet mineral, of something faintly organic underneath — not decay, but the musk of warm bodies packed close. There is moisture enough to keep the moss alive, not enough to form streams. Water appears as breath, as film, as the thin sheen on stone that makes the whole lower chamber gleam faintly when the moss-light catches it.

There is evidence that others have been here.

Not built things. No walls, no shelters, no marked paths. But at the base of the Cleave, where the scree meets a flat shelf of exposed granite, there are stones arranged in a loose circle — not stacked, not mortared, simply placed. A fist-sized river-smooth rock at the center, and around it, six or seven others, each slightly angled inward as though they were set down by hands that expected the circle to hold something. It is old — the stones have the same thin patina of mineral deposit as the surrounding rock, as though they grew from the ground. But they did not. Someone carried them. Someone with hands, or something that served as hands. Near the circle, scratched into the granite with a harder stone, there is a pattern — concentric arcs, incomplete, trailing off into nothing at the edge of the stone where the carver's reach ended. It is not art, not exactly. It is not notation. It is a gesture repeated until it became landscape.

Primitive tools are scattered about — not deliberately, not discarded, but simply dropped where they were last used. A length of knotted cordage made from some fibrous bark, bleached pale by altitude and time. A hand-axe of dark chert, its edge still sharp, left on a flat stone as though someone set it down to free both hands and never returned. A bundle of thin sticks bound with sinew, the purpose indeterminate, propped against a rock at an angle that suggests it was once part of something taller.

No one is here now. The Cleave belongs to the winged things and the moss and the slow dripping of condensation and the settling of young stone.

Time moves differently here. The erosion is so young that every surface feels provisional — a draft, a sketch, the world's first attempt at this particular arrangement of void and boundary. The rock will round. The ledges will crumble. The moss will spread or fail. The winged things will adapt or thin or surge. But none of this will happen quickly. The cool and the altitude and the thin air slow everything to a patient crawl. A ledge collapses and it takes forty years for the scree to find its angle of repose. A colony of moss dies and it takes a century for a new patch to take hold. The circle of stones sinks, millimeter by millimeter, into the granite.

And always, the sound. The shuffling. The clicking. The slow tearing of rock still pulling itself into place. The Cleave listens to itself, and what it hears is life — dense, strange, layered, pressing close against the dark.
