# The Quiet Plateau

You see it first as texture.

From a distance — a great distance, because this place is continental in its reach — the surface of the plateau looks like grey fabric pulled taut over the bones of the world. Ridges and basins so old they've lost the drama of their origin. No sharp peaks here. Erosion has had billions of rotations to work, and what remains is a landscape of gradual transitions: escarpments that take a full day to crest, valleys so broad their far walls are indistinguishable from the horizon, and everywhere the patient geometry of water that ran long ago and left its signature in striated rock, in alluvial fans hardened to stone, in mineral veins that glitter faintly in the dim light like cracked porcelain.

The elevation is such that the sky doesn't so much arch overhead as press down — a heavy pewter ceiling, low and featureless, that diffuses light into something without source or direction. There is no sun-disc here, not as you'd recognize it. A pale region of brightness, sometimes, near the apex of the sky, like looking up into the inside of a cloud. It brightens and dims on cycles too long to feel like weather. Most of the time, the world exists in a state of perpetual late afternoon, except the afternoon never turns golden. It stays pewter, graphite, ash. The shadows are faint things — more suggestions of depth than actual darkness. And yet warmth never arrives. The cold is not violent, not biting. It is a patient cold, the cold of deep caves and deep space, that settles into your joints and makes your movements feel borrowed.

Water exists here, but it keeps its distance. You find it in films — a thin slick on exposed rock surfaces that appears during the slow warming phase of the brightness cycle and vanishes again. Condensation gathers on the downwind sides of ridges in the form of hoar that looks almost engineered in its precision: every crystal the same size, every surface uniformly furred. There are springs in the valleys, low-pressure seepages that emerge from fractures in the stone and pool in shallow depressions before the cold reclaims them into ice that sublimates directly into the dry air. You drink from them by chipping ice from the lip of the seep and letting it melt in your mouth. The water tastes of dissolved mineral — chalky, faintly alkaline, and old.

Vegetation is present, but only in the loosest sense of the word. There are no stems, no leaves, nothing that rises more than a centimetre from the surface. What grows here are dark crusts — communities of organism that is neither quite plant nor quite something else. They form thin mats across the broad valley floors and lower slopes, black-green when dry and almost black when wet, and they seem to be the only thing that holds the fine regolith in place. Up close, the crust is a universe: intricate filaments, tiny pod-like structures, surfaces that bead moisture into perfect spheres that roll across the colony like mercury. When the light-cycle brightens, they sometimes open — their filaments extending millimetres into the air — and for a few hours the mat looks almost alive in the way that grasslands look alive. Then it contracts, and you're back to looking at rock.

But the crusts are not the most numerous living thing here. They are not even the second most numerous.

---

What strikes you — what stays with you, long after you leave, in dreams and involuntary memory — is the fauna.

They are everywhere. That is the only way to describe it. The plateau is covered in them, and not in the way a meadow is covered in grass. This is coverage in the way a reef is covered in coral, or the way a forest floor is covered in fungi — a density of bodies so high that individual creatures blur into masses, and the masses blur into terrain. You walk across a valley floor and you are walking on them, or between them, or over them. You stop and look down, and the ground is a palimpsest of small hard shapes: ovoid, segmented, smooth or ridged, and all of them motionless.

They are perhaps fifteen centimetres across at their largest. Most are smaller. Their shells — if shell is the word — are mineral-dark, ranging from iron-grey to near-black, with occasional iridescent fractures that catch the dim plateau-light and throw back colours that don't seem to belong to the spectrum. Some are spherical. Some are flattened along one axis, like stones worn by a river. Some have radial symmetry that suggests they grew outward from a central point, adding chamber after chamber like a nautilus, except the chambers are sealed and solid. They sit on the ground with their ventral surfaces flush against the stone, and they do not visibly move.

But they do move. You learn this on the second day, perhaps the third, when you come back to a spot you marked and see that the arrangement has changed — not much, not in any way your first glance would catch, but the angle of one near your boot-print has rotated fifteen degrees. The cluster you sat on has closed ranks where your weight forced them apart.

They are slow in a way that breaks your sense of what speed means. Over hours, they shift. Over days, they migrate centimetres. Over seasons, they rearrange the texture of the ground. They eat the crusts, or eat what the crusts leave behind, or eat the mineral films on the rock — no one watching them closely has ever been certain of their metabolism. They do not seem to breathe. They do not seem to interact with one another except to touch, and to weight, and to slowly push.

There are tiers of fauna, too. The ground-creatures are the foundation, but there are others. Thin-bodied things that live in the narrow spaces between rocks, flattened to an extraordinary degree, that ripple when they sense vibration. Thread-like organisms anchored to cliff faces that extend into the air during the bright phase and retract during the dim, filtering what drifts past at heights of three or four metres. And in the valleys, where subsurface water occasionally pools, there are larger things — sluggish, many-limbed, half-submerged — that are rare enough to seem solitary but whose tracks fill the muddy basin floors with overlapping trails.

Fauna signs are written across every surface. Tracks, trails, bore-holes, scratch-marks, chemical deposits left behind when shells shift. You pass a rock face and it is furred with their residue — a pale mineral bloom that traces the outline of ten thousand bodies pressed against stone over years. You begin to see the landscape as biogenic, shaped as much by the slow accumulation of animal presence as by water and wind. The erosion patterns on the lower slopes are not purely geological. They follow the trails of creatures that have worn grooves into soft rock over centuries of passing through.

And all of this — all of it — in near-silence.

The quiet here is not empty. It is full. But the fullness is at the threshold of perception. If you press your hand flat against the ground where the fauna are thickest, you feel it: a vibration so low it occupies the border between tactile and auditory. Not a pulse, not a rhythm, but a continuous subsonic hum, as if the entire plateau were a single instrument bowed by currents of air moving across its surface and through the interstices of a billion hard bodies. The sound is below the frequency of speech. Below the frequency of most music. It sits in the register of geological time, of tectonic drift, of the slow turning of a world. You feel it and it rearranges something in your chest.

That may be communication. Or it may be incidental. No one has established it either way.

---

The industrial traces are old.

You find them in the escarpments and at the edges of deep valleys — evidence that someone, at some point, came here with purpose. Shaft mouths, dark and square, cut into cliff faces at regular intervals. The access trails leading to them are long buried under mineral deposits and the slow redistribution of surface material, but you can trace them by the blasted rock that still resists vegetation, by the subtle discoloration of stone that was heated and cooled in unnatural cycles. The shafts go deep. Past the water table. Past the fossil-bearing strata. Into rock so old it predates the fauna.

Piping — or what remains of piping — threads along the cliff faces above the valleys. Heavily corroded. Some sections have fallen and lie on the valley floors like the ribs of enormous beasts. Others are still in place, partially encased in mineral growth, and on cold mornings they weep with condensation that runs in thin runnels down the rock and collects in the same seep-pools the fauna drink from. Whether the fauna existed before the pipes or the pipes catalyzed them is a question without a settled answer.

There are boreholes in the plateau surface itself — small ones, spaced at intervals that suggest mechanical precision, now partially filled with windblown regolith and the accumulated debris of tiny shells. Some emit faint venting — not steam, not gas, but a thin column of air that moves at a speed just perceptible to the skin, cold air rising from depths where the temperature drops below what even this cold plateau can sustain. You stand near one and you feel the updraft and you know that far below, in corridors no one has walked in centuries, something in the deep geology is still active.

No one knows what was extracted. The infrastructure suggests energy transfer and mineral processing — the pipe diameters, the shaft dimensions, the layout patterns all speak to an industrial operation of considerable scale. But there are no surface works. No processing facilities. No transport corridors wide enough for vehicles. Everything was built to go down, not out. The plateau was treated as a vertical resource and the surface was left — or deliberately preserved — as it was.

Or maybe what was left behind is not what was taken. Maybe the plateau remembers extraction the way a body remembers a wound: not as damage, but as part of its current shape.

---

Night — or what passes for night here — arrives not with darkness but with further dimming. The pewter sky darkens to charcoal. The faint shadows sharpen and then dissolve. The subsonic hum of the plateau seems to deepen, or perhaps your perception adjusts. You sit on a ridge and look out across the vast, featureless expanse and you understand, with a clarity that feels geological itself, that you are not observing this place. You are being observed by it. The fauna are aware. Not individually — there is no intelligence here, no directed attention — but collectively, in the way a forest responds to wind, in the way a reef responds to current, there is a sensitivity to presence that predates anything human. You are a new variable in an equation that has been balanced for longer than you can imagine.

The cold intensifies. The hoar begins to form on your clothing, on your eyelashes, in the tiny spaces between your gear. The crusts retract. The ground-creatures settle deeper into their positions, pressing themselves flat, their shells clicking faintly — so faintly you wonder if you imagined it — against stone.

Hours pass. The sky does not change. It only darkens further, and then less darkens further still, and you lose the boundary between the world and the absence above it.

You sleep, eventually. Not because you are safe, but because the cold and the silence and the slow weight of ten thousand quiet lives pressing against the thin membrane of reality have made wakefulness feel unnecessary. You dream of a sound below hearing. A vibration in the sternum. The memory of a plateau that was already ancient before the first shaft was sunk, and will remain long after the last pipe has corroded into mineral memory.

Before dawn — if that word means anything here — the brightness-phase begins. A faint diffusion in the pewter. The crusts extend their filaments. The thread-things reach upward. The ground shifts, imperceptibly, beneath a billion small and patient lives, and the plateau continues its slow conversation with itself, indifferent and complete.
