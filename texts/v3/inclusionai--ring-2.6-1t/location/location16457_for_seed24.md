# The Sunken Mouth

You would not find it unless you were lost, or unless the ground gave way beneath you.

It is a shallow bowl pressed into the earth — perhaps two hundred paces across at its widest, perhaps thirty paces down from the lowest lip of the rim to the floor. The walls are not sheer; they slope inward in a long, tired curve of dark stone, pocked and scalloped by uncountable years of wind that carries no moisture in it. The stone is basalt, or something like it: a blue-black that looks almost wet in certain angles of dim light, but is always dry to the touch. Always. The air down here holds no dampness. It holds almost nothing at all.

The floor is salt.

Not the white salt of oceans — there has never been an ocean here. This salt is pale grey, almost the colour of old bone, and it crusts the ground in uneven plates that crack and shift underfoot with a sound like a whispered word you can't quite hear. In the centre of the basin the salt is thick — inches, perhaps feet — and it has buckled into low ridges and shallow troughs, a frozen geography of pressure and slow subsidence. Toward the edges, where the slope begins, the salt thins and gives way to bare dark stone, and in those places you can see the long mineral stains that have wept down the walls in faint ochre and chalk-white threads: iron, calcium, something nameless. They have been drying there so long they are part of the stone now.

There is almost nothing that grows here. But not quite nothing.

In the cracks of the salt — where the crust has fractured and opened narrow veins to whatever lies beneath — you find thin filaments of grey-green life. They are not quite lichen, not quite root. They cling in translucent threads, barely visible unless the light catches them at the right angle, which is rarely. They seem to metabolize the mineral itself, pulling sustenance from the salt and the stone in some chemistry that predates anything recognizable. They do not move. They do not seem to grow so much as *persist*. Some of them may be centuries old. There is no way to know. They ask nothing of the air and the air asks nothing of them.

The air is warm. Not aggressively so — it does not burn, does not shimmer. It sits on the skin like a second skin, a constant, mild pressure. This is the strangest thing about the basin: it should be cool, deep-set as it is, sheltered from sun by the rim walls. But the stone has been absorbing heat for longer than memory — or longer than any record — and it gives the warmth back slowly, steadily, without fluctuation. The temperature does not change between what you might call morning and evening. There is no morning here. There is no evening. The light arrives muted, filtered through haze and the high mineral dust that hangs perpetually in the air above the rim, and it arrives in such small quantities that the basin lives in a kind of permanent twilight. What light there is seems to emanate from nowhere in particular — reflected, scattered, reduced to a grey-amber dimness that makes the salt floor glow faintly and gives the dark stone walls a look of depth, as though the basin is deeper than it is, as though the walls recede into a distance that doesn't exist.

There are no animals. No insects. No birds wheeling above the rim. The silence here is not dramatic — it does not roar or press on the ears. It is simply the absence of anything that would break it. If you stand still long enough, you begin to hear something: a faint, low creak, as if the salt is contracting in the warmth, or as if the earth beneath it is breathing in its sleep. It may be the stone settling. It may be the heat moving in micro-currents through the basin air. It may be nothing at all. But it is the only sound, and it comes and goes without rhythm or reason, and you realize you have been listening to it so long that you have forgotten what silence sounds like when it is absolute.

The erosion here is old and finished. Nothing is being carved. Nothing is being deposited. The walls were shaped by a process that has already concluded — wind-scouring, mineral dissolution, the slow patient grinding of thermal expansion over millennia — and what remains is a form in equilibrium. The ridges of salt do not grow. The stains on the wall do not lengthen. The filaments of grey-green life neither advance nor retreat. The place arrived at its current state a very long time ago and has simply remained, with the patience of geology, with the indifference of a system that has reached its lowest energy and has no further reason to change.

There are no tools here. No paths. No marks of anything that has passed through, unless you count the thin, barely perceptible depressions in the salt where something heavy once rested, or once fell, ages ago — before the salt closed over it and erased the memory. There is no metal. There is no charcoal. There is nothing that says *someone was here.*

But there is a quality to the place — a stillness so thorough, a warmth so constant, a silence so complete — that it feels held. Not abandoned. Not forgotten. *Kept*. As if the basin itself were the artifact, and the salt its preserved interior, and the dim light its atmosphere of age. You stand in it and you feel, without evidence, without reason, that this place was always like this, and always will be. The warmth will not increase. The salt will not dissolve. The filaments will endure. The silence will not be broken.

You step carefully across the salt plates. They hold your weight, then settle back.

Nothing else moves.
