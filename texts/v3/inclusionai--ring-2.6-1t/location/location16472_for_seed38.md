# The Thallus

---

You feel the altitude first. Not as thinness exactly — the air is too humid for that — but as a kind of *pressure behind the eyes*, a faint fullness, as though the plateau is gently holding you in place. You stand on something that is not quite ground. It gives beneath your weight with the resistance of a mattress — springy, alive, faintly warm from below. The surface looks like soil from a distance, but close it is nothing like soil. It is a mat: a living crust, meters deep, made of interlocked roots, compressed moss, fungal tissue, and something older that has no botanical name. It is the oldest thing here. It has been here so long that it has become geology.

The mat extends in every direction to the horizon.

You cannot see the horizon. The haze eats it. What you see instead is a wall of green-brown dimness, featureless, rising up from the mat like breath on glass. The nearest structures — you cannot call them trees — stand among the mat in regular intervals, as if planted by a patience so vast it looks like mathematics. They are dark, columnar, maybe fifteen meters tall, with no branches in any way you recognize. Instead, from their tops extend broad flat shelves of photosynthetic tissue, layered and overlapping like the hands of a crowd pressed together. These shelves catch the faint filtered light and return it as a dim, jaundiced glow. They create a permanent understory beneath them — a ceiling of living leaf that blocks most of the sky. You walk under them the way you'd walk through a crypt: hunched, quiet, aware of the weight of what's above you.

The light is never bright here. Even if you somehow reached an open stretch between the columns, the atmosphere itself is too wet, too thick with evaporated moisture and floating spores, to transmit the sun cleanly. What light arrives is amber-green and diffused, the color of old resin, falling through layers of condensation and hanging there, suspended, as if the air is made of stained glass that no one installed.

Water is everywhere but never in one place. The mat drinks it. The columns sweat it. Droplets bead on every surface and gather into threads that run down flanks of bark and root and slowly fall — *plink, plink, plink* — into the mat below. You hear this sound more than any other. It is not rhythmic. It is arrhythmic and close, a constant scatter of tiny percussions, like someone dropping pins on a carpet in another room. Sometimes, in the wettest hours — there are no hours here, only gradations of moisture — the dripping becomes a soft continuous whisper, and the air grows heavy enough to feel on your skin like oil.

There is standing water, but barely. You encounter it as patches — a depression in the mat that holds a few inches of black still water, reflecting nothing because there is nothing to reflect. These pools are warm. They smell faintly of iron and overripe fruit. They are not deep enough to drown in but deep enough to lose a boot. The mat around them is soft, and if you step wrong your foot goes through the crust into the living substrate beneath, which closes around your ankle with a gentle, organic pressure — not hostile, just present, like a hand that has forgotten to let go.

---

The fauna is abundant but you must learn to see it differently than you're used to.

Nothing here is large by the standards of open landscapes. Nothing charges. Nothing screams. The creatures of the Thallus have evolved inside a geometry of columns and shelves and mat, and they are built for the spaces between. You see movement at the edges of your vision — a flicker of too many legs disappearing into the undergrowth, or a slow gliding shape that resolves, if you catch it, into something like a manta ray with the wings segmented and veined like a leaf. It drifts on the warm updrafts rising from the mat and barely moves its wings at all. It is the color of wet bark and dead leaf. When it passes over a drip-line it flexes its body to drink from the falling water, a motion so precise and practiced it looks choreographed.

There are smaller things — clouds of them — moth-sized, pale, drifting in the dim air like spores that have decided to move. Their wings are almost transparent, and when they pass through a beam of what little light reaches the understory, they cast no shadow. They make a sound: a collective hum so faint it might be your own blood. Closer, you can hear individual clicks — mechanical, too regular — a ticking that sounds almost like counting. No one has counted with them. No one has tried. There is no reason to believe they are counting. But the regularity bothers you. It sits in the back of your head like a word you've forgotten.

Larger presences move far below the mat. You feel them as vibrations through the soles of your feet before you hear or see anything. A deep, slow pulse, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. Something heavy shifting in the deep substrate. What lives down there, in the oldest layers of the living mat where the compressed organic matter transitions into something almost mineral, is unknown. The fauna here do not dig down. They do not speak of it, for they do not speak at all, but their behavior curdles around those zones — a sudden absence of clicking, a scattering of the small pale wings, a columnar structure that seems to lean slightly away.

---

The strangeness of this place does not announce itself. It seeps.

You notice it first in the spacing. The columns are too regular. Not perfectly — there are variations, asymmetries, the occasional tight pair or outlier — but the overall pattern suggests something deeper than competition for light or random germination. It looks *organized*. The way cells in a tissue are organized. The way organs are organized inside a body.

Then you notice the mat-marks.

In stretches where the mat is thin, or where a pool has receded and left the surface exposed, the substrate is textured with patterns. Spirals. Concentric rings. Networks of veins that branch and rejoin in ways that look vascular. In a few places — always at the base of the tallest columns — the patterns resolve into something closer to writing. Not symbols, exactly. More like *pressure marks*, as if the mat once pushed upward against something from below and left a record of that push in its own surface. The marks are ancient — they've been partially overgrown, partially dissolved — but they remain readable in the way a scar remains readable: not through information, but through shape.

Is the Thallus alive? Of course. But is it *one* alive? This is the question that the strangeness asks of you, quietly, from the margins of your attention. The mat connects everything. The columns root into it. The fauna live on it and in it and of it. The patterned marks suggest a communication between its deepest layers and its surface — a body talking to its own extremities across a distance of kilometers. If this is a single organism, then you are standing on its skin. The warmth beneath the surface is not geothermal. It is metabolic. The slow pulses you feel through your feet are not seismic shifts. They are *peristaltic*.

You cannot prove this. You cannot disprove it. The columns stand at their regular intervals and the mat stretches in every direction and the light filters through amber haze and nothing contradicts the idea. It simply sits beneath you, breathing, with the patience of something that has been breathing for millennia.

---

There are bones.

They lie half-incorporated into the mat, being slowly consumed and transformed. The largest are curved — ribs, maybe, or fragments of a carapace — each one longer than you are tall, pale and smooth, with a grain that suggests ivory but is harder, denser, almost ceramic. They are not from anything that walks the surface now. They are from something that walked here before the mat took over, before the columns grew to their present height, before the patterns settled into the substrate like a final exhalation. What killed them, or what replaced them, is impossible to say. They are simply here — another layer in the Thallus's long history of becoming, compressed into the present the way fossils are compressed into stone, except that here the stone is still alive and it is still pressing them in.

In one place — and you might walk for days before finding it — there is a ring of stones. They are dark, dense, too regular in shape and too uniform in spacing to be natural. But they are *old*. Older than the mat's present surface. They protrude from the living crust like teeth through gums, slowly being swallowed. Around the base of each stone, the mat has grown in a tight spiral, as if it recognized something in the rock and responded. The stones are not carved. They are not marked with language or art. They are simply *placed*, and the placing is so old that the intention behind it has become geological — a fact of the landscape rather than an event in it.

There may have been builders. There may have been a civilization, or a species with hands, or something that understood the sky. The Thallus does not tell you. It has covered the evidence with its patient, living skin. The mat is the archive, and it records in its own medium, in growth rings and pressure patterns and slow chemical depositions that no one currently living can read. If the Thallus is a body, then the stones are foreign objects — splinters that healed around them. The body does not reject them. It has forgotten they are there.

---

Sound here is an exercise in subtraction.

What you hear is what the Thallus does not absorb. The dripping — constant, arrhythmic, close. The creak of a column adjusting under the weight of its own shelves. The almost-silence of the fauna: a click, a hum, a brief rustle like cloth drawn slowly across wet stone. These sounds do not travel far. The mat absorbs them. The heavy air swallows them. You exist in a sphere of hearing maybe fifteen meters in radius, and beyond that the world is a kind of living quiet that is not empty but *full* — full of presence that has made a decision, over the long centuries, to make no noise.

On still days — and there are long stretches that are still, hours that are nothing but drip and breath — the quiet becomes a substance. It thickens. It sits on your shoulders. You begin to hear your own body with startling clarity: your heartbeat, the wet sound of your breathing, the faint crackling of your joints. The Thallus makes you aware of your own interior, as if the silence is externalized, as if the landscape has asked you to be louder simply so there is something to hear.

Once, in the deep night-equivalent — the dim hours when the haze grows even thicker and the faint green glow of the mat's bioluminescent underlayer rises to the surface like cold fire — you may hear what sounds like breathing. Deep. Slow. The interval between inhale and exhale measured in minutes. It comes from everywhere. It comes from beneath you. Whether the mat is breathing or whether you have simply begun to project the rhythm of your own lungs onto a landscape that does nothing, you cannot tell. The Thallus offers no correction.

---

Time moves differently here.

Not metaphorically. Not poetically. The Thallus is a place where *time is structurally different* — where the interval between significant events is so long that change, from a human perspective, barely exists. A column grows a new shelf every forty years. The mat expands its reach by centimeters over a century. A pool dries up over the course of a decade, and when it returns the fauna re-emerges from dormancy in the substrate as if no time has passed at all. Seasons here are not the cycle you know. They are long, slow gradients of moisture and warmth that roll across the plateau like weather systems the size of nations. A wet season may take six months to arrive from the east, and you can watch it coming — a line of haze on the horizon that thickens by degrees, the drip-pattern intensifying by imperceptible increments day by day until one morning you realize the air is heavier and the mat is darker and the quiet has changed its pitch.

If you stay long enough — weeks, months — you will notice changes, but they will be so gradual they seem like features of the landscape that were always there. A new shelf has unfurled. The spiral in the mat at the base of a column has tightened by a quarter-turn. A bone that was protruding from the crust is now flush with the surface, as if the living ground has simply *decided* to swallow it a little faster. These changes are the landscape's version of thought: slow, deliberate, and almost imperceptible.

You do not conquer this place. You do not even traverse it. You are absorbed into its rhythm, the way a stone is absorbed into the bed of a river — not quickly, not dramatically, but by the patient insistence of something far larger than you moving in a direction you cannot refuse.

---

The Thallus does not care if you are here.

It was here before you. It will be here after. Its shelves will continue to unfurl, its mat will continue to grow, its quiet processes will continue their ancient work of turning everything — bone, stone, rain, light — into more of itself. It is not alive in the way a forest is alive. It is alive in the way a *world* is alive: deeply, completely, and without the slightest interest in whether you understand it.

Walk softly. The ground is listening. The ground has always been listening. It simply hasn't had reason to respond.
