## The New Coast

You hear it before you see it — or rather, you hear the absence of what you expected to hear. No birdsong chorus, no crash of surf. Just a low, patient lapping, and somewhere far off, a single drawn-out cry from something with wide wings that tilts on thermals you can feel but not see. The air is warm and so thick with moisture it sits on your skin like a second textile.

You are standing on a shelf of dark, raw stone that juts out of warm water at about waist height. The stone is young — you can tell because it hasn't yet decided what color it wants to be. In places it's a deep iron-black, in others a ruddy ochre where minerals are just beginning to oxidize, and in still others a pale, almost chalky grey that looks like bone. The edges of the shelf are angular. They have not been rounded. They have not been softened. The rock was pushed up — recently, by the measure of stone-time — and the water rushed in before erosion could begin its patient filing.

The sea that surrounds you is not deep. At least not here. It's a warm flooded world, a high plateau that broke the surface of the ocean perhaps a few centuries ago — perhaps less — and the water has not yet agreed to leave. It pools between the ridges in broad, shallow basins, and moves between them through narrow channels worn into the young rock by a process that is just barely underway. In some of these channels the water is so clear you can count the small, dark shapes that drift against the pale sand bottom — slow-moving fish, their movements so languid they look like thoughts rather than actions. In others the water is a deep, tannic amber, colored by roots and leaves that have been steeping in it since the first plants found purchase on this new land and began to weep their chemistry into it.

The vegetation is dense in a way that feels almost architectural. It does not just grow here. It builds. Closest to the water are the root-thickets — trees that might be mangroves or might be something unnamed, with pale, arching prop roots that descend through the water and grip the stone below. They grow so close together that in places their roots fuse, forming latticed walls beneath the surface, and you realize that what you thought was a shallow lagoon is actually a forest floor, five or six feet below the waterline. The trunks rise through the surface like dark columns holding up a canopy that is, in some spots, solid enough to walk on — a mat of waxy green leaves, thick as carpet, woven together by creeping vines and the occasional broad-leafed epiphyte whose roots dangle into the air like the fingers of someone reaching for something they've forgotten.

Farther out, where the stone shelves rise higher and break the waterline, the growth is different but no less dense. Here, mosses and ferns colonize every crack and fold in the rock, their fronds so thick and overlapping that the stone beneath is invisible. Small flowering plants — white, pale violet, a yellow so bright it looks electric — push up from the thinnest seams in the cliff faces. Vines spiral upward around the trunks of young trees, some of them so thick at the base that they have begun to constrict the trunks they cling to, creating a slow, botanical wrestling match that will take decades to resolve.

There is a geometry to it all that is not quite natural, or rather, is natural in a way that exceeds what you're used to. The root-thickets form corridors — not straight, not regular, but deliberate enough that you catch yourself thinking *pathway* before you catch yourself thinking *random growth*. The pools between the ridges are almost perfectly circular. The channels between them are almost exactly the width a large animal might use to pass through. You stand at the edge of one such channel and look along it and, at the far end, the rock walls meet at an angle that is just a few degrees too sharp to be coincidence — a narrow grotto mouth, dark with shadow, the water inside it black and still.

*Probably just erosion*, you think. But you don't believe this, because the erosion here has only just begun. The sharp features of the young highland — jagged ridgelines, sheer cliff faces, angular outcroppings that look almost sculpted — have not yet been worn into gentleness. If anything, the landscape is *sharpening*, the water cutting new edges into stone that has barely finished rising. You see this most clearly from the highest point you can reach — a narrow ridge of black rock, maybe twenty feet above the waterline, from which you can see the full scale of this place. It extends in every direction, a wide drowned plateau broken by dozens of ridges and pools, all shimmering under a bright, open sky. The horizon is distant. The scale is regional — you cannot see the edges of this place, and the nearest island or headland is a smudge of dark green on the horizon, too far away to reach by swimming in a single day.

The quiet is immense. Not absolute — there is always the lapping of water, the soft creak of root against stone, the occasional plop of a fish breaking the surface. Insects hum at a low, constant register, more felt than heard. And sometimes, in the deepest of the water-pools, there is a sound like a breath drawn in and held — a faint, subaqueous exhalation that might be gas from the rock-bottom decomposing, or might be something else entirely. The fauna here is scarce. You see almost no animals. But you are aware, constantly, that the place is *watched*. A ripple that has no source. A shadow moving beneath the surface of water too clear to hide anything large. A single heron-like bird on a distant spire, motionless, watching you with an attention that feels personal.

Sometimes you notice that the water in the shallowest pools is not still, in a way that the water in the channels is. It shivers. Very slightly. As if something beneath the surface were breathing. You kneel and look closely. The shiver passes across the pool in a slow wave, taking perhaps ten seconds to cross from one side to the other. When it reaches the far wall, it seems to hesitate, then return.

At dusk, the light turns the water to hammered copper. The canopy of the thickest groves catches the last sun and holds it, so that the flooded forest glows from within, a green-gold luminescence that lingers for minutes after the sky has darkened. And then the stars come out, and the water reflects them, and you notice — or maybe it's just the strangeness playing tricks on your eyes — that the reflection is not quite right. There are stars in the water that are not in the sky.

Nothing moves quickly here. The water rises and falls on a schedule that doesn't match the tides you know. The plants grow visibly but slowly — you can almost see the vines extending, millimeter by millimeter, if you watch long enough. The stone cracks a little wider each season. This is a place that is still becoming itself, that arrived recently into existence and hasn't yet decided what it will be when it's done.

You step back from the channel and turn toward the ridge. The stone underfoot is warm, and sharp enough to draw blood if you're not careful. Behind you, the forest of roots and water exhales its slow, green breath into the darkening air.
