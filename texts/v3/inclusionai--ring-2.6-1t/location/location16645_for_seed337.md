# The Young Hollow

You hear it before you see it — or rather, you feel it. The air is a wall. It presses against your skin with a cool, insistent dampness, like pressing your face to the inside of a cave that has only just remembered it is a cave. The temperature sits a few degrees below comfort, not cold enough to make you gasp but cold enough to remind you, constantly, that your body is generating its own small furnace against the chill. It is the kind of cool that settles into your joints if you stand still too long.

The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. Along the upper seams of the rock — fractures that the diggers have opened but not yet smoothed — a pale, sourceless luminescence clings. It is not daylight, exactly, but your eyes adjust to it the way they would to an overcast sky. A washed-out white-blue, slightly green, as though the stone itself were pregnant with weather. It never changes. There is no dawn here, no dusk, no shifting angle of sun. The light is a constant, and after a while you stop noticing it, and after a longer while you stop trusting that it is real.

The city — and it is a city, though no one who built it would have called it that — is still being born.

---

The architecture is all intention and no habit. The walls are rough. Not rough in the decorative, hand-wrought way of a place that has chosen simplicity, but rough in the way of a thing that hasn't been finished yet. Chisel marks everywhere, overlapping, contradicting each other. Some cuts are deep and confident, driven by someone who knew the stone. Others are tentative, scratched in by hands still learning what rock will yield to and what it will refuse. The passages are narrow — not planned narrow, but *circumstance* narrow, the result of too many people digging in too little space, each one following the wall of whoever came before. In places the ceiling drops to where you must duck. In others the ceiling was never ceiling at all, just the underside of whatever lay above, left ragged and raw.

The density is what strikes you most. Everything presses against everything else. A support beam braces a wall which butts against a dwelling which abuts a water channel which runs alongside a root shelf which is wedged into a ventilation shaft that was never really a ventilation shaft but started as a drainage cut someone abandoned halfway through. Nothing was placed with an architect's eye. Everything was placed because it needed to be *somewhere*, and the somewhere was whichever pocket of emptiness had not yet been spoken for. The city is a puzzle assembled by hundreds of hands that never saw the picture on the box.

And yet it is not chaotic. Not exactly. There is a grammar to it, visible if you look — the way that the water channels always run downhill, even if the downhill was determined by a crack in the rock that predates the city. The way that the cooking areas cluster near the air-flow fissures, and the sleeping alcoves cluster away from them. The way that a wide, roughly circular space has begun to emerge — not planned, but *converged upon*, the way a plaza forms when enough paths cross. Someone has set a fire-ring of unworked stones in its center. The stones are still warm from this morning's fire.

---

The water. God, the water.

It comes through the ceiling. Not in torrents — this is not a place of flooding — but persistently, in thin threads and droplets that find the fractures and follow them down. You can trace the history of the city's construction by the water stains on the walls: darker patches where the rock drank deep, lighter halos where moisture bloomed and evaporated and bloomed again. The channels carved into the floor are recent, scratched into stone with hand-tools, angled to carry runoff toward the lowest point of the settlement. There, a pool has formed — not large, maybe twice the length of a tall person, dark, its surface barely moving. The water is clear at the edges and opaque at the center, clouded by the fine sediment that the diggers' boots carry in on every return from the tunnels.

Along the channel walls, moisture condenses and stays. And here, in these damp seams, the sparse life of the Hollow takes hold.

---

There is not much vegetation. What there is clings to the water lines: thin mats of moss, dark green almost to the point of black, spreading in slow radial patterns across wet stone. They are soft to the touch and smell of something ancient and faintly sweet, like overripe fruit left in cool earth. In a few places — always where the light-fissures are widest — something more ambitious has taken root. A pale shoot, thick as a finger, rising from a crack in the floor. One. Just one. It is not yet identifiable as anything. It has leaves that are more like scales, overlapping tightly, and it bends toward the light with the slow, patient conviction of a thing that has never known any other direction. No one here has named it yet. Some of the diggers have scraped it back twice. It keeps coming.

There are fungal growths too, in the deeper passages where the air is thickest. Small, shelf-like clusters protruding from the walls, pale as bone, faintly phosphorescent. They provide a dim glow of their own — not enough to read by, but enough to make the darkness feel *populated*. Workers who go down to the lower tunnels have learned to trust them as way-markers. They are reliable. They do not move. They do not go out. Unlike everything else down here, they are patient in a way that feels older than patience.

---

The fauna is scarce. A few things have come in with the people, or have wandered in from whatever lay above and found that the Hollow suited them. Small, grey lizards — no longer than a forearm — that have taken to basking on the warmest stones near the cooking areas. They are quiet and quick and tolerated for reasons the settlers cannot fully articulate, though some say the lizards eat the insects that come in through the upper fissures, and the settlers have not yet worked up the will to argue.

There are insects, too. Not many. Beetles with hard, dark shells that click when you touch them. Something winged that appears only near the pool at irregular intervals and is gone before anyone can properly see it. The settlers watch these creatures with a kind of proprietary attention, as though cataloguing them were a civic duty. They have not yet developed the language for what they are doing when they watch. They call it "listening to the stone's small animals."

---

The sounds are quiet, but they are many.

The dominant sound is the scrape and tap of tools on rock. Flint against limestone. Bone against shale. The rhythmic, almost musical percussion of someone who has carved ten thousand identical cuts and is now making the ten-thousand-and-first without thinking about it. Underneath this, the water: its thin, wandering trickle through the channels, the occasional *plip* of a drop falling into the pool from ceiling-height, the faint, continuous whisper of condensation gathering on a cold surface. Voices, low — not because anyone has imposed a rule of quiet, but because the stone absorbs volume, and shouting here feels wasteful, a currency spent carelessly. You learn to speak in the Hollow the way you learn to walk: carefully, aware of the ground, aware that the space is shared and every disturbance ripples outward.

There is a sound that none of the settlers can identify. It comes from deep below — not deep in the way of a lower tunnel, but deep in the way of *beneath*. A low, almost tonal hum, felt more than heard, that seems to rise through the soles of the feet rather than enter through the ears. Some say it is the rock settling. Some say it is water moving through a space too large to see. Some say nothing. Everyone, eventually, stops noticing it — the way you stop noticing the pressure of clothing on your skin. But on nights when the work is done and the fire in the central ring has burned to embers, and the pale light in the fissures has not changed, and the lizards have gone still on their warm stones, and the whole city holds its collective breath — then the hum is the only thing that remains, and it sounds almost like the place is *thinking*.

---

The people are few but certain.

They have been here — the oldest among them say — for the length of two growing seasons, measured by a tally carved into a stone at the main passage's mouth, where the air from outside reaches them. They came down for reasons that differ from family to family, but the reasons share a common skeleton: the ground above was unreliable. Something shifted — not violently, not yet, but in a way that made reliability feel like a premise rather than a fact. And so they dug.

Their tools are simple. Hand-axes with flint heads, bound to wooden shafts with cord made from shredded root-fiber. Scrapers — flat, oval stones with edges honed to a keen curve. Digging sticks, worn smooth and dark with use. A single, precious awl made of copper, traded up from a settlement three valleys away, used only for the finest work and guarded with a seriousness that borders on ritual. They know of metal but do not mine it. They know of cities but did not build them before, and what they have built here does not quite match the word. It is closer, in spirit, to a scar — a wound in the earth that the body is slowly learning to close.

---

The rules of the place are emerging the way the place itself is emerging: loosely, and under constant revision.

The digging rights are informal but respected. You dig where you have dug, and you do not dig into someone's space. The channels are shared, maintained by a rotating duty that everyone takes turns at and no one particularly likes. Food is stored in sealed clay pots on high shelves carved into the walls, and the rule is simple: do not take from someone else's pot, and do not carve your shelf where it blocks someone else's air. There is a council of sorts — five people, chosen by a shifting, unspoken consensus — who settle the disputes that the informal rules cannot absorb. They meet at the fire-ring in the central space, and their meetings are not formal. They are closer to conversations.

There is one rule that is not written and not spoken but is understood by everyone: *the city is not done.* Nothing here is permanent. The walls may shift. The channels may clog. The ceiling may crack in ways that have not yet cracked. Everyone is building, and everyone knows that what they build may need to be rebuilt, and everyone is, despite this, building with care — carving their alcove a little more smoothly, lining their section of channel a little more neatly, as though the act of care were itself the point.

---

The dynamic of the place is its pulse.

Work begins before the light-shift (there is no true night in the Hollow, only a dimming of the stone-light that the people have learned to approximate through a system of covered and uncovered reflective panels that no one remembers who invented). The diggers go to the tunnels first, their flint tools striking sparks of pale stone-dust into the quiet air. The water-keepers follow, checking the channels, clearing the sediment that has settled overnight. The fire-tenders arrive at the central ring. The lizards emerge from under the warm stones and resume their patrols with the unhurried air of creatures who own the place more authentically than its builders.

By mid-cycle — the people divide the light-phase into rough sections based on how the moss-light near the ceiling seems to brighten and dim — the sound of the Hollow reaches its peak. Tools, voices, water, the scuff of bare feet on stone, the clatter of a clay pot being set down, the call of a child (there are children; there have always been children) echoing through a passage. It is not loud. It is layered. It sounds like a place that is alive and knows it is alive and is not yet sure what to do with the fact.

In the quieter hours, the Hollow breathes. The air moves through its fissures in slow, tidal cycles that no one fully understands but everyone relies on. The pool reflects the stone-light, and the moss drinks, and the fungal shelves glow their patient glow, and the hum from below continues — steady, unbroken, proprietary — as though the Hollow were not a place that people built, but a place that was always waiting, and is only now, slowly, recognizing that it has been found.

---

There is a spot — a recently opened chamber, one of the largest, carved where a vein of soft stone met a harder one and the diggers found the boundary easier to follow than to fight. It is not yet assigned a purpose. Someone has left a digging stick in the corner. Someone else has marked the wall with a series of lines — not tallies, not words, just a rhythm of marks, spaced with a consistency that feels deliberate. The air in the chamber is still and dry compared to the rest of the Hollow, and the stone-light here is brighter, several fissures converging overhead like the ribs of a half-formed canopy.

People pass through the chamber and pause. They do not know yet what it will become. Something is gathering there — not a plan, not a decision, but a feeling, the way a room in an unfinished house begins to feel like a kitchen before anyone has put a stove in it. It is the Hollow's way of telling its people what it wants to be next. They are learning to listen.

It is, after all, the only thing they have that is truly theirs — the unfinished work, the city still dreaming itself into shape in the dark, steady warmth of the deep ground.
