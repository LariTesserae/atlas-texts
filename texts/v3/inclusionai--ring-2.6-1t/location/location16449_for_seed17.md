## The Equilibrium Sea

You hear it before you see anything else.

A low, vast drone — not a single sound but a continent of sound, layered and permanent, as though the world itself has a mechanical heartbeat. It fills every space, rolls across the water's surface, travels up through the cold mineral-thin liquid and into the air where it rings against the basalt ridges. The sound does not arrive. It was already there. It has always been there. It will never stop.

The sea stretches to every horizon, if the word *horizon* can apply to a place where the far shore is the sky. The water is shallow — knee-deep in places, waist-deep in others — and so clear it seems barely present at all, a film over the bones of the world beneath. It is not blue. It is not any single color. Under the pale and constant light it shifts between silver and the faint greenish-white of old bone, and where it pools deeper in the crevasses of the young terrain it darkens to something the color of graphite diluted with milk. It is cold beyond what cold should feel like in water — a cold that has had eons to understand the concept of a body's warmth and to remove it methodically. The water does not sting. It simply takes, without apology.

The terrain beneath the water and above it is young — violently, freshly young. The basalt formations have not yet had time to soften. They rise in sharp fins, in ridges, in walls of hexagonal columns that crack apart at their edges and collapse into the shallows with a sound like breaking pottery. There are calderas still open, their walls glass-smooth from the heat that recently fled them, now flooded to their rims and emitting nothing but a faint mineral vapor that the water swallows without remark. Cliffs of volcanic glass stand at angles that feel accusatory, as though the earth only just thrust them up and has not yet decided whether to keep them. Everything is angular. Nothing has been sanded. The landscape looks furious and incomplete, though it has been this way — unchanged, unchanging — for longer than any record exists.

There are no plants. Not one. No moss on the glass, no algae slicking the stones, no kelp threading through the shallows. The volcanic substrate is instead colonized by a thin, mineral crust — a fungal substrate, barely visible, almost a stain on the rock. It is gray-white, slightly iridescent, and it covers everything the water touches. It is the base of the food chain in a place where food chains were never supposed to exist. The fungus processes the volcanic minerals directly, converting sulfur and iron into energy through a chemistry so alien it took visiting scholars decades to accept it was not merely chemosynthesis but something else — something that shouldn't work and does, with the quiet persistence of all things that have never known competition.

And from this fungal crust, fauna has exploded. Abundantly, overwhelmingly, improbably.

There are grazers — armored things the size of dogs, low-slung and deliberate, with shells segmented like luggage that they shed and regrow in rings as they age. They move across the submerged ridges on jointed legs, scraping the fungal mat from the glass with mandibles that click in metronomic unison, adding their ticking to the vast mechanical drone. There are filter-feeders anchored to the columns, their bodies sac-like and translucent, pulsing gently as they strain the mineral-rich water for particles the fungus has loosened. Some are sixty feet tall, their feeding filaments extended into the current like the rigging of ghost-ships, trembling with captured motion. There are smaller things — quick, dark, multilegged — that scavenge the molted shells of the grazers, that pick at the dead filter-feeders when their sacs finally tear. Everything eats something. Nothing is wasted. The ecology is a closed machine as relentless as the geology beneath it.

And then — the ruins.

They are scattered, as the parameters say, like thoughts someone started and abandoned. A factory complex on the summit of the tallest ridge: six stories of riveted iron and corroded steel, half-consumed by the rising water, its lower floors flooded and dark, its upper floors open to the vast empty sky. The smokestacks still vent — not smoke, but columns of hot steam that rise and are immediately flattened by the cold air into horizontal curtains of mist. They hiss. It is one of the dominant notes in the soundscape, a sibilant white noise that the water amplifies and carries for miles.

Elsewhere: a processing shed with its conveyor belt still running, carrying volcanic chunks from an intake hopper to an output pile that has long since overflowed into the shallows, forming an artificial island of sorted mineral debris. The belt motor whines at a frequency that sits in the chest like a second heartbeat. A collection of cylindrical tanks, corroded green, some split and spilling their long-cold contents into the water — whatever those contents were, the local fauna now gathers at the spill sites in dense, feeding clusters. A single watchtower on four iron legs, leaning slightly, its platform sixty feet up offering a view of nothing but water and basalt and steam and the pale indifferent sky. The ladder still rattles in the windless air, a rhythmic metallic clatter — *clank-clank-clank-clank* — caused by nothing visible, perhaps by the resonance of the ambient sound itself vibrating the corroded joints loose.

The built things are industrial in origin, unmistakable. But they are not maintained. There are no workers, no overseers. The machines run because the equilibrium allows them to — the geothermal vents that power the steam systems still vent, the conveyor motors still receive enough current from corroded but functional generators that have not yet fully surrendered their chemical potential. Everything is in a state of partial operation, and this partial operation is stable, and this stability is absolute, and it has no endpoint.

The light is strange. It comes from above, as light does, but the sky is a pale, washed-out dome with no sun visible — just a uniform luminance, as though someone dissolved the sun into the atmosphere and distributed its brightness evenly across the entire vault. Shadows exist but are soft-edged, pale things. At "night" — if the dimming of the luminance by perhaps fifteen percent counts as night — nothing changes. The machines hum the same. The fauna move the same. The grazers continue their clicking, the filter-feeders their pulse, the conveyor belt its slow procession. The equilibrium accommodates diurnal variation as a courtesy, a mild fluctuation it neither generates nor resists.

The elevation of the place is notable in the only way it can be: the water's surface sits far above the remnants of any lower geography. Below the surface, canyons plunge — flooded chasms in the young rock, their walls unweathered, their depths unmapped. Pressurized jets of mineral-rich water occasionally erupt from fissures in these canyons, spouting a hundred feet into the air before falling back into the shallows with enormous, percussive *cracks* that are among the loudest sounds in the landscape. They happen at irregular intervals but are themselves part of the equilibrium — unpredictable in timing but inevitable in occurrence, a rhythm that contains its own disruption.

The scale is the thing that finally undoes any visitor. The water extends in every direction to the edge of perception and beyond. The ridges run for hundreds of miles. The factory complexes are separated by hours of wading through knee-deep cold. The fauna colonies are continental in extent. The sky presses down with its emptiness. The density of presence is almost nothing — a grazer here, a filter-feeder there, a factory complex every two hundred miles — and between these scattered points: water, basalt, fungal crust, silence broken only by the drone, the hiss, the click, the distant cry of something with too many legs dragging itself across a glass cliff in the eternal, unchanging middle of an afternoon.

Nothing here was built to last. Everything here has lasted. The water has no shore. The machines have no purpose. The fauna have no predators beyond each other. The land has no soil, only glass and mineral and the thin smear of life that found a way to eat stone. And the sound — the deafening, constant, mechanical, biological, geological sound — is the only proof that any of this is happening at all.

Stand on the watchtower. Feel it rattle. Look out over the silver water to the far ridge where steam rises from a fissure that opened before your species existed and will not close long after it has ended. The grazer on the ridge below looks up. Its mandibles click.

The equilibrium holds.
