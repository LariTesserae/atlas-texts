## The Weeping Terrace

You hear it before you see it. A low, continuous roar — not of any single source but of everything at once: water pulling itself through stone, the groan of young rock still remembering its own formation, and a wind that doesn't so much blow as *lean*, pressing cool and heavy against your skin like a damp cloth. The sound is immense, but patient. It has been building for a long time and intends to go on.

The terrace reveals itself in layers.

You are standing on a broad, elevated platform of raw geology — a place that looks less *shaped* than *emerging*, as though the land beneath your feet decided to exist only recently and hasn't yet grown accustomed to the idea. The stone underfoot is dark — basalt or something close to it — still striated with the vertical seams where it cooled and cracked. Those seams haven't fully separated. They hold. You can see the fissures breathing: thin threads of water creep through them, not falling but *oozing*, as if the rock is sweating from an exertion it completed long ago and hasn't quite stopped performing. The water gathers on the surface in films so thin they look like the stone has been oiled. It is everywhere. Every vertical face weeps. Every horizontal surface holds a skin of standing water that trembles without cause, catching the grey-white light that falls from a sky pressed low overhead.

The elevation gives you a sense of exposure. The terrace sits above the surrounding terrain — not dramatically, but enough that you feel the openness, the slight vertigo of standing on a raised platform with mist curling around the edges below. The air is cool. It sits on your forearms and behind your knees. Breath comes out in brief, dissolving ghosts. The temperature doesn't bite; it *clings*. And the moisture is absolute — everything is damp, everything is condensing, everything smells of wet mineral and something faintly vegetal underneath, like the memory of rot that hasn't happened yet.

The vegetation is lush in the way that deep-ocean life is lush: concentrated, extravagant, and startlingly alone.

Between the structures of stone, enormous plants have taken hold — but they are *far apart*. Each one commands a territory of thirty or forty meters, a single organism sprawling outward from a central root-mass into a dense thicket of fronds, broad wet leaves, and climbing mosses that colonize every vertical surface nearby. They look like ferns, but wrong — too thick-stemmed, too deliberate in their curling, each frond unfurling with a visible patience that makes you think you can watch and still not see the motion. Their color is deep: black-green, blue-green, the green of deep water. They drink from the weeping stone. Some have root systems so exposed and extensive that they look like the veins of a body laid open, gripping the basalt with pale, mineral-stained fingers.

The spaces between them are almost bare. Raw stone, shallow pools, the thin mineral mud that forms when water and dust meet. These gaps are not empty-feeling in the way of neglect. They feel *intentional*, as though the plants have staked their claims and the land has consented to hold the rest in reserve. Walking between them, you cross distances that feel architectural — you move from the shadow of one great green mass, through open mist, toward the next, and each arrival feels like entering a room.

And then — the settlement.

It sits on the highest point of the terrace, at the center, like a knot in wood. A dozen structures, maybe fifteen. They are built from the same dark stone the terrace is made of, but *worked* — hand-hewn, though the hewing is imperfect, leaving surfaces that are more shaped than polished, with chisel marks still visible in long, rhythmic scars. The walls are thick, nearly a meter in places, packed with a mudite mortar that has begun to mineralize in the perpetual damp, turning the joints between stones a pale, chalky white. The construction is organized. There are roads — not paved, but compacted, worn channels of hard-packed earth and gravel that connect the structures in a layout that feels planned but incomplete. A few of the roads end abruptly. A few haven't been started yet, visible only as shallow trenches scratched into the rock, waiting.

The structures themselves are simple: rectangular or slightly curved, with low doorways and no windows. Some have partial roofs of layered stone slabs; others are open-walled, more like enclosures than rooms. Their scale is human but generous — each one the size of a large room or a small hall, and they sit together with the easy proximity of a hamlet that grew slowly, one building at a time, without pressure.

In the gaps between buildings, channels have been cut — shallow grooves in the stone that direct the weeping water into collection pools. Early infrastructure. The channels are lined with flat stones placed with care, and the pools they feed are rimmed with moss so thick and green it looks painted. Someone has been tending this place. Someone thought about *where the water goes*.

The technology is pre-metal in practice, though you catch the occasional glint — a copper-alloy binding on a gate, a thin-walled pot sitting beside a hearth-stone, darkened with use. Tools are stone and bone, shaped with a competence that suggests generations of repetition. Nothing here is decorative, but nothing is careless. The settlement has the feeling of a place that has been *occupied* — lived in, worked in, argued over, maintained — for a time long enough that habit has become form.

There is almost no fauna. No birds. No insects that you can see. The occasional movement — a ripple in a shallow pool, a trembling in the undergrowth of some vast plant-mass — suggests something lives here, but it hides. The place belongs to stone, water, moss, and the patience of roots.

And the weirdness.

It's subtle. It accumulates.

The water in the channels sometimes runs *uphill* for a stretch — just a few meters, just a slight defiance of what you expect — before remembering itself and resuming its descent. The mist doesn't drift. It *gathers*, thickening in specific spots as though it has weight and preference, pooling in hollows and between structures like something being stored. The stone beneath your hand, when you press it, is warm. Not geothermally warm — not volcanic, not dangerous — but warm the way a sleeping body is warm, as though the terrace's formation is a process that hasn't fully cooled from its own exertion.

And the plants. If you stand still long enough, watching a single great fern-mass from a distance, you can almost — *almost* — see the fronds shift. Not in wind. There is no wind strong enough here. They lean, infinitesimally, toward each other across the empty spaces, as though conversation is occurring at a speed that requires patience on a human scale to detect at all.

The light is the grey-white brightness of an overcast high-altitude day, even, diffuse, without harsh shadows. It makes the place look flat and deep simultaneously — every surface equally visible, and yet the mist provides layers of soft occlusion that give the terrace a depth like looking into cloudy water. Sound carries with startling clarity through this light: the splash of a drip falling thirty meters away, the long creak of stone adjusting to stone, the faint, wet whisper of moss drinking.

Nothing here is in a hurry. The settlement is still being built. The geology is still settling. The plants are still reaching. The terrace itself — this broad, high, wet, echoing platform — is still becoming what it will be. It has the feeling of a place caught mid-sentence in a language older than the words being spoken, and the sentence is long, and the speaker is not yet finished thinking about what they mean.

It is loud with slowness. It is empty with abundance. It is young, and it has been young for longer than you can hold in your attention at once.

You stand on the Weeping Terrace, and the terrace weeps back.
