# The Worn Reach

---

You see it long before you arrive. Not the ground itself — that lies below the curvature of sight — but the sky above it, which is wrong. At this altitude the sky should be thin and pale, a faded rinse at the top of the world. Instead it is dense. Layered. Bands of cloud sit at eye level, lit from within by something that is not quite the sun, and they move fast, dragged by winds that have nothing to stop them across ten, twenty, a hundred kilometers of bare expanse. The clouds drag shadows across the plateau, and those shadows race, and the whole overhead sky has the quality of a tide going out in a tremendous hurry.

You feel the place before you see it: pressure in the ears. The elevation is severe — far above the line where breath comes easy — but the air is warm. Not temperate. *Warm*, the way a kiln is warm, the way a fever-skin is warm. It comes up from below. The ground is breathing it out through a billion tiny fractures in the stone. Walk barefoot here — no one does, but if you did — you would feel the heat pulse up through the soles of your feet in slow, arrhythmic waves, as though something enormous and far below were sleeping and dreaming of turning over.

The plateau is what remains of a mountain range that no longer exists. That is the only honest way to describe it. Once, this was a spine of peaks that would have blocked the horizon in every direction, white-capped, sheer, impassable. Over an almost incomprehensible stretch of time, erosion ground them down — water, wind, frost, chemical dissolution — working them the way a river works a stone, slowly reducing every feature to its most stubborn residue. What is left is not a landscape of peaks. It is a landscape of *remainders*.

The flat is not truly flat. It undulates in long, low swells, the way a sheet pulled taut over buried objects would. Those objects are the last bones of the mountains: dense volcanic cores, ancient intrusions of mineral that resisted the grinding longer than everything around them. They protrude as ridges, as low mesas, as stumpy towers no taller than a house but so weathered that they look like the exposed vertebrae of something colossal. Between them, basins — shallow, mineral-crusted depressions that collect whatever water the sky provides.

And the sky does provide, though unreliably. Rain here comes in sudden, violent sheets, arriving without visible origin, hammering the stone for minutes or hours, then stopping as if a valve had been closed. In that brief window the basins fill. Water sheets across the flat in thin, fast flows that find the depressions and pool there, mixing with mineral deposits that have been concentrating for millennia. The water is not clear. It is colored — copper-brown, iron-orange, in some basins a green so vivid and shifting it looks like dye poured onto glass. Algae and microbial mats live in these pools, thick and rubbery, forming colonies of such density and intensity of color that from a distance they look like stained glass laid on the ground.

Between the pools and the stone remnants: nothing. Emptiness. The ground is a crust of white and grey mineral deposits — salts, calcium,ite, compounds with names that taste metallic on the tongue — and it stretches and stretches, flat to the horizon, interrupted only by the occasional upright stone or the faint lip of a basin. The emptiness is the thing that defines the place. It is not barren in the way a desert is barren — this ground is chemically rich, biologically active in its small zones. But the volume between those zones is vast beyond reason. Stand on the flat and you are a speck in a pan. The horizon is close (the curvature of the earth is visible from this height) and the sky presses down, and the distance between you and the next solid landmark might be three hours of walking, five, ten — and that landmark might be nothing more than a stump of stone half-buried in crust.

The sound is the thing that undoes you.

It begins, always, with the wind. There is nothing to impede it across the flat. It builds velocity unobstructed, and when it meets the remnant stones — the stumps and ridges and half-collapsed towers — it is forced through narrow passages, over carved alcoves, into cavities that the ancient erosion has bored through resistant rock. The result is not a howl. It is a *chord*. The stone remnants become instruments. Each one, depending on its shape, its cavity geometry, the angle of the wind, produces a different tone: a deep drone from the larger stumps, a thin keening from the narrow ridges, a rhythmic pulse from the stone with tubular holes worn through its core. The wind plays them all at once, continuously, and the sound carries across the flat with nothing to absorb it, so it arrives at your ears layered — dozens of tones, harmonics, overtones, some steady and some flickering as gusts fluctuate, some so low you feel them in the chest more than hear them in the ears.

Under the wind: water. The thermal seeps make a constant background chatter — drip, trickle, gurgle — from water emerging through cracks in the stone, running downhill in thin sheets, feeding the edge of a pool that wasn't there last season. In some areas, water moves through underground cavities and the stone itself seems to be speaking: a hollow booming that comes from nowhere specific, reverberating as the water sloshes through subterranean chambers. The geyser fields — clustered in a few zones where the geothermal heat is most intense — contribute their own percussion. Eruptions are irregular. Some geysers go off every few minutes, sharp and sudden, a punch of steam and hot water that hits the air with a crack like a rifle shot, followed by the hiss of evaporation. Others are slower, building over hours, a deepening gurgle that rises to a roar before the column of water and steam punches upward, sometimes twenty meters, sometimes forty, and falls back steaming and sizzling onto the crust.

And above it all, the rain, when it comes, is a wall of white noise that subsumes everything else — the wind, the water, the stone — into a single roaring presence. And when it stops, the silence that follows is not silence at all, because everything is dripping, flowing, settling, and the sound of a trillion droplets hitting mineral crust is itself enormous.

The fauna is sparse and strange. Nothing here lives in abundance. What lives has adapted to the thermal zones, the mineral pools, the pockets of warmth. Small creatures — six-legged, armored, with too many joints — cluster around the geyser margins, feeding on microbial mats. They are pale and translucent, and when they move they make a sound like tiny needles on glass. Larger things are heard more often than seen: something that moves through the stone remnant corridors on legs that click and scrape against mineral crust, leaving tracks that appear overnight and dry to nothing by morning. No one has seen it whole. The rare creatures are elusive, and the emptiness of the plateau makes any movement conspicuous — a dot crossing the flat at a distance, too small to identify, gone when you reach the spot.

The vegetation, where it exists, is *aggressive*. Not in the way that plants in wet lowlands are aggressive — not sprawling, not competing for light in a crowded canopy. Here, in the warm mineral-rich water and steam, growth is *vertical*. Around the thermal pools, dense stands of tall, hollow-stemmed plants rise — some as high as three meters — with fronds that spread at the top into broad mineral-absorbing surfaces. They are colored by what they take from the water: orange-stemmed near the copper pools, green-black near the iron-rich seeps. Mosses form thick cushions on the warm stone surfaces, so dense they look like velvet. In the condensation zones — where cold night air meets the warm stone and moisture beads on every surface — entire stumps and ridges become coated in a layer of living film so thick it looks like fur. This lushness contains itself. It does not spread. It fills the zones where warmth and moisture coincide and stays there, concentrated, almost jewel-like in its intensity against the blank mineral flat.

The built things — and there are built things, though you might walk the plateau for days before finding one — are simple and old. Stone circles, low and deliberate, placed on the highest points of the flat where they can be seen from distance. Not large — three or four stones each, set upright, spaced evenly, in no pattern that immediately announces itself. But when you map them, when you trace lines between circles that are visible as dots on the horizon, the geometry reveals: they form a network. A grid. Something intentional and vast and patient. Near some of them, you find tools. Simple ones. Chipped stone hand-axes, scrapers, grinding stones worn into shallow concavities by years of use. They are not weathered. They are, impossibly, sharp still. As though they were placed recently, though the stone circles beneath them are sunk deep into the crust with age.

Carvings. On the remnant stones — the stumps, the mesas — there are marks. Not decorative, not pictorial. Lines. Parallel lines, grouped in clusters, sometimes intersecting, sometimes branching. They are cut deep into the hardest stone faces, which means they were made with something harder, or with immense patience, or with something that is no longer available. They predate the erosion that has shaped the stones around them. In some cases, the carving is so old that it has begun to resemble a natural feature — a vein of quartz, a fracture line — and you walk past it without seeing it, and then you see it, and the recognition is vertiginous.

The weirdness is not dramatic. It is not a place of impossible physics or alien geometry. It is a place where your perception is slightly, constantly, *off*. The scale plays tricks: a pool that looks reachable turns out to be an hour's walk away. A stump that looks small turns out to be large enough to shelter inside. Sounds arrive before their source becomes visible, or after. The mineral-stained pools, when you look down into them, show reflections that lag slightly behind your movement — not the reflection of where you are, but where you were a half-second ago, as though the light in the water is slower. The carved lines on the stones, in certain light, seem to shift — not physically, but perceptually, as if the pattern your brain assembles from them rearranges itself when you look away and look back. The wind-chord produced by the stone remnants sometimes, for a measure or two, resolves into something that sounds like structure. Like *intent*. Like a phrase from a language almost recognized.

The plateau is not dying. It is not alive. It is in a state that language strains toward and cannot reach — it is the landscape at the far end of time, where erosion has finished its primary work but has not yet begun to relax. The stone is still being worn. The pools are still forming and evaporating and reforming. The thermal vents are still opening and closing. The wind is still arranging the remnant stones into chords that play continuously, for no one, across the vast and empty mineral flat.

Things are still happening here. They have been happening for so long that they look like stillness.
