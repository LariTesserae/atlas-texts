You don't find the basin. The basin finds you.

The trail that brings you here — a dry riverbed of pale calcite that threads between faulted ridges a thousand meters up — simply ends at a lip of stone no different from any other in the escarpment. You step over it the way you'd step over a doorframe, and the world changes.

Not dramatically. Not at first. Just the sound. Sound arrives before sight does here, the way it does when you put your head underwater. A wall of it: layered, omnidirectional, alive. It pours up from below like heat off tarmac. By the time you've taken three steps down the natural ramp of packedite andite scree, you cannot hear your own footsteps anymore. You have been swallowed by the chorus.

The basin is roughly circular, maybe four hundred meters across. The erosion has done something to it that erosion does only after very long stretches of time and very little interference: it has made everything level. Not flat — the basin floor has a gentle convex curve, like the inside of an enormous bowl that has slowly been squeezed upward from below and then released, settling into a dome of stone no taller than a person's knee. The whole place has this quality of *almost flatness*, the landscape's final answer to gravity after losing the argument over several million years.

The walls rise around you — not cliffs, but the last bones of a mountain that has been patient enough to let itself be eaten away. They are smooth, but not featureless. Close examination reveals a texture like hammered copper, mineral striations in rust and cream and a grey-blue that doesn't quite have a name. In places, the stone is threaded with veins of something that isn't stone: a dark material, glassy, with the faintest iridescence, too regular to be obsidian, too organic to be slag. It runs through the bedrock like a nervous system you can see from the outside. The veins pulse. Not visibly — you look again and again and you cannot confirm they pulse — but you *sense* a rhythm in them, a kind of damp oscillation, the way you feel a bell's note in your sternum after the strike.

This is the tech. Or what's left of it.

There are structures, but calling them structures is generous. To your left, twenty meters into the basin, a wall stands — waist-high, maybe three meters long, built from the same copper-toned stone as the basin walls but arranged with unmistakable intention. Courses. Mortar. The mortar has turned toite over the ages,ite that's cracking in fractal patterns along lines of molecular stress. But set into the wall, at regular intervals, are nodules of that dark glassy material, and they are not eroding. Not at all. Theite around them has worn away; they sit in little alcoves like dark eyes, still flush and seamless, still faintly warm to the touch despite the air temperature, which holds at a level your body registers as *late morning in a place you'd happily live.*

The wall once belonged to something. It's impossible to say what. Three of the original five nodules remain. The wall is perhaps a thousand years old. The basin is older than that by orders of magnitude. The wall arrived late here, like a sentence spoken in the middle of a paragraph about something else entirely.

To your right, farther into the basin, another artifact: a single column, upright, twice your height, made of the dark glassy material with almost no stone encroaching on it. It does not taper. It does not widen. It is a perfect right cylinder, standing on a base that seems to have been poured into a depression in the rock and fused there. Its surface is not smooth — it is covered in shallow, branching grooves, like the pattern a river delta would make if a river were given a thousand years to decide, and like rivers, the pattern is fractally self-similar: zoom in and the grooves branch into smaller grooves, and smaller still. Looking at it for too long produces a faint unease, the feeling that the pattern is *closer to finishing than it was yesterday.*

But you can only study the column for a moment. The sound pulls your attention back.

It is not one sound. It is dozens, and they do not compete — they occupy separate frequencies, separate heights, separate times of day, so that the soundscape functions less like a cacophony and more like a recording of a living room in a town you can't find on a map. Low underneath: a percussion of insects, beetles the size of your thumb clicking their elytra in synchronized bursts, a rhythm that travels up through the sole of your boot and lives in your molars. Above that, a sustained mid-register hum — beetles? No. Amphibians. You find them in the shallow depressions on the basin floor, coin-sized creatures, mottled amber and black, their throats ballooning in rippling waves. They don't croak exactly. They resonate. Each one a different pitch, and there are hundreds of them, scattered across the sparse depressions where the thin water collects — dampness, not water. Condensation that forms on the interior surfaces of the basin at dawn and trickles down in slow, mineral-rich threads, pooling in the shallow divots of the ancient floor. The amphibians live in these pools, and they sing, and the sound of it is not beautiful in any way you'd recognize. It is *thick.* It has texture. It sits in the air like humidity.

Higher still — birds. Not many species. Perhaps three. But they are *present* in numbers that seem to exceed the basin's capacity. They move in loose, overlapping circuits, and their calls are sharp, declarative things, like someone snapping a ruler against a desk. Every few seconds, from every direction. They roost in the sparse trees.

The trees. This is where the lushness concentrates.

There are perhaps fifteen of them in the entire basin. Fifteen trees, and they are each extraordinary. They have trunks the color of wet clay, smooth and faintly warm, no bark, no visible branching until you reach a height of six or seven meters, where the crown explodes outward in a horizontal architecture of limbs that spread and then droop and then rise again, like a conversation frozen in gesture. The leaves are small, densely packed, a grey-green that almost has silver in it. They move constantly — not in wind, because there is no wind here, the basin walls deflecting everything — but in some convection of air that the trees themselves seem to generate. Warmth rising from the dark veins in the stone below, perhaps. Or something the trees know that you don't.

Between the trees: open ground. This is the sparseness. The space between presences. The ground is covered in a low, dense mat of moss — not green but a burnished bronze — and threaded through it are roots, pale and half-exposed, that belong to the trees but behave differently from any root you've seen. They *lift* from the ground in shallow arcs, sometimes ascending to half a meter before returning, as if the trees are breathing through their roots. And in the gaps between root-arches, you see things: small objects. Pebble-sized, dark, smooth. They are too regular to be stones. You pick one up. It is light, almost weightless, and its surface has that same branching-groove pattern as the column. It is warm. It hums — not audibly, but in your hand, a vibration more felt than heard. You put it down. The amphibia increase their pitch by a fraction of a tone. You are not imagining this.

The fauna becomes aware of you now. Not alarmed. Not curious in any way you'd project. *Aware.* The birds have stopped calling. The amphibia have gone a half-tone flat. The insects have shifted their rhythm — not broken, but *adapted*, the way a jazz ensemble drops volume when a new soloist takes the floor. And from behind one of the trees, at the basin's far edge, something moves.

It is the size of a large dog but shaped like nothing you have a frame for. Four limbs but the proportions are wrong — the forelimbs are longer and held in a flexed posture, like a swimmer's reach. Its body is covered in short filaments, not fur, each one a slightly different length, and they move in what looks like slow motion, as if they are tracking something in the air — moisture, electromagnetic variation, the faint thermal signature of your hand still holding the dark pebble. Its head is too large for its neck. Its eyes are reflective and set wide, but the reflective layer is not the red-orange of a nocturnal animal. It is a pale, opalescent silver, and when it blinks — which it does rarely — a second, translucent eyelid slides horizontally, like a lens cap.

It looks at you. You feel *scanned*, not visually but through some other sense you can't name. The filaments on its body orient toward you, and for a moment the air between you tightens, as if the space has become a membrane.

Then it turns and walks — *flows* — behind the tree and is gone. The amphibia resume their pitch. The insects rebuild their rhythm. One bird calls, then another, and the sound floods back in, filling the basin the way it was before you arrived.

This place does not need you. It was here before the wall, and before the column, and before the trees — which may not be trees at all, but something older that learned the shape of trees because it was useful. The basin is an engine that has been running on its own logic for so long that the logic has become geology. The technology is not abandoned. It is *composted.* It has been metabolized into stone and root and the humming pebble at your feet, and the only evidence that it was once something else is the faint wrongness of it — the way the light doesn't quite refract correctly through the air near the dark veins, the way the column's grooves are a fraction too perfect, the way the animal looked at you with eyes that saw not light but *structure.*

The air smells of wet stone and something sweet and faintly metallic, like the taste of a battery touched by a wet tongue. The warmth is even, without source — not the sun's direct heat (the basin walls block most of the sky) but a rising warmth from below, as if the earth here is running a low fever. At the center of the basin, the convex floor rises by a few inches, and at the very apex, there is a small depression no larger than your fist. In it: water. Clear, still, and when you look into it, the reflection is yours, but delayed by a fraction of a second. You raise your hand. The reflection raises its hand a heartbeat later.

You stay a while longer, but the basin does not change around you. It does not need to. It is in its final form. Everything here has settled, compressed, found the configuration that lasts. The fauna will continue their layered chorus. The column will continue its imperceptible branching. The trees will breathe through their roots. The dark veins will hum beneath theite that crumbles grain by grain.

You leave the way you came, over the lip of the escarpment, and the wind hits you and the silence is enormous.

Behind you, below, the basin continues its loud, ancient, living hum.
