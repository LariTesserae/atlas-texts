# The Resonance Flats

You hear it before you see anything. Before you register color or distance or whether what you're standing on is ground or wall. The sound arrives as a pressure change in the chest, a subsonic throb that lives behind the sternum, and then the higher layers break through — clicking, seething, dripping, grinding — ten thousand processes happening in every direction at once, none of them willing to pause for you.

The Flats stretch across a basin the size of a small continent, cupped between geological ridges so old they've been absorbed into the architecture. Nothing here is clearly natural and nothing is clearly built. That distinction doesn't survive the first mile.

The ground — if you can call it ground — is a crust of calciumite anditeiteite and sintered fungal chitin, warm underfoot even through boot-soles, perpetually damp. It gives slightly, like walking on a sponge that has opinions about your weight. Water seeps up through every joint, every crack, not in rivers but in a continuous wet breath. The surface is a membrane. Step hard and it wells. Step gently and it holds. The water is warm, faintly saline, carrying mineral traces that taste like copper and old pennies. It condenses out of the air too — the atmosphere is so saturated that visibility shifts in pulses, as though the world is breathing vapor in and out. On a bad day — and most days are bad — the air is a white-grey haze, and the Flats disappear upward into nothing, and you navigate by sound alone.

Everything is covered. Not by moss or lichen — nothing so simple. The dominant growths are fungal, but fungal only in the way a coral reef is "animal." Pale, semi-translucent mats of mycelial weave spread across every horizontal surface, creeping up walls and across ceilings in sheets an inch thick, threaded with veins of bioluminescent amber that pulse in slow, arrhythmic waves. These mats are structural. They bindite calciumite particles together. They redistribute moisture. They harden where they're stressed and soften where they're not. Underfoot, the crunch of boots breaking through a crust of mineral and living tissue is the Flats' most constant small-scale sound — a thousand tiny fractures per step.

Above the mats, everything else has been claimed. The infrastructure of the Flats — and it is infrastructure, though "built" is too simple a word — rises in layers. The lowest stratum is a lattice ofiteiteiteite and ceramic composites that looks geological until you notice its regularity: hexagonal columns, three meters across at the base, rising two or three meters before branching into smaller subdivisions, like a city's drainage system designed by someone who thought in terms of root systems. These columns are ancient. They show erosion patterns — smoothing at the joints, mineral deposits in the creases, fungal mats colonizing every sheltered surface — but they are not abandoned. They hum. Deep in each column, something vibrates at a frequency you feel more than hear, a sustained middle-C drone that the Flats use as a baseline for everything else.

Between the columns, the space is packed. Not empty channels or corridors — packed. Conduits of varying diameter thread through in every direction, some carrying water, some carrying something thicker and darker that moves with a sluggish deliberateness, some carrying light: fiber-optic threads so dense they hang in curtains, casting shifting moiré patterns across the fungal mats. The walls between conduits are alive — not metaphorically. Neural-ganglia clusters the size of fists sit at every junction, processing, rerouting. Occasionally one pulses a sharp blue-white and the sound changes pitch. The fauna has learned this. The larger creatures time their movements to the pulses, riding the brief sonic shifts the way birds ride thermals.

And the fauna. They are everywhere. The low forms are arthropod-scale — six-legged, chitinous, ranging from pale translucent shrimp-things that graze the fungal mats with mandible-combs to beetle-sized creatures with carapaces ofiteiteite that they shed and re-ingest, recycling their own shells for the calcium. These are not unusual. They are the background noise, the source of most of that constant clicking — thousands of tiny legs on mineral, the scritch-scratch of feeding, the sudden scatter-sound when something larger moves nearby.

The larger things are where the Flats get dangerous, or beautiful, or both, depending on your angle. There are amphibian-scale creatures, three or four meters long, with bodies that look like inflatable bladders covered inite mesh. They drift through the lower corridors on currents of their own making — they expel water from pores along their flanks in rhythmic jets, maneuvering with a slow, deliberate grace that reminds you of ships. They feed on the medium-dark sludge in the conduits, filtering it through baleen-like structures in their throats that vibrate audibly — a deep, wet thrumming that harmonizes with the columns. A group of five passing overhead sounds like a cathedral organ warming up.

Higher up, in the canopy of conduits and fungal curtains, there are things that fly — or more accurately, things that fall in controlled directions, membrane-winged shapes that navigate by echolocation clicks so loud they register as physical taps on your skin. They hunt the smaller arthropods in bursts of shrieking motion, and the sound of a hunting-flock passing through a conduit-cluster is one of the few things in the Flats that might make you duck for cover. Not because they'll attack you. They won't. But the sound is overwhelming, a wall of broadband noise that wipes out thought.

The scale of the place is what unmakes you. TheFlats are not a ruin. They are not abandoned. They are not even, strictly, a city — though they function as one on a continental scale. Every surface is occupied. Every conduit is in use. Every column is vibrating. The infrastructure has been here so long that it has become geology, and the geology has been colonized so thoroughly that it has become infrastructure again, and the loop has closed. Walking through the Flats is less like exploring a place than like being processed by one. You are warm, damp, loud, alive, and packed in on all sides by something that was here before you and will be here after, indifferent to your presence but not hostile — simply too engaged in its own ten-thousand-layered metabolism to notice you as anything other than a brief disturbance in an ongoing process.

The rain comes from below. Not from clouds — the upper atmosphere of the basin is hazy and still, a lid of warm vapor that rarely clears. The rain condenses on the underside of the conduit canopy, gathers on the fungal mats, and weeps downward in a continuous drizzle that keeps everything slick and dripping. At night — or what passes for night when the bioluminescent mats dim to a low, coal-red glow and the fiber-optic curtains shift to cooler blues — the rain sounds different. Quieter individually, but collectively louder, a billion small impacts on mineral and chitin and ceramic, a sound like static, like the whole Flats is trying to tell you something in a language made entirely of water.

You learn quickly not to stand still. The Flats fill in around you. A mat will begin growing on your boot within an hour. A conduit will shift to route around your body within a day. Within a week, if you stayed, you would be part of the infrastructure — warm, saturated, humming, home.
