## The High Verdance

---

You would call it a plateau if plateaus were verbs instead of nouns — a place that is still *becoming*, still pushing itself upward out of the planet's body with the slow violence of a birth that hasn't decided whether to finish. The elevation is immense: a continental shelf of rock and root and steam suspended kilometers above the surrounding lowlands, visible from impossibly far away as a dark green wall against the sky's pale ceiling. But you cannot see it from far away for long, because the air thickens as you approach, and the haze — that permanent, amber-dim haze — swallows distance whole.

It is warm here. It should not be warm. At this height the air should be thin and bitten and blue with cold, but the crust beneath your feet is thin too, and the mantle presses close. Heat bleeds up through every fissure, every crack in the fresh-cut stone. The rock is young — striated in blacks and iron-reds, still cooling from whatever tectonic rage heaved it into the sky. In places you can press your hand to a cliff face and feel the warmth of it, almost uncomfortable, as though the mountain remembers being liquid. Steam vents open and close without schedule: a hiss from a fissure in the morning, a pillar of white vapor by midday that collapses into mist by evening, gone by morning. The water that comes from these vents feeds everything and nothing — pooling in basalt bowls, sinking into porous stone, resurfacing a kilometer away in warm springs that run briefly downhill before the ground shifts and they find a new course.

*Damp.* That is the word, but it is inadequate. It is damp the way a living body is damp — not wet so much as permeable. Condensation beads on every surface overnight and stays through the morning, turning the rock into something that glistens black and green. The air holds moisture like a cloth holds dye, never fully dry, never fully soaked. Rain comes — you can hear it approaching from miles away, a low tonal wash that rises and falls — but it passes through certain zones and not others, so you can stand in dry air and watch rain fall half a kilometer away, curtains of water hanging in midair with no wind to bend them. The boundaries are sharp. Inside a rain-curtain, everything is loud and dripping and alive. Outside it, silence and dry stone.

The vegetation has colonized this youth with ferocity. The ground, where it exists — in the crevices between rock slabs, on the thin skin of soil that has accumulated in sheltered depressions over mere decades — is *dense*. Not the polite density of a managed forest but a layered, grasping, near-solid mass of life. Mosses the texture of wet velvet coat every surface that holds still long enough. Ferns unfurl from cracks in rock faces, their fronds overlapping into continuous green canopies that hang in the air like curtains. Root systems are visible, exposed, gripping stone with a tenacity that looks almost intentional — and given the weirdness of this place, perhaps they are. Trees grow here, but not like trees you'd recognize from lower elevations: their trunks are short and thick, branched low, their canopies wide and flat, layered upon one another so that the forest floor exists in a permanent dim green twilight beneath three or four continuous layers of foliage. Climbing plants thread through everything, binding tree to tree to rock face to cliff edge into a single continuous organism of cellulose and water. The saturation is so high that the air itself seems green — you breathe in chlorophyll and moisture and something mineral and warm.

Fauna is everywhere and nowhere, present in numbers that surprise you given how quiet the place is. Large animals move through the lower vegetation like shapes seen through frosted glass — the suggestion of bulk, a slow displacement of fern and vine, a breath sound, and then gone. They are silent, or nearly so; the largest creatures communicate in subsonic pulses you feel in your sternum rather than hear. Smaller animals are more visible — colonies of six-legged things that scuttle across rock faces, iridescent, reflexively climbing as surfaces shift beneath them. Birds of a kind: membrane-winged gliders that ride the thermals rising from the geothermal vents, circling in slow patient loops, uttering single notes at long intervals that hang in the thick air and do not decay. The density of life is such that you rarely see bare ground. Something is always moving underfoot, in the moss, in the soil — a rustle, a vibration, a brief scuttle and silence.

---

And then there are the machines.

They are scattered across the plateau with no apparent logic — no grid, no corridor, no settlement. Just *presence*, like stones. A massive piston assembly, open to the sky, its cylinder rising and falling with metronomic regularity, driven by steam from a vent at its base. It has been here long enough for moss to colonize its housing and for vines to thread through its linkage arms, but it has not stopped. It has not slowed. It drives a pump — you can see the pump, a hundred meters away, connected by a pipe that disappears underground — and the pump moves water from a warm spring to a place where no water gathers, where the pipe simply ends, open, dripping into a fern bed that grows more densely than anywhere else on the plateau. Whether this matters, no one can say. There is no one to say.

A flywheel, three meters across, turns slowly in the open air beside a cliff edge. Nothing connects to it. It has no visible load. It just turns. Its surface is scored with markings that might be functional — measurement, calibration, instruction — or might be erosion patterns that merely resemble language. Nearby, a conveyor belt runs at ankle height, carrying dark mineral fragments uphill for no destination. The belt is old — the material of it is cracked, patched, knotted — but it functions, driven by a motor housed in an open iron frame. Rain falls on it. Roots grow over it. It carries its stones.

There is no architecture. No walls, no roofs, no enclosed spaces. Only machines, exposed, operating in open air as though the concept of shelter never occurred to whatever intelligence — human, post-human, or otherwise — deployed them. Or as though it deployed them so long ago that the buildings have eroded away and only the mechanized hearts remain, too robust, too well-founded in the bedrock, to have been claimed by the young geology that is still settling.

---

The quiet is not peaceful. It is the quiet of density — too many presences in too small a space, each one muffling the others, the sound absorbed by vegetation and warm air and the sheer mass of life pressing against itself. When something does make a sharp sound — a branch falling, a rock breaking free from a cliff face, the sudden exhalation of a steam vent — it does not echo. It stops. The landscape absorbs it. The silence that follows feels thicker than the silence before.

The soundscape is mainly geological: the slow creak of rock under pressure, the hiss and gurgle of water finding new paths, the distant tonal hum of the machines. Wind exists but is unreliable — it comes in gusts from no discernible direction, warm and heavy with moisture, and dies in seconds. In the deep quiet between gusts, you can hear the plateau settling. It sounds like a lung emptying.

---

And here is where the place becomes something else.

The reality breakdown is not theatrical. It does not announce itself with spectacle. It is a wrongness that accumulates slowly in your perception, like a note played slightly flat that you can't identify but makes your chest ache. 

Gravity here is *suggestive*. The broad plateau surface holds you — mostly. But at the edges, at the rims of cliffs and the crests of knife-ridge formations where the erosion is freshest, you feel it: a slackening. Pebbles do not always roll downhill. Some sit at angles that should be impossible, resting on surfaces they have no friction with, until they don't, and suddenly they slide *upward*, briefly, before falling. Water, in certain channels carved into the rock, runs the wrong way for a stretch before correcting itself, as though the stream remembers a different gradient than the one the mountain currently holds. You pour water from a canteen and it hesitates — just a flicker — before falling. You tell yourself it was the wind. It was not the wind.

Time is the deeper problem. Walking across the plateau, you notice that your shadow sometimes leads you. Not by much. A half-step. A flicker of offset, as though the light reaching you from the dim sky above arrived a fraction of a second after the light that casts your shadow — as though two different moments are overlapping and the boundary between them is thinner than you'd like. In certain places — more often near the steam vents, near the machines — the overlap thickens. You see a rock where a rock is not. You step forward and the rock is new, freshly broken, still warm, but your eyes already carry an image of it weathered and moss-covered, and both images coexist for a disorienting moment before one of them fades. The landscape is rendering itself in multiple tenses simultaneously. The erosion of the cliff face you're looking at is happening now, but you can *see* the erosion that will happen, a ghost-image of the same cliff forty years from now overlaid on its current sharpness, and the two do not reconcile.

The scale compounds it. This plateau is continental. Vast enough to generate its own weather — and it does, but the weather is not fully committed to this reality. Storm cells form that rain in one location and snow in another, and the locations are adjacent, the boundary between rain and snow a sharp line across which the temperature does not change. Lightning arcs not downward but *laterally*, across the underside of cloud layers, illuminating the plateau's underside in flashes that reveal — briefly — a topography that does not match the one above. An inverted landscape, a mirror, a possible world. Then it's gone and you're looking at the real cliffs again, and you can't be sure the real cliffs are the ones you were standing on a moment ago.

The turbulence is constant. Not violent — not usually — but *active*. The ground shifts. Not earthquakes, not tectonic movement, but a slow churning, as though the plateau is breathing. Cracks open and close. A path you walked yesterday is today blocked by a slab of fresh stone that was not there, still warm, still sweating geothermal moisture. A gully you skirted is now a smooth slope. The landscape is editing itself. The machines continue their work regardless. The fauna continue theirs. The vegetation fills in the gaps with an urgency that feels almost aware — roots reaching for new soil, seeds germinating in stone that was solid an hour ago.

This is a place that is building itself while it is still forming, colonized by life that is older than the ground it grows on, threaded through with mechanisms whose purpose has been forgotten by any intelligence that might have understood them, all of it suspended in a state of ontological uncertainty — real enough to walk on, warm enough to live in, solid enough to build on if you dared to build, but fundamentally, quietly, *continuously* unsure of what it is.

You stand on a ridge. The haze extends in every direction to a horizon that is close enough to touch and far enough to contain a continent. Below you, a gorge is forming — its walls fresh, its floor a river of loose stone that rearranges itself audibly, a constant dry rattle like dice in a cup. Steam rises from the gorge's depths, warm and dim-lit, carrying the smell of sulfur and green growth. A machine — something with rotating arms, something that looks like it was meant to measure or to cut — stands at the gorge's edge, half-swallowed by ferns, its motion steady and purposeless and certain. A glider passes overhead, casting a shadow that arrives a half-second before the shape that casts it.

The rock beneath your feet is warm. It hums faintly. The haze closes in. The plateau continues to make itself, in every direction, without end.
