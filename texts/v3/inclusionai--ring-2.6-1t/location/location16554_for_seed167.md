## The Drowned Quarter

You hear it before you see it — or rather, you feel it before you hear it. A low, subsonic pulse that moves through the water around your legs, through the stone beneath your feet, up through the column of your body and into the back of your teeth. It is not mechanical. It is not geological. It comes from everywhere and from something alive.

The city — if that is what it is — extends past the edges of what you can perceive from any single vantage point. From the central arch of what was once perhaps a market hall, you can see colonnades disappearing into grey-green haze, archways opening onto other archways, tiers of balconies and ledges receding into a darkness that is not absolute but close to it. The sky, when you can glimpse it through the lattice of crumbling stonework and the heavy webbing of aerial roots, is a pale amber membrane stretched low, too low, pressing down with warm moisture. There is no sun you can point to. There is only a general luminosity that suffuses the atmosphere like light passing through old resin, and it seems to come from the combination of a hidden sun overhead and something else — something biotic — threading through the fog.

The water does not behave like ocean water. It is not salt. It is not brine. It is warm the way a living body is warm — consistently, almost disturbingly, around the temperature of blood. It rises and falls in long, slow oscillations that take no notice of the architecture they inhabit. At the moment the water stands at mid-thigh on the lowest step of the market hall's entrance, but you know from the tidelines staining the walls — pale mineral rings, colonies of small pale sessile creatures — that it will rise another two feet before the cycle turns. Everything here is tideline. Everything here has been marked by water and adapted to water and accepted water, the way a long-married couple accepts the snoring in the dark.

The stone of the buildings is a dark limestone, nearly black when dry, but nothing here is dry. Centuries — perhaps millennia — of warm water have softened every edge. Corners are blunted. Carved reliefs have become suggestions. The script that once ran in bands across the facades has been reduced to a texture, a dimpling of the surface that might be decorative or might be erosion or might, if you pressed your palm against it and closed your eyes, be something closer to a record. The erosion here is not violent. It is patient and total. The water has had time. The water has had more time than you can imagine, and it has used it all to round, to polish, to dissolve selectively, leaving harder nodules protruding from softer matrix like teeth from a gum.

And the life — the life is what makes this place unlike anything else.

Vegetation, as you would recognize it, is nearly absent. There are no trees. There are no grasses. What passes for plant life here is a thin, dark, velvet-like coating that appears on any surface that has been submerged for more than a few weeks — a sort of algal felt, black-green, no more than a millimeter thick, that photosynthesizes with what little light filters down and gives off, in certain conditions, a faint phosphorescent glow. This glow is the source of the ambient luminescence. On a still night — if there is such a thing as night here — the entire submerged quarter shines with a dim, sourceless chartreuse light, as if the city itself were dreaming.

There are also, growing from the upper reaches of walls and archways where spray and mist can reach, thin gray tendrils — not quite vine, not quite fungus, not quite root. They hang in curtains, sometimes thirty feet long, and they do not move in the water but they move when the air touches them, slow and deliberate, like fingers exploring. The locals — if there are locals; you have not yet determined this — call them the hanging veins, and they are said to be older than the buildings themselves, older than the flood, older than memory. They do not photosynthesize. They feed on the humidity and on the small insects that shelter among them.

And the insects. The insects.

They are everywhere. Not biting insects, not hostile insects — just present, in numbers that redefine the concept of abundant. Winged insects with iridescent shells the size of your thumbnail that cluster on every wet surface in pulsing, breathing clouds. They do not scatter when you move through them. They barely notice you. They are engaged in their own purposes — mating, feeding on the algal felt, depositing eggs in the hairline cracks of the stonework. Their collective hum is the loudest sound in the quarter, and yet it is still quiet, because it is so constant it becomes a condition rather than an event. It is the baseline note of the place, the drone against which all other sounds are measured.

The other fauna is stranger.

There are eel-things — you can only call them that for lack of a better word — that move through the submerged streets in loose, unhurried formations. They are perhaps four feet long, pale, with segmented bodies that flex in an S-motion through the water. They have no visible eyes. They have, instead, a fan of fleshy filaments along their flanks that seem to sense vibration and pressure. They feed on the insects that fall to the water's surface, and on the algal felt, and apparently on each other, though you have not witnessed predation — only the sudden and unexplained absence of one individual from a group, and the slightly larger girth of its nearest neighbor.

Higher up, in the air-filled chambers and dry balconies above the waterline, there are colonies of small, warm-bodied creatures — part bat, part something else. They have broad, leathery wings but also long, dexterous forelimbs with fingers that grip the stonework. They nest in the upper rooms of the buildings, in the hollow spaces behind walls where the water cannot reach. They are silent fliers. They leave the roosts at the dim-light hours and return with their bellies full of the winged insects from below. They have large eyes, reflective and golden, and they watch you from the dark cavities of doorways as you pass.

And then there are the builders.

This is the part that unsettles you. This is the source of the weirdness, the 0.9 that you felt but could not name when you first arrived. The architecture of this quarter is not entirely the architecture it started as. There have been additions. Not recent ones — nothing so clean. But over the long centuries, the fauna has built. The eel-things, or their ancestors, have cemented shells and sediment and dead insects into structures along the lower walls — buttresses, almost, or reinforcement ribs, following the stress fractures in the original stonework. The hanging veins have grown into gaps and expanded them, creating new channels, new chambers. The bat-things have carried sediment andite andite and compacted it into shelf-like extensions of the upper walls. No single organism intended what has resulted, but the result is something that looks almost designed — as if the city has grown a second skin, a biological exoskeleton, and the original builders' work is now a skeleton within a skeleton, an inner scaffolding that supports layers of living accretion.

You can see this most clearly in the central square — if it can be called a square, as it is no longer open to the sky but partially roofed by a vast interweaving of hanging veins and calcium deposits. The original paving stones are still there, but they are raised nearly two feet above the current waterline by a platform of consolidated sediment, shell fragments, and pale root-like growths that have fused into something as hard asite. At the center of the square is a fountain — or what was once a fountain. The basin is choked with algal felt. The statue that once stood in it, if there was one, has been reduced to a smooth mound protruding from the felt, featureless and dark, something like a head, something like a capstone, something like a seed that has not yet decided what to grow into.

The rules of this place are not the rules you are used to.

Here, the border between architecture and organism is not a metaphor. It is a fact of structure. Load-bearing walls are partly stone, partlyite. Stairways have been partially resorbed by the hanging veins and partially reinforced by theiteiteite of the bat-things. You cannot say where the city ends and the reef begins.

Here, darkness is not absence. It is a medium, a substance, something that has weight and texture. The air is thick with it — warm, saturated, faintly phosphorescent. You can see your hand in front of your face, but barely. Shapes resolve themselves slowly. Edges are uncertain. You find yourself trusting sound more than sight, smell more than either.

Here, time is not linear. It is layered, the way the deposits are layered — each stratum a record of a particular equilibrium between the water and the stone and the life that colonizes both. You can, if you know how to read it, taste the difference between a century of high water and a century of low water in the mineral deposits on a wall. The place remembers everything and expresses nothing in a way that is immediately legible.

And here, you are not alone, but you are not among your kind. The fauna does not fear you. The fauna does not welcome you. You are simply a new presence in an established system, and the system will absorb you the way it has absorbed everything else — slowly, without malice, without intention, the way a city absorbs rain.

You step down from the fountain's rim into the shallow water. The eel-things part around you with no haste, no alarm. The water closes behind you. The algal felt glows faintly under your passage, leaving a brief green afterimage in the dark, like a footprint that remembers being a foot.

Somewhere above, the bat-things shift on their perches. Somewhere below, the eel-things resume their circuit. The hanging veins sway in a draft you cannot feel. The insects drone on.

The city breathes.

It has been breathing for a very long time.
