**The Warm Seam**

You enter through a cut in the rock face that should not be as warm as it is. The air meets you before you cross the threshold — heavy, mineral-wet, carrying a sweet chemical smell like iron filings left in rain. Not unpleasant. Not quite right either.

The cut is recent. You can tell because nothing has had time to settle. The walls are raw — freshly exposed basite andite with the fracture patterns still crisp, no glaze of moisture-wicking mineral bloom, no smoothing from long water. The rock looks almost angry in its newness, surfaces catching what little light there is in edges and ridges. Where the ceiling has been shored, you see old timber — not rotted, not yet, but darkening, bowing under the weight of the mountain above. The bolts are rusted in bloom patterns that look almost decorative. Almost.

There is a room here. Not natural — too regular. Sixty meters across, maybe thirty wide, with a vaulted ceiling that recalls a ribcage. The proportions are almost liturgical. Someone dug this deliberately, or found it and cleared it, and the effort involved is just distant enough to feel absurd. The scale is intimate enough that you could nearly touch both walls if you stretched your arms. Which you would not want to do. The walls sweat.

The heat builds slowly. At the entrance it is merely warm, a dampness on the skin, the beginning of condensation beading on exposed metal. Deeper in, the air thickens. It does not feel dangerous — the temperature rises in such gentle increments that your body adjusts before it protests. But it is hot down here. Consistently, deeply hot, the kind of heat that has a texture to it, that sits on you like a second skin. The rock itself seems to be breathing warmth outward, and the air carries it everywhere with a sluggish patience, so that even the slightest movement sends a convection current rising toward the vault above.

And the water. The water is everywhere but never in the way you expect. It does not pool so much as present itself — a thin film on every horizontal surface, condensation dripping in slow metronome from joints in the ceiling, occasionally a vein of moisture weeping down a wall in a line so fine it looks drawn with a brush. There is a stream, barely more than a suggestion, cutting a shallow channel across the floor's lowest point. It is warm to the touch. It smells faintly of sulfur and something else — something vegetal, almost sweet, like overripe melon rind. The water moves with no urgency at all.

What strikes you most, after the heat and the dark, is the green.

It starts as a suggestion — a darkening of the rock near the waterline, a softening of the mineral grey. Then the mosses. They colonize the walls in patches at first, then in great slow migrations, creeping along joints and seams, filling the concavities left by old anchor points. The moss is thick and deep emerald, almost black in the low light, and it is warm to the touch. Some of it is tall — finger-length in places where conditions favor it, brushing against your hand as you pass. The texture is not quite right. It is softer than moss should be, yielding like velvet, and when you look closely — though you have to bring your eye very close in this light — you notice that the individual filaments are translucent at their tips, backlit by some bioluminescence so faint you might be imagining it. A suggestion of greenish-gold light, gone the moment you try to focus.

There are larger plants. Not the mosses. Something else.

They grow from crevices in the walls and from cracks in the floor, thin stalks — pale, almost white — that rise to an even height of about a meter and a half before unfurling a single broad leaf each. The leaves are dark, almost black, and they hang downward like fingers, catching the condensate that runs along the walls. The stalks are warm. They are mildly warm to the touch, and when you press one gently it gives like rubber, then slowly returns. They have no flowers. They make no sound. They exist with a stillness so complete it feels architectural.

You will later try to describe these plants to someone and realize you have no language for them. They are not ferns. They are not fungi. They look, if you are being honest, like something drawn from a memory of a plant rather than from direct observation — the idea of a plant, executed in a material you do not recognize.

The industrial remnants are scattered. Not ruin, exactly — too little ruin for that. A generator the size of a wardrobe sits against the far wall, its casing intact but its surface colonized by a fine tracery of something that might be mineral deposit or might be growth. Its cables trail off into the dark and do not connect to anything. A short length of rail, standard gauge, pokes out of the floor at an angle and vanishes into the rock after a meter as if the track were laid and then the wall closed around it. A wooden crate, collapsed, its nails oxidizing into filaments of rust that look like tiny roots. A fire extinguisher, still red, still legible, mounted on a bracket that has been slowly swallowed by a thickening of the moss. There is no soot. There is no ash. Whatever work was done here left its tools behind and left cleanly.

The silence is the most notable thing. Not an absence of sound — there is sound here — but a quality of sound so reduced that each element becomes distinct and strange. The dripping of water, irregular and unhurried. A faint, subsonic hum that you feel more in the chest than in the ears, as though the rock itself were operating at a frequency just below perception. The occasional creak of timber, the slow settlement of the mountain's weight expressing itself in groans so low and so widely spaced that you forget you heard them and then hear another and it feels new again.

There are no animals here. You understand this before you confirm it — the space simply does not accommodate the quick, the skittish, the small-and-moving. Everything in this room is slow. Everything is rooted or still. The air does not carry scent from anything living. The warmth does not seem metabolically produced. Whatever process sustains the green, the warmth, the faint subsonic thrum, it does not involve respiration in any recognizable sense.

You are not afraid. Not exactly. But you are aware of a subtle wrongness — like a note in a chord that is almost in key but not quite. The proportions of the room are a little too convenient. The warmth is a little too uniform. The plants hang at exactly the same angle, as if responding to a gravity that is not quite the same as the one above. The far wall has a quality — you cannot say what — that makes you feel as though it is closer than it should be. As though the room is slightly smaller than the measurements would suggest. You step along the perimeter and count your steps and the count is sensible and the count is wrong.

Nothing changes while you are here. You stand for ten minutes. The same drops fall. The same filaments glow at the margins of your vision and retreat when you look. The warmth holds. The silence holds. The room holds. But you have the strong sense, which you cannot justify, that the room has been more still than usual. That your presence has introduced a perturbation — small, local, temporary — into a system that was, until a moment ago, perfectly patient in its slowness. And that it will return to that patience when you leave, with the slow and thorough indifference of something that has been warm and dark and green for longer than you can imagine, and will continue so for longer than you can imagine after the last timber collapses and the last rusted bolt dissolves back into the seam.

The space does not end. It merely stops being accessible — the passage narrowing to a crack too tight for your shoulders, then for your hips, then for light at all. But you can hear, or believe you can hear, the hum continuing past the point where you can go. Steady. Thermal. Patient as stone. Patient as something that learned patience from stone and then decided stone was too hasty.

You back out. Behind you, the moss closes the gap you made when you entered, slowly, over hours, over days, over a duration you cannot hold in your mind. The cut heals. The warmth remains. The green darkens.

The seam holds.
