# The Sediment Kingdoms

The first thing you understand, standing at the threshold and looking in, is that nothing here was ever young.

The cavern opens slowly — not like a door, not like a mouth, but like a word you've forgotten the ending of. The ceiling withdraws upward into a darkness that never clarifies. The floor, when you step onto it, barely gives. It is dense with the accumulated patience of ages: a floor of compressed biologicalite, the mineral bones of ten thousand calcified growth-cycles pressed into something almost like tile, almost like marble, but warm to the touch and faintly ridged with channels that were once vascular systems. Veins of old plumbing. The circulatory system of a place that outlived its heart.

There is no water here. Not a drop. The air tastes of copper and chalk and something faintly sweet, like the inside of a seed pod cracked open and left to dry in a pocket for a century. Moisture is a concept that does not exist in this cavern, and yet the vegetation is — the only word is — lush.

It rises in clusters and drifts, in continent-like shelves across the undulating floor. Not green. Nothing here is green. The dominant palette is umber, ochre, pale violet, and a bruised amber that glows faintly in the dimness, as though the plants remember a light that once existed here and are still trying to reproduce its color. The fronds are thick and dry, not wilting but *comfortable* in their stillness, broad leaves of a material that feels more like hide than leaf, matte and slightly tacky to the touch. Root-masses spread across the ground in vast interlocking mats — you can see them where the floor-thin mineral crust has cracked — and they extend laterally for what must be hundreds of meters, connecting one growth-cluster to the next in a network so dense and so old that it has become a geological stratum in its own right.

Trees — if they are trees — stand at irregular intervals, their trunks heavy and columnar, wrapped in bark that flakes into a fine ochre powder. They do not taper. They do not branch until very high up, where the dim light filters down from fissures in the ceiling you cannot see from the ground. Then they explode into broad, flat canopies of overlapping leaves, arranged not in spirals or alternation but in dense tessellations, like scales on a fish, each one angled to catch whatever faint phosphorescence descends from above. Their roots have merged with their neighbors so thoroughly that you cannot tell where one organism ends and another begins. They are, in every meaningful sense, a single city of trunks.

The urbanism of this place is not imposed on the landscape. It *is* the landscape. Clusters of the larger growths have been hollowed — not cut, not carved, but hollowed by the slow accumulation of decades of animal habitation, of root-growth pulling chambers open, of biological acid eating through calcified walls until what remains is a warren of interconnected spaces: rooms, corridors, plazas, archways. The architecture is organic and yet unmistakably civic. There are clear sight-lines between open spaces. There are gathering places where the floor has been worn flat by the passage of thousands of bodies over thousands of years. There are structures that rise in tiered platforms — grown, not stacked — with ledges and shelves and recesses that serve, by their shape and placement, as walls, as roofs, as stairways. Nothing was designed. Everything arrived at organization through the sheer pressure of time and use.

The fauna is everywhere, and it is loud.

That is the first assault: the sound. It does not build. It does not approach. It is simply *present*, the way heat is present in a desert — total, immersive, inescapable. The cavern acoustics are catastrophic in their generosity. Every call, every wingbeat, every dragging footfall, every trill and bark and subsonic thrum reverberates off the ancient walls and returns multiplied, layered, harmonized into a wall of noise so rich and dense that after a few minutes your brain stops trying to parse individual sounds and simply accepts the roar as a condition of existence, like gravity.

There are creatures the size of your hand — six-legged, glossy-shelled, with antennae that sweep the air in rapid arcs — that produce a high, crystalline clicking, thousands of them at once, a sound like glass beads being shaken in a tin. Larger animals move through the frond-canopies with a slow, deliberate grace, their bodies long and multi-segmented, their limbs hooking into the tessellated leaves with a soft *schk-schk-schk* that accumulates into a rain-like patter. Somewhere deeper in the cavern, something enormous breathes. You hear it not as a single exhalation but as a rhythm, a tidal pull, a slow bellows-sound that moves air through the corridors and makes the frond-canopies sway in unison. The dry leaves rasp against each other. The sound is not unlike applause heard from a great distance.

There are flyers — many flyers — with membrane wings that catch the faint upwellings and spiral upward in slow, widening gyres. Their calls are low and throaty, almost musical, and they echo in the cavern's vaults until they overlap and become a drone, a chord, a tonal center around which all the other sounds seem to organize. You begin, without meaning to, to hear the soundscape as music. It is not music. It is ten thousand organisms each pursuing its own imperative, and the room they share is so large and so geometrically regular in its erosion-smoothed walls that it turns their noise into something with structure. You feel it in your sternum before you understand it with your ears.

The light is low and diffuse. It comes, as near as you can tell, from three sources: the faint amber bioluminescence of certain fungal mats that line the upper walls and ceiling fissures, throwing a sickly but steady glow that makes everything look as though it's been dipped in old resin; the phosphorescent tips of certain fronds, which have evolved to concentrate whatever photons descend from the distant surface-fissures into pinpoints of pale greenish-white; and the slow, occasional pulse of color from colonies of tiny organisms that cling to the wet-feeling (but not wet — there is no wet here) surfaces of the oldest growths, cycling through shades of violet and blue in rhythms so long you cannot tell if they are responding to time-of-day, time-of-season, or time-of-century.

Everything moves slowly. Not lazily. Not without purpose. Slowly the way a river moves slowly when you watch it from high enough up — the sense of motion is there, the certainty of direction, but the moment you look, it seems almost still. A creature crosses your field of vision and takes twenty seconds to pass. A frond unfurls — you can only tell you're watching it unfold because you remember its position a minute ago. A fungal glow brightens, holds, dims, over the course of a breath. The entire ecosystem operates on a timescale that makes patience feel like a frenzy.

The structures — the civic, the architectural, the genuinely urban quality of this place — begin to make sense the longer you stay. The walls of the larger chambers are textured with generations of claw-marks, nesting hollows, chewed mineral-deposits, and the smooth, oil-dark polish of bodies brushing against stone over and over and over again. There are pathways — not all of them, but some — where the vegetation has been worn into corridors by the habitual passage of fauna, the root-mat compressed into something firm and almost geometric. There are places where the architecture of growth and the architecture of habitation have become indistinguishable: a natural arch formed by the fused roots of two trees serves as the entrance to a chamber whose walls are pocked with nesting hollows, whose floor is a thick carpet of shed husks and dried organic matter, whose ceiling resonates with the calls of the colony that lives there.

This is not a ruin. It has not fallen into disrepair. It is something more unsettling: it is complete. It has reached its final form. The erosion you see — the smoothing, the rounding, the way every edge has been softened into a suggestion of what it once was — is not decay. It is the settled equilibrium of a system that has been running for so long that it has worn itself into a perfect, frictionless stasis. Nothing is collapsing. Nothing is growing. Everything is *maintaining*. The vegetation holds its territory. The fauna holds its patterns. The architecture stands because the organisms that live in it maintain it the way a body maintains a bone — slowly, continuously, without thought.

You will hear them before you see them, always. The sound precedes everything. It rises from the floor, descends from the ceiling, arrives from directions you cannot name. It is the sound of a place that has been alive for longer than anyone can measure, and it does not care whether you are listening.

It never stops.
