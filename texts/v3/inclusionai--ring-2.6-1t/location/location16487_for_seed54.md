## The Caermarn

You hear it before you know you've arrived.

Sound comes first as pressure — a thickening of the air against the chest, a granular hum that isn't one voice or one engine or one wind but all three folded together and then folded again. By the time your ears have sorted the first layer, two more have stacked on top. There is a low register that might be geological — the groan of subsurface ice shifting under mineral load, or machinery the size of watersheds cycling through a phase too slow to see but easy to hear. There is a mid-register that is alive: clicking, trilling, a buzz-saw whir of chitin-on-stone, thousands of overlapping calls that share no rhythm but somehow never collide. And there is a high, glassy overtone, almost tonal, almost music, that seems to come from the walls themselves — the infrastructure resonating with whatever passes through it.

There is no light.

Not darkness as you know it from a closed room or a night with cloud cover. This is a darkness that has weight. It was never the absence of something that was supposed to be there. The Caermarn has never had light in the way you mean. What luminescence exists is biological and industrial and indistinguishable: vein-networks of chemoluminescent mineral filament the color of a struck match head run through every vertical surface in threads so fine you only see them when the moisture catches them, and you don't see them so much as they briefly, for a half-second, introduce the idea of shape before the dark reasserts its claim.

You are standing on something. That much you know. The ground is not flat. It is flat in the way a hand is flat — broadly, with the understanding that there are knuckles and tendons beneath the surface. The terrain is a high plateau, or what was a high plateau before it was converted. Elevation enough that the cold has authority. The cold here is not hostile — it is administrative. It manages things. It keeps the moisture from evaporating and so the moisture stays, pooling in mineral basins, beading on every surface, running in thin films down constructed walls that rise at angles not quite natural and not quite engineered. The water is warm where it comes from the ground — mineral springs, iron-heavy, carrying dissolved compounds you cannot taste because your tongue refuses the temperature. Where the water meets the air it condenses into fog that never lifts, only thickens and thins in cycles too long to clock. Some channels carry water openly. Others run through the walls, visible through translucent panels of a material that is neither glass nor stone but holds light the way stone does and water the way glass does.

There is no vegetation. None. Not a single cell that photosynthesizes. The mineral world stands exposed — basalt,ite,ite, calcite crusts, volcanic glass ground to a matte black powder that serves as soil for nothing. What grows here is fungal, or simpler. Mat colonies of chemotrophic filament spread across wet surfaces in patches that are pale, almost translucent, almost pink, and they pulse — not rhythmically, but in response to chemical gradients so slow you'd swear they were dead until you watched long enough to see the color migrate. They are not plants. They are not even trying to be. They are processing. They dissolve mineral substrates and excrete new compounds that the infrastructure then harvests through capillary systems no wider than a hair.

The city — and it is a city, though the word feels provincial for something this distributed — occupies the entire plateau. It extends past the immediate in every direction, a regional sprawl of layered construction. There is no horizon line, only density. Structures rise in tiers, some geological, some fabricated, most both. The original rock has been scored, channeled, filled, reinforced, and then scored again by new infrastructure built on top of infrastructure built on top of infrastructure, so that the walls are palimpsests — you can read eras in the cross-sections where erosion or maintenance has exposed them. Lower layers are older and cruder: fitted stone, hydraulic channels cut by hand or by machines whose principles are no longer in use. Middle layers show a precision that approaches the crystalline — surfaces so smooth they seem grown, joints so tight they hold water in or out by molecular intention. Upper layers — what you can reach, what the fauna inhabit — are the most recent and the most alien. They are soft-walled. They curve. They breathe, slightly, expanding and contracting in cycles measured in hours, and the walls are threaded with tubing that carries water, waste, nutrients, data — you can no longer tell which.

The density is what strikes you most, after the sound. Everything is near-everything. Structures touch. Pathways overlap. There is no wasted space. Between two walls a meter apart, an entire ecology exists — the fungal mats, the mineral deposits, the fauna. And the fauna.

They are everywhere. Not scattered. Not occasional. *Everywhere.* The fauna of the Caermarn have filled every niche, and they have filled niches that didn't exist until they made them. They range from organisms the size of a pinhead — colonies that move as single decision-making masses across wet stone, leaving faint trails of dissolved mineral — to creatures large enough that you hear them before you feel the vibration of their movement through the floor. The large ones are rare, or perhaps just hidden. You catch glimpses: a limb, segmented and jointed and wrong in the way that too-many-joints is wrong, retracting around a corner. A carapace briefly lit from within by its own circulatory chemiluminescence sliding along a ceiling you didn't know was a ceiling until you saw the thing crawling across it. Most of the fauna are mid-sized — forearm-length to torso-length — armored or soft-bodied, social or solitary depending on the hour-cycle, vocal in ways that account for most of the sound.

They are not hostile. They are not friendly. They are present the way weather is present. You do not negotiate with weather. You learn its patterns.

Some of the fauna tend the infrastructure. You realize this slowly. The creatures are not parasites, not symbiotes in any comfortable sense. They *maintain*. They clean mineral deposits from filtration channels. They redistribute fungal colonies to surfaces where chemotrophic processing is most efficient. They carry materials — small pellets ofite, beads of resin-analogue, fragments ofite — to construction sites that may be centuries in the making. They do this not because they were designed to but because the ecology has reached a state where maintenance of the built environment *is* the ecological niche. The city is not a structure the fauna live in. The city is an organism the fauna *are*.

The technology that makes this possible — that *is* this, really — operates at scales you can perceive only by their effects. Nanoscale assemblers are embedded in the walls, the water, the bodies of the fauna themselves. They repair, restructure, redistribute matter at a pace so slow it registers as geology. A wall shifts by a millimeter over a decade. A channel widens over a century. A new room opens up inside the plateau and by the time anyone notices, it is already colonized, already populated, already part of the city. The tech does not build. It *tends*. It adjusts. It holds the place in a state of perpetual almost-finished, which is the only state a structure this complex can sustain.

The sound rises. A chorus. No — not a chorus, a chorus implies coordination. This is a cacophony held together by something harder to name than rhythm. It is the sound of a system that works not because every part agrees but because every part is necessary. The clicking of mandibles on stone. The gurgle of water through capillary networks. The low, subsonic groan of the plateau itself, settling under its own impossible weight. Somewhere far above — or far below, in the context of layered construction, the direction is a suggestion rather than a certainty — something large exhales, and the fog shifts, and for three seconds the air tastes ofite and deep mineral cold, and then it passes.

The Caermarn does not end. It extends, regionally, across the plateau and down into the substrate and up into whatever the atmosphere becomes above the fog. It has always been here in some form. It is always becoming a new form. The erosion is emergent — the landscape is still constructing itself, still deciding what it is, and the fauna are not its inhabitants so much as its argument with itself about what should exist next.

You stand in it. The cold settles into your joints. The sound enters you through the soles of your feet, through the walls of your chest, through the thin membrane between being-part-of and observing-from-outside, and you realize that line was never clean. The water beads on your clothing and does not evaporate. The dark hums with a life that has no sun, no green, no photosynthesis, no plants, and does not miss them.

It has replaced the need.

It has replaced almost everything.

But not the sound. The sound was always the truest thing here.
