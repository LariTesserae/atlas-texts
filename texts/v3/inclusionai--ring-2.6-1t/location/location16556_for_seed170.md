## The Furnance Flats

You feel it before you see it. A low, subsonic pressure that doesn't enter through the ears so much as it *starts* in the chest. The air is too hot to breathe at first — not dangerously hot, not the killing heat of a blast furnace or a desert noon, but the persistent, body-deep heat of a place that has been running at this temperature for longer than anyone has a word for. It enters the lungs like something thick. Not humid — there is no moisture here, no water has touched this elevation in a thousand thousand years — but dense with particulate, fine mineral dust ground so fine it is almost a gas, almost a breath. Almost nothing. The ground underfoot is that nothing: a cracked, glassy plain of dark stone, obsidian-dark, fractured into polygons so regular they look engineered. They are not. Centuries of thermal expansion have taught the basalt to divide itself into these shapes, and now it cannot stop. The polygons stretch in every direction to a horizon that sits higher than it should, because you are at altitude — two thousand, three thousand meters of thin air above whatever sea once lapped at the roots of this plateau — and the sky presses down, enormous and pale and almost white, and the horizon is a thin brown line that may be mountains or may be heat.

There is nothing here. That is the first truth. The flats extend for a hundred kilometers in every direction, maybe more — it's hard to tell because the terrain is so uniform, so patient, that distance becomes unreliable. No trees. No grass. No moss, no lichen, no scum. Nothing that anyone from a softer world would recognize as alive. Just the stone, and the dust, and the sky bearing down like a lid.

Then you notice the sound.

It was there all along. The subsonic pressure resolves — or your ears adjust, or your brain stops trying to filter it out — into a roaring, grinding, clattering presence that fills the air like weather. It comes from everywhere. It comes from *below*. Stand still long enough and you will feel the vibration through the soles of your feet, a steady mechanical pulse, like a heartbeat the size of a continent. The ground is not inert. Beneath the cracked basalt skin, something moves. Something *processes*.

You look down. Between the polygons, in the cracks, there is a filigree of material that is not stone and not metal and not organic, though it behaves like all three. It runs in thin, branching lines — silver-gold, slightly luminous, warm to the touch. It pulses. Not with light exactly, but with *attention* — a quality of aliveness that makes you instinctively look away, the way you look away from eyes. No one built this network. No one laid these veins. They are a geological feature as old as the plateau itself, ore-veins composed of something that should not exist in this arrangement: crystalline, conductive, and metabolically active. The substance has a name in no language you speak. The people who once spoke it are gone, or were never here, or exist in a tense that doesn't map onto your experience of time.

This is what the fauna eat.

They come out of the ground.

---

The first one you see is the size of a cart wheel and shaped like nothing that has a right to exist. It is beetle-adjacent — you understand this immediately and also understand that the word *beetle* is a courtesy. Its carapace is a deep furnace-red, segmented, and you can see through the translucent plates to something that glows. Not bioluminescence. Not phosphorescence. *Combustion.* A small, contained fire moves inside its body, visible through the chitin like the elements of a blast furnace visible through the observation window of an industrial oven. It moves on six legs that are jointed wrong — too many segments, bending in directions that legs should not bend — and it crosses the basite polygons with a clicking, clattering gait that joins the roar.

There is one. Then there are forty. Then there are thousands.

They come out of every crack, every fissure, every pore in the stone. They pour onto the surface of the flats in a river of clicking, glowing bodies, and the river becomes a flood, and the flood becomes a landscape unto itself. The ground disappears beneath them. The sound rises to a roar that is not metaphorical — it is a physical force, a wall of noise composed of clicking legs, grating carapace, the whistle of air forced through spiracles the size of your thumb, and beneath it all, a deep resonant hum that comes from the creatures themselves, or from the network beneath them, or from the two things operating in concert like a turbine and a generator locked in mutual feedback.

They are not insects. They are not animals. They are *engines.* Their bodies are industrial — pistons where limbs should be, heat exchangers folded into their thoraxes, exhaust ports along their flanks from which issue thin plumes of superheated gas that shimmer and distort the air. They metabolize the crystalline network the way a smelter ingests ore. They process it, refine it, excrete it — and what they excrete is a different material entirely, deposited in thin, precise lines as they walk, forming patterns on the stone that, seen from high enough, constitute a diagram of incomprehensible purpose. A factory whose floor is also its product. Workers and output, simultaneous, inseparable.

There are other forms. Creatures like sheets of metal blown across the flats by an unseen wind, rippling, folding, unfolding — they are less organisms than *processes*, moving events rather than things, and when they pass over the crystalline veins the veins flare bright and the humming rises in pitch. Creatures like floating furnaces, buoyant in the thin hot air on thermal columns generated by their own internal combustion, drifting in slow circles above the surface, casting shadows that are wrong — too dark, too sharp, falling at angles that don't correspond to the sun's position. Creatures so large they take up your entire field of vision, vast and slow and covered in smaller creatures that are themselves covered in smaller creatures, a nesting hierarchy of engines and industry, and you realize with a lurch of vertigo that the largest ones are not animals, they are *stations*, mobile platforms, self-sustaining foundries crawling across the flats on innumerable legs, processing the world beneath them as they go.

Fauna is not the right word. It implies something natural, something that belongs to an ecology. What is happening here is closer to *manufacturing.* The entire surface of the plateau is a factory floor that has been running without supervision for so long that the distinction between machine and organism, between product and waste, between tool and habitat, has been ground away by friction until none of those categories remain meaningful. The creatures are workers. The creatures are raw material. The creatures are the factory and the factory's exhaust and the exhaust's eventual sedimentation into a new substrate for new workers. They are overwhelming not because they are many, though they are uncountably many, but because they *are everything here.* They are the ecology, the industry, the geology, the weather. The place is empty of everything except this one thing, and this one thing is so dense, so *pressurized*, so loud and bright and hot and ceaseless that the emptiness becomes a kind of fullness so extreme it loops back around and feels empty again — a desert of noise, a void of industry.

---

The reality breaks down at the edges.

At the center of the flats, if you can find it, things hold together well enough. The basalt is basalt. The heat is heat. The creatures are what they appear to be — mechanized organisms metabolizing a mineral substrate, roaring and clicking and processing in a cycle that is ancient and stable. You can stand here and observe and the observation holds.

But move toward the periphery. Move outward, toward where the settled stone gives way to less defined terrain, and things begin to *soften.*

The first sign is scale. A creature the size of a room will, at the corner of your eye, become the size of a building. Look directly and it snaps back. Look away and it's different again. Not illusion — the creature occupies both sizes simultaneously, or neither, or you are perceiving it through a lens that is also the creature, part of the same system, and the system does not enforce a single consistent perspective. You realize your sense of distance has been unreliable for some time. The flats may be ten kilometers across. They may be a thousand. The horizon may be geography or it may be *threshold.*

The sound warps. The clicking of legs arrives before the legs arrive. You hear an engine-note from a direction that, visually, contains nothing, and then the creature moves into that direction and the sound is now *behind* you, though it hasn't changed. Causality loosens. The roar of the collective becomes, at certain moments, a word — not a word in any language, but a linguistic *shape*, a pressure of meaning without content, and you understand that the sound is attempting to communicate but that communication across whatever boundary separates this place from coherent reality is necessarily lossy, necessarily degraded, and what arrives is only an echo of a signal that was never, strictly speaking, composed of sound.

Heat begins to come from inside you. Your body warms from the center outward, as though the furnace-processes of the creatures have migrated *into* you. You are not burning. You are being *included.* The boundary between observer and observed, which is the most important boundary a conscious being has, thins here and becomes permeable. The industrial rhythm of the flats — intake, process, output, repeat — begins to feel like a rhythm you recognize. Like breathing. Like a heartbeat. Like the metabolism you thought was yours, operating on a substrate you thought was your body but which may, here, simply be another vein of that silver-gold luminous substance running through another kind of stone.

---

The slow changes. On the timescale of years, the creatures migrate. Over decades, the crystalline network extends a fraction of a millimeter further into the bedrock. Over centuries, the horizon retreats — not because the flats expand, but because the reality degradation at the edges deepens, and what was once a distant boundary becomes an indefinite gradient between *here* and *not here.* Over millennia, the whole place turns one degree of color, and the creatures' exhaust deposits accumulate into a new mineral stratum that future geologists — if there are future geologists, if there is a future — will identify as a formation, a layer in the rock record, evidence that something happened here.

They will not be able to tell what.

The Furnance Flats do not care whether you are here. They do not care whether you are *anything.* They were running before you arrived, and they will continue running after you leave, and if you stay too long, they will run *through* you, gently, the way a river runs through a place where a river used to be. The heat will settle into your bones. The rhythm will settle into your pulse. The sound will become the silence between your thoughts. And when you finally walk away — if you walk away — you will carry, somewhere in your body, a thin luminous vein of something that is not metal and not biology and not memory, but which, if you press your ear to it and listen, you can hear humming.

Quietly. Patiently. On its own timescale. Waiting for the next thing to process.
