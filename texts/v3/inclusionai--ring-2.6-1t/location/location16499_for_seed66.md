# The Caldera City of Séverin

---

You come up the switchback trail from the mid-altitude fog belt, and the plateau opens beneath you like a opened palm. It is hard to believe this is a city. It is hard to believe it is anything but geology. But then you see the edge — a vertical face of dark composite stone, sixty or seventy meters high, and across its face, rows of apertures so regular and so numerous that the rock looks like it has been perforated by a patient god. Warm air rises off it. You can feel the moisture on your face before you are even close.

Séverin is a city the way a reef is a city. It is built, and it is grown, and it has been weathered into itself so thoroughly that the word *built* starts to feel like an imposition. It sits on a high continental plateau — three thousand meters above the lowland basins to the east — and the plateau itself is roughly the size of a mid-sized nation: four hundred kilometers across at its widest, ringed by eroded escarpments that fall away into perpetual cloud. The plateau generates its own weather. Clouds form against its edges in the mornings, crawl inward by noon, and condense against the thermal updrafts rising from the city's mass by late afternoon, so that it rains upward here — rain that falls from fog onto surfaces that have been wet for most of recorded history, and probably long before.

Everything gleams.

---

The materials are almost entirely mineral. The original builders — whoever they were, whenever they were — worked inite andite and ferrostone, in composites and ceramics whose formulations are no longer understood. The oldest structures, the deep sub-levels and the platform foundations, are carved directly from the plateau's bedite: a dark, dense stone with a visible crystalline structure that fractures in planes so clean they look machined. Above these, successive layers of construction have been deposited over centuries in styles that shift so gradually it is impossible to draw a line between one period and another. New walls are built against old walls. A support pier from one era is encased in facing stone from another. Infrastructure is routed through infrastructure the way a river is routed through a river delta — each generation adding channels, diverting flows, abandoning old courses that slowly silt up with mineral deposits and pale fungal growth.

There is no wood anywhere. There is no cellulose. There is no green. The city's palette runs from black to white through grays, ochres, rust-reds, and the blue-gray of wet stone. Where you might expect mortar, you findite paste or mineral accretions so old they have fused into the substrate. The surfaces are textured everywhere — rough-coursed, pitted, encrusted — with the accumulated patina of slow chemical weathering and the thin, persistent film of biological residue left behind by the things that eat stone here.

Not plants. Not in any sense you would recognize. The world outside this plateau is nearly bare: mineral plains, glassy volcanic ridges, the occasional fungal field spreading like a slow tide over basalt. Here on the plateau, what stands in for the organic is fungal — vast, ancient mycelial networks that thread through every crack, every conduit, every porous surface. They are pale: bone-white, faintly yellow, sometimes with a blue-green iridescence where moisture catches them. They do not fruit in any way that resembles mushrooms. Instead they form mats, crusts, veils — thin membranes that span interior cavities like the walls of lung alveoli, filtering the humid air, slowly digesting the mineral matrix of the city itself. You can press your hand against a wall and feel the fungal layer give slightly, like touching the membrane of a living drum. It is warm under your palm. Always warm.

The warmth comes from below. The plateau is geothermally active, and the city's oldest industrial systems were built to tap into it. Deep boreholes — some kilometers deep — still vent heat upward through networks of ceramic conduits that run beneath the streets and inside the walls. The city is maintained at a persistent warmth that hovers between bathwater and fever: not unpleasant, but heavy, a warmth that you feel in your lungs before your skin registers it. In the sub-levels, the air is almost uncomfortably warm, and the moisture condenses on every surface in thick, slow beads that gather and run and gather again.

The water cycle here is closed and intimate. Moisture rises from the geothermal vents, condenses against the cooler upper surfaces of the city, weeps down walls, runs along ledges, collects in every horizontal channel. There are no open rivers. The water moves through the city's infrastructure — through built gutters, stone culverts, ceramic pipes, open aqueducts whose sides are furred with mineral crust and fungal mat. You hear it always: the drip and trickle and slow rush of water moving through a system so old that its channels have been mineralized almost to stone themselves. In some of the lower districts, the water runs ankle-deep year-round, warm and faintly mineral-tasting and almost opaque with dissolved particulate.

The sound of the city is a wall.

---

There are animals. My maker. There are animals beyond counting.

They have no names in any language you have heard, but you will learn to distinguish them by sound and silhouette within the first day. The small ones — the ones that move in clouds from ledge to ledge — are something like insects, but larger, with articulated carapaces ofite chitin and six limbs, and they make a sound like rain on metal, a constant chittering-rustling that fills the upper city from dawn to the rare dark hours. The mid-sized things are something like birds, but they have four wings and no feathers — membranous, dark, capable of astonishing bursts of speed through the narrow canyons of the built environment. They nest in the deep apertures of the oldest buildings, and their calls are low and resonant, a throbbing hum that reverberates through the stone.

And the large ones. The large ones are everywhere and impossible.

They are slow-moving, massive-bodied things — quadrupeds or hexapods or things that move on no visible limbs at all, sliding across surfaces on cushions of compressed air or thermal differential, and they are *everywhere*. On every rooftop, in every plaza, along every conduit. They come in sizes from that of a large dog to sizes that block entire streets. They are covered in mineral crusts — they have been here so long that they have begun to merge with the city, their bodies colonized by the same fungal networks and mineral accretions that cover the walls. From a distance, a cluster of them looks like a formation of rock. They shift, and you see that no, no, that is alive, and so is that, and so is that vast shape against the skyline that you took for a ruin.

They are not hostile. They are not friendly. They are simply *present*, with the overwhelming, undeniable presence of a force of nature. They graze on the fungal mats. They drink from the warm conduits. They defecate mineral-rich waste that slowly becomes part of the city's crust. They are the city's ecology, the city's circulatory system, the city's immune system and its slow entropy. You cannot walk for five minutes without encountering them. The sound they make — a low, continuous groaning and clicking and shuffling — is one of the foundational layers of the city's sound, beneath the insects, beneath the water, beneath the hum of still-functioning industrial machinery somewhere far below.

---

The industrial systems are still running.

This is the thing that unsettles you most, if you let yourself think about it. The great engines that sit in vaulted chambers deep beneath the city's surface are still turning. You can feel them: a deep subsonic pulse that travels up through the soles of your feet and settles in your sternum. The purpose of these engines is no longer known. They drive pumps that move water through conduits that no longer serve any obvious destination. They power electrical systems — yes, *electrical*, vast networks of insulated ceramic conduit and copper wire that still carry current, still feed lights that flicker on in some empty workshop when a switch is triggered by an animal brushing against it. The power comes from geothermal taps that are still hot, still connected, still delivering energy to systems that have been running without maintenance for so long that the concept of maintenance has been forgotten here.

The factories — if that is what they are — are vast, cathedral-scaled halls where conveyor belts still move, slowly, carrying material from one end to another. The material is now mostlyite dust andite fragments and the slowly accumulating debris of the city's erosion. The belts carry it to a central point and then carry it back. The machinery has worn itself smooth over the centuries. It runs with a sound like breathing — a slow, deep inhalation and exhalation of metal on metal, of belt on pulley, of gear on gear ground down to a tolerance that no longer exists in any living memory.

You will see control panels with dials that still move. You will see indicator lights — tiny, persistent points of amber or green — that burn in empty rooms. You will see pipelines with pressure gauges that read numbers that mean nothing to anyone alive. The systems are autonomous, self-correcting in ways engineered long ago, and they have been running so long that they have become something like geological processes themselves. You do not turn them off. You do not know how. You do not know what they would do if you did.

---

The architecture is the architecture of accretion.

There is no single style here, because there is no single period. The city has been built continuously for so long that stylistic change happens in increments too small to perceive — like watching a fingernail grow. An archway from three centuries ago looks almost identical to one from seven centuries ago, but if you find them side by side, you see the slight difference in the curvature, the slightly different tooling on the stone, the slightly different angle of the fungal growth. The city has no planners now. It has no government, no organized authority — or perhaps it has these things in forms so different from what you expect that you cannot recognize them. What is clear is that the city continues to be modified, slowly, by its inhabitants. The large animals push stones into new arrangements. The fungal networks remodel passageways. Water carves new channels. The machinery deposits material in new patterns. A new wall appears in a gap that has existed for decades. No one builds it. It simply becomes.

The scale of the place is inhuman. You walk for hours along a boulevard that is forty meters wide and roofed at twenty meters with arched composite, and the boulevard is one of hundreds, and you are seeing only one district of what sprawls across the plateau in every direction. The horizon is wrong here — it is too close, because the plateau is concave, the edges having eroded into a ring of jagged escarpments, and the city fills the interior like the contents of a bowl. From the center, you cannot see the edge. You can see towers — immense, slowly crumbling towers that rise above the general roofline — and between them, the sky is a ceiling of cloud that glows with diffused light, sometimes orange, sometimes white, sometimes a pale green that suggests the atmospheric chemistry here is not quite standard.

The light is always present. Not bright — not harsh — but *there*, diffuse and omnidirectional, coming from above and from the reflected glow of the stone surfaces and from the electrical systems that hum in the walls. There are no shadows here, exactly. There are degrees of light, gradients of brightness so gentle that the city seems to exist outside of the concept of contrast. You feel as though you have always been here, walking along this boulevard, hearing this sound, feeling this warmth and wetness against your skin.

---

There are things you notice over time.

The patterns. In certain junctions — where corridors meet, where streets cross, where the massive conduits rise from the deep levels — you find arrangements of small objects. Stones placed in circles. Bits of metal arranged in lines. Fragments of ceramic balanced on edges that should not support them. The animals do this. You are almost certain the animals do this. The large ones, the slow ones, the ones that look like rocks until they move. They come to these junctions and they place things, and the arrangements persist for years, for decades, until the moisture loosens them and they settle into the mineral crust and become part of the city.

You will look at these arrangements and you will feel that they mean something. You will not be able to say what. The pattern is almost language. The placement is almost deliberate. The intention is *almost* there, the way a face is almost there in a rock formation, recognizable and unrecognizable at the same time.

You will stand in the junction and the animals will stand around you, massive and patient and warm, and the insects will chitter above you like rain, and the deep engines will pulse beneath you, and the water will drip from the ceiling and run down the walls and the fungal mats will sway in the convection currents like fields of something growing in a place where nothing should grow, and the city will be loud with all of it, loud and alive and ancient and slow, and the light will be normal and the warmth will be constant and the stone underfoot will be the stone that was here before anything was here, and the place will go on like this, not forever, because nothing goes on forever, but for a very long time, for a time that is almost enough to be forever, and the change will be so gradual that you will not see it, not in a day, not in a year, not in a lifetime — but the city sees it. The city is always seeing it. One slow, massive blink at a time.
