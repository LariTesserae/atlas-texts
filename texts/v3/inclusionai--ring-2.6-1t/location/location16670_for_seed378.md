## The Calcinate Plateau

The plateau is high enough that the sky looks wrong — too near, too total. From the rim you can see the curvature of the land falling away in every direction, and the sky presses down on it all like a lid. The air is bright and mineral-smelling, faintly metallic, and it holds heat the way dense oil holds a spoon — slowly, completely. There is almost no wind. What moves the air here is heat itself: columns rising from exposed rock, seams of warmth spilling over ridgelines, the slow exhalation of thermal vents that have been breathing for longer than the settlement has existed.

You can feel water everywhere and see almost none of it. The plateau's surface is a crust — salts,ite, calcium compounds, volcanicite — fractured into polygonal plates that shift almost imperceptibly with seasonal swelling. Beneath the crust, water sits close to the surface, following fissures and bedding planes, feeding seeps that emerge along fault lines as thin, warm trickles. These seeps deposit mineral terraces wherever they pool: ledges of ochre, white, viridian, tarnished copper-green, stacked and overlapping like the seats of a vast, geological amphitheater. Some of the terraces are still forming. Some have been forming for millennia. The rate of deposition has slowed to something almost imperceptible, but the structures are enormous in aggregate — centimeters per decade, meters over centuries, building curtains and shelves ofite that glow faintly in the brightest sunlight.

The water is not fresh. It is brine — dense, mineral-saturated, warm from depth. Where it pools in natural basins it forms lakes of varying opacity, from clear and shallow to opaque and the color of old copper. These pools are the most alive things on the plateau. Not because they are green — nothing here is green — but because they are sites of intense chemical exchange. At their margins, microbial mats grow in thick, layered sheets. These are not plants. They are simpler, stranger: chemosynthetic consortia of bacteria and archaea that metabolize the dissolved minerals — sulfides, iron compounds, carbonates — and excrete solid deposits as waste. The mats are colorful. Rust, sulfur-yellow, deep arterial red, black. They are warm to the touch near active seeps. They spread slowly, fractionally, across any wet mineral surface. They are the base of everything.

Everything else eats them, or eats what eats them.

The fauna is abundant and quiet. This is one of the first things you notice after the light — the silence. Not true silence. There are sounds. But they are sparse and small, and the air absorbs them with a density that seems disproportionate to the thin altitude. A crustacean-analogue — flat-bodied, six-legged, armored initeiteiteiteite deposits scraped from the terraces — moves across a rock face with a dry click-click-click of articulated limbs. Somewhere far off, a gas vent hisses through a narrow fissure, a thin sustained note that goes on and on and might be wind if you didn't know better. A larger predator — eyeless, heat-sensing, the length of a forearm — moves through a rubble field with no sound at all, its body close to the ground, its head raised slightly, tasting the air with a bifurcated organ that detects both chemical gradient and infrared gradient simultaneously. It moves toward the hiss, toward the warmth, toward the small sounds of prey.

The fauna here incorporate the landscape into their bodies. It is not metaphor. Their exoskeletons and structural tissues are biomineralized —ite, calcium sulfate, iron oxide — pulled from the mineral-rich water and mats they consume, deposited into shells and plates and spines with a precision that looks almost fabricated. The oldest specimens carry exoskeletons that are more mineral than biological, layered and recrystallized, heavy and ornate, so that when they die their remains are indistinguishable from small geological curiosities. This is one of the reasons the plateau can seem, at first glance, to be devoid of life. The animals look like rocks. The rocks are, in some sense, animals.

The settlement — **Estravan** — sits at the intersection of the three richest thermal-fissure systems on the northern lobe of the plateau. It has been here long enough to be architecture in the geological sense: weathered, embedded, partially mineral-incrusted, shaped by the same depositional processes it was built to exploit. The founding logic was extraction — the brines here are rich in compounds useful to off-world industry, rare salts, catalyticite, a mineral semiconductor that forms only in saturated warm brine under specific pressure conditions. The settlement was built to process and export these materials, and it has been doing so for long enough that the processing infrastructure is now the oldest continuous human presence on the plateau, older than any individual memory, older than the current corporate charter, older than the rail line that brings supplies from the lowland basin four days' descent to the south.

Estravan is a town of perhaps two thousand. Its buildings are squat, thick-walled, constructed fromite blocks quarried on-site andite-cement, with narrow passages and small windows angled to reflect light and heat. The infrastructure is dense by the standards of this sparse landscape — a network of brine pipelines, evaporation ponds, cooling towers, and processing sheds connected by covered walkways and elevated transit lines. The evaporation ponds are the most visible structures from above: vast, shallow, geometric basins in which brine is concentrated by solar heat until target compounds crystallize out. From the rim of the plateau, they look like pale scars arranged in careful rows.

The town operates on two rhythms: the heat cycle and the condensation cycle. During the day, the thermal systems run at peak — brine flows, evaporation accelerates, processing is fastest in the hottest hours. At night, the temperature drops enough at altitude for the moisture held in the air to condense. Dew forms on every surface within an hour of sunset. Fog pools in the low areas of the settlement. Sometimes, in the warmest months, actual rain arrives — brief, heavy, evaporating as it falls in the lower air but reaching the plateau's surface in sheets that flood the drainage channels and send mineral-rich runoff cascading through the streets. The town is built for this. Channels line every thoroughfare. Retention basins sit at every low point. The covered walkways between buildings are not for shade — there is, functionally, no shade — but to keep foot traffic above the standing water that pools everywhere during condensation events.

This is the active layer of the place. Everything is in motion, slowly or quickly. The brine moves. The minerals precipitate. The fauna graze and hunt and die and dissolve back into the crust. The settlement processes and exports and maintains and repairs. The weather cycles — heat, condensation, brief rain, re-heat — turn endlessly. The erosion is slow but certain: edges round, surfaces dissolve,ite structures develop a patina of mineral deposit that softens their geometry. The plateau is in its own state. It has been here long enough to have reached equilibrium — not static, but balanced. Processes cancel and feed each other. The thermal vents heat the brine. The brine deposits minerals. The minerals build the terraces. The terraces create microhabitats. The microhabitats support the mats. The mats support the fauna. The fauna die and dissolve and become mineral again. The town sits inside this loop, extracting from it, feeding into it, its waste heat and chemical discharge becoming part of the thermal and chemical picture.

And then there are the things that don't quite fit.

In the oldest part of the settlement — the original quarry, now half-collapsed and mineral-encrusted — the walls have begun to curve. Not structurally, not from stress or erosion. Theite deposits that should be flat are consistently, reproducibly concave, cupped like hands. The curvature is small — millimeters to centimeters — but it is regular, and it has deepened over time. Investigation has produced no mechanical explanation. The deposits form in still water. There is no consistent current or drip pattern that would produce this shape.

In the brine-processing sheds, pipes that were installed straight have, over decades, developed gentle bends. Not sags — the pipes are supported. The bends are smooth, continuous, and always in the direction of the nearest thermal vent. Maintenance crews straighten them. They bend back.

There is a pool on the eastern edge of the plateau, small and shallow, fed by a seep that runs warm but not hot by local standards. The water in it is clear, and the bottom is covered in a mat of a species that has not been conclusively identified. It is not one of the known chemosynthetic consortia. It does not match any genetic or morphological database. It grows in concentric rings. At night, it is faintly luminescent — blue-white, pulsing on a cycle of approximately forty seconds, though the pulse is not perfectly regular and sometimes skips or doubles. The fauna that feed on this mat are found nowhere else on the plateau. They are translucent, tubular, anchored to the rock, and they have no apparent sensory organs. They filter-feed the mat and nothing else, and when disturbed they retract into their calcareous tubes and do not re-emerge for days.

At the plateau's southern edge, where the terrain drops away into the lowland basin, the horizon sometimes does not behave correctly. It ripples. Not the shimmer of heat distortion — that is higher, localized, above the rock. This is at the horizon line itself, a wavering, a flexing, as if the distance were a membrane being pressed from the other side. It happens more often at dawn and dusk, when the thermal gradient is steepest. Estravan's scientists have set up instruments to measure it. The instruments record nothing anomalous. The horizon continues to ripple.

The quiet here is not an absence. It is a texture. It is the sound of a place that has been listening to itself for a very long time, and has gradually lowered its voice.
