# The High Quiet

You feel the cold before anything else. Not a biting cold, not the kind that announces itself — a *settling* cold, the kind that has been sitting in a place so long it has become part of the architecture. It enters through the soles of your feet and stays there, a low hum in the bones.

The plateau stretches. That is the first true thing about it: it does not end. Or rather, it ends in a way that makes you doubt whether the end is geographic or perceptual. The horizon is close — the elevation is so great that the curvature of the world bends visibly at the edges, a subtle upward tilt of the terrain like the rim of an enormous bowl. You are inside something. The sky overhead is pale, washed out, not dark enough to be threatening and not bright enough to feel generous. Just light. Normal, even light, the kind that on flat ground would feel ordinary, but here — at this altitude, on this scale — it feels *administrative*. Light applied. Light by policy.

The ground beneath you is not rock, not exactly, and not soil. It has been both for so long that it has become a third thing. The erosion here is so ancient that thinking about it in years stops making sense. The mountains that once defined this plateau were worn down not merely to hills but to *suggestions* — gentle undulations in the terrain, broad swells and shallow depressions, as if the land were breathing in its sleep. Where once there were ridgelines, now there are textures: a ripple of pale stone here, a depression that collects mist there. The terrain reads like a sentence that has been translated too many times. You understand the meaning but not the language.

And the moss. God, the moss.

It is everywhere. Not as a covering but as a *presence*. The vegetation on this plateau is lush in a way that defies the cold, defies the elevation, defies every reasonable expectation of where lushness should and should not be. Mosses in greens so deep they look black in shadow and luminous chartreuse where the light catches them. Lichens in fractal sprawls across every surface — stone, dead wood, the crumbling lips of old cisterns. Low-growing fern-things that are not technically ferns, whose fronds uncurl with a patience that is almost sentient. They move. You can't see them move, but you *know* they moved when you look away and look back. A tilt. A spreading. A slow and deliberate claiming of space.

There are no animals. There is no sound that could be called a voice. The quiet here is not empty — it is *occupied*. The quiet has mass. You feel it pressing against your eardrums like pressure in deep water. The only sounds are incidental: the soft compression of your own footfall on damp moss, the faint creak of a settlement's wooden beam contracting in the night cold, and sometimes — distantly, always distantly — a low tone that might be wind moving through a broad stone passage or might be the plateau itself vibrating at a frequency just below hearing. You can't tell. No one can. It has always been there.

---

The towns.

They are scattered. That is the word — *scattered* — as though someone had taken a handful of settlement and let it fall from height, and where each piece landed, it grew. There is no grid, no grand plan. Each town is an accretion, a thing that accumulated: a central kiln, a water cistern carved from a single stone, a ring of structures built outward from necessity. The buildings are low, wide, made of dried mud reinforced withite fibrous plant matter from the mosses — a kind of adobe that has been adapted, over generations, to the cold. The walls are thick. The windows are small and shuttered from inside. Smoke comes from hearths, thin pale threads that rise and flatten against the low ceiling of cloud before dissolving.

The population is small. Each town perhaps holds a few hundred souls, maybe a thousand at most for the largest. The people are sparse in the way everything here is sparse — widely spaced, self-sufficient, comfortable with distance. They dress in layered wool and cured moss-fiber, and they move through the landscape with a quiet competence that suggests they have been here longer than any written record. They do not explain the plateau. They do not seem to think it needs explaining.

The roads between towns are not paved. They are simply paths where foot traffic has compressed the moss and exposed the ancient stone beneath — pale, glassy in places, worn to a smoothness that feels almost liquid underfoot. These paths follow the contours of the land, curving around swells, dipping into shallow valleys where fog collects like standing water. Walking between towns takes days. You see no one on the way. This is as it should be.

---

There is something wrong with the place.

Not wrong as in dangerous. Not wrong as in ruined. Wrong as in *indeterminate*. The ancient erosion has done something to this landscape that goes past geology. The stone, having been worn down for so long that it has forgotten its original mineral structure, has begun to lose other certainties as well. In places, the density of rock shifts — you pick up a piece and it is heavy, you set it down and pick it up again and it is lighter. Not much. Just enough to make your arm hesitate. The moss grows in patterns that are almost geometric, and if you stare at the pattern long enough, it stops looking like a pattern at all and starts looking like a language you once knew.

Distances are unreliable in the thin valleys between the swells. You can see a town from a ridgeline — clear, close, smoke rising — and walk toward it for hours and arrive at a place that feels like where you started, the town now visible again in the same position, same distance, as though the space between you and it folded and unfolded like cloth. The people in the towns do not seem troubled by this. They mark their paths. They walk them. They do not discuss the discrepancy.

And the water. The damp. It is everywhere — not raining, not misting, just *present*, as condensation, as a film on every surface, as a slickness on the stone that never fully dries. The cisterns fill themselves. The moss drinks and drinks and stays wet. On certain mornings, the air is so saturated that the boundary between air and water becomes unclear, and you walk through what feels like a room-temperature fog so thick your clothes are soaked by the time you emerge. The water tastes of nothing and of minerals simultaneously. It is the taste of age.

At night — and night comes quickly at this elevation, the sun dropping behind the rim of the world like a stone dropped into dark water — the moss glows. Faintly. Biochemically, without explanation, a cold green phosphorescence that spreads across the landscape in slow pulses, as though the ground itself were breathing light. The towns are visible from a distance as darker shapes within this glow, the absence of light more defining than any presence. There is no fire in the streets at night. The people stay inside. Even they do not fully understand why.

---

This is a place that has been here long enough to become its own law. The cold, the moss, the stone, the fog, the towns, the people — all of them have entered a kind of agreement with entropy. Things erode. Things grow. Things settle. The land is so old it has begun to dissolve into something that is not quite matter and not quite memory, and the people who live on it have learned to move gently through that uncertainty, the way you move through a room where someone is sleeping.

Nothing is ending here. But nothing is beginning either. The plateau simply *persists*, in a state so stable it has become its own form of motion: the slowest possible weather, the deepest possible silence, the oldest possible green.

You stand on it and you feel it holding you, and the holding feels like forgetting, and the forgetting feels like home.
