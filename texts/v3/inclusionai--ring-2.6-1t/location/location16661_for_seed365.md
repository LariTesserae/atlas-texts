# The Weeping Terrace

You come onto it without realizing. The land simply stops climbing and opens, and you are standing in something like a held breath.

---

**The Bowl**

It is a basin — maybe three hundred paces across — cupped between ridgelines that rose too recently. The mountains around it are young in the way that only stone can be young: everything is edges. The ridgelines are sharp as snapped bone. Gullies have started to form along the slopes but haven't committed yet, still shallow, still tentative, as if the water is trying to remember where it wants to run. Talus fields of angular rubble — pale grey, faintly violet in the dim light — slope down in fans where the walls have recently given way. Nothing is rounded. Nothing is soft. Every surface looks like it was made yesterday and hasn't yet decided if it will stay.

The basin floor is not flat. It tilts gently, maybe fifteen degrees from the northern lip down to a shallow depression in the south where a cluster of dark pools sits — three or four of them, each no larger than a dining table, separated by plates of exposed rock. The water is still. Almost impossibly still. It does not reflect the sky because the sky, such as it is, offers nothing worth reflecting. There is a low overcast here that never lifts, not clouds exactly but a permanent gauze, a thin mineral haze that hangs at the ridgeline height and diffuses whatever light is coming from somewhere above and beyond it. The result is a kind of light that has no source. Everything is lit but from nowhere. Faces look flatter than they should. Shadows are faint, grey-lilac, and fall at odd angles.

**The Veining**

The stone of the basin weeps. Not dramatically — you would not call it rain. But in patches along the lower northern slope, the rock darkens and a slow seepage beads along grain boundaries, gathering in thin rivulets that find the cracks between the rubble and disappear. It happens intermittently and without obvious rhythm. Sometimes the stone is dry for hours. Sometimes it is wet when you arrive at camp and still wet when you leave. The people here say the mountain is deciding. When the stone darkens they stop what they are doing and wait, the way you might wait for someone to finish a sentence. The water that collects tastes of almost nothing. Almost. There is a mineral note underneath, faintly sweet, and a strange coolness that lingers on the tongue for minutes after swallowing.

The pools in the south are fed by this seepage. Their edges rise in thin mineral rims, pale and porous, growing slowly, millimetre by millimetre. The water itself is clear to the point of absence — you look down into it and see the pale bottom at arm's depth and it takes a moment to register that there is water at all. Small things live in them. Not fish as you would know them. Something more like translucent, flattened tadpoles with too-wide mouths, grazing the pool bottoms in slow spirals. You see three, five, sometimes a dozen. You never see just one. They move in loose synchrony, as if connected by a signal too subtle to detect. When you step near the pool's edge they scatter — not in panic, but with a deliberate, unhurried withdrawal, like a tide pulling back.

**The Growth**

Vegetation here is everywhere and yet somehow nowhere. The basin is lush in the sense that life is *insistent*, but it is sparse in the way it distributes itself, as though the plants need a great deal of room and will not be crowded. The dominant growths are something you might call trees if you were generous, or columns if you were precise. They rise from the rubble and the thin soil pockets — straight, unbranched, sixty or seventy feet tall, with bark the colour of old parchment. No leaves in any conventional sense. Instead, from the upper quarter of each trunk, a spray of long, thin filaments extends outward and downward, hanging motionless in the still air like the fringe of a curtain that was disturbed hours ago and has almost finished settling. They are pale green at their base, darkening to near-black at the tips. They move — barely — and the movement is so slow you can only confirm it by returning hours later.

Between these columns, the ground is given over to low, spreading mats of something that is not quite moss. It is thick and spongy underfoot, faintly blue-green, and it releases a cool vapour when you step on it — a faint exhalation, like breath held and then offered. In patches where the mats grow thick, they produce small pale fruiting bodies, no bigger than a thumbnail, shaped like flattened stars. The animals eat them. You can see the evidence: neat, crescent-shaped bite-marks, and the faint, sweet smell of something fermenting just beneath the surface.

There are flowers, but they behave incorrectly. They grow directly from the stone — from cracks, from the rims of the pools, from the vertical faces of the talus — and they are warm to the touch. Not metaphorically. Physically warm, several degrees above the ambient air temperature, and in the dim light of the basin they emit a faint bioluminescence, a dull amber glow that is visible only in peripheral vision. Look directly at one and it dims. Glance past it and there it is: a soft point of light, hanging in the grey air like a lantern seen from a great distance. No one has ever confirmed that they are actually lanterns. But no one disputes it, either.

**The Animals**

This is the overwhelming thing. The fauna here is present in numbers that unsettle. You hear it first — or rather, you almost hear it. The sound is beneath the threshold of attention. A low, aggregate rustle, like ten thousand small bodies moving through dry grass, except there is no grass and the movement is not quite sound. It is closer to *pressure change*. A faint, rhythmic displacement of air, as if the atmosphere itself is being breathed by something vast.

They are ungulate-shaped. That is the closest frame. Four-legged, roughly the size of a large dog, with long necks they carry in a gentle S-curve, and heads that are too large for the body — broad, flat, with wide-set eyes arranged not in pairs but in crescents, so each face has six eyes, three on each side, and the eyes are a pale, reflective amber that matches the glow of the stone-flowers. Their hides are short-haired, dark brown, almost black, and they move in loose aggregations of forty to eighty individuals that drift through the basin like smoke. They eat the moss-mats. They drink from the pools — bending those long necks down with a strange, mechanical grace, the flat heads touching the water's surface without breaking it, sipping through something like a proboscis that extends from the lower jaw.

They are quiet. Individually silent. But in aggregate, the sound of sixty of them moving through the blue-green moss is that low, collective exhale — the sound of the basin breathing. They do not flee from the settlement. They pass through it, between the buildings, close enough to touch. The residents do not touch them. There is an agreement, spoken or understood or simply *present* the way weather is present. The animals pass. The people watch. The arrangement is old.

At dusk — which here is simply the dimming of the already-dim, a subtle subtraction of light rather than a spectacle — the animals gather in the open centre of the basin and stand in a broad circle, facing outward, motionless, for perhaps an hour. Their amber eyes catch the faint light. From above, it must look like a ring of small, low fires. No one here has seen it from above. No one climbs the ridgelines. The mountains are too new and too sharp. They cut. The people know this without needing to verify it.

There are other animals. Smaller ones, quick, with too many legs — eight, sometimes ten — that skitter through the rubble at dusk, hunting insects that emerge from the stone-warm cracks. They are iridescent, opalescent, the colour of oil on water, and they make no sound at all. They are beautiful in the way that something is beautiful when it has no use for you.

And beneath the pools, something larger moves. You can see it only as a displacement in the water — a slow, lateral shift of the surface that is not caused by wind (there is no wind here) or by the small tadpole-things. It passes under the centre of each pool in turn, on a circuit, and takes perhaps a full day to complete. The water dims slightly as it passes, as though a shadow had fallen across it from below. No one has seen what it is. The settlement was built around the pools, not despite them. The decision, whatever it was, was made a long time ago.

**The Settlement**

Hamlet-scale. Sixteen structures, though the count shifts slightly depending on the season — an outbuilding added, a collapsed wall not yet rebuilt. The construction is stone-and-timber, post-and-beam, with walls of dry-stacked rubble taken from the basin floor and roofs of layered hide stretched over curved wooden frames. The wood is pale, dense, cut from a tree species that grows in the thin soil along the southern ridge — a twisted, slow-growing hardwood with a resin that smells faintly of copper. The buildings are low, single-storey, and set in a loose ring around the central open ground where the animals gather. They have no windows on the ground floor, only low doorways and narrow ventilation slits near the roofline. Inside: packed earth floors, stone sleeping platforms, shelves carved directly into the walls, and a pervasive smell of smoke, dried moss, and the faint sweet-mineral tang of the weeping stone.

The settlement has no name in any language you would recognize. Its people — perhaps forty souls, though counting them is complicated by the fact that some come and go on long circuits of the ridgelines, returning days later with leather bags full of things harvested from the high slopes: resin, pale mineral salt, the shed filaments from the great trees, bundled and dried. They use hand tools exclusively. Flaked-stone blades, bone awls, wooden mallets. Their metalwork is limited — small, soft copper pins for fastening hides, nothing alloyed, nothing hardened. They know copper exists higher up. They have not gone to get it. They have made a decision, or inherited one, about what level of extraction this place will sustain.

There is infrastructure, barely: a system of shallow stone-lined channels that redirect the weeping water from the northern slope down into the southern pools, keeping them full. The channels are old — older than the oldest buildings — and their construction is more precise than anything in the settlement. The walls are fitted stone, tight-jointed, with a slight convex curvature that resists erosion. No one here claims to have built them. The origin is not discussed. It is not a forbidden topic. It is simply a fact as stable and unremarkable as the ridgelines.

**The Atmosphere**

The air is cool and thin, with a faint metallic taste at the back of the throat. The haze that hangs over the basin gives all distances a soft, indistinct quality. Sounds carry strangely — the scrape of a foot on rubble is sharp and immediate, but it dies quickly, absorbed by the still air as though the atmosphere itself is padded. The dominant silence is not empty. It is full. It hums at the edge of perception, the aggregate presence of the animals, the faint vibration of the warm flowers, the slow drip of condensation from the great trees' filaments into the pools below. If you stand still long enough, you begin to feel the silence as a pressure, gentle and all-encompassing, like being held.

At night — which arrives as a deepening of the dim rather than a departure of light — the stone-flowers glow more brightly. The amber intensifies to a soft orange, and the basin becomes a field of low, hovering lights, scattered among the dark columns of the trees and the pale shapes of the standing animals, whose eyes also catch and return the glow. It is beautiful in the way that a place is beautiful when it was not made for you and does not care whether you are present. The air smells of cold stone and green growth and something faintly electric, the smell before rain in a place where rain is rare.

**The Rules**

Things here follow patterns that are not hostile but are indifferent. The sharp stone cuts what is careless. The pools sustain what is patient. The animals move on circuits older than memory, and the space between them is the space in which the settlement exists — tolerated, peripheral, minor. The young geology reshapes itself slowly, a new gully opened each wet season, a new face sheared off by frost on the northern ridge, and the people who live here understand that the land is not finished. It is still becoming. They build with this knowledge. Their walls are low because tall things fall. Their roofs are heavy and curved because the wind, when it comes, comes hard and brief.

Nothing is explained here. Nothing needs to be. The basin is a system — biological, geological, hydrological — that functions with a coherence that predates and will outlast the small, warm, temporary settlement at its centre. The people know this. They are not humbled by it. They have simply incorporated it into their understanding the way a person incorporates the rhythm of their own heartbeat — present, vital, unremarkable, and not entirely their own.

---

You stand at the basin's northern lip, looking down. The air is cool on your face. The haze softens every edge. Somewhere below, the moss exhales its faint vapour. A stone weeps. Something large passes beneath the southern pool, and the surface dimples in a slow, spreading ring. The filaments of the great trees hang motionless and almost — almost — you swear you can see them settle, a fraction of an inch, in a wind that isn't there.

You step down into the bowl. The stone is warm underfoot. The silence hums.
