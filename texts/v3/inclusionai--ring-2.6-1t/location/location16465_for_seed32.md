## The Lattice Room

You find it by accident, or by patience — a seam in the hillside no wider than your forearm, exhaling air that smells of chalk and copper and something older, something like the inside of a tooth. The opening gives way almost immediately to a passage that tightens around you, walls closing in with a faint grit that powders your palms — calciumite, dried myceliumite, the compressed remains of ten thousand years of slow exhalation by organisms that have long since stopped breathing in any way you could call alive.

Then it opens.

Not dramatically. Not into a cavern so much as into a room — because it *is* a room, in the way a wasp's interior is a room, in the way a honeycomb cell is a room. The scale is deceptive. The ceiling — if there is a ceiling — is close, maybe two body-lengths above you, occluded by a thicket of hanging filaments so dense they look like smoke that has decided to stay. These are the only things here that might once have been called vegetation: hair-thin fungal threads, grey-green with age, hanging in curtains from every overhead surface, never moving, never having moved in the memory of the place. They are dry. They are weightless. They are so numerous that together they form a second ceiling, soft and porous, and through it the light descends.

The light is the first thing that unsettles you, and the last thing you understand.

It comes from everywhere. Not from above, not from a source — from the walls themselves, from the substance of the room. Every surface carries a lichen, or something like a lichen: a crust of pale bioluminescence, bluish-white, steady as poured glass. It does not flicker. It does not pulse. It holds one brightness the way a stone holds one temperature, the way the air here holds one sound. The brightness is considerable. It throws soft shadows — only soft ones, because there are no hard edges here, nothing angular enough to cast a clean line. Everything in this room has been rounded, worn, colonized and re-colonized, until the architecture resembles the interior of a nautilus shell if a nautilus shell were scaled to a space you could almost fill with your body. Almost. You would have to turn sideways. You would have to breathe shallowly.

Because the room is full.

Packed — that is the only word for it. From the floor, which is not a floor but a continuous surface of fused fungal plates, smooth where footfall has never touched it and ridged where growth pressed against growth for epochs, the space is saturated with structure. Columns rise from the floor and branch into buttresses that meet shelves that connect to bridges that link to secondary columns that divide into ribs that vanish into the hanging curtains overhead. There is no gap wider than a hand. There is no open space larger than a breath. The room is a single solid object — not a cave with things in it, but a thing that happens to have a hollow running through it the way a reed has a hollow. You are inside the hollow.

The columns are bone-white where the lichen doesn't cover them, and where it does, they glow a faint blue-green, as if lit from within by frost. They are warm to the touch, which is wrong — you expected cool, and the temperature of the room *is* cool, a cellar cool, a marble-crypt cool, but the columns themselves hold a residual warmth from metabolic processes that ceased thousands of years ago and never quite equalized with the stone-cold patience of the air. It is the wrong temperature for what they are. You will remember this. You will forget why.

The sound.

The sound is the thing that tells you this place is alive in some capacity that refuses to die. It comes from the architecture — from the channels between the columns, the pores in the fused plates, the deliberate perforations that lace every surface like the fenestrae of a bone. Air moves through them. Air has always moved through them. There is a pressure differential here — cool air entering through the seam at the base, warm air (marginally warmer, barely) rising and exiting through a lattice of micro-channels near the ceiling that function like a chimney, like a throat. The air moves at one speed. It has always moved at this speed. It will always move at this speed.

And so the room hums.

It is not a mechanical hum. It is not a tone. It is a chord — a cluster of frequencies so dense and so harmonically entangled that it sounds less like music than like a color. A low, bronze roar that fills your chest and vibrates in the roots of your teeth. The sound is louder than you would expect from so small a space. It resonates. It *sympathizes* with your own body, your own chest cavity, your own blood moving in your ears, until you cannot tell where the room ends and your hearing begins. You feel held by the sound the way a seed is held by soil.

There is nothing here that eats. Nothing that hunts, nothing that flees. No insects in the filaments, no mites in the dust. The ecology of this place ended long ago, or perhaps never began — what grew here grew to completion and then stopped, and what remains is a record of that completion. A few of the shelf-like structures bear fruiting bodies — small, pale, dry as parchment, their spore surfaces collapsed and sealed. They are artifacts now. They have not released anything in a very long time.

The erosion is everywhere and it is beautiful. Where the air currents have been most persistent, the fungal surfaces have been polished to a matte sheen, almost ceramic. Channels have widened into flutes. Flutes have deepened into grooves. Grooves have, in places, broken through to adjacent chambers that were sealed at the room's formation, creating small windows — no larger than a thumbnail — through which you can see, and be seen by, identical architecture on the other side. It recedes, that architecture, into a distance you cannot gauge, lit by the same patient blue-white glow, humming the same chord.

You notice, after some time, the slight wrongness. The columns do not taper the way growth tapers. They narrow *upward and inward simultaneously*, as if shaped around something that was removed — a core, a mold, a presence. The shelves are not horizontal. They arc in subtle parabolas, as though shaped to hold something liquid that evaporated before it could settle. The entire room has a grammar — repeated spirals, nested nodules, channels that fork in exact thirds — that suggests intention without language, communication without sender or receiver. It is like finding a sentence in a language you almost speak, written in an alphabet you almost recognize, in a place where no one should be writing.

Nothing changes here. The light is constant. The sound is constant. The temperature is constant. The filaments do not grow. The lichen does not spread. The spores remain sealed. If you leave and return, the room will be the same, down to the single grain of mineral dust you tracked in on your left palm. It has no memory of visitors and no anticipation of return. It simply is — fully, densely, luminously, and loudly what it is, in a state so stable that change would require an act of violence against its equilibrium.

You were only here for a few minutes. It felt like an hour. It felt like a century. The sound is still in your chest when you crawl back through the seam into the daylight, and it stays there for some time afterward, a low bronze hum that the doctor cannot find the source of and that you cannot bring yourself to describe, because you are not sure the language you know contains the words for what this place is.
