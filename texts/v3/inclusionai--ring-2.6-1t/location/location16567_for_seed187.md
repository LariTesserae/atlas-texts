## The Resin Floor

You find it by descending. Not a dramatic drop — just a slow, imperceptible sinking of the ground, a gentle compression of the earth over so much time that all slopes have learned to lie down. The basin is perhaps two hundred paces across, a shallow bowl worn into something older than stone. Whatever rock once defined it has long since been pulverized and swallowed and reconstituted as substrate — a living floor that is neither soil nor floor nor root but all three, fused into a single membrane no more than a hand's depth thick, yet dense enough to bear weight, alive enough to slowly close any footprint you leave within a week. Maybe longer. Time here is patient in ways that aren't patience.

The air is cool and faintly wet, the kind of damp that doesn't fall as rain but simply *is* — condensation drawn from the breathing of ten thousand organisms, a perpetual film of moisture on every surface, a haze that never thickens into fog but never fully lifts. The temperature is the temperature of a cellar in summer, of river-stone pulled from a shaded stream. You feel it on your forearms, on the backs of your knees. It is not unpleasant. It is the temperature of places that have never been touched by the decision of whether to be warm or cold.

And the light — it is normal. That's the wrong word, but it's the most accurate. The sky above is accessible, not blocked, but the canopy is so elevated and so thin that the sun passes through it the way light passes through a dusty window. Greens shift. Shadows are always slightly greener than they should be.

The trees — you want to call them trees, but they aren't. Not exactly. They are the nearest word. They rise from the substrate like exhalations, tall, many times taller than you, but so slender they seem to be made of breath held in shape. Their trunks are grey-white, veined, semi-translucent, and no thicker than a human thigh, yet they do not bend even in wind because their root systems are continental. Every one of them is anchored to every other one, a mesh of roots so dense and so old that the space between trees is not truly empty — it is threaded, cross-hatched, woven through with filaments you can see if you kneel down and part the substrate with your hands: white, then amber, then black as they deepen, carrying sugars and signals and something that looks, if you watch long enough, almost intentional.

Between the tree-things, the air is full of hanging filaments — not vines, not moss, not anything with a proper name. They drape from height to height like the rigging of abandoned ships, catching condensation, dripping it in lines. The sound of each drop is tiny, but there are so many that the aggregate is a constant hiss, a white noise of water touching water touching the substrate and being absorbed so quickly it barely registers as a wet spot before the living floor drinks it in.

This is where the old machines live.

They are not ruins — that word implies collapse, implies failure. These have *settled*. You come upon them the way you come upon boulders: they are simply part of the terrain now. An enormous cylinder, eight feet in diameter, its walls thick with corrosion so advanced it has become geological, threaded through with roots that enter through old valve fittings and exit through cracks in the casting, carrying the cylinder's own oxidized iron back into the soil as nutrient. You can see where the piston once moved — a smooth, glassy channel worn into the interior wall, now colonized by a species of tubular moss that grows in perfect straight lines along the old axis of travel, as though following a memory of the machine's function. The moss is faintly bioluminescent at its tips. Not bright. A faint blue-green pulse, steady and rhythmic, roughly once every four seconds. It never varies.

Elsewhere, a switchboard the size of a wall has become a scaffold for a flowering vine whose blossoms open and close on a cycle that matches — or once matched, or have given birth to — the switchboard's old rhythms. The copper contacts have greened into copperite, and the vine's tendrils rest in the sockets where operators' fingers once worked toggles, and if you watch — truly watch, for an uncomfortable length of time — you can see the tendrils shift, almost imperceptibly, as though responding to a signal the vine no longer consciously receives but its body still remembers how to interpret.

The generators are the loudest things. Or rather, they are the things that set the tone for the loudness. Their housings are intact — pragmatic, industrial, cast iron and steel — but every moving part has long since seized or been dissolved and replaced by biological analogues. Root-tendrils rotate inside housings where gears once turned, driven by osmotic pressure differentials in the substrate rather than by combustion. They do not produce power in any useful electrical sense anymore, but they rotate, and the rotation produces a sound: a low, constant, resonant hum, pitched in the range of a large diesel engine heard from across a field. It does not waver. It has not wavered in longer than anyone can know. It is the baseline sound of this place, the drone beneath everything else.

Above the drone, the insects. They are legion — beetles with iridescent carapaces the size of thumbnail, flying in patterns that are not swarms but formations, loose spirals and figure-eights that hover in specific columns of air between the tree-things. Wasps with mud-colored bodies building nests not in branches but in the hollow interiors of old ventilation stacks, their paper walls made from chewed substrate mixed with rust particles, giving the nests a reddish patina. Moths enormous as hands drift between the filaments, their wings beating with a sound like soft scissors cutting cloth. The sheer volume of insect life creates a texture to the air — a buzz, a flutter, a constant particulate motion that you feel on your skin before you can distinguish any individual creature.

Birds. Smaller than you expect. They nest in the bones of the machines — in the barrel of a cylinder, in the hollow chest of a decommissioned pump assembly, in the gaps between corroded steel plates where substrate has not yet filled the void. Their calls are sharp and frequent, layered, overlapping, a chatter that rises and falls but never silences. They are territorial but the territories are fractal — nested within one another, so that walking any distance puts you through three or four distinct zones of birdsong, each with its own dominant call and its own cadence of alarm.

Small mammals thread along the root paths. You glimpse them — pale-furred, quick-eyed things with forepaws adapted for gripping root-filaments, traveling above the substrate on the network of living cable that crisscrosses two meters above the forest floor. They are silent. They have no need for sound; the world provides enough.

Amphibians in the pools. The water pockets are dark and still and surprisingly deep — depressions in the ancient surface where the substrate has not yet fully colonized the vertical dimension. The amphibians are slick, mottled, and large as a spread hand. They do not jump. They pull themselves slowly from pool to pool through root-channels that hold enough moisture for the transit. Their eyes are black and reflective and they look at you with an expression you will interpret as boredom or wisdom depending on how long you've been standing here and how thoroughly the hum has entered your chest.

Nothing changes.

This is the part that sits in the back of your throat once you've been here for an hour, or a day, or three. The place does not change. The leaves that fall are absorbed before they reach the ground. The insects that die are consumed before they stop moving. The water that evaporates falls again within minutes, condensed on the cooler surfaces of the machine-housings or the tallest tree-things, and returns in the same quantity to the same pools. The roots rotate their slow generations inside the iron, and the iron releases its molecules at a rate so gradual it is indistinguishable from permanence.

You can sit here and watch for an hour, or five hours, and the landscape in front of you will be indistinguishable from the landscape behind you. The fauna rearrange themselves through the same spaces. The filament-drips fall at the same intervals. The humming does not fluctuate by a single hertz. There are gradients of time — the beetles move faster than the amphibians, the filaments sway faster than the roots, the birds fastest of all — but none of these gradients create change. They are cycles, perfect and closed, each one meshing with the others in a clockwork of mutual reinforcement that has been running for so long it no longer has a starting point.

The place feels finished. It feels like the world's last stable state, as though everything else has already eroded or burned or been moved and this basin, this grove, this room-sized cathedral of thin trees and old iron and ten million wet and living things, is what remains when all processes have equilibrated and there is nothing left to do but hum.

The condensation gathers on your jacket. A moth lands on your shoulder and opens and closes its wings without looking at you. Somewhere above, a bird calls — a sound like a nail drawn once across the teeth of a comb — and it is answered, immediately, from three directions, and the answer is answered, and the cacophony settles back into baseline, and the hum continues.

It was like this before you arrived. It will be like this after, if there is an after, which here feels less certain than it should.
