# The Rooted Borough

You come to it through a long switchback on a cliff face still scarred by the last ice. The rock is raw — striated, freshly exposed, the grey of newly broken teeth. Lichens have already colonized the cracks in thin white seams, as though the place is healing itself in real time. But the switchback ends, and the ground simply *opens*, and what opens is not what you expect at this altitude, not in this cold.

The basin is roughly the size of a walled town — three hundred paces across, ringed by the remnants of the cliff you climbed, which rise in pale, sharp shelves above the basin's edge. The walls channel wind and cloud inward, and the air is always thick with moisture: not rain exactly, but a persistent condensation that beads on every surface, beading on the fine hairs of mosses, collecting in the concave fronds of liverworts, running in thin silver threads down every vertical thing. It is not wet so much as *weeping*. Everything drips. Everything gleams.

The floor of the basin is not ground. That is the first thing you understand, standing at the rim and looking down. There is no mineral soil visible anywhere. What covers the basin floor, edge to edge, is life — a layered, breathing mass of vegetation so dense and so continuous that it has the structural coherence of a city. It has, unmistakably, the *form* of a city.

Closest to the basin walls, where the cliff runoff descends in thin rivulets, the growth is low and impossibly thick: cushion plants and mosses pressed together into a mattress that is pale green, grey-green, almost silver where the light catches the moisture on their surfaces. This is the substrate — the foundation layer — and it is soft underfoot, spongy, yielding, the way a living thing is yielding. Walking on it you feel it compress and slowly recover. It is warm relative to the air, and it exhales a smell like wet hay and something older, something fungal and mineral.

From this base rise the structures.

They are not trees, though they are tree-like. They are columnar growths of interlocked sedges, clubmosses, and something that looks like a dwarf conifer but is not — it is a heath relative, dense and dark, its branches knitted so tightly with aerial roots and climbing mosses that the columns appear to be carved from a single material, the way a stonemason might shape a pier. These columns stand at irregular intervals, two or three feet in diameter, rising five to eight feet high, and they spread at their tops into broad, flat canopy mats — not leaves exactly, but layered fronds and wiry stems fused into continuous platforms overhead. The platforms overlap from column to column, creating a roof — not complete, not sealed, but dense enough that the light beneath is dim and green-tinged, the quality of light under a heavy cloud diffused through several additional layers of life.

Between the columns, the "streets" are narrow — shoulder-width or less — and they are not bare. They are carpeted in the same dense cushion moss, threaded with the pale runners of a bindweed-like plant that has no flowers you can see, only tight spirals of tiny leaves that grip the surface and pull it together. The runners stitch the floor into a single textile. You cannot see the gap between one cushion and the next. The streets are alive under your feet.

Higher up, where the columnar canopies nearly meet, there is a second layer — a network of what appear to be woody stems, but they are not wood. They are the structural stems of a massive epiphytic orchid relative, pale and jointed, that has colonized every upper surface and draped itself from every overhang in curtains of root and fiber. Through this lattice, moisture collects and hangs. Droplets form on the fine root-hairs and gather until they are heavy enough to fall, and there is a constant, soft patter — not rain, not dripping from a leak, but the sound of a place that is *perspiring*. It is the loudest sound down here. It never stops.

In places the canopy-mats have grown together into continuous bridges — you can see them arching between columns that were once separate, now bound into a single structure by years (decades? centuries?) of growth. These bridges thicken, and on them smaller plants sprout: ferns no taller than your hand, their fronds curled inward against the cold, and tiny succulents clustered in the mossy seams. Everything grows on everything else. A dead branch becomes a shelf for a crustose lichen that becomes a foothold for a filmy fern that becomes a nursery for a creeping vine that thickens the whole assembly by a fraction of an inch. The place is not growing *in* a location. It is growing *into itself*.

There are no animals. Not one. You see no tracks in the moss, no droppings, no chewed fronds. Occasionally, in the far corner of your perception, you sense movement — a tremor in a curtain of root, a faint sway that is not wind — but when you look directly, there is nothing. The place is alive in the way a body is alive, all process and response, but the large moving things are absent. The fauna is microbial, fungal, entomological at most — the miniature, the unseen, the things that work in the dark wet interior of the substrate. The ecosystem runs on growth and decay in equal measure. You see patches where a column has begun to soften, its base spongy with decomposition, and already the mosses are thickening there, preparing to absorb the nutrients, the architecture reshaping itself in slow real time, a city that rebuilds itself from its own ruins without ceasing to be itself.

The light is dim and does not change much. The cloud cover sits low on the cliffs, and the sun is a pale disc behind several layers of vapour and canopy. The green-tinged twilight under the columnar canopy makes it hard to tell how much time has passed. In the open patches near the basin walls, where the canopy breaks, the light is brighter but colder — a grey-white that sharpens the edges of things and makes the wet surfaces glitter. There is no warmth in it. The temperature is low enough that your breath makes clouds, and the water everywhere feels near to freezing. The cold keeps things slow. Growth here is patient. Decay is patient. The whole place moves at the speed of a root tip.

And yet there is an undeniable intentionality to it. Walking through the columns, along the narrow living streets, you feel the resemblance to a settlement more than a resemblance to a forest. The columns are spaced with something like regularity — not identical, not gridded, but clustered and nested the way buildings cluster in an old town, the way density breeds density, the way one structure abuts another and they share a wall or a roof or a root system. The bridges overhead form at consistent heights, creating a second level that you can reach by climbing a column of springy heath-wood and pulling yourself onto a broad, damp platform of fused canopy. From up there, you can see the whole basin — the pale moss floor, the dark columns rising from it, the lattice of pale epiphytic stems overhead, the mist hanging in layers, the cliff walls beyond closing the place in like a walled district.

It is quiet. Not silent. The dripping, the occasional creak of a stem thickening or a bridge settling under new growth, the faint hiss of condensation forming on a leaf surface — these are the only sounds. No wind penetrates the basin's walls effectively; the air moves sluggishly, in convection cells you can almost see in the mist. The place breathes.

It occurred to you, standing there, that if something were to fall — a stone from the cliff, a dead column collapsing — the place would absorb it. The mosses would take the minerals. The fungi would take the cellulose. The space it occupied would be filled, by growth, within a season. This is not a place that tolerates gaps. Everything is occupied. Everything is in use. The city of plants does not have a past tense. It has only the present continuous — *growing, covering, knitting, thickening, dripping, dimming* — and it will go on like this until the cold or the altitude or the slow starvation of its own nutrients forces a change that will not come soon, because everything here is recent, the basin itself is young, the sharp edges of the cliff are still losing stone, and the rain has only just begun to find its rhythm on this new, living, breathing, silent, wet, green, cold, enclosed, walled, miniature, architecture-shaped, endlessly repaired surface of the world.
