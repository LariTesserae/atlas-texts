## The Drowned Range

The water doesn't begin — it simply is.

You step off the heli-ramp and your boots hit concrete that is already wet, already salt-eaten, already cracking along seams that the maintenance crews filled decades ago with a polymer that has since gone brittle and grey. The platform you stand on is maybe forty meters across, bolted to the crown of what was once called Mount Averill, or something like it. The name is on a riveted plaque near the door, half-scraped away by decades of wind-driven grit. The elevation gauge — a digital readout still powered by some stubborn battery — reads 2,460 meters above the old datum. That datum being sea level, which no longer means what it used to.

The ocean below is not blue. It is a deep, cold green that darkens to near-black in the channels between the peaks. You are standing on one of perhaps forty mountaintops that break the surface of a drowned continental interior. The peaks form a rough archipelago — a range that someone once crossed by car, by foot, by train. Now you cross it by boat, by cable car, or not at all. The distances between the summits range from a few hundred meters to several kilometers of open, heaving water. The sea is never still. The swell has a confused, sourceless rhythm — wind-driven chop overlapping the deep, long-period swells that travel across the basin from storms you can't yet see. Every surface vibrates with it. You feel it in your teeth.

The mountain itself is smooth. Not smooth like sanded wood — smooth like a bone that has been tumbled in water for a million years. The original granite is still there beneath your hand, but it's been ground to curves, hollowed into shallow bowls, polished to a dull sheen where the stone is exposed above the waterline and the lichen hasn't yet claimed it. Most of it has been claimed. The lower two-thirds of Averill — everything below about forty meters above the current waterline — is submerged, and the waterline is a tide mark: a black stripe of kelp, mineral deposits, and salt crust that wraps the mountain like a belt. At low tide, the exposed rock glistens. At high tide, only the top of the platform stays dry.

Kelp. That's the first thing you notice as vegetation — kelp everywhere, in forests so thick they look like terrestrial jungle canopy when you look down into the water. The stipes are thick as your torso, the blades broad and leathery, forming a dense mat that moves in the current like a field of grass in wind. Beneath the kelp layer, the water darkens further. There are mid-depths you cannot see into. Shapes move there — large, deliberate shapes. You never see them clearly. You see a shadow pass beneath you, forty meters down, and your hand goes to the rail. The marine biologist on the last rotation told you it's probably just grey nurse sharks, or large rays. She said it calmly. She didn't believe it herself.

But the visible life is astonishing in its density. Mussels and barnacles crust every submerged surface. Gooseneck barnacles cluster so thickly on the lower cables that the cables look man-made, organic — like chitinous growths on the infrastructure. Cormorants and gulls cover every flat surface above the waterline, standing shoulder to shoulder, their calls a wall of noise that mixes with the wind and the sea into a single, roaring wash of sound. There is never silence here. There is a baseline: the boom and hiss of surf against rock, the metallic whine of wind through cable stays, the low rumble of the generators two levels below your feet, the distant crash of something heavy falling into water — a section of old scaffold, maybe, or a bluff edge that finally gave way. You stop hearing the individual sounds after the first day. You live inside the roar.

The industrial installations are old. Not ruins — they're still functioning, barely, in the way that old machines function when no one has the resources or authorization to shut them down and no one has the initiative to maintain them beyond what keeps them from catastrophically failing. The platform beneath your feet contains a desalination unit, a power relay station, and a weather-monitoring array. The desal unit coughs out a fresh trickle every six hours into a tank that serves the six-person crew. The power relay routes electricity from a geothermal tap on the adjacent peak through underwater cables — cables that fail every few months and require divers to repair in water cold enough to kill in under twenty minutes without proper equipment. The weather array is the most reliable thing. It has to be. The weather here is violent in its suddenness. A clear morning can become a force-eight gale within an hour. The warm current from the south collides with the cold upwelling from the deep basin and the atmosphere above it simply convulses. Lightning arcs between the peaks. Rain doesn't fall here — it's driven, horizontal, so thick you can't see the railing from the door.

There are other platforms. You can see two from where you stand: one on a peak to the northwest, maybe seven kilometers away, a red light blinking at its peak; another to the southeast, just a smudge of artificial light on a darker smudge of rock. Others exist beyond the visible range. Communication is maintained by radio — old, crackly, occasionally dropping out for hours when the electromagnetic weather gets wild. There is a cargo barge that makes the circuit between the platforms once a month, delivering supplies and collecting waste. It's the only regular contact with the outside world, and its arrival is the closest thing this place has to an event.

The built environment is sparse but tenacious. Metal everywhere — steel, iron, corroding aluminum. Walkways. Chain bridges. Stairways that descend from the platform to lower ledges where old supply depots sit, their doors warped, their contents slowly becoming part of the ecology. Rats live there. So do nesting petrels and, in one case, a family of foxes that swam out from the mainland decades ago and never left. The foxes are lean and salt-stained. They hunt the bird colonies at dawn. The crew has named the alpha female. She has no interest in you.

The atmosphere of the place is defined by edges. You are always standing at the border between stone and air and water. The horizon is a suggestion more than a line — the peaks are low enough and far enough apart that in fog, which comes often and without warning, you can be completely surrounded by white nothing, hearing the water but not seeing it, the whole world reduced to the sound of it and the feel of cold moisture on your face. In clear weather, the scale is staggering. You can see the curvature of individual waves. You can watch a storm front approach as a wall of dark glass, lit from within by flashes. The green of the sea is beautiful in a way that feels geological — not alive so much as ancient, the color of deep minerals, of compressed time.

At night, the peaks are lit by their aviation beacons and by the platforms' floodlights, which attract clouds of insects and, in turn, the birds that feed on them. The sound changes character after dark. The birds quiet (some; others call more). The generators take center stage — their hum, their occasional metallic ping as heated metal contracts. The water sounds closer in the dark. More deliberate.

It is not a place that wants you. It is not a place that rejects you. It is a place that has forgotten you are here — ancient, eroded to essentials, still churning with a life that predates and will outlast every structure bolted to its skin. You are a temporary annotation on a very long sentence. The mountain will be here, smooth and wave-carved, long after the last rivet rusts through and the last platform slumps into the green dark.

The water laps at the base. The generators hum. The birds cry. The wind does not stop.
