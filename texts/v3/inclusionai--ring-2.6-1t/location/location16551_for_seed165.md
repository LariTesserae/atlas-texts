# The Karst Above Breath

You do not arrive here. You are permitted proximity.

The ridgeline that marks the edge of accessible ground is not a line at all but a suggestion — a place where stone stops pretending to be continuous and begins instead to become sky. The elevation is such that the air has forgotten its loyalty to your lungs. It gives you about sixty percent of what you need and does so without apology, the way a deep room gives you ceiling. You simply exist inside it.

The terrain is karst — but not the word you know. Not the textbook sinkholes and caving tropes. This is karst that has been allowed to become what it always threatened to become: a lattice of void. The limestone — it *is* limestone, though it insists otherwise by its color, which is sometimes white, sometimes a bruise-purple, sometimes a blue so pale it registers as forgetting — has been dissolved not by water alone but by something water carries on its better days. The solutional channels run hundreds of meters deep, and from the surface they look like ordinary cracks until you notice that some of them echo back a syllable before you speak.

That is the sound. It is always happening.

The mountain groans. Not metaphorically. Not wind through a narrow fissure, though there are those too. The ridgeline produces a continuous low-frequency resonance that sits below hearing and above feeling — you sense it in the molars, in the fluid of your inner ear, in the pads of your fingers when you press flat against the cliff face. It rises and falls in cycles so long that a single oscillation can take the better part of an hour. There is a deep bass note that comes from somewhere beneath the root systems of the world, and threaded through it, at irregular intervals, a sharp crystalline ping — like a fingernail flicked against the rim of a wine glass the size of a chapel. The pings arrive from no discernible direction. They do not echo. They simply occur and then stop, and in the silence that follows you can hear your own blood rearranging itself.

The place is not wind-blasted. Wind here is an occasional visitor, not a resident. The air is still and cold and damp in a way that does not condense into rain so much as it *stays* — suspended moisture that makes the world look perpetually viewed through the breath on glass. At dawn — there are dawns, though they lack conviction, the light arriving as a pale suggestion rather than a declaration — the stone weeps. Thin films of water bead on every exposed surface, drawn from the air itself, and then vanish within an hour as if the rock had simply licked them away. In sheltered concavities, small pools collect. They are no more than puddles, ankle-deep, their surfaces so still they do not reflect so much as *remember* the sky above them. If you look into one long enough, the reflection it returns is several seconds delayed. By the time you notice, the moment it shows has already passed.

Nothing grows here. That is too strong. A few things persist. Tufts of a pale, wire-thick grass cling to sheltered ledges, their roots threading into the dissolution channels where the stone is softest. Lichen in thin grey films coats the north-facing surfaces — not spreading, not growing, simply *present*, as if it arrived a very long time ago and has not found reason to leave. There are no trees. There are no shrubs. There is moss in exactly four places, each patch no larger than a closed fist, each separated by kilometers of bare stone, and each, if you visited them in sequence, would feel as though it belonged to a slightly different kind of world.

The fauna is impossibly sparse. There is a species of pale beetle, no larger than a grain of rice, that lives in the thin wet seams between rock faces. It has no eyes — there is nothing here worth seeing — and it navigates by vibration, reading the mountain's constant low groan the way a sailor reads the sea. There are, on occasion, birds. They pass through. They do not belong. You might see one in a week: a dark shape against the pale sky, flying with the frantic purposelessness of something that knows it has made a mistake. You will never see where it lands.

The built things — and they exist, scattered, faint — are not ruins. They have not decayed because they were never complete. A wall that rises a meter and a half from the scree, built of fitted stone without mortar, then simply stops. A cairn, six stones tall, on a flat prominence, the stones too regular, too pale, the wrong rock for the mountain they sit upon. A shallow depression in the stone that might be a foundation or might be a sinkhole that chose to be square. No tools have been left behind. No evidence of who raised these partial gestures toward shelter, or why they stopped. The mountain does not preserve them out of respect. It preserves them out of indifference, the way a word repeated enough times loses its meaning but does not disappear.

Here is the thing that cannot be explained and does not need to be: the space here is not continuous.

Walk a straight line along the ridgeline and count your steps. Turn around and walk back. The count will be different. Not much — a few steps, maybe a dozen — but consistently, measurably, reproductibly different. The terrain does not change between the two passes. The stone is the same. The puddles are in the same places. But the *distance* has quietly rearranged itself while you were not looking at it, the way a sentence can change meaning between readings without any of the words moving.

There are folds. In certain spots, standing still, you can feel the air thicken — not altitude, not cold, but a spatial density, as though the place beneath your feet has doubled over and is lying on top of itself. Looking out from these points, the landscape appears normal but *slightly redundant*: two ridgelines where logic permits one, a shadow that falls uphill, a brief flicker of a structure on a distant slope that, when you walk toward it, resolves into a rock formation that resembles architecture the way a cloud resembles a face.

The mountain has always been this way. The erosion confirms it — the stone is old, deeply weathered, settled into forms that no longer correspond to the forces that shaped them. The dissolution channels are too deep for the water that feeds them. The puddles are too still for their altitude. The whole place has spent so long in its own equilibrium that equilibrium itself has begun to curve.

There is no day-to-day change here. The temperature holds at something just above the threshold where cold becomes a philosophical concept. The moisture falls and retreats on a schedule so consistent it might be mechanical. The resonance drones. The beetle moves through its seams. The light does its pale best. Nothing accelerates. Nothing diminishes. The landscape is a single, sustained note — not frozen, not dead, simply so deeply settled into itself that change would require a force larger than intention.

You can stand at the ridgeline's edge — the false edge, the one that looks like the last solid ground — and look down into what appears to be a vast canyon filled with pale fog. The fog does not move. The bottom does not appear. Every few minutes, a sound rises from it that is almost a voice, almost a word, almost the thing you were about to say but held back. It is not an echo. It is not the wind. It is the mountain, clearing its throat in a language it has spoken to itself for so long it has forgotten there was ever anyone else to listen.

You are permitted proximity. That is enough. That is more than most places offer.
