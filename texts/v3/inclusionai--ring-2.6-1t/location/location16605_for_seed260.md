## The Plateau of Unfinished Psalm

You hear it before you see anything at all.

It is not a single sound. It is a chord — sustained, massive, tuned to a frequency that your skeleton recognizes before your ears can parse it. You feel the vibration through whatever you're standing on, which is already wrong, because what you're standing on should not bear weight. It should not exist. But it does, and it hums.

The elevation is such that the sky has become a ceiling rather than a vista. You are high — dizzyingly, punishingly high — and the air is thin enough that each breath feels borrowed, as though the atmosphere here is only a suggestion, a courtesy extended by a host that isn't sure it wants you. The temperature sits at the kind of cool that doesn't sting but *preserves*, the way a cellar is cool, the way a cave is cool, the way a thing stays unchanged for a long time when it's cold enough. It is not the cold of ice. It is the cold of patience.

And the ground.

The ground is still deciding what it is.

You can see this if you look down. The surface is not soil, not rock, not sand — it is a substrate in the process of declaring itself. Veins of something dark and glassy push up in parallel ridges, splitting and re-splitting like sentences written in a language that keeps revising itself. Elsewhere the terrain has the texture of a concretion that hasn't finished hardening — porous, faintly warm to the touch, the consistency of a thought half-completed. In places the surface buckles upward in smooth domes, as though something beneath is breathing. In other places it fractures into tessellated plates separated by finger-widths of nothing, dark gaps that emit a low sub-bass tone when the wind — there is always wind, here, or something that behaves like wind — passes through them.

The sound builds from these gaps. It multiplies. Every fissure is an organ pipe. Every ridge a tuning fork. The whole plateau is an instrument being played by forces too large and too slow to witness directly, and what you hear is their long exhalation — a sound like a mountain range remembering its own name, a drone that lives in the space between one note and the next and has no intention of resolving.

Now look around. Look at the *growth*.

There is no water here. You have checked. You have looked in every channel, every depression, every concavity in the trembling ground. There is nothing to drink. There is nothing that should be alive. And yet the vegetation — if that word can be stretched this far — is *lush*. Impossibly, aggressively lush.

They rise from the fractured mineral surface in broad, flat rosettes the color of dried blood and tarnished copper. Not leaves, exactly. Flanges. Laminar extensions that unfold in slow spirals from a central stalk that has no pith, no cambium, nothing terrestrial about it at all. They are mineral themselves — silicate-based, perhaps, or something stranger, their tissue a lattice of crystalline fibers that catch the dim light and refract it into colors that don't have names. They photosynthesize, perhaps, but not from sunlight alone. They pull energy from vibration. From the ceaseless tone that the plateau emits. You can see it in the way they sway in perfect synchrony with the loudest frequency, their edges bending like cilia responding to a stimulus, feeding on sound.

In the low places between the rosettes, a second kind of growth sprawls — thin, filamentous, pale as spider silk. It spreads in mats over the exposed substrate, and where it touches the dark glassy veins, the veins glow faintly, a bioluminescent phosphorescence the color of tarnished silver. The filaments seem to be conducting something. Electricity, or whatever passes for electricity here. They network across dozens of meters, and if you watch them long enough — and you should, because time moves strangely here — you will see pulses travel along them, slow and rhythmic, like signals. Or like a language.

There is no fauna. Nothing moves with intention. Nothing hunts, nothing flees, nothing reproduces in any way you can recognize. And yet the place does not feel dead. It feels *occupied*. The vegetation responds to your presence — subtly, but it does. The rosettes angle their broad surfaces toward you when you step near, as though you are a light source. The filaments dim where you cast a shadow. You are a disruption in a system that has only known geological rhythms, and it is recalibrating around you.

The land itself is evidence of a world under construction. Cliffs stand half-formed, their faces still striated with the vertical grooves of upward-flowing material that cooled mid-ascent. Ridgelines are sharp where they should be rounded by ages of weathering, and in the distance — because you can see a great distance here, the elevation granting a view that extends and extends until haze and the curvature of a horizon you don't quite trust cut it short — in the distance, other formations rise: towers, needles, mesas, all of them fresh-looking, all of them still shedding dust andite fragments that the thin wind carries in slow curtains across the gaps between.

And scattered across this open, aching expanse — small structures.

They are not large. None of them rises above the height of a person. They are built from the same dark glassy material as the substrate, cut or broken into blocks and stacked with a precision that is almost but not quite perfect. Walls. Small enclosures. What might be altars or markers. In one place, a low-walled rectangle surrounds a depression in the ground where the filaments grow thickest, converging like roots toward a center point, as though the builders knew exactly what they were harnessing. In another, a single standing slab has been set upright in a cradle of smaller stones, and it vibrates. You can feel it from thirty paces — a sympathetic frequency, matching the chord of the plateau, but tuned a quarter-step sharp, dissonant, deliberate.

There is no writing on the structures. No decoration. No evidence of who built them or why. The construction is primitive — no mortar, no metal, no mechanical advantage beyond leverage and patience. Whoever raised these blocks did so by hand, over time, with an understanding of the land's hum that borders on intimacy.

And the light.

The light is dim but it is not dark. It comes from no identifiable source — there is a sun, perhaps, but it sits behind a permanent overcast of mineral particulate suspended in the thin atmosphere, a haze ofite dust that diffuses everything into a flat, silver-grey luminance with no shadows to speak of. The light does not change. There is no morning here, no evening. Just this: the dim, unchanging pallor that makes distance and proximity feel identical, that erases depth until you almost walk into a formation you saw from far away and realized, too late, that it was closer than it appeared. Or farther. Distance itself is unreliable here. You walk what feels like a hundred meters to a ridge and it takes twenty minutes. You turn around and a formation you placed behind you appears to be beside you. The plateau does not respect Euclidean geometry in all places. Corners exceed ninety degrees. Parallel lines converge and then diverge again. At the edges of your vision, the landscape seems to have more *content* than it should — features stacked behind features, a depth of detail that thins and vanishes when you turn to look directly. The reality here is not broken so much as it is *fractal*, self-similar at every scale, and what you see depends on whether you are looking or being seen.

You can hear it all, though. The sound is constant, layered, deafening in its totality even though no single element of it is sharp. The drone of the ground. The crystalline whistle of the wind through the filament networks. The deep tectonic groan of the plateau still rising, millimeter by millimeter, a process that will continue for centuries. And beneath all of it, almost subliminal, a tonal shift — a harmonic that rises and falls with a rhythm that is almost, but not quite, a breath.

The place is alive, but not with life as biology defines it. It is alive the way a crystal is alive — growing, resonating, organizing itself from disorder, beautiful and deafening and utterly unconcerned with you.

You stand on the Plateau of Unfinished Psalm, and the plateau sings, and it has been singing since before the first stone settled, and it will sing long after you are mineral dust scattered in a dim silver light on a ground that is still deciding what it is.

The sound swells.

The rosettes turn toward you.

You are very, very small here, and the song has no end.
