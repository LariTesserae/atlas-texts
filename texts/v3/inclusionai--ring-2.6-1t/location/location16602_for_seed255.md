## The Low Boroughs

---

You hear it before you see it. That is the first fact of the place.

Sound arrives in layers. There is a low, pervasive grinding that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere — a subsonic pulse that you feel in the balls of your feet before you register it with your ears. Over that, water: not a stream exactly, but a thousand small runnels, capillaries of flow threading through channels no wider than your hand, whispering and gurgling and occasionally roaring as they hit a drop you can't see in the dark. And beneath both of those, something rhythmic. A knocking. Not mechanical, not organic — something that has simply *always been happening here* and so cannot be called strange anymore, though it should be.

The light is almost nothing. A grey dimness that has no source you can identify — not sky, not luminescence, not reflected sun. The air itself seems to hold a low luminance, the way a bruise holds color. You can see perhaps sixty feet in any direction before the world dissolves into suggestion: shapes, edges, the implication of walls or columns or faces that might be geological or might be carved. Beyond sixty feet, the darkness is not empty. It is *dense*, as though the air itself thickens and refuses to carry detail.

You are standing on a floor that is not a floor.

It rises toward you in a gentle slope, made of a material you cannot name. It is not stone, not metal, not concrete, not bone, but it has the qualities of all of these. It is smooth in patches, ridged in others, and warm to the touch despite the cool air. The surface is damp — always damp — and your boot leaves a print that fills with condensation a moment after you lift it. There is an inch of standing water in every low spot, and the water is not clear. It has a faint opalescence, a milkiness that is not pollution but something closer to saturation, as though the water is holding as much of the material around it as it can.

The place is a basin. You realize this after perhaps an hour of walking. The terrain slopes gently downward in every direction toward a center you cannot reach because *something is in the way*. The low boroughs — for that is what they, the few people who have been here, have come to call them — are a construction of impossible scale built at ground level in a shallow depression that may have been carved or may have been poured. Everything is constructed. There is no natural substrate. When you dig — and people have dug, obsessively, desperately — you find more of the same material, laid down in strata that suggest centuries of deposition, each layer slightly different in texture and color, as though the builders changed their recipe with the generations. There is no bedrock. There may not be a bottom.

The structures.

They are everywhere — that is the first overwhelming thing. The density of construction is staggering. Walls abut walls. Arches buttress buttresses. Stairways lead to platforms that lead to ramps that lead to ceilings that become floors of the level above. It is not a city, exactly, because there is no planning logic you can detect, no grid, no district, no center. But it is not random either. There is a *grammar* to it. Repetitions. Rhythms. The same arch profile recurs at scales from hand-span to cathedral-vast, and in between are passages so narrow you must turn sideways and chambers so vast their ceilings disappear into the grey dimness overhead. The walls are thick — two feet, three feet, sometimes five — and they are *layered*. You can see the construction history in cross-section where erosion or damage has exposed an inner wall beneath an outer wall beneath a still-older inner wall. Generation upon generation of building, each phase slightly misaligned with the last, creating a subtle lurching geometry that makes your inner ear unreliable.

The technology that built this place is primitive in the extreme and yet the result is anything but simple. There are no precision cuts. No right angles. The material — whatever it is — has been shaped by hand tools: rough chisels, stone hammers, bone gouges, woven baskets for carrying. You find tool marks everywhere, overlapping, frantic, patient. The scale of the project is incommensurate with the crudeness of the means. This is a place that took an almost incomprehensible number of workers an almost incomprehensible number of years to make, and they did it with nothing more than leverage, rope, and the relentless application of effort. Who were they? You don't know. No writing. No art. Only the building itself, and the building does not speak of its makers. It speaks only of the work.

There is no vegetation. Nothing. No moss on the walls, no lichen, no fungal growth — or rather, there *might* be. There are things on the walls, in the damp crevices, pale and spreading, but they are not fungal in any sense you recognize. They are mineral. They grow the way calcite grows in a cave, slow and indifferent, depositing themselves in branching filigrees that look almost like frost patterns or almost like veins. They are not alive. But they are not exactly not alive either. They respond to stimulus — touch one and the branching pattern will, over days, redirect itself toward or away from your finger. They drink the dampness. They pull it from the air. They leave behind a residue that is slightly harder than the material of the walls, and so the place is *growing*, slowly, of its own accord, in a fashion that has nothing to do with biology as you understand it. The erosion parameter — *emerging*, *under construction* — applies not just to the history of the place but to its present tense. It is still becoming. Things are still settling. The construction may not be finished. New filigrees appear on walls that were bare last season. Small protrusions extend from ledges. The basin deepens, imperceptibly, year by year.

The water is a system unto itself. It condenses on the upper surfaces of the construction — on those vast, rough ceilings that may be floors of a level above — and drips down in a constant, maddening patter that is the backbone of the place's sound. But it also flows laterally, through channels that were clearly carved or formed and which now carry water no one intended them to carry. The runnels have carved their own secondary erosion patterns into the walls of the primary construction, and these secondary patterns are beginning to take on the branching quality of the mineral growths. The place is hydrating itself. The dampness is not a feature of the climate. The dampness is a *process*. The air is cool enough that condensation forms on every surface, and the surfaces are packed so close together — the density, the near-touching — that there is no room for dry air. Everything is damp. Everything is slowly, patiently dissolving and reconstituting itself.

The fauna. There are things here, but rarely. You may go days without seeing one. What you see will be small, pale, eyeless, and fast — creatures that move through the wall-channels like water through the runnels. They are not insects, not rodents, not anything with a proper name. They have too many legs or not enough. Their bodies are faintly translucent, and you can see inside them a architecture of fine mineral threads, as though they have incorporated the place's own material into themselves. They drink condensation. They eat the filigree growths. They are preyed upon by something larger that you never see but that leaves behind splintered bone fragments in the passages — bone that is not bone, that is the same warm, unidentifiable material as everything else here, but shaped into the approximate structure of a vertebrate skeleton by some process no one can explain.

The sound. It is enormous. The density of the construction means that every drip, every footfall, every breath is reflected, refracted, amplified. The arches act as resonators. The narrow passages act as flue pipes. When wind finds its way in from outside — and there *is* an outside, you know, somewhere above and beyond the dim reaches of the basin's rim — it enters through a thousand small openings and is immediately taken up by the geometry of the place, turned into a moaning, thrumming chord that shifts as the wind shifts. At night — if there is night here, if the grey dimness cycles at all — the sound deepens. The place groans. It sounds like something enormous and patient turning over in its sleep.

And it is strange. Not horrible, not beautiful, but *strange* in the way that a word repeated too many times becomes strange. The wrongness is proportional. The closer you look, the more the place seems almost recognizable — a doorway that is almost a doorway, a room that is almost a room, a stairway that is almost going somewhere — but the proportions are off by just enough that your mind keeps stuttering, keeps catching on the discrepancy. The arches are too wide. The passages are too tall. The stairs rise at an angle that should be possible and yet isn't. You can walk them, but your body knows they're wrong.

This is the Low Boroughs. It was built, or it is building itself, or it is being built still by the water and the mineral growths and the pale creatures threading through its walls. It is densely packed in the dark, cool air, extending in every direction past the edge of visibility, loud with its own slow processes, and it does not care whether you are here to see it.

It did not need you to begin. It will not notice when you leave.
