# The Bright Seam

---

You find it by accident, or by a kind of attention you didn't know you had. A crack in the ground no wider than your forearm, in a stretch of hillside that looks like every other stretch for miles — dry grass, exposed limestone, the memory of old frost-heave. But if you press your eye close to the crack, light comes up at you. Not reflects off anything inside. *Comes up*, the way warmth comes off a road in August, except this is white, cool, and carries no heat.

The entrance resists being found twice. It shifts — not dramatically, but enough. A stone that was a handhold yesterday is now flush with the slope. The crack is half a palm wider on the left side than it was, narrower on the right. You could argue with yourself about whether you remember it correctly. It doesn't matter. You'll find it anyway, the way you find a word stuck on the tip of your tongue — by not trying, then trying all at once.

You slip through.

---

The first thing that's wrong is the size. The fissure opens into a space that shouldn't fit where it is. From outside, the hillside is modest — a gentle rise, nothing geological, nothing that should contain a room. But inside, there's a chamber. Maybe ten paces across, maybe seven deep. The ceiling is low — you can touch it with a flat hand, though if you straighten, the crown of your head clears it by a finger's width. The walls are raw stone. Not rough in the worn way of old caves, but *incomplete*, the way a wound is incomplete before it closes. The rock is pale — a chalky grey-white streaked with veins of something darker, almost blue, almost black — and it has the look of having been deposited rather than laid down by time. As if this chamber was poured.

The surfaces are not yet claimed by erosion. The floor is nearly level but not quite — there are shallow depressions and subtle rises, like the landscape of a world before rain had learned what to do with it. Small puddles sit in the hollows, not draining, not evaporating, simply *there*, clear and faintly warm to the touch, smelling of wet chalk and something faintly metallic, like a copper coin held under your tongue. The water does not reflect the ceiling. It reflects something else — a sky that doesn't exist, or exists somewhere else, pale and cloud-wracked, impossibly far below the water's surface if you look too long.

The light has no source. It is everywhere and nowhere. Bright enough to read by — bright enough to make you squint when you first step fully inside — but the walls cast no shadows. The ceiling casts none. Your body casts none, or casts one in a direction that doesn't correspond to where you think the light should be coming from. You see yourself dimly in the wet patches on the floor: a silhouette, but wrong — slightly too tall, or slightly too flat, or with your hands in positions you aren't making. Looking away from your reflection is easy. Deciding whether you actually looked away is not.

---

There is not much plant life here. Which is perhaps the wrong word for what's present.

At the edges of the chamber, where the walls meet the floor, there are things that grow. They are not roots, exactly. Not stems. Filaments — pale, translucent, threaded through the rock in networks so fine they look like cracks until you see them move. They move slowly. Not like plants tracking light. More like the settling of a resting animal. They pulse — if that's the word — in a rhythm that's close to a heartbeat but arrhythmic, accelerating, slowing, stopping for a beat, then resuming at a pace that doesn't match the previous one. They are wet with the seep-water and they glow faintly, a dull rose color, as if embarrassed by the brightness of the rest of the room.

On one wall, a patch of something moss-like clings to the stone. It is dark green, almost black, and dry to the touch despite the dampness of the room. When you breathe near it, it contracts — slowly, over seconds, like a sea anemone retreating. You watch it for a while. It does not reopen.

That's all. A few filaments, a patch of un-nameable moss. The chamber is mostly bare. The stone is the dominant presence, and it feels new, *willed* into place rather than accumulated.

---

But the life. The life is the thing.

The fauna is everywhere, and it is impossible to be sure you've seen any single creature twice.

There are insects — or insect-shaped things — the size of your smallest fingernail, iridescent, moving in paths that are too regular to be random and too irregular to be mechanical. They travel along the walls, across the ceiling, through the puddles without breaking the surface tension. Their bodies seem to phase slightly as they move — not translucent, but *uncertain*, as if the pixels of them are loading slightly out of order. When they pass behind a vein of dark stone, they do not reappear immediately on the other side. There is a delay. A fraction of a second, or several seconds, or — you glance away and back and they are gone entirely, and you cannot tell if this is because they've all passed out of sight or because they've ceased to exist until you look again.

Something small and warm moves along the base of the wall to your left. It has too many legs — or the right number, but they move in a sequence that's wrong, like a filmstrip shuffled. You crouch. The creature — you think it's a creature; it has the silhouette of something between a beetle and a mouse, but the silhouette keeps *softening*, the way a drawing does when you smudge it with your thumb — pauses and orients toward you. Its eyes, if they are eyes, are dark and wet and seem to focus a foot behind your face rather than at it.

It makes no sound.

Nothing here makes sound. This is the second thing you notice — the first being the light, the second being the silence. The insects do not buzz. Your footsteps on the damp floor make no noise, even when you stamp deliberately, even when you drop a small stone and watch it fall toward the ground in what might be slow motion or might be real-time with your sense of time misfiring. The stone lands — and there is no impact-sound, only an impression of one, felt in your chest like a swallowed thud.

The fauna goes about its business silently. Flights, crawls, sudden accelerations, creatures that may be hunting or may be courting or may be performing some behavior that has no analogue in your experience. A pair of larger shapes — pale, angular, like goats rendered in origami — stand motionless on the far side of the chamber, facing each other. You watch for five minutes. They do not move. You look down at the puddle between your feet, and when you look back, one of them is closer. Not much. Half a body-length. But it was *moving* and you did not see it move and you did not hear it.

---

The built things.

Against the far wall, half-consumed by the filament-networks, there is a structure. It is made of the same pale stone as the chamber itself, or of something that has become the same pale stone over time — it's impossible to tell if it was placed here or if it grew here. It is roughly the height of your waist and shaped like a partial rectangle: two uprights, a lintel that extends about two-thirds of the way across, as if someone started building a doorway and stopped, or as if the rest of it is still *forming* and hasn't arrived yet. The lintel is perfectly level. The uprights are not — they lean inward very slightly, at angles that are almost identical but not quite, as if the structure is holding its breath.

There are other traces. Scratches on the floor that might be tool-marks or might be claw-patterns or might be features of the stone itself that only look deliberate in this light. A depression in the floor, almost perfectly circular, the size of a cupped hand, that collects water and in that water you sometimes see a reflection that is not yours.

Someone has been here, or something *like* someone. Or the chamber is generating the idea of *someone having been here*, the way it generates the idea of light.

---

The temperature is cool but not cold — the air temperature of a cellar in autumn, or of skin under a thin shirt. The air itself is still. Not stagnant; *still*, the way held breath is still. There is a faint mineral taste at the back of your throat. Your lungs feel cleaner here than they did outside, as if the air is not exactly fresh but *reduced*, stripped to its essentials, oxygen and nothing else, no pollen, no dust, no memory of exhaust or smoke.

Time is strange. You know you've been inside for what feels like an hour. Your phone, if you brought one, is blank. Not dead — blank. The screen reflects the chamber back to you, but the chamber in the screen has no you in it. If you brought a watch, the hands are still, or they move backward in tiny increments, or they spin with a slow, drunken dignity that suggests time here is not directional but *textural*, a thing you feel rather than measure.

You notice that the puddles have risen. Not much. A quarter-inch. You are certain you would have noticed if you'd looked five minutes ago, but you are not certain five minutes have passed.

---

The things that live here are not hostile. They are not friendly. They are not indifferent in the way animals are indifferent to things that don't concern them. They are *inconclusive* about you. You are a variable that hasn't resolved yet. The origami-goats may come closer. The insects may cluster on your skin in patterns that feel deliberate. The filaments on the wall may extend toward your shadow — or what passes for your shadow in a room without shadows.

And the chamber itself is still becoming. You can hear it, almost — a hum below the threshold of sound, a vibration in the stone under your palms when you press them flat against the wall. The walls are *not done*. The water in the puddles is arranging itself into geometries that suggest a larger structure underneath, a network of spaces that this chamber is only the first node of. The erosion hasn't started because the erosion *is* the activity — this place is being shaped not by wind or water but by the fact of its own ongoing creation, a geology of *happening* rather than *weathering*.

You will leave. You know this. The fissure will still be there — or a fissure will be there, or a way will be there, which is not the same thing but may be close enough. You'll emerge on the hillside and the grass will be the same, the sky will be the same, and after a few minutes the whole thing will feel like a dream you're remembering incorrectly. The details will blur. The impossibly bright room, the silent crawling life, the unfinished doorway.

But something will stay. A filament-thin thread of wrongness in your sense of space. The way you'll sometimes reach for a door handle and pause, your hand a fraction of a second too early or too late, as if your body remembers a place where time was not a line but a surface — cool, damp, bright, and still unmaking itself.

Just beneath the surface.

Always just beneath the surface.
