## The Condensation Plate

You feel it before you see it: a cold so thorough it seems to come from below. Not the sharp bite of wind-driven frost, but a deep, patient cold that enters through the soles of your feet and settles into the marrow. The air is thin. It tastes of mineral and something faintly electric, the way the air tastes near a machine that has been running for a very long time.

The landscape opens around you without preamble. No tree line to pass through, no gradual transition from habitable land to this. One step, the world has undergrowth and soil. The next, you're standing on stone — vast, dark, still-warm-to-the-touch stone that stretches to every horizon in flat-topped ridges and broad, shallow basins. The geology is young. You can tell. The edges of everything are too sharp. The faces of the rock haven't yet learned to be round. They rise in angular shelves, columnar fractures still perfect, as though the stone only recently decided to become solid and hasn't yet forgotten the geometry of its cooling.

This place is still being made.

The surface extends for what feels like hundreds of kilometers — a high-altitude plateau whose edges drop away into lower, cloud-wrapped country you can only glimpse as grey smudges on the distant curve of the world. The elevation is enough to press your ears. Not enough to make you gasp, but enough to make silence louder. Here, the atmospheric pressure is lower, and it makes every small sound carry — your breathing, the scuff of a boot on wet basalt, the faint metallic creak of a ridge adjusting its own weight as the planet slowly settles beneath it.

It is wet. Not raining — the sky is a uniform pewter dome, featureless except for the slow migration of fog banks that move across the plateau at ankle height, then waist height, then overhead, like the landscape is breathing moisture in and out. Condensation forms on every exposed surface. The stone glistens. Every fracture weeps a thin bead of water that catches the grey light and holds it. In the shallow depressions between ridges, water gathers in sheets so thin they are almost not puddles — just a darkening of the stone, a few millimeters of standing water that reflects the sky with a clarity that makes the ground seem to open into another sky beneath you.

There is not much growing. Nothing you'd call a forest, nothing you'd call a meadow. On the lee side of the larger ridges, where warmth from the stone's residual heat collects, you find thin crusts of organism — lichen-analogues, dark matte films that spread across the rock in fractal patterns. They are alive, or something like alive: mineral-processing, moisture-harvesting, photosynthetic in the way that term is stretched to mean here. In the cracks and fissures, fibrous growths — call them plants if you're generous — rise no higher than your ankle. They are pale, almost translucent, their stems stiff and tubular, drawing water from the condensing air through surfaces too fine to see. They do not sway. They do not need wind. They stand like tiny sentinels in their fissures, patient and sparse, separated by long stretches of bare stone.

And yet the fauna is astonishingly present.

You see the first one from a great distance — a shape moving against the dark stone, a silhouette that resolves slowly as it approaches. It is large. Ungulate-shaped, perhaps, but wrong in the proportions: too long in the leg, too narrow in the torso, its hide a dark iridescent material that catches the light in oil-slick flashes. It walks with the unhurried certainty of something that has never known a predator it couldn't outrun or outlast. Its eyes are bright — not animal-bright, but precisely luminous, as though lit from within by a cold source. It passes within thirty meters of you, regards you with brief disinterest, and continues across the plateau toward a ridge you cannot see the top of.

There are others. Not herds — this landscape does not support herds. But scattered individuals, pairs, small groups distributed across the emptiness with the precision of a system that knows exactly how many organisms a given area of stone can sustain. Smaller creatures move through the stone itself: six-limbed arthropod-analogues with legs like pick-axes, burrowing into the still-soft seams of the geology, feeding on mineral accretions and the microbial films that line every fracture. You hear them faintly — not their voices, but the sound of their movement inside the rock, a delicate scratching that you feel more than hear, transmitted through the stone into your feet.

The settlements.

You can see five of them from where you stand. They look, at first, like geological features. Low angular masses rising from the plateau surface, their profiles too regular to be natural rock, but made of the same dark stone, so that the eye struggles to find the boundary between terrain and construction. Closer, the distinction becomes apparent — but only barely. The structures are grown, not built. Their surfaces have a texture like solidified flow, smooth where the stone is rough, patterned with channels and seams that serve as infrastructure: atmospheric processors, water redistribution networks, energy conduits running in lattices through the walls and floors, all operating at a scale so fine it looks like veining in stone.

Each settlement is home to perhaps a few hundred individuals. The population is low. The structures are tall, though — some rise three or four stories, their interiors carved or formed into spaces that wind and stack vertically, making the most of small footprints. From the outside they appear monolithic. Inside, you're told, they are labyrinths of engineered climate: warm corridors where the condensation is controlled, growing chambers where the pale plants are cultivated, communal spaces where the residents — whoever they are — gather in the low amber light that seems to emanate from the walls themselves.

The technology operating here is so deeply integrated that it barely announces itself. There is no visible machinery. No wires, no vents, no generators. The infrastructure is molecular, nanoscale, woven into the material of the buildings and the stone beneath them. The air is breathable because the plateau itself processes it — mineral surfaces catalyzed at the atomic level to split and recombine atmospheric compounds, maintaining a balance that is almost but not quite natural. The water is purified by passage through engineered crystal matrices in the stone's pore structure. Heat is generated by the slow decay of embedded isotopes, distributed through the geology like blood through a body.

The sounds of all of this are below perception. You hear the condensation. You hear the distant call of one of the large fauna — a low, resonant tone that rolls across the plateau like thunder in miniature. You hear nothing mechanical. The vast infrastructure hums at a frequency your body feels before your ears do, a subsonic drone that might be geological or might be engineered and has long since stopped being distinguishable.

The light is grey and even. No shadows worth the name. The fog diffuses everything into a soft, directionless luminance that makes the world look like a photograph taken through frosted glass. On the rare occasions the fog lifts, the sun — or whatever serves as one here — appears as a pale disc without heat, and the stone darkens and brightens in response, and you can see the full scope of the place: the ridges, the basins, the distant settlements like dark knots on an endless flat plain, the fauna moving between them in slow, deliberate arcs, the horizon curving just enough to remind you that you are standing on a world that is larger than you can comprehend and younger than you can believe.

The erosion has barely begun. In a thousand years, maybe ten thousand, the ridges will soften, the basins will fill, the sharp geometry will give way to rolling forms. But for now, this landscape is still in the act of becoming. The stone still remembers being liquid. The air still carries the memory of outgassing. The settlements still taste new — their walls not yet colonized by time, their systems running at peak efficiency, their purpose still immediate rather than historical.

It is a place of engineering and geology, of engineered-geology, of life distributed thinly across an enormous young surface that is still cooling, still condensing, still becoming itself.

It is quiet here. It is vast. It is almost empty, and almost alive.

It is almost finished, and it has barely begun.
