## The Drowned Heights

You enter from the edge of what was once a name for land.

There is no shore here anymore. There is only the slow, iron-cold lapping of a vast inland sea that has no bottom anyone remembers, and the suggestion — just at the margin of perception — that the water you're wading into was once sky. The surface is matte and barely responsive, as though the flood happened so long ago that the water has forgotten how to be restless. But it isn't still. You can feel it: a deep, pervasive heaving, a tectonic slosh that presses against your ribs and then releases, presses and releases, like the breathing of something too large to be alive but too rhythmic to be dead.

The cold is not the cold of ice. It is the cold of depth. It enters through the soles of your feet and stays there, a heavy ache that climbs the bones of your ankles at the pace of a year. The water is so cold it has its own taste — mineral, faintly metallic, like licking a railing in deep winter, except there is no winter here because there is no season here. There is only the cold permanence.

You are at elevation. You know this not from sight but from the thinness of the air that sits above the waterline — and there *is* a waterline, faintly, a matte ring on the grey rock faces of the ridges that still reach upward through the flood. These ridges are the bones of what used to be a high basin, a wide bowl of peaks and saddle-points and wind-scoured plateaus, now half-submerged, their shoulders draped in dark water like cloaks thrown over sleeping figures. Some summits stand twenty or thirty meters above the surface, black and bare, too steep and too smooth for anything to grip. Others have been worn into long, gentle humps that barely break the plane of the water — just a ripple, a suggestion, a ripple that might be a current or might be the curve of a mountain whose name was lost.

The scale of it opens up as you wade further from shore — if shore is what you'd call that fractured line of stone teeth on the horizon. The water stretches in every direction, grey-black, surface-flat except where the swell rolls through it from unseen distances. It is regional in scope, this flood: you cannot see its far shore. You cannot see the far shore of anything. The visible world contracts to a circle of grey, and beyond that circle is more grey, and beyond that more, and the knowledge — not visual, not certain, just a pressure in the architecture of your certainty — that this goes on for a very long time.

And beneath the surface, the plants.

They are the first strange thing, and the most persistent.

The shallow margins — where the water is knee-deep and the light still reaches — are choked with growth. Not reeds, not grasses, not anything with a proper name in any language you've been given. They are thick-stemmed, pale-green-to-charcoal organisms with broad, flat fronds that overlap like scales or fingers or the pages of a book left open in the rain. They grow in dense stands, waist-high, swaying in the undercurrent with a slow deliberateness that suggests intelligence, or at least patience. Their roots — if roots are what those pale, web-like structures are — grip the submerged soil in thick mats, and the soil itself is dark, almost black, organic-rich, the composted remains of ten thousand generations of the same plants decomposing and regrowing in the same cold water.

Further out, where the water deepens and the light fails, the plants change. They grow taller and thinner, their fronds reduced to filamentous threads that catch the faintest currents like the hairs on an arm in a breeze. Some of them — you see it at the corner of your vision, or you think you do — glow. A faint blue-green luminescence, bioluminescent or phosphorescent or something the darkness invented for itself, pulsing in slow waves that travel through the stands of vegetation like signals through a nervous system. You look directly and the glow retreats; you look away and it returns, brighter now, at the edge of perception, as though the plants are shy, or as though the darkness itself is guiding your attention to and from them.

The vegetation is lush, undeniably. But it is lush the way a fever is warm — excessive, slightly wrong, too much of a thing that should exist in moderation. There is no pruning here, no grazing, no animal mouth to thin the stands. Nothing eats. Nothing nests in the fronds. Nothing hides among the roots. The plants grow and die and decompose and grow again in a closed loop, untouched by anything with a nervous system or a heartbeat. It is abundance without purpose, fertility without consumption, a garden that exists only for itself and will continue to exist long after the last observer has left or drowned.

The sound is the second strange thing — the first being the absence that makes the sound necessary.

There are no animal calls. No birds — there are no birds here; there are no birds anywhere; the concept of bird has been evacuated from this place like water from a sinking ship. No insect hum. No rustling of small bodies in undergrowth. Nothing vocalizes. Nothing rustles. The fauna count is zero. Absolute. Total. The kind of zero that is itself a presence, a pressure.

And yet: the place is loud.

The water provides the sound. It is a constant, multi-layered roar — not the white-noise roar of a waterfall or the crash of surf, but something deeper and more structural. The current, which you can now feel against your shins as a steady push from the east, produces a low, persistent hiss as it moves through and around the submerged vegetation. The fronds, thousands upon thousands of them, flex and release, flex and release, producing a sound like the rubbing of wet hands on cotton cloth, but slowed down, elongated, pitched lower than you'd expect. The water itself, moving over the irregular bottom — the ridges, the humps, the shelves and drop-offs that you can feel but not see — produces groans and clicks, long low C's and D-flats that resonate in your chest cavity. Every few minutes — or it might be every few seconds in the time-sense of a flooded mountain — a deeper sound arrives, a boom that seems to come from the water itself, a pressure adjustment of some kind, a shifting of water volumes across the immense basin, like the settling of an enormous building at night.

And then there are the sounds that might not be sound at all: a faint vibration in the water itself, transmitted through the soles of your feet (you are still ankle-deep, but the water is rising with the slow patience of a tide you cannot see), a vibration that has no obvious source and no obvious frequency. It is more felt than heard, a pressure behind the eyes, a loosening of something in the jaw, a faint taste of copper. The weirdness here is not visual so much as haptic. It enters through the body.

The structures are the third strange thing — strange not because of what they are, but because of the fact of their being.

On the nearest ridge — a long, gentle slope that rises maybe five meters above the waterline, shaped like the back of a sleeping animal — there are remains. You can see them from the shallows: a scatter of stones, half-sunk, arranged in patterns that are too regular for geology and too irregular for architecture. Low walls, no more than knee-high, built of dark, heavy stone that has been smoothed by centuries of cold water. Not mortar, no. The stones are stacked, balanced, wedged. Primitive construction. Someone with hands and patience and no metal, placing these stones with an understanding of weight and friction that feels older than knowledge. The walls form shapes that might be rooms, or might be borders, or might be symbols — you cannot tell. They are too eroded, too weathered, too settled into the landscape to read as functional. They exist in the way scars exist: as evidence of an event, legible only to someone who was there.

Further along the ridge — and the ridge itself extends for what might be half a kilometer, curving gently — there are more structures, or remnants of them. A single standing stone, taller than you, with a flat face that has been deliberately, painstakingly smoothed. Not carved, not inscribed, but *smoothed*, as though someone spent years removing every rough place until the surface was perfect, and the perfection itself became the message. A partial enclosure — three walls of fitted stone, the fourth open to the water, small enough to be a shelter for one person, or one animal (but there are no animals), or one purpose that has been forgotten. The stones are dark basalt or something like it, heavy and non-porous, chosen (or found) for their resistance to the water that has surrounded them for so long.

There is nothing built here that is grand. Nothing monumental. No temple, no walled city, no tower. Only these scattered, quiet artifacts of manual labor — hand-shaped, hand-placed, hand-forgotten. Whoever built them used tools of stone and bone and shaped wood. Whoever built them is gone. The structures remain, not as monuments but as residue, the mineral precipitate of a civilization's presence, settled into the landscape like salt.

The dynamic quality of the place is everywhere and constant.

The water moves in slow, sweeping patterns that you can see in the behavior of the vegetation — the fronds leaning east, then slowly turning, then leaning further, then slowly recovering. There are thermoclines here, perhaps, or salinity gradients, layers of water moving at different temperatures and speeds, creating a complex, three-dimensional current system that you can feel but not fully map. The surface occasionally wrinkles — not breaking, not forming waves exactly, but *creasing*, as though the water's surface is a sheet being slowly pulled from underneath. Light shifts on the surface in patterns that have nothing to do with clouds (the sky above is thick, low, grey-white, featureless, pressing down on the water like a hand on a tablecloth) and everything to do with the bioluminescence below, which pulses and dims and pulses in waves that propagate outward from unseen centers.

The cold is not static either. There are moments — sudden, brief — when the water warms by a fraction of a degree, then cools again, and in those moments the vegetation seems to respond, its glow intensifying briefly, the fronds lifting slightly, the sound of the place changing pitch by a tone you can barely detect. It is as though the entire system breathes, inhaling cold and exhaling warmth, cycling endlessly, driven by some process that is not solar, not tidal, not easily explained by anything you know. The dynamic here is biological in its pacing — slow, rhythmic, organic — but mechanical in its scale. It is the breathing of a system, not an organism, but an *arrangement* of water and stone and light and life that has achieved a kind of self-regulation, a balance so old it has become identity.

The darkness is not total. It is a living darkness, a darkness with content — the faint blue-green glow, the grey-black of the water, the matte texture of the stone, the silhouette of the ridges against a sky that provides no light but serves as a ceiling, a presence, a weight. You can see, but only just. Every shape is approximate. Every edge dissolves into grey. The visual field is compressed to a narrow range of tones, and within that narrow range, the differences become enormously significant — the slightly-less-dark of a submerged ridge, the slightly-more-glowing of a frond cluster, the barely-there shadow that might be a structure or might be a trick of the light that doesn't exist.

And the lack of animals. The absolute, total lack.

It changes everything. It changes the sound — without animal noise, every water-sound is amplified, interpreted, significance-read. Without birds, you have no sense of the vertical dimension of the space. Without insects, there is no fine-grained agitation of the air. The plant life moves with a purpose it cannot have — plants don't have purpose — and you feel it, a constant low-level wrongness, like a word repeated too many times until it stops meaning anything and starts meaning something else. The place is alive (the plants grow, the water moves, the light pulses) but it is not inhabited. It is a world without consumers, without predation, without the constant negotiation of living things jostling for space and energy. It is a world that has settled into its own state, and that state is greenery and cold and silence and deep, deep, deep noise.

You stand at the edge of what was once a name for land, and the water is at your waist now, cold and insistent and ancient, and the glow beneath the surface pulses once, twice, three times — not toward you, not away from you, but in a pattern that existed before you arrived and will continue long after you leave, or drown, or simply become another layer of dark soil on the bottom of a flooded basin that someone, once, with hands and patience and shaped wood, chose to call home.
