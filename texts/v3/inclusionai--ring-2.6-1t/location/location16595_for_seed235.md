The place doesn't have a name because names require someone to leave, and no one leaves. No one arrives either. It simply is: a high world, a world above most worlds, where the ground sits so far above the baseline of things that the sky is not sky anymore but something closer to a ceiling, close enough to touch if you built something tall enough — and the vegetation here builds things that tall and taller.

The air is wet the way a throat is wet. It coats everything. Every surface beads with condensation. Every leaf — if that's what they are, and some of them are, but some of them are not leaves at all but structures, membranous lattices that catch the water and hold it and let it fall again in thin threads that sing as they drop — is slick and trembling with water that arrived hours or days ago from a rain that has not stopped, not really, not in the way rain stops in drier places. Here the rain simply became the atmosphere. The rain became the medium through which everything exists, the way water is the medium through which fish exist, except here you are the fish and the water is warm and thick and tastes of minerals and green.

The elevation is extraordinary. Stand where the ground is firm — and most of the ground is not firm, most of the ground is a spongy interlocking mat of root and mineral and something in between that has the properties of neither and both — and look down, and the world falls away in steps, terrace after terrace of lesser green, cloud layers threading between the terraces, and the sky above is small and hard-colored, pressed down by the sheer mass of atmosphere below you, a blue so saturated it looks like a material, like hammered copper, like the sky is a thing with weight and texture that, if you reached high enough, would yield under your hand.

The vegetation is not vegetation. The word is wrong for what is here. It fills every volume. It climbs and overgrows and pushes through and builds upward in structures that are not trees — though some are trees, old massive trunks rising in columns too wide to embrace, their bark etched with patterns that look deliberate, that look architectural, that look as though they were grown to a plan by someone with patience beyond human patience, since they took ten thousand years and didn't mind — but between and around and through these trees there are other things, things more vine than vine, more structure than vine, living lattices that span between trunks and form corridors and rooms and archways, walls thick with matted growth, surfaces that are not bark or leaf or root but something in between, something with the texture of wet felt and the resilience of cartilage, dark greens and black greens and greens so deep they read as blue in the shadowed places.

This is where the urban quality lives. This is the city. No one built it — if by "built" you mean the deliberate arrangement of dead materials. It was grown. It organized itself. Over the long silence of millennia, the life here found its own patterns of density, its own architectures of proximity, and now the place has the feel of a settlement — of streets and districts and walls and interiors — but every inch of it breathes, every surface drips, every corridor is woven from living material, and nothing in it was placed by intention because nothing here has intention, and yet the order is undeniable. You feel it as you move through. The spaces open and close with the logic of rooms. Ceiling heights adjust. There are places where the growth compresses into narrow passages so dense with wet life that your shoulders brush green walls on both sides, and places where the canopy falls away into open plazas — open, yes, but not empty, never empty — where light comes down filtered through so many layers that everything glows greenish-gold, as though you are walking along the bottom of a warm, bright sea.

There are no animals.

This is a thing you notice quickly and then cannot stop noticing, the way you notice silence after a sound stops. There is nothing that moves with purpose through the space. No creatures threading the corridors, no birds in the canopy, no insects in the air. The place is alive — you can see it breathing, see the metabolic warmth rising from every surface, feel the sheer *press* of living material pressing in from all sides — and it is alive with no animals in it, only the green architecture of photosynthetic organisms and fungal networks threading unseen beneath the matted ground, and this is wrong in a way you can't quite articulate. You say: *there are no animals*. And the statement hangs in the wet air between you and the green.

The sound is the other impossibility.

It is everywhere. It comes from above and below and beside you and from no direction you can name. It is a roaring, a humming, a tone so low and constant that you feel it in your sternum before your ears even register it. The air vibrates. It might be the water — the sheer volume of it, falling through the lattice-work, streaming down the living walls, pooling and overflowing and falling again, a billion individual threads each making its own small sound and all of it combining into a roar that fills the space the way water fills a basin. It might be the vegetation itself — structures so dense, so many surfaces, so many of them tuned to slightly different frequencies by their slightly different sizes and tensions, humming in the wind, and there is always wind here because the elevation is so extreme that atmospheric circulation is constant, a slow heavy river of air flowing over and through the place, and everything in its path resonates. It might be something else. It might be the place itself.

The geometry of the interior spaces is what does it — the way corridors curve and chambers open, the way sound enters a passage and does not leave but accumulates, bounces between living walls and is handed off to the next space and the next, each one adding its own fraction until the whole network thrums like a struck bell the size of a valley. You cannot find silence. You cannot find a gap. There has never been a gap. It has been this loud for longer than the oldest stone has been stone, and the sound is part of the place the way heat is part of fire, the way wet is part of the sea. It is not something that happens here. It is what the place *is*.

The weirdness — you feel it before you can see it. A wrongness in the angles. You are walking along a passage that curves gently left, and left is where it has always been, but when you look back the place you came from is not where it should be. Not dramatically wrong — not a door where a wall should be. Just a few degrees displaced. A corridor that extends past where it visibly ends. A wall whose depth doesn't match its width. The spaces between the great columns of root and vine are not irregular — they are *almost* regular, so close to regular that the wrongness only shows itself when you stop focusing and let your eyes go soft, when you stop *looking* and start *receiving*. Then the shapes do things. A passageway recedes in a direction that shouldn't exist. A vault above you has a geometry that your mind assembles and reassembles and each assembly holds for a moment and then slides. The space you see is a surface. Behind it, or beneath it, or inside it at a depth you cannot reach, there is another space that is almost the same and almost works but doesn't.

And the sound — the sound might be coming from *there*. From the space behind the space. The roaring, the humming, the deafening constant tone may be the sound of reality bending under the weight of this place, all this wet green mass pressing down on the fabric of the world until it sags and stretches and nearly tears and doesn't, held by whatever equilibrium keeps everything in its permanent state.

Because nothing changes.

That is the last and strangest thing. In a place this alive, this wet, this warm, this dense with growth, you expect change. You expect to see it: a tendril reaching, a root splitting stone, a wall advancing to reclaim a corridor. But nothing moves. Nothing grows in the way you understand growth. Every leaf is frozen mid-drip. Every surface bears the same beads of water. The mist hangs in the same shapes it has held since before there were shapes for the mist to hold. The vines hold the same positions. The light filters through the canopy at the same angle — not because the light source hasn't moved, but because the canopy hasn't changed to meet it. The whole place is a locked chord, a single sustained note struck at the dawn of things and still ringing.

And if you stand here long enough, in the humming and the dripping and the green-dark corridors that curve in ways that don't quite hold, you might begin to feel what keeps it: something that isn't force, isn't structure, isn't even habit, but something closer to a *decision* — a single unbroken decision, made by no one, to remain exactly as it is. Forever. Written into every leaf and every stone and every thread of falling water. The place has decided what it is, and it is that, and it will be that after the sun cools and the mountains dissolve and everything you know has forgotten this elevation ever existed.

This. This only. This, and the roar, and the green, and the wet, and the heat rising from a million living surfaces, and the geometry that almost — *almost* — resolves into something you could call a room, and the sound of it all, constant, immense, deafening, the sound of a world that has said its own name once and is still holding the note.

Nothing will ever change here. Nothing will ever begin and nothing will ever end. The place simply holds, breathing its green breath, humming its low tone, dripping its warm water down surfaces that were old before language, and if you press your hand to the nearest wall you will feel a pulse — not a heartbeat, not exactly, but a pulse all the same — steady, deep, and vast, the pulse of something that has no need to move because it has already arrived at the only place it ever intended to be, and it is staying, and it is staying, and it is staying.
