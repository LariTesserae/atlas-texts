# The Splay

---

You hear it before you see anything at all.

It arrives as pressure — a deep, rhythmic compression against the chest, a pulse that could be geological or cardiac. The sound fills the basin in layers: a low subsonic groan that lives behind your hearing, over which sit higher cries — keening, metallic, alive — that rise and fracture like glass thrown against stone. Somewhere to the left, something enormous exhales, and the air itself thickens, carrying the scent of wetite and copper and the sweet rotten sweetness of spadera blossoms opening in the perpetual twilight.

The light is not absent, but it is diminished — filtered through a permanent gauze of airborne spore-matter that hangs at shoulder height across the whole basin. It gives everything the appearance of being viewed through smoke-stained glass: colors are present but muted, edges softened, shadows stretched long and violet regardless of the hour. There is no direct sun here, not anymore. What light reaches the floor of the Splay has passed through three hundred meters of living haze — the spore-canopy of the cestral groves — and it arrives as a diffuse, sourceless glow, as though the air itself is remembering sunlight.

The Splay is a basin. Twenty-odd kilometers across, cupped between ridges of newborn stone that jut from the lowland like the knuckles of a fist slowly opening. The rock is young — barely two centuries exposed, still sharp-edged, still carrying the memory of the uplift that tore it from shallow water. You can see where the sea was. The basin floor is layered withite — pale sedimentary chalk still soft enough to gouge with a thumb — and threaded with rivers that appeared only last decade, carving paths through calciumite the way thought carve paths through doubt: quickly, and then irrevocably.

There is almost no understory.

This is the first thing you understand when you enter the Splay, before you can name what you're looking at. The space between things is the landscape. The ground is visible — pale, ridged, damp where the rivers reach it — and it stretches out in every direction with almost nothing on it. Not barren. Not dead. Just profoundly, conspicuously spare. You could throw a stone and hit nothing but air for sixty meters in any direction. Then, at the periphery of your vision, something rises — vast, slow, structurally impossible in its slenderness — and you realize the emptiness is not empty at all. It is full of distance.

The trees — if they are trees — stand at intervals of a hundred meters or more. Each one is enormous: columns of dark fibrous bark that rise forty, fifty meters before splitting into canopies so wide they shade an acre apiece. Their roots are architectural, visible above the chalk in great buttressed arches, pale with mineral deposits, threaded with runnels of water that appear and disappear according to some schedule you cannot discern. Some of the roots have grown into shapes that echo load-bearing trusses — you keep trying to see the engineering in them, and then catch yourself, and the recognition is worse.

They are called **vathines**, the locals say, if there are locals. They move their sap visibly — dark amber columns rising through translucent outer bark in pulses, and each pulse takes several seconds, which is wrong for vascular tissue, which is wrong for anything, but the tree does not care about your sense of pace. The sap glows faintly in the dim light, bioluminescent or phosphorescent or something the language hasn't caught up to, and at night — if night is the word for the deepening of the Splay's already-dim cycle — the vathines stand like columns of fire seen through frosted glass.

Between them: the void. The air. The sound.

The sound is what undoes you. The basin acts as a resonance chamber — the young rock walls are smooth and concave in places, and every noise generated on the floor is caught, amplified, redirected. A bird call at three hundred meters away arrives at your ear at the volume of a shout. The wind moving through the vathine canopies becomes a roar. And the animals —

God, the animals.

They are everywhere, and nowhere close. You hear them constantly: the staccato percussion of something heavy moving through chalk scrub half a kilometer south, the long melodic sequences of what the field guides — the few that have been written, by people who came and left quickly — call **parametric callers**, creatures whose vocalizations shift frequency in mathematical progressions, ascending and descending in intervals that feel less biological than compositional. You hear them triangulate around you, one calling from the east ridge, one from the north, one from somewhere above — above? — and their overlapping sequences create a three-dimensional cage of sound that is, by any acoustic measure, deafening. At full chorus the basin reaches volumes that would be painful in an enclosed space, and here it is open, unbounded, and yet the pressure sits behind your eyes like a migraine.

The fauna themselves — when you see them — are large-bodied, unhurried, and deeply unconcerned by the emptiness around them. A grazer stands knee-deep in chalk-silt at the river's edge, fifteen meters from you. It is the color ofite, with six limbs arranged in bilateral pairs, each limb ending in a broad pad that distributes its weight so perfectly it barely dents the surface. Its head is a smooth, curved thing with no visible eyes — you realize later there are eyes, recessed beneath the cranial plate, wide and dark and spinning slowly like lighthouse lenses. It regards you for a long time. It returns to cropping something from the chalk that you cannot see, and it makes a sound like a tuba being slowly submerged in water.

There are should be more. You hear them, you feel the vibrations of their movement through the soles of your boots, but the sightlines are absurd. The basin's flatness and the vathines' positioning create corridors of visibility that stretch for hundreds of meters, and through them you catch fragments: a tail disappearing behind a chalk outcropping, the silhouette of something flying at altitude against the spore-haze (wingspan too large to estimate, moving with the mechanical patience of a system with no doubt about its aerodynamics), the distant geometry of a structure that might be nest or territory marker or machine.

And then: the built things.

You come on the first one at roughly the center of the basin. A structure. It is difficult to describe because your first assumption is wrong — you assume **building**, which implies walls and a roof and human intent stacked in layers — and then you see that the structure is partly grown, partly assembled, partly poured, and partly *appearing*, as though the boundary between construction and emergence has dissolved. It rises from the chalk on a base of fused vathine root, which has been shaped — heated? guided? — into parabolic arches that support a roof of something translucent and faintly iridescent. The material is not glass, not stone, not organic, though it shares properties with all three. It hums when you touch it. The hum is not a vibration you hear with your ears — it bypasses the ears entirely and manifests as a sensation in the bones of your forearm.

There are others. Across the basin, at distances that feel absurd given how empty the terrain is, more structures repeat — each different, each built from materials and methods that don't correspond to any single technology you know. One is a tower of stacked hexagonal plates that rotate independently, slowly, producing a faintly audible chime. One is a subterranean entrance — you catch only the descending lip, a smooth ramp of dark metalite — and from below comes a sound like machinery, or like breathing, or like machinery that has been breathing for so long the distinction has eroded. One is barely there at all: a flat platform on four pillars, and on the platform, a garden — arranged, intentional, geometric — of plants that are clearly not native, clearly not from this basin, clearly placed here by something that wanted beauty, or data, or both.

The technology is so far beyond legibility that it loops back around to looking natural. You keep waiting to find the seams — the bolts, the weld lines, the code — and instead you find growth patterns, mineral deposits, weathering. The structures have been here long enough to begin integrating with the landscape, and the landscape has been here long enough to begin accepting them. Vathine roots are creeping up one tower's base. The chalk silt is filling in around another's foundation. A river is being gently, imperceptibly redirected by a buried foundation, and the river does not resist.

At the edges of the basin, where the ridges rise and the spore-haze thins, the sky appears — pale, colorless, too high to matter. Clouds form and dissolve in hours, producing the occasional rain: brief, warm, heavy drops that strike the chalk and vanish into it within minutes. The rain smells of ozone and something sweeter, something floral you cannot locate. After a rain, the Splay is briefly louder, as though the water activates sounds that were dormant in the stone, and the fauna emerge from wherever they shelter — not in greater numbers, but closer, as though the rain has made the distances between things feel less absolute.

There are rules here, though they are not spoken and may not be knowable. The spacing of the vathines is too regular to be ecological and too irregular to be architectural. The parametric callers maintain intervals between themselves that feel territorial but mathematical. The structures avoid each other with a precision that borders on politeness. The fauna do not cross certain lines on the chalk floor that are invisible but structurally real — you can see where a grazer's path turns abruptly, where a flying thing banks and climbs rather than cross a space that offers no physical obstruction.

And beneath all of it, the sound. Constant, layered, deafening — a roar so textured and omnidirectional that it becomes its own terrain. You stop trying to identify individual sources after a time. The sound is the weather here. The sound is the ground. The sound is the light that isn't quite there, translated into a frequency you can feel in your molars.

The Splay does not welcome you. The Splay does not exclude you. The Splay is happening, and you are somewhere in it, and the distance between the next vathine and the next structure and the next living thing is enormous and humming and alive.

You walk forward. The chalk holds your weight. The air smells of spores and spadera and something metallic and ancient. The sound swells. Something is moving north, very large, very slowly, and you can feel its footfalls in the stone before you hear them, and when you hear them they are everything.
