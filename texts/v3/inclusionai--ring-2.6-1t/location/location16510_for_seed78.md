# The Pallid Terrace

You would call it a plateau if it had edges, but it does not — or rather, its edges are so far away that they behave more like a horizon than a boundary. The land rises and simply *continues*. The Pale Terrace, the ones who have seen it from below call it, though no one from below has climbed high enough to confirm it is a terrace rather than a world. The air thins immediately. By the time you are standing on it, your lungs have entered a new agreement with your body — breathe less, breathe slower, or go home. The cold does not announce itself. It simply *is*, as if the temperature here preceded the land and the land grew up into it.

It is young. Everything is young. The stone beneath your feet was underground five thousand years ago. It still carries the memory of pressure — a greasy, mineral smell clings to every surface, and the edges of every outcrop are sharp enough to draw blood without effort. No frost has yet softened them. No root has yet pried them apart. The plateau has barely arrived and already things are living on it, as if life could not bear to wait.

And there is vegetation — everywhere and nowhere. The plants here are not like anything you would call a tree or a shrub or moss. Each one is vast, and each one is alone.

---

The nearest one stands three hundred paces from where you are standing. You can see it from here because it glows.

It rises from a single rosette of root — a disc of pale, fibrous tissue pressed flat against the stone, perhaps forty paces across, so dense and interlaced it looks almost mineral. From this root, a central stalk ascends: not a trunk, not a column, but something closer to a frozen fountain, pale grey-green, ridged vertically, maybe sixty feet tall at its apex before it divides and divides again into branching lattices of tubular tissue. No leaves. No flowers as you would recognize them. Instead, the lattice ends in thousands of translucent vesicles, each the size of a fist, and each emitting a faint, steady luminescence — blue-white at the core, shading to a bruised amber at the margins. The light they produce is not firelight, not daylight. It is something closer to the glow of breath on a winter morning, given form and patience.

There are perhaps two hundred of these organisms visible from where you stand. The nearest is three hundred paces away. The farthest are nearly a mile off, their glow shrunk to pinpricks against the dark. Between them — nothing. Bare rock. Frost. The occasional crack in the stone where moisture has seeped and frozen into a vein of clear ice that hums when the wind passes over it. The distance between individual plants is immense, and standing among them you feel the emptiness as a physical pressure — the emptiness and yet the *fullness*, because the emptiness is occupied by air that smells of resin and cold chemistry and something faintly metallic, as if the plants are slowly thinking.

They are not plants in any botanical sense you learned. They have no chlorophyll. They do not photosynthesize. They are chemotrophic — they draw energy from the mineral-rich rock itself, dissolving stone with secretions from their roots and converting the trace metals and sulfides into the slow, cold-burning bioluminescence that is their metabolism and their speech. In the dark, they glow. When the temperature drops — and here, it is always dropping — they glow brighter. The Terrace brightens in the deep hours before dawn (though there is no dawn here; there is only a slow, imperceptible brightening of the southern horizon that never fully arrives) and the effect, seen from above, is of a scattered city coming to life.

They make sounds. Low ones. A vesicle swelling with gas will audibly creak as its membrane stretches — a sound like a finger drawn across the rim of a glass, but sustained, and deep enough to vibrate in your sternum. When one of the great organisms sheds a dead lattice-branch (which it does every few days, the broken tubes falling and shattering on the rock with a sound like safety glass), the others nearby flare briefly, their vesicles brightening in a ripple that travels outward from the point of breakage at a pace you can almost track. Whether this is communication or mere chemical reflex, you cannot tell. You watch it for an hour. The ripple takes forty minutes to reach the nearest neighbor, three hundred paces away.

---

The fauna are loud.

That is the first thing you notice after the cold and the dark and the impossible spacing of the plants. It is a world that has grown up to fill silence with sound.

There are grazers — you think grazers, though the word is almost meaningless here. They are six-legged, low-slung, covered in a dense pelt of hollow hairs that trap air and provide insulation against the cold stone. Their mouths are wide and flat, adapted to scraping the mineral crust from the roots of the great plants without damaging the living tissue beneath. They move in loose aggregations of ten to thirty, and they are almost silent, but their passage is not: the sound of dozens of hard hooves on fresh stone carries across the emptiness with a clarity that is almost painful. You hear them long before you see them — a stuttering rhythm, like stones dropped on a drumskin, growing louder, then softer, then gone.

But the predators are what fill the air.

They are not large — the size of a fox, perhaps, though their proportions are wrong. Long forelimbs, short hind, a body that sits low to the stone and seems to flow rather than run. Their skin — if it is skin — is matte black and absorbs light so completely that you cannot see them until they move, at which point their edges blur with a faint distortion, as if the air around them is being bent. They hunt by sound. When a grazer breaks from the herd, or stumbles, or cracks a stone too loudly with its hoof, the nearest predator is there — not running, but *arriving*, as if the distance between two points on the Terrace is negotiable for them in a way it is not for you.

They shriek. This is the dominant sound of the Pallid Terrace. Not the groaning plants, not the wind, not the cracking frost — the shriek. It rises from below the range of easy hearing and crests into a frequency that makes your teeth ache. It is a hunting call, but also a territorial call, and also a social call; it serves all three purposes and none of them, because on the Terrace, a single shriek contains too much information for a single meaning. You hear them in staccato bursts across the dark plateau, sometimes a dozen at once, and the sound reverberates off the distant rock walls of the plateau's interior gorges — gorges so fresh and sharp-walled they look as if they were cut by a blade yesterday — and the echoes multiply until it sounds like the whole Terrace is screaming.

There are other creatures. Burrowers, visible only by the mounds of powdered stone they leave at their tunnel-mouths, each mound arranged in a perfect spiral. Flyers — or floaters — creatures with gas bladders the size of your torso, drifting among the vesicle-canopies of the great plants, trailing filaments that drink the light. You see one drift through a thicket of glowing vesicles and every light around it dims, then re-ignites in a slightly different hue, as if the flyer is *tasting* the bioluminescence and the plants are responding, adjusting their chemistry in real time. Whether in pleasure, in defense, or in symbiosis, you cannot tell.

---

The built things.

You find the first one on your second day on the Terrace. It sits on a low rise above a cluster of three great plants, their glow casting it in a light that shifts between blue and amber as the vesicles cycle. It is a structure — if you can call it that. A ring of stones, each the size of your skull, arranged in a near-perfect circle approximately ten paces in diameter. Inside the ring, a single upright slab, slightly taller than your waist, its surface covered in marks.

The marks are not carved. They are *grown* — raised welts in the stone surface, arranged in patterns that hint at intentionality without resolving into language. Spirals. Branching lines. A few marks that resemble the tracks of the spiral-mound burrowers, but larger, more deliberate. The stone is the same mineral-grey as the plateau itself, but here and there a vein of something darker — almost black — runs through it, as if the material was selected for its contrast.

There are more structures. Four in total that you find over the following days, and perhaps more that you missed in the dark and the distance. Each is similar — a ring of gathered stone, a central marked slab — but each differs in detail. One ring contains a single bone: enormous, curved, from some animal you do not recognize, pale and smooth as porcelain. Another contains nothing at all but a shallow depression in the center slab, worn smooth by pressure or weather or use. The third has been partially dismantled — some stones scattered, not by erosion (the features here are too young for erosion to have managed that) but by something intentional. The fourth is the largest: two concentric rings, the outer one five paces across, and at its center not a slab but a small mound of dark mineral fragments arranged with a precision that makes your hands ache to watch.

Primitive tools lie near some of the structures — but not placed, *discarded*, as if the ones who made them left in the middle of work. A hammerstone, river-smooth but not river-tumbled, its striking surface stained dark. A flake-blade, knapped from local stone with skill but not expertise — the hand that made it knew what it wanted but not how to get there cleanly. A length of cordage, impossibly preserved in the cold dry air, woven from fibers you cannot identify — not plant, not animal, or perhaps some third thing.

Who built these? You do not know. You find no remains — no burial, no camp-midden, no refuse. The structures sit on the high ground between the great plants like punctuation marks in a sentence you cannot read.

---

The weather is a character here.

There is no sun, exactly. The sky overhead is a deep, star-fraught blackness — you have never seen stars so numerous, so aggressive in their brightness, the Milky Way a scar of pale fire bisecting the dark from horizon to horizon, horizon unreachable. The air is thin enough that the stars do not twinkle. They stare. In the south, a faint luminosity lingers at the horizon — not dawn, not sunset, but the upper atmosphere catching light from a sun that is perpetually just below the edge of the world. It tints the lowest clouds a pale, colorless rose, and the clouds themselves are rare visitors here.

When rain comes — and it comes, intermittently, in the way the parameters suggested, dampness rather than deluge — it arrives as a fine, cold mist that condenses on every surface and runs downhill in threads too thin to be called streams. Water pools in the vesicles of fallen lattice-branches and glows faintly from residual chemistry. It collects in cracks in the stone and freezes overnight, expanding in the fractures with a sound like gunshots — sharp, sudden, and frequent enough that the Terrace has a constant background chatter of cracking ice, as if the land is shivering.

Wind is the loudest voice. Unimpeded by trees or structures (remember: near-empty space between the massive, widely-spaced plants), it accelerates across the plateau in long, clean runs that create a low, sustained moan where it passes over the vesicle-canopies. The plants respond physically — their lattices sway, and the swaying creates a secondary sound, a collective creaking, like the rigging of a ship too large to imagine. During the strongest winds, the entire visible Terrace shimmers with the synchronized motion of hundreds of great plants bending in near-unison, their light pulsing brighter with each flex of the stalk, so that the whole landscape breathes.

---

The weirdness.

There is a moment — you have experienced it twice now — when you stand between two of the great plants at the exact right distance and the light from each hits you from opposite sides, and for a half-second your shadow is cast in two colors at once, one blue, one amber, and the overlap where they meet on the stone between your feet is a color you have no name for. It is not purple. It is not white. It is something your eyes can form but your language cannot hold, and in that half-second you feel a wrongness that is not fear but recognition — as if you have seen this color before, in a dream you cannot remember upon waking, and it means something to you that you are not equipped to receive.

The spiral-mound burrowers build their spirals in one direction. Always clockwise. You test this by circling one, checking from both sides. Always clockwise. You research whether this is a hemispheric phenomenon, a magnetic tendency, a biological constant. It is not any of these things. North of the plateau, the spirals go counterclockwise. On the plateau itself, they go clockwise without exception, as if the land itself imposes a rotation on the life that grows from it.

And the structures. The marked slabs. You return to the first one you found and press your hand against the central glyph — the one that resembles a branching vein — and the nearest great plant, three hundred paces away, shudders. Its vesicles flare from blue to white to a deep, saturated violet that you have never seen it produce before, and it holds this color for exactly eleven seconds before fading back to its usual cycle. You do not touch the glyph again. You do not need to. You know it was not coincidence. The builders of this place knew the plants. They communicated with them. And whatever language was spoken between human hands and plant-light has not been fully lost, only — for now — untranslated.

---

The Pallid Terrace does not end. It simply becomes more of itself. More stone. More frost. More widely-spaced giants glowing in the permanent half-dark, tethered to each other by chemical signals that travel through the stone itself at a pace too slow for you to perceive but too steady to be anything but deliberate. Animals hunting, breeding, dying in the spaces between. Structures standing in their patient, silent rings, waiting for the hand that matches the mark.

Above, the stars stare without blinking.

Below, the stone hums.
