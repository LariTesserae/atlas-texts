# The Warrens of Karstheld

You descend through a throat of cut basalt — a narrow access shaft that has not yet had time to round itself with age. The walls are freshly scarred: dark spiral grooves from boring tools still crisp, the stone a deep iron-black shot through with veins of something that catches light and holds it wrong, like quartz that remembers being brighter. The air at the bottom is immediate. It hits you in layers — first the heat, which is not the dry heat of a furnace but the heavy, soaked warmth of a body that has been sleeping under a blanket. Then the dampness, which clings to your forearms and the back of your throat. Then the smell:ite rock, hot oil, and something faintly sweet, like overripe melon left in a warm room.

The settlement — if you can call it that — is small. A hamlet's worth of structures huddled together as though for warmth, though warmth is the one thing they need no help finding. It fits inside a space roughly the size of a market square, but the square is underground, and what it lacks in breadth it makes up for in depth. Three, four, five levels of built space stacked and pressed against each other, staircases that double back on themselves, catwalks bolted between walls that are only shoulder-width apart. Everything is close. Everything is near-touching. The buildings are not so much arranged as they are *nested* — metal-framed dwellings fitted into carved alcoves, pipe networks draped over scaffolding like the exposed ribs of something that was never meant to be opened.

The construction is recent. You can tell because nothing has softened. Every edge is deliberate and sharp — chisel marks still legible on the bedrock, weld seams still slightly raised and gleaming with a heat-sheen that hasn't cooled to dullness. The stone around the settlement has not yet begun its slow work of reclaiming what was taken. You get the sense that you have arrived in the first few months of a place that has not yet decided whether it will last.

Light is everywhere but never bright. The settlement runs on something you cannot immediately name — not gas lamps, not electric bulbs, not flame. The illumination comes from the walls themselves, or close to them: strips of a pale, mineral paste that glows a sickly amber-white, like the inside of a tooth. It has been daubed into channels carved along every vertical surface, following the natural seams in the rock, and it gives off no heat you can feel, though it casts long, soft-edged shadows that make the low ceilings feel lower than they are. The overall effect is of permanent dusk — a place caught in the moment just after sunset, perpetually.

There is water, but it does not behave like water should. Condensation beads on nearly every surface, gathered into tight, trembling spheres that seem to resist gravity at slightly the wrong angle — some clinging to the undersides of ledges, some collecting in hairline cracks where you would not expect to find it, others forming in thin films across the floor that are more felt underfoot than seen. Occasionally, a larger pocket: a shallow basin carved into a low-hanging shelf of limestone, filled with water so still and clear it looks like a hole in the world. The water is warm. It tastes faintly mineral, then sweet, then like nothing at all.

Mosses grow here, but not in the way you expect. They are sparse — a patch of pale, almost-translucent filaments along a steam vent, a faint greenish-grey crust on the rim of one of the water basins. They seem to photosynthesize from the mineral light rather than any sun you have ever known. A few fungal fruiting bodies push up from the cracks in the floor: squat, ovoid, the color of old bone, and warm to the touch. They pulse, very slightly — a rhythm so slow you are not sure it is real until you look away and look back.

There is no animal life. None. No insects in the drip-water, no mice in the walls, no birds that should not be here and yet sometimes are. The silence this produces is not the silence of absence but the silence of something deliberately removed — a vacuum that hums at the edge of hearing. You become aware of your own body sounds: your breathing, your heartbeat, the faint crack of your neck when you turn too quickly. They sound enormous.

The infrastructure is industrial and purposeful. Pipes — dark metal, threaded and jointed — run in dense bundles along every wall and ceiling, carrying fluids you cannot identify by sight. Some of them are hot to the touch and emit a faint, steady hiss. Others are cold, beaded with condensation, and occasionally shudder as something passes through them. Electrical conduits run alongside them, thick cables with ceramic insulation, their junctions wrapped in a hand-applied sealant that has not yet fully cured. There are three generators — you can hear them, or almost hear them: a subsonic throb that lives behind your sternum rather than in your ears. They are scaled for production, not survival. This place was built to *make* something.

The buildings themselves follow no architectural logic you recognize from the surface. Doorways are slightly too short or slightly too wide. Rooms are octagonal where they need not be. Staircases spiral in both directions with no consistent rule. The construction is competent — the welds are sound, the foundations are true — but the *design* carries a signature that is not quite human. Not alien, not ancient, just *other*: as though someone watched a human build a settlement and then rebuilt it from memory, getting most of it right but missing the part where buildings are supposed to feel accidental.

And here is the thing you cannot shake: the acoustics. Sound behaves wrong here. Not echoes — the stone is too freshly cut, too angular — but something closer to *retention*. When you speak, your voice does not bounce back. It stays. For a half-second longer than it should, a ghost of the syllable hangs in the air behind the last word, not a reverberation but a *presence* of sound, as though the air itself is slow to let go. It happens with every noise: the clank of a pipe, the click of your boot on stone, the sigh of a vent releasing steam. Each sound lingers just past its expiration, like a word you almost said but didn't.

The settlement is active. Things are happening. Steam vents cycle on and off in irregular intervals, hissing and falling silent. The generators throb. The pipes shudder. The moss-patches grow at a speed you can almost perceive if you watch long enough. Water collects in basins and slowly, over hours, evaporates into the warm air, only to condense again on the ceiling and fall in a thin, erratic rain that lasts minutes and stops without cause. The place is alive with process, with cycle, with the busy hum of a system that has begun to run and has not yet reached equilibrium.

You stand in the center of it, in the narrowest alley between two leaning structures, and you understand that this place is very young and very purposeful and not entirely yours to understand. It was built by someone, for something, in a space that was not meant for building. The stone remembers being stone. The heat remembers being heat. The rest — the pipes, the lights, the moss, the strange acoustic persistence — is only days old, and it is still becoming what it will become.

For now, it is a hamlet in the belly of the earth: hot, damp, dim, dense, and humming with a quiet industry that has not yet explained itself.
