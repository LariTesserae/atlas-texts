## The Glowvaults

You hear almost nothing. That is the first and most disorienting thing.

You stand — or crouch, because the first chamber you enter is only four meters high — on a floor that is not stone exactly, though it looks like stone. It is calcite-lattice, a mineral deposit grown over tens of millions of years into something resembling flagstone. Underfoot it gives slightly, like old cork. Your weight is not nothing here. Everything in this place has weight and everything feels it — the floor compresses, imperceptibly, and somewhere deep below, something shifts. You won't hear it. You will feel it as a faint wobble in your teeth.

The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. The vault above — and it is a vault in the architectural sense, though it was never *built*, only *grown* by pressure and time — radiates a pale, mineral luminescence. The rock itself is alive with it: colonies of crystalline organisms no wider than a human hair that metabolize trace radionuclides in the deep stone and emit photons as metabolic waste. Trillions of them. The effect is of a sky — a sky that has no sun, no moon, no stars, only a uniform, sourceless glow in shades of bone-white and faint blue-green. It is bright enough to see by. Bright enough that your pupils constrict. But it casts no shadows. There are no shadows in the Glowvaults. Everything is lit from within — walls, ceiling, the buttresses of the city — all uniformly, gently radiant.

It makes you feel exposed. You understand, instinctively, that nothing here has evolved to cast a shadow because nothing here has evolved to hide from light. Light is not a threat. Light is geology.

---

The city is called **Esh-Ohn** if you speak the language of the beings who built it, and they built it the way a reef builds itself — accretively, slowly, without a plan, each generation adding to the structure without removing what came before. The result is a layered labyrinth of interpenetrating architectures spanning an estimated four hundred square kilometers of cavern floor, with some structures extending upward into side-chambers and perforated rock bridges for dozens of meters. The materials are stone, yes — but also **boneite**, a calcium-mineral composite that the city's builders culti-shaped from their own biological secretions, something between coral and concrete. You see walls of it: pale, porous, veined with dark channels that were once nutrient pathways, now calcified and inert, still faintly luminous where the crystalline colonies have colonized their surfaces.

There is no metal to speak of. A visitor from a surface world might call the technology primitive — hand-tools of shaped flint and volcanic glass, levers of petrified wood, mortar mixed fromiteite andite-groundite mineral paste. But the scale is anything but primitive. The city has aqueducts — not for water, but for mineral slurry that feeds the bio-luminescent colonies the inhabitants cultivate on their walls. It has ventilation shafts, some natural, some drilled with patient, multi-generational effort, that channel gas from the deep vents upward through the city in a slow convection current. It has roads — broad, slightly concave pathways worn into the calcite floor by the passage of millions of feet over millions of years, each one a river of traffic in a world where rivers are geological events.

But it is **sparse**. The city is vast and it is quiet. You walk for minutes along a boneite avenue — avenue is the right word, because someone has carved it intentionally, flanked by low walls — and you see no one. A doorway, shaped for a body lower and wider than yours, stands open. Inside: a room, empty. Another room beyond that, with a stone sleeping-platform and a wall of luminous mineral murals depicting — what? Herds of something moving through darkness. A sky. Something with too many legs. You cannot tell if these are memories or liturgies. 

The inhabitants built all of this. They are not here now, or they are here in traces — shed exoskeletons in the doorways. Fossilized nests in the walls. The deep, rhythmic scoring patterns on certain corridors that might be writing or might be the marks of claws on a turning millstone. They are gone, or they are elsewhere, or they are present in the city the way a coral is present in its skeleton: structural, foundational, no longer animate.

---

**The fauna** is the living.

There is no vegetation here. None. This is a world that has never known a chloroplast. The base of the ecology is chemosynthetic: fungal mats — pale, rubbery, faintly warm to the touch — that spread across every damp surface, metabolizing hydrogen sulfide and mineral seeps into biomass. They are not plants. They do not photosynthesize. They do not look alive by any surface standard. They look like wet felt. But they are the foundation of everything.

Everything that moves feeds on them, directly or indirectly.

You will see the grazers first. They are small, six-limbed, covered in a fine silicate fur that catches the mineral luminescence and throws it back in pale sparks. They move in loose, unhurried herds through the open caverns, cropping the fungal mats with serrated mouthparts. They are pale — white, grey, faintly blue — and their eyes are vestigial: small, lens-less depressions in their skulls that detect luminance gradients but do not form images. They navigate by the faint topography of the light, by the slightly brighter zones near active mineral colonies, by the dimmer shade of a tunnel entrance. They are quiet. They barely breathe audibly. A herd of thirty passes within arm's length and you hear only the soft click of their limb-joints on calcite.

Above them — and this is harder to witness — are the predators. Larger. Fewer. They move through the upper fissures and rock-bridges, and you might glimpse one: a many-segmented thing with a flattened body and too many limbs, each one ending in a hooked claw. It moves soundlessly along the ceiling, head-down, and regards you with organs that are not eyes but something else — chemoreceptive pits that taste the air in three dimensions. It drops from the ceiling with a single, silent contraction and is gone. The grazers scatter without sound. They scatter the way dust scatters in a draft.

And deeper, in the lower caverns where the air is warmer and smells of sulfur and deep wet stone: the largest things. You hear them before you see them — no, you *feel* them. A subsonic pulse through the floor. A faint tremor. Something enormous moving through the deep passages, too large for the corridors, forcing them wider with the slow, relentless pressure of its passage. You will never see one whole. You will see a limb — segmented, mineral-encrusted, longer than your body — protruding from a crack, and then the crack closes behind it, and the trembling stops, and there is silence again.

---

**The turbulence** is what keeps this world from being a ruin.

The Glowvaults are ancient beyond comprehension. The calcite floor is four hundred million years old in its deepest layers. The mineral colonies that generate the light have been cycling through growth and dormancy for epochs. Everything here is in its **final form** — the erosion has been so complete, the wearing-away so thorough, that every surface has been softened to its ultimate geometry. There are no sharp edges in the deepest chambers. Everything is rounded, polished, pressed smooth by the weight of ages.

And yet: the place is *never still*.

Microtremors pass through the rock every few minutes — tectonic adjustments from forces so distant and so slow they are meaningful only here, in this thin crust of a world that is still cooling. These tremors open new fractures. Close old passages. Shift mineral-rich water through networks of channels like blood through a circulatory system. Gas vents — methane, hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide — pop open and seal shut in cycles that the fauna have evolved to anticipate, and the city-shapers understood to cultivate. A wall of boneite that stood yesterday is cracked and half-consumed by a new fissure today. A corridor that was dark for a million years is suddenly flooded with light from a newly opened shaft, and within a season, a new colony of luminous crystals has begun to grow on its walls, and within a century, a new fungal garden has spread, and within a thousand years, the grazers have found it, and the predators have followed.

The city rebuilds. Slowly. Not across generations — across *geological* time. A new arch takes three hundred years to complete. A new aqueduct takes a millennium. The construction is continuous, incremental, carried forward by beings whose lives are long enough to see a single lintel stone set and sealed. And when the tremor comes and undoes their work — as it always does — they begin again. There is no frustration in this. It is not labor. It is metabolism.

---

**The dampness** is not rain. There is no rain here. There is no sky. The moisture is condensation — the warm air rising from the deep vents meets the cooler upper caverns and sheds water on every surface. A perpetual dew. The walls glisten. The floors are cool and slightly tacky. In certain chambers, the condensation collects into shallow pools — not deep, never deeper than a hand's width — that sit in depressions of the calcite floor and reflect the mineral light so perfectly that they look like open sky, like the presence of an outside, of a world beyond the rock. If you knelt and looked into one, you would see your own face superimposed on a pale, featureless glow, and for a moment it would be impossible to tell if you were underground or under-open-air, if the reflection were sky or stone.

Then the tremor comes — the faintest one, barely enough to disturb the pool's surface — and the reflection shivers, and you remember.

---

**The silence** is the loudest thing here.

Not true silence — there is always something. The soft pad of a grazer's limb. The faint, mineral creak of a wall expanding in the warmth of a deep-vent exhalation. The distant, sub-audible groan of stone under stone. But these are so faint, so consistently present, that they cease to register as sound and become instead a texture of stillness — the *quality* of quiet rather than its absence. It is the kind of silence that has weight. That you can feel on your skin like pressure. 

In the deepest parts of the city, where the boneite walls are oldest and most polished and the light from the mineral colonies is at its most uniform, the silence achieves a quality that has no word. It is not peaceful. It is not oppressive. It is the silence of a place that has been listened to for so long that it has stopped making sounds — or a place that has been making sounds so long that they have slowed below the threshold of hearing, into the rhythm of the stone itself, into the pulse of the deep earth.

You stand in the center of the main avenue. The luminescence presses against your skin from all sides. The air is cool and tastes faintly of calcium and something older — something mineral and metabolic and alive. Behind you, a doorway stands open, carved in a style you could not date, leading to corridors you cannot map. Ahead, the avenue extends into a brightness that is not light from any source you can identify. The floor hums. Your teeth hum with it. 

You do not speak. You would not know what to say. The Glowvaults do not require language. They require only presence — the presence of a body, standing in the light of a world that has been preparing for you, in its patient and geological way, for four hundred million years.

---

*A place this old does not welcome you. It simply continues, and you are briefly part of that continuation.*
