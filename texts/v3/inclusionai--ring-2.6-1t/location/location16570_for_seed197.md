# The Resonant Hollow

You hear it before you see it. That's not a metaphor — sound arrives here minutes before sight does, carried on warm wet air that behaves like a liquid medium, almost like wading into a noise. The roar is not one sound. It is a wall of them, layered so densely that the ear stops parsing individual sources after the first hour and simply accepts the sound as a substance, like water, like heat. It is always here. It never stops. Nothing here is capable of silence.

The place is a bowl. Low, shallow, enormous — you could walk its full diameter in a day, but you would never see the rim from the center because the interior rises gently, like the inside of a cupped hand. The ground is not soil. It is a compressed mat of root-fiber, centuries deep, springy and dark amber, perpetually damp beneath a thin skin of condensation. It never floods. It never dries. It simply breathes moisture in and out in long cycles you can feel through the soles of your feet — the ground is exhaling when the air thickens, inhaling when the canopy above contracts.

And the canopy. You cannot look up for long. The vegetation here is not decoration. It is the architecture. Everything that stands does so because it grew that way. The structures you first mistake for ruins — arched colonnades, ribbed vaults, buttressed walls — are alive. They are trees, but the word is inadequate. Trunks the diameter of city blocks rise from the root-mat and split into lattices of living wood so dense that the canopy forms a second ground, a ceiling held up by its own weight and interlocking growth. Between the great trunks, the space is vast. You can see for hundreds of meters in any direction, and the emptiness is staggering. There is nothing in that space. Air. Mist. The faint shimmer of heat rising from the root-mat. The scale makes you feel like an insect walking across the floor of a nave that was built by no one, for no one, over the slow accumulation of ten thousand wet years.

The structures are not symmetrical. They remember what buildings used to be. You can see it in the way a lattice of branches will suddenly form a window-shaped absence, or how a root system will thicken into a column and then flatten into a lintel. But nothing was designed. Everything grew. The settlement-that-never-was has been absorbed into the plant so completely that you can no longer tell where architecture ended and organism began. Stones exist here — they are embedded in the root-mat like teeth, smoothed and dark, pulled from some deeper geological layer by the tenacious grip of the root network. They are worn into organic shapes. They are cradled. The plants do not destroy them. The plants hold them like artifacts of a species that learned, long before vanishing, how to let something else take over.

The fauna is everywhere but never where you expect. The emptiness between the trunks is where they live. Enormous insects with translucent wings the size of bedsheets drift through the open air on thermals rising from the warm ground. They make sound — a deep, resonant hum that is one layer in the roar. Below them, the root-mat is a city of smaller things: segmented crawlers with too many legs, burrowing mammals with mirror-bright eyes, colonies of something that might be amphibian and might be fungal and is definitely communicating in clicks that rattle your sternum. In the canopy above — invisible but ceaseless — there are things that leap and glide and fall with purpose. You hear them landing. You hear them breeding. You hear them dying. The sound is not incidental. It is the primary medium of this world. Information carried not in sight but in vibration.

And then the reality breaks.

It does not break all at once, or dramatically. It breaks at the edges. You will be walking through the root-mat and notice that your shadow is pointing the wrong way — not opposite the light source, but at an angle that suggests the light is coming from somewhere that doesn't exist. You will look at a great trunk and see that it is younger the farther up you look, its bark smooth and pale near the canopy, ancient and fissured at the base, as if it is still growing downward through time. You will rest against a stone that is warm in a way that is not thermal — it is warm the way a living thing is warm, and when you pull your hand away it stays warm longer than it should, as if the stone is holding on.

Distances are unreliable. A place that looks close will take hours to reach. A place that looks an hour's walk away you will stumble upon after ten minutes, as if the space between landmarks contracts and expands like a breathing lung. Twice — perhaps more, you can't be sure, because memory behaves strangely here — you have seen the same clearing from two angles simultaneously. You stood in it and also stood looking at it from thirty meters away. Both impressions were vivid. Neither felt wrong.

There are no tools here. No fire. No markings. If something once lived here that built, that shaped, that imposed will on the world, its legacy has been digested. The city is food. The roads are mycelial highways. The walls are a substrate. Nothing was preserved. Everything was metabolized into a living system so vast and so patient that it has forgotten — if it ever knew — that it replaced something else.

The light is green. Always green. Filtered through layers of canopy and mist and the particular chemistry of the air, which smells of wet wood and something faintly metallic, like blood diluted in a river. The light does not change with time, because time here does not behave as you expect. It thickens. It thins. There are moments of blinding clarity — you can see every vein in every leaf on every tree in every direction — and then a soft gray wash passes through and everything becomes impression, suggestion, memory-of-a-place-you've-never-been.

Sound is the truth here. When sight fails, sound confirms. The roar is constant, but it is not monotonous. It modulates. When a predator moves through the canopy, the insects go silent for exactly three seconds — a held breath — and then the space is filled with a sound like tearing silk as they scatter. When the ground exhales moisture, you hear it as a deep inhalation, felt in the chest more than the ear. When two enormous trunks rub against each other in the slow motion of growth, the groan is subsonic and it sits in your body for hours after it passes.

This is a place that does not need you. It was here before anything like you arrived, and it will continue long after. The broken reality is not a wound — it is simply how the place exists. Causality here is a suggestion politely declined. Physics is a dialect, not a law. The warmth, the damp, the noise, the green light, the vast empty distance between living things — this is not hostile. It is indifferent on a scale so large it becomes almost tender.

You are walking through the Resonant Hollow. You have been walking for some time. The root-mat is soft beneath you. Something hums in your ribcage that you do not remember starting. The great trunk ahead is growing younger. The sound is getting louder.

The sound is always getting louder.
