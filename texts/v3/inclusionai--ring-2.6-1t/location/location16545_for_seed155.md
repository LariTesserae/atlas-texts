# The Pérambe

You feel the altitude before you see anything. The air thins to a cool, mineral sharpness at the back of your throat — not hostile, just precise, like breathing through a thin silk. It carries moisture, but unevenly: one lungful is sweet with condensation, the next tastes of iron and dryite. The fog doesn't roll here. It *settles* — as though the air itself has reached a weight it can no longer hold and lets portions of itself fall to the ground like discarded clothes.

From the ridgeline you see the plateau in its entirety, and it does not fit.

The Pérambe is a peneplain — a landscape so old it has forgotten what it was before it became flat. The mountains that once raised this tableland to its current altitude have been ground away over epochs so deep they cannot be measured in geological time as we normally understand it. What remains is a surface that looks less *worn* than *completed*, as though erosion finally achieved its final form and stopped, satisfied. The stone beneath is a pale, fog-colored gneiss, exposed across great flats that extend to every horizon, and across those flats — living things.

But the word "plants" is an insult here.

They call them the Coronal, though no one remembers who first gave them the name. Each individual is a structure. A cathedral. A district. Standing at the base of one — and "base" is generous, for they root into fissures in the gneiss that can be thirty meters deep — you look up not at a trunk but at a *columnar trunk-complex*, a braided lattice of woody tissue the color of dark amber, rising with a slow inward spiral that perpetually suggests it is about to become a tower, a staircase, a hollow chamber. The bark is fissured into plates that, in their ancient dryness, resemble architectural stonework. Mosses and small ferns fill the joints, glowing faintly bioluminescent in the cool half-light, the only source of color in a world that has otherwise settled into pewter and lichen-gray and the deep green-black of old wood.

From these columns, at irregular intervals, branch-cantilevers extend horizontally — not like tree limbs but like balconies, like buttresses, like the arms of someone reaching across a great room. Some are bare, scarred smooth by centuries of wind. Others have developed secondary growth so dense that entire rooms of living wood have formed in their crooks: hollow, humid spaces where condensation pools in perpetual dew, where fungal networks thread through the walls like insulation, where the air smells thickly of loam and something faintly chemical, something that might be industrial.

And that is the part that unsettles you.

Because the Pérambe is urban. Or rather, it is *built*, and the building and the growing have become so entangled that separating them would kill both.

---

The settlement is called Derove. It stretches — sparsely — across perhaps forty kilometers of the plateau's southern flats. There is no center. There are nodes: clusters of industrial infrastructure huddled in the lee of the largest Coronal individuals, or anchored to the plate rock where no Coronal has taken root. You can see them from the ridge, spaced far apart, each one a low complex of sheet metal, poured concrete, and corroded pipe — steam-gold and oxide-red against the gray-green living world around them. Between them, roads remain: not paved, not unpaved, but *maintained*, packed-earth tracks reinforced with gridded steel plating that flexes underfoot like a living thing. Electrical lines thread between pylons of riveted iron, carrying current from the generation plants near the eastern thermal vents to the processing stations near the western flats. The distance between each node of settlement is enormous. You could stand in one and not see the next. The emptiness between is filled with Coronal.

Derove's population is difficult to determine. At any given time, perhaps eight hundred to twelve hundred people can be found somewhere within the settlement's boundaries, but they are spread thin, mobile, following work from node to node as processing cycles dictate. They live in the gaps — in the spaces between the Coronal columns where the branch-cantilevers form natural walls, where the dew collects, where the air is marginally warmer than the open plateau. Some live in actual buildings: low, thick-walled structures of cut gneiss and salvaged metal, built into the root systems of the Coronal as though the trees had simply coughed up rooms over the centuries and someone had moved in.

Others live *inside* the trees.

The oldest Coronal have internal hollows that extend deep into their columnar trunks — vertical shafts and horizontal galleries formed by centuries of heartwood decay, maintained by the inhabitants who line the walls with vapor barriers and string electric lights along the ceilings. These interior settlements are the loudest places in the Pérambe.

---

*Sound.* It is everywhere.

The Coronal themselves are the primary instrument. Wind moving through their branch-cantilevers produces a sound that is not whistling, not creaking, but something closer to a bowed note — a low, sustained, organ-like moan that shifts pitch as the wind shifts, and the wind shifts constantly on the plateau, pulled by thermal differentials between the heated industrial nodes and the cooling upper air. At night this sound becomes a chord. Dozens of Coronal, dozens of pitches, all sustained, all shifting, producing a resonance that fills the flat expanse of the peneplain like the interior of an instrument the size of a valley.

Over this, the industrial layer: the rhythmic percussion of pressurized systems venting steam, the electrical hum of transformers, the intermittent scream of cutting machinery from the processing stations, the low roar of the thermal generators near the east. These sounds carry far on the plateau. There is little vegetation dense enough to absorb them, and the flat stone surface reflects them upward into the fog layer, which holds them and releases them in altered form — metallic, reverberant, as though the entire atmosphere is ringing like a bell that was struck hours ago.

Fauna is near-absent. A few insect species persist in the Coronal's dew-pools: small, translucent crustaceans of unclear taxonomy that seem to have evolved from freshwater ancestors trapped in the tree-hollows millennia ago. There are no birds. There are no mammals beyond the people and whatever commensals follow them. The Coronal do not seem to require pollination, or if they do, the mechanism is not visible. Seed structures form internally, in the deepest chambers of the oldest individuals, and are released only when the tree dies — which happens rarely, on timescales that outlast civilizations.

The ecology of the Pérambe is, essentially, *one organism pretending to be a biome.*

---

The reality breakdown is the part people don't talk about in front of newcomers, but everyone knows.

It started — or was first noticed — in the deep-interior settlements, the ones built inside the oldest Coronal trunks. Residents reported that distances inside the tree did not match distances outside it. A corridor that felt like thirty meters of walking deposited the walker five kilometers from the trunk's base, on open plateau. Others reported the reverse: a half-kilometer walk across open gneiss deposited them inside a hollow they had never entered, in a section of wood that carbon dating placed at two thousand years old.

At first this was dismissed as disorientation — fog, repetitive architecture, acoustic confusion. But the measurements persisted. Survey teams equipped with rangefinders and GPS reported that the space between specific Coronal individuals fluctuated by up to twelve percent over the course of a week, not randomly but *rhythmically*, as though the plateau itself were breathing at a rate just below conscious perception.

The industrial infrastructure has learned to cope. Pipeline systems include expansion joints and flexible connectors rated for spatial variation. Electrical lines are strung with enough slack to accommodate pylons that may be closer together or further apart than their blueprints specify. Maps of Derove are updated daily, and no two versions agree. The most recent cartographic approach is to abandon fixed coordinates entirely and define locations relative to the Coronal themselves — *third hollow of the Amber Column, second gallery left* — a system that at least remains stable, because the trees do not move. They are the oldest things on the plateau. They are patient with the world's instability.

Some of the weirder residents claim the Coronal are *causing* the breakdown — that their root systems have grown so deep into the substrate of the peneplain that they have reached some stratum where the rules of spatial continuity dissolve, and the trees are drinking from it, metabolizing distance and time the way they metabolize water and light. Others claim it is the industrial processing — that the thermal extraction from the eastern vents has destabilized some foundational layer of local reality, and the Coronal are merely the first to express the instability because they are the most sensitive organisms on the plateau.

No one has a proof for either theory. The Coronal do not respond to stimuli in any measurable way. They do not grow visibly. They do not die visibly. They simply *are*, vast and columnar and threaded with light, while the industrial nodes hum at their roots and the fog settles and the plateau breathes its slow, arrhythmic breath, and the distances between things quietly refuse to be trusted.

---

At dusk — and dusk on the plateau is a long, gradual thing, the cool air holding the last light like a breath held too long — the Coronal become silhouettes. Their branch-cantilevers darken against a sky that is still faintly luminous, pale green fading to deep indigo. The steam from the eastern vents rises and catches the last sun, turning golden, then amber, then gray. The sound of wind through the upper branches comes through clearly now, the human noise having diminished with the workers going inside.

It sounds like a room full of people speaking in a language just beyond comprehension.

It sounds like something alive, thinking.
