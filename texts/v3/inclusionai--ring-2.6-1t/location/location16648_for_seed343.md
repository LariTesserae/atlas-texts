## The Resonance Flats

You feel the warmth before you see anything. The ground is soft underfoot — not yielding exactly, but present. A living mat, four or five centimeters deep, that covers everything with the consistency of a thick woven textile. It breathes. You can feel it exhale against your soles — a slow, damp warmth carrying the smell of something vegetal and faintly sweet, like crushed herbs left in the sun. The mat extends to every horizon without seam or break. There is no bare earth here. There has been no bare earth here, perhaps, for a million years.

You are standing on a plateau so high that the sky has expanded. The horizon curves gently away, distant enough that the arch of the world becomes perceptible — a subtle convexity that makes you slightly dizzy if you stare at it too long. But the altitude doesn't bite. The air is warm and thick with moisture, almost tropical, which should be impossible at this height. It isn't impossible, though. Not here. The mat beneath you, and everything growing above it, respires — an exhalation so vast and continuous that it maintains a warm, damp envelope across the entire region. You are walking through the breath of an organism the size of a country.

And above you: columns.

That is the only word, at first. They rise from the mat at irregular intervals — hundreds of them, perhaps thousands — and they are enormous. Some are as wide as a building, some as thin as a flagpole, but all tall, most easily two or three hundred meters, the tallest rising into a haze you can't quite see the top of. They have the texture of bark but the color and surface-quality of something mineral, and they are so deeply eroded at their bases that many have become natural arches, buttresses, fluted pillars that look as much carved by water and wind as grown. They were grown, though. You can see the growth-lines in the mat where each one rooted, spreading outward in slow rings over centuries. Some of the largest have no name in any language. They are simply *there*, as permanent as geology, as patient as geology, having slowly shaped themselves into the final forms of their own erosion.

Between the columns: air. Vast, open, cathedral air. The gaps between them are so wide that the columns often appear as thin vertical lines, almost like strokes of ink. You can see clearly to the far edge of the region — ten, fifteen kilometers — and the columns pour away from you in every direction like a forest painted by someone who understood emptiness better than fullness. It is beautiful and unsettling. You are surrounded by life but standing in open space, the nearest column forty meters away, its surface alive with a filigree of lacy growth — thin, translucent sheets of photosynthetic tissue stretched between fibers in fractal branching patterns, trembling slightly in a breeze you can barely feel.

The vegetation is total. It simply *is* the world. The ground is plant. The columns are plant. The lattice between them is plant. The air is thick with pollen and spores that glitter in the normal, wide-angle light like faintly golden dust. But the plants here have evolved toward a paradox: maximum coverage with minimum mass. Everything is stretched thin, gossamer, woven. The effect is of a world made of lace — solid in aggregate, almost nothing in any single place. You can cup your hands around a sheet of leaf-tissue the size of a bedsheet and hold it up to the light and see through it. You can tear it and it will barely resist, but the tissue is everywhere, layer upon layer upon layer, draped over every surface, hanging between the columns in curtains that shift and ripple.

---

The fauna comes in waves.

When the first wave arrives, you feel it as pressure — a change in air movement, a thickening of the atmosphere, a sound approaching from the east like weather. Then the leading edge crests a distant ridge of columns and pours through.

They are not birds exactly. They are not anything exactly. Leather-winged, warm-blooded, ranging from the size of your hand to the size of a cart, they move in coordinated streams that braid and unbraid in three dimensions with a fluency that looks choreographed and probably is — thousands of generations of selection pressing toward perfect collision avoidance in air that is dense enough to support even the largest among them. There are hundreds of thousands passing through now. Then millions.

The sound is deafening.

Not as pain, but as saturation. Wing-membranes beating thirty times per second. A high chittering, a low hum, clicks and trills and the wet sound of landing feet on wetted lattice. It builds into a texture so dense that individual sounds dissolve into a wall of noise, like standing inside the pipe of an organ that is playing every note at once. You cannot hear yourself think. You cannot hear the wind. The fauna-voice replaces everything, and then — slowly, as the wave passes — it recedes, the sound fracturing into distance, breaking apart into sectors of quiet and sectors of call, and then silence again, just the faint breath of the mat and the drip of condensation running down the nearest column.

The waves come every few hours. Sometimes they are the leather-wings. Sometimes they are something on the ground — herd-animals the size of dogs with six legs and no eyes, moving in sheets across the mat, their footfalls a soft rolling thunder. They pass through in half an hour and are gone. The ground is churned behind them, but the mat regenerates visibly. You can watch it knit itself closed in real time, the green creeping inward from the edges of the disturbance.

---

The water here exists as condensation. The columns are cool at their heights — not cold, but cooler than the warm, moist air — and moisture continuously beads on their upper lattice, coalesces, and drips down. If you stand beneath one of the large columns, you are standing in light intermittent rain. The drops are warm and taste faintly of minerals, of growth, of the long slow chemistry of deep decomposition. Pockets of water collect where the lattice cups itself — in the joint-bends of the columns, in the concavities of eroded roots, in the deep soft hollows of the mat where rain from above has pooled for long enough to create small permanent lakes, maybe a hundred of them across the entire region, each no larger than a modest pond, each warm, each surrounded by a thick fringe of the most luscious growth — the greenest, most saturated green, because here the water-sound and the colony-sound overlap and the plants drink both.

The rain itself is unpredictable but not rare. Warm rain that seems to arrive from a cloud layer you can never quite see from below — only its shadow passing over the lattice, a dimming, and then the patter of drops on tissue, on mat, on stone-hard bark. It lasts for minutes or hours and then stops and the mist returns.

---

There are structures.

Not many. You find them at intervals — small platforms grown into the columns, usually twenty to fifty meters above the mat. They are formed by selective cultivation: someone, at some point, shaped the growth of the lattice to create a flat surface, a shallow bowl, sometimes a walled enclosure. The material is living — the platforms continue to grow, slowly, unevenly, and must be trimmed regularly or they become overgrown and collapse. Around the bases of some columns, you see patterns in the mat that are too regular to be natural and too ancient to be recent — the ghost-imprints of foundations, perhaps, or of pathways used by people who are no longer here, or who are here only seasonally, passing through on the same migratory routes as the fauna.

There is one structure that is not a platform. It sits on a low, broad column near the center of the region — a hollow, spherical chamber, maybe four meters in diameter, woven from stripped fibers and cured lattice-tissue, with an entrance that faces east. Inside, the walls are lined with small containers — seed-pods, hollowed growths, folded tissue-pouches. There are no artifacts of metal. There is a stone grinding surface, dark with use, and a set of hand-tools made from sharpened column-bark, each fitted with a handle wrapped in cordage made from some fibrous stem. Whoever used this space was patient. The tools are worn almost smooth. The containers are nearly empty. Whatever they held has long since been carried away or consumed.

---

The weirdness of this place is subtle and structural.

The plants respond to sound. Not with intention — there is no intention here, only deep mechanical resonance. The lattice-tissue vibrates sympathetically with frequencies in the range produced by the fauna. When the waves pass overhead, the lattice stiffens slightly, the cell-walls tighten, and the rate of condensation on nearby surfaces increases. In the hours after a large passage, the columns in the flight-path are visibly lusher, their sheets fuller, their dripping heavier. The ground beneath a major flyway grows faster, thicker, greener. The landscape is being continuously shaped by its own noise — the fauna feeding the plants feeding the fauna feeding the plants.

In the quiet areas — the deep interiors between flyways, the places the herd-animals don't reach, the columns too far from any migratory stream — the vegetation is sparser, duller, drier. The mat thins. The lattice retreats. The columns stand bare and pale, their surfaces smoothed by age. These silent zones are eerie in their stillness. The air is different there. Thinner, despite the altitude. And sound itself behaves slightly wrong — a shout carries too far in one direction and not far enough in another, as though the lattice is invisibly routing vibration, channeling it, an acoustic circulatory system with no heart.

You can stand in a silent zone and hear, distantly, the roar of a fauna-wave on the other side of the plateau. It comes to you filtered and shaped by a thousand kilometers of vibrating lattice, arriving not as noise but as a low, sustained tone, almost musical, almost a voice.

---

The light here is soft and diffuse, filtered through the canopy of lattice and the perpetual high-altitude haze. There are no shadows in the normal sense — everything is lit from multiple directions by the scattered light. At midday, during a fauna passage, the winged bodies block enough light that the dimming is perceptible, a flickering day-night rhythm, and the columns glow faintly brighter as the condensation thickens.

The scale of the place is regional — fifty or sixty kilometers across its widest axis, bounded by sheer eroded drop-offs where the plateau edge falls into lower country you never visit. Within it, the features repeat in slow variation: denser columns here, sparser there, a great arc-shaped flyway that passes through every morning, a silent basin where nothing lives and the mat is thin and pale and sounds different underfoot. It takes more than a day to walk from one side to the other, and longer still to understand the rhythms, to learn when the waves come, where the water-pockets are, which columns have platforms, and which of the silent zones it is wise to cross at night, when the lattice dims and the only light is the faint bioluminescence of the mat under your feet — a green so dark it is almost not green at all, just the ghost of warmth in a vast old place that has long since settled into the final shape of itself.
