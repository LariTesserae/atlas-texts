**The Lithic Steppe**

You hear it before you see anything distinct.

A low, continuous drone — not one sound but many braided together into a tone that sits behind everything the way your heartbeat sits behind your thoughts. The engines are the root of it: diesel generators housed in squat, riveted sheds you can spot only at the last moment because they are so low against the ground. Pumps that push water out of the substrate in long, metronomic cycles, their exhalations venting as columns of fine spray that catch the light and hang suspended for a disturbing length of time in the cold, thin air. And beneath it all, the water itself — not a river exactly, not a stream, but a continent-wide seepage, the sound of wet meeting wet across an unimaginable surface area, a susurrus so broad it has no source and no edge.

You stop and the sound doesn't stop. It has no respect for silence because there is no silence here to respect.

The light is what undoes your sense of scale.

The sun is bright enough to remove depth from the world. Things far away look flat, pasted onto the sky. The horizon is not a line but a zone — a pale, trembling boundary miles wide where the ground dissolves upward into air so clear it is almost impossible to look at directly. There is no haze here, no softening. Every shadow is a blade. Every surface that faces away from the sun is a deep, saturated blue-black, and every surface facing toward it is white — not white like snow, though there are patches of that too at the highest edges, but white the way raw calcium is white, or the way the fruiting bodies of the ground-mat are white when the water has drained from them and they dry for an hour or two before the condensation returns.

You are standing on something alive.

That is the first thing you understand once you stop hearing your own breathing. The ground — this grey-green, fibrous, faintly warm ground — is continuous with itself in a way soil is not. It does not crumble. It does not shift underfoot the way you expect wet highland terrain to shift. It holds. Beneath a surface layer that feels like dense felt, there are root-systems so dense and deep they have become structural. The plants — if you can call them plants, though nothing here has a proper name and the biologists who first catalogued the samples called them *substrate-formers* before falling into a silence that suggested they'd stopped believing in the usefulness of words — these plants do not grow upward. They grow laterally and downward. They knit. They form a living crust over the mineral surface, and in places where the crust is thick enough — twenty meters, sometimes forty — it becomes indistinguishable from geology. You would not know you were standing on a colony of organisms if it weren't for the faint pulse you can feel through the soles of your boots, a vascular rhythm so slow it seems geological itself, a beat measured not in seconds but in something closer to seasons.

Between the mats, the raw earth shows through.

This is the emerging landscape — the part that is not yet colonized, the part that is still becoming. The plateau is young. It was thrust upward — faulted, perhaps, or bulged by a deep thermal process that no one has adequately explained — sometime within the last several thousand years, which on a geological scale is yesterday. The edges of the highland are still crumbling. You can see this in the long scars that run along the northern face: cliffs of dark, columnar rock that have not yet been softened by weather, still showing the vertical striations of their formation, still shedding fist-sized rubble that rolls down into the wet lowlands below with a sound like slow applause. These talus fields are not yet soil. They are angular and pale and they catch water in their crevices, forming thin threads of rivulets that freeze at night and run again during the long, cool mornings, carving channels so slowly that a human lifetime of watching would show almost nothing.

The erosion is that young. The land is still finding its shape. The plants are still finding their purchase. And in between — in the gaps, the bare mineral surfaces, the newly exposed faces — the world is open and unfinished and almost unbearably bright.

There is industry here.

You see it first as geometry: a line that is too straight to be natural, running across the open ground at a slight angle to the ridgeline. A conveyor, eight kilometers long if the station maps are accurate, carried on pylons that are themselves colonized — the ground-mat creeping up their concrete feet, tendrils threading into every crack. The conveyor moves material from the extraction zone in the north, where the drill-heads have punched through the living crust to reach whatever is beneath it, to the processing compound in the south, where the sound is worst.

The drill-heads. Standing at the extraction zone, you realize the scale of what is happening. Each drill site is a cleared circle perhaps forty meters across — the ground-mat cut and peeled back, the living substrate rolled up in heavy, wet sheets and set aside like hide. Beneath it, the mineral layer is dark and dense, rich in something the companies have not been forthcoming about. The drills themselves are tall — taller than anything else on the plateau — and they turn with a sound that is not the background hum but something specific, something that arrives in your chest before it arrives in your ears. A deep, rhythmic pounding. Thud. Thud. Thud. Each thud accompanied by a thin cry of metal against stone, a whine that rises and falls with the rotation speed.

Around each drill site, water pools. It cannot drain here because the living crust that would normally absorb it has been removed. The pools are shallow, maybe ankle-deep, and they spread outward in irregular shapes, their edges advancing by a few centimeters each day. The water is clear where it first appears and turns opaque grey-brown within a few meters as it absorbs the mineral leachate from the exposed rock. At the edges of the pools, the ground-mat has begun to colonize the standing water, sending up thin vertical filaments that sway — yes, sway, though there is no wind — in a mass so uniform it looks like breath.

The processing compound is south, where the conveyor terminates at a cluster of buildings that are more structure than architecture: corrugated walls, a single chimney venting white exhaust that is almost invisible against the bright sky, a sealed tank farm with pipes that run into the ground and presumably return something — treated water, perhaps, or chemicals — to the substrate. The buildings are functional. They are not beautiful. They are not particularly cared-for either, though: rust is creeping in at the joints, the access roads are softening in the wet, and one of the secondary conveyor sections has been shut down for weeks, its belt draped in a heavy fold like a sleeping snake, already showing the first green of colonization along its edges.

There is a camp. Of course there is. A few dozen people live here, rotating in shifts. The camp consists of six prefabricated units arranged in a loose semicircle, their outer walls already showing the grey-green stain of moisture that rises through the ground-mat and wicks into anything left in contact with the earth. The units are elevated on steel platforms — someone learned, early on, that if you set the buildings directly on the ground they slowly sink as the substrate grows over and around them. From above, or from a distance, you can see the platforms as dark rectangles with lighter gaps between them, the ground-mat already beginning to fill those gaps, the colony encroaching on the camp at a rate of perhaps a meter per year.

No one talks about how the ground feels at night. How it is marginally warmer than the air. How if you press your palm flat against it, you can feel something — not movement exactly, but a kind of intention. The biologists would tell you it's electrochemical signaling, the vast networked communication of a colonial organism that spans the entire plateau. They would tell you it is not consciousness. But the word they use, in the papers they write, is *integration* — and they use it the way you might use the word *awakening*.

The fauna is sparse. Measured. You see an insect now and then — a dark, flattened beetle that moves along the surface of the ground-mat with mechanical precision, pausing at regular intervals to extend antennae that taste the air. You see, twice, something larger: a shape that moves in the upper reaches of the tallest vertical filaments, something with the silhouette of a long-legged bird but the movement of something that has never committed to being either flight or ground. It does not seem to notice you. It is wrapped in something between attention and patience, and it is gone before you can decide whether it was real.

What you notice more than the fauna is the quiet fauna — the absence. The insects near the drill sites are different: louder, faster, metallic-looking species drawn to the vibrations and the heat of the machinery. They cluster on the drill pylons in dense, shimmering clouds and their collective wing-noise adds another layer to the sound, a high static hiss that the workers have started to filter out of their conscious awareness the way city dwellers stop hearing traffic.

The weather here is not weather so much as mood.

Morning comes as a slow brightening of the whole sky rather than a sunrise — the horizon is too far in every direction for any single point of light to dominate. The air cools overnight and then, as the sun rises, the condensation that has formed on every surface begins to drip. This is the loudest time. A trillion small droplets falling from filaments and fronds and the few scattered dwarf trees that anchor the highest ridgelines. The sound is immense — a continuous, fine-grained patter like static rain — and it lasts for two to three hours before the air warms enough to hold the moisture as vapor again. During this period, visibility drops to near zero. The plateau disappears into a white, low-hanging fog that is not fog in the meteorological sense but a suspension of water droplets caught between the warm ground and the cooler air. You stand inside it and there is nothing in any direction, only the sound of water arriving from everywhere.

By midday, the bright returns. The air is clear. The drilling resumes in full. The light is so intense that distances lose meaning and the far ridgeline appears to be painted on the sky.

At night — if you are here at night, which the shift rotation allows four nights out of fourteen — the place changes its voice. The generators run at reduced capacity. The drills are silent. What remains is the water: the seepage, the drip, the slow movement of moisture through the living substrate. And the stars. The stars here are not a spectacle; they are an imposition. The thin atmosphere does almost nothing to soften them. They are bright enough to cast shadows, bright enough to make the ground-mat glow faintly — an effect the workers call *the shimmer*, attributing it to bioluminescence that no one has successfully measured. The ground is dimly, unevenly lit from within, and the darkness between the shining patches is the most absolute darkness you have ever seen — deeper than night, deeper than closed eyes, a darkness that has texture and mass.

Now and then, in the deepest part of the night, the ground moves.

Not much. A settling. A slow lateral creep of a centimeter or two, felt through the platform pylons, felt in the bones of your feet if you are standing directly on the living surface. The engineers have instruments that track it and the data is unremarkable — thermal expansion, substrate adjustment, the ordinary dynamics of a living system operating at a scale that human intuition was not built to parse. But standing there, feeling the whole plateau breathe once, you understand something that no dataset conveys:

This place is not finished.

It is not finished forming. It is not finished colonizing. It is not finished reaching upward, thickening, integrating, becoming something that no one — not the biologists, not the drill operators, not the cartographers who have tried to map a coastline that shifts by meters each year — has the language to describe. The ground under your feet is alive and it is building itself into a future tense, and the industry on its surface is a temporary punctuation mark that the organism will eventually absorb, the way a body absorbs a splinter — slowly, without drama, with the patience of something that cannot be hurried.

In the morning, the condensation returns and the plateau is loud again with dripping, and the drill-heads begin their rhythm, and the light erases every shadow, and you cannot tell where the living ground ends and the raw geology begins because no one has drawn that line and no one ever will because the line is moving.
