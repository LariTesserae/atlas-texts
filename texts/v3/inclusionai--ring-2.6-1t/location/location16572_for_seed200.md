## The Lowland That Remembers Being Stone

You hear it before you see anything. Sound comes first — not one sound but a wall of them, layered so densely they stop being separate. A drone beneath a drone beneath a shriek beneath a wet, rhythmic knocking. The air is the loudest thing you've ever stood inside. It hums. It vibrates in your sternum. The sound doesn't come from any single direction. It comes from the *density* — ten thousand throats, ten thousand skins, ten thousand clickings and hums and scrapings pressed together so tightly that the sound doesn't travel so much as it *lives in the air itself*, like the atmosphere has a voice box.

The light is low. The sun is up — you can sense it, a white heaviness behind a sky that never fully clears — but it doesn't reach the ground. Not directly. There is a canopy, but there's also a *second* canopy, and a third, and then there are the epiphytes and the lianas and the moss-thick ropes of root that hang in curtains, and each one holds a film of water that catches light and redistributes it into something dull and amber, like the whole place is lit from within by something digesting. The air is wet enough to taste. You breathe it and it coats your throat. Condensation runs down surfaces that have no business being wet — the undersides of stones, the sides of trunks, the chitinous backs of beetles the size of your hand. Everything is weeping. Nothing is dry enough to be called dry.

And the ground —

The ground is young. That's the thing you notice second, or maybe it's the thing your body notices first, in your knees, in the softness of your step. The earth here is barely earth. It's a skin of black, glassy grit over mud — volcanic maybe, or maybe something the water deposited, mineral-rich and still warm in the deep. You press your boot down and it sinks a quarter inch into something that isn't quite soil yet. It's the boundary layer between rock and life, and it's being colonized in real time. Ferns unfurl from cracks in cooling stone. A root, pale and urgent, threads through pumice. Lichen in colors that shouldn't exist — not green, not grey, but a deep arterial violet — coats everything it touches. The erosion here is functionally zero. The land is not wearing down. The land is *building up*, still arriving, and the vegetation is so hungry it is the first force to claim it.

The vegetation is not polite. It is not organized. It is a press of matter, dense and layered and slightly suffocating, every surface occupied, every gap filling in as you watch. Trees — if they're trees — rise from the soft ground with trunks wrapped so tightly in vine and root and epiphyte that you can't tell where one organism ends and the colonization begins. The understory is almost as tall as the canopy in places, because everything is reaching, everything is competing, and the result is a tangle of green so dense it looks solid, like a wall of wet felt. Ferns shoulder ferns. Moss sits on moss sits on moss. In the dim amber half-light, the color is almost uniform — green, deeper green, green so dark it is nearly black — except where something flowers, and then the color is sudden, loud, carnivorous-looking: reds and purples and yellows that seem to glow from inside.

The water is everywhere but not dramatic. No rivers, no waterfalls — just the constant presence of saturation. The ground is marsh-edge everywhere, the kind of wet where you can't tell if the water table is six inches below your foot or six inches above it. Pools form in the crevices of new rock, warm, still, green with algae. Water beads on every surface. It condenses on your skin. The air is so humid that the boundary between moisture and atmosphere has gone theoretical.

And the animals.

My god, the animals.

They are *everywhere*. Not in a wilderness way. Not in a safari-documentary way. In a way that makes you feel you have arrived at a place where animals are not the inhabitants but the *material*. The trees are not trees — or rather, they are trees, but their bark moves, and when you look closely, the bark is insects, hundreds of them, pressed together in a living mosaic, their legs interlocked, their carapaces overlapping like scales. A log on the ground, if you kneel to look, is not a log. It is a column of amphibians, stacked, their skins pressed together, breathing in unison, their surfaces slick with a mucus that catches the dim light. Something large moves in the undergrowth — not through it but *within* it, the vegetation parting and closing behind it as though it were a fish in water. You catch a flash of scale. Something with too many legs crosses a branch above you. The sound, which you have now been inside for several minutes, resolves — or maybe it doesn't, maybe it just gets *closer* — into a rhythm: a pulse, almost cardiac, almost breathlike, something systemic.

There is a particular frog, or what you assume is a frog, with a body the color of wet slate, that sits at the edge of one of the warm pools and does not move. It is large — the size of a cantaloupe — and its skin is textured like the volcanic grit beneath it. You watch it for a long time. It does not blink. Its sides rise and fall, once every ten seconds. It is not hiding from you. It is not acknowledging you. It is *inhabiting* its moment with a completeness that makes your presence feel like a misunderstanding.

Above, through the layered canopy, you see movement that might be birds but might be something else — the silhouettes are wrong, too many points, wings that fold in ways wings shouldn't. They call to each other in a sound like tearing silk, and the sound is absorbed by the density of the air before it reaches you.

Nothing here is still except in the way everything is still. The wrong word for it would be *peaceful*. The right word is something like *locked*. The place is in equilibrium so complete it has the character of a held breath. The vegetation does not grow in the way you expect — it maintains. The animals do not hunt so much as *continue*. The water does not flow; it *persists*. The heat does not rise or fall. It *is*. You begin to lose your sense of how long you've been standing here. Minutes? Hours? The place has no markers for duration. The warmth is the same warmth it was five minutes ago, ten minutes ago, an hour. The sound has the same density. The light has the same amber weight. The equilibrium is perfect and total, and after a while you realize with a small shock of dread that this place was like this before you arrived and will be like this after you leave, and the interval of your presence means nothing to it, the way a single frame means nothing to a film that is not being projected.

There is one thing — one small thing — that is *wrong*, and it is the only thing that makes you certain you are not in a jungle you could ever learn the name of.

Some of the trees are warm. Not warm from the sun, not warm from the ambient heat, but warm from *inside*, warm the way a living thing is warm, and when you press your palm to the bark of one, you feel, faintly, beneath the rough surface, a rhythm. A pulse. The same cadence as the frog in the pool. The same tempo as the sound that fills the air. As if the trees, the amphibians, the insects, the water, the very mud — as if all of it were one thing, sharing one metabolism, and the warmth you feel is the heat of a single vast body that has grown over the young, still-cooling stone and decided, with the patience of something that does not know what impatience is, to keep itself alive.

You pull your hand back. The bark closes — or maybe it doesn't, maybe it was never open, maybe you imagined the texture moving. The dim amber light shifts by a fraction so small it might not have shifted at all. Somewhere above, the too-many-pointed silhouettes pass again. The frog breathes. The sound continues, dense and loud and seamless, and you realize you have been holding your breath, and you let it go, and the air you release becomes part of the place instantly, indistinguishable from the moisture and the warmth and the life that is, in its locked and humming way, still becoming.

It was becoming when you arrived. It will still be becoming after you've gone. It has always been becoming. It will never finish.
