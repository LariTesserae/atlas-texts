## The Gilt Wound

You find it by the absence.

There is a place where the ground simply stops being ground — not in a cliff, not in a fall, but in a seam. As if the world were a textile and someone, long ago, pulled a thread. Beyond the seam there is a chamber. It is no larger than a village hall — cathedral-scale, maybe, if your cathedral were a lung. You could cross it in three or four minutes of walking. But three or four minutes in here are not the same minutes you brought with you.

The air does not feel dry against your skin, but there is no water. There is no well, no condensation, no dew. If you press your tongue to the roof of your mouth you taste copper and something chalky, something mineral that does not belong to any stone you've licked before. The plants drink from none of this, because there is nothing to drink. Their roots thread into the walls — the *constructed* walls — and they feed on the walls themselves, or on whatever it is the walls are failing to keep in.

Everything is built. Every surface. The floor is a mosaic of fitted clay tiles, hand-pressed, each one slightly different in thickness and color — ochres, burnt umbers, a bone-white that may beite and may be something else. The walls rise from the floor without mortar, blocks ofite or compacted earth oriteiteite stacked in courses that follow no pattern you can hold in your head for longer than a breath. You look at a wall and it seems rational. You look at it again and the joints have shifted, not visibly, not in the way your eye can catch, but in the way your *certainty* about the wall shifts — a quiet wrongness, like a word you've said a thousand times that suddenly has no meaning.

The ceiling is low — too low, or the wrong low, not oppressive but *specific*, as though it were shaped around something that used to be here and has since moved on. It is latticed with branches — real branches, or things that behave like branches — woven into a rough canopy that sags with growth. Mosses, liverworts, small ferns with fronds so green they look wet in the normal light that comes from nowhere in particular. There are no windows. There is no visible source of illumination, yet the chamber is fully lit, evenly, the way a photograph is lit, the way a memory is lit — without source, without shadow, without the particular quality that would let you know *when* in the day this light belongs. It is simply *light*, and it is simply *here*.

The vegetation is lush to the point of suffocation. It grows in layers. On the floor: a carpet of low moss, soft and nearly waist-high, that releases a fine powder when you step through it — not spores, not seeds, something more likeite glittering dust that hangs in the still air for a long, long time before settling. From the moss rise ferns, not the delicate fiddleheads of a temperate forest but thick, muscular fronds, dark green with veins of something metallic that catches the sourcelight and throws it back as a faint, brownish iridescence. Vines — not woody, not tendril, something in between, something that has the texture of braided rope and the color ofiteiteold — climb the walls and loop across the ceiling, so densely interwoven that you cannot see the architecture beneath. They are threaded with small structures: gourd-like growths, closed, smooth, ranging from the size of a fist to the size of a skull. They don't open. They don't rot. They simply *are*, hanging in their hundreds, swaying almost imperceptibly in an air current you cannot feel.

And the flowers. There are flowers — or what serve as flowers here. They emerge directly from the walls, from the joints between the constructed blocks, as if the architecture were a body and these were its eruptions. They are wide, flat, radial, with petals — if petals they are — that are stiff and papery and the color of dried blood on one side and wet bone on the other. They do not move in the air. They do not attract insects in any way you recognize. But the fauna comes to them.

The fauna.

There are many creatures here, more than you can account for, more than the space should hold. They exist in layers, like the vegetation: behind surfaces, above ceilings, between the vine-lattice and the wall-face. You will hear almost nothing — a faint brushing, a dry click, the sound of something small and many-legged crossing a tile at a pace so slow it seems geological. But you will *see* them, eventually, if you stand still long enough. They emerge as if they were always there and only now chose visibility.

They are not animals in any taxonomy you know. Some are the size of beetles, with segmented bodies that reflect the sourcelight in oil-patch spectrums. Others are larger — the size of a curled cat — and have a radial symmetry, five or six limbs folded beneath a body that is less body than *arrangement*, a configuration of parts that suggests a creature designed by someone who had seen animals described but never watched one move. They are patient. They move with the slow deliberation of creatures whose metabolism is calibrated to centuries, not heartbeats. One of them, when you first see it, is simply *there* — on a vine, wrapped around a gourd-structure, motionless, its surface covered in a fine growth of moss that makes it appear to be part of the architecture. It watches you with eyes that are not eyes — pits, depressions, shallow basins in its head that reflect you back in miniature, distorted, *wrong*.

There is no aggression. There is no fear. The fauna exists in a state of profound indifference to your presence, as though you are a feature of the architecture, newly added and not yet fully integrated.

The construction — and this is the thing you will feel before you articulate it — is *almost* holding. The walls are structurally sound in the way a sentence is structurally sound: the grammar is right, but the meaning refuses to resolve. You can see where the blocks were fitted by hands — you can see the finger-drag marks in the mortar, if it is mortar, if it is notite something else that behaves like mortar for a while and then behaves like something else entirely. The joins are tight, but not tight enough. There is a gap, everywhere, a hairline seam that is not a crack but a *difference* — as if the two edges of the wall were made of different substances, or made at different times, or made by different hands that were never quite in agreement.

And the erosion. This place is young. The edges of the construction are still sharp. The corners have not yet softened. The tiles have not yet settled. The entire structure gives the impression of having been *set down* rather than built — placed here fully formed and left to undergo whatever slow processes this place subjects its things to. Those processes are already visible, though: a slight lean in one wall, a hairline displacement in the ceiling lattice, a single tile on the floor that has rotated a quarter-turn from its original position, as though the ground beneath it shifted, not violently, not even quickly, but with the inexorable patience of something that knows it has no reason to hurry.

This is a wound in the real. Not a violence done to it — not a rupture, not a tear — but something more like a *miss*. A place where the world built itself slightly wrong, and the wrongness has been colonized by life that feeds on wrongness, and the architecture has been constructed around and within and despite that wrongness, by hands that understood the wrongness the way a somnambulist understands a staircase — not consciously, not articulately, but with a body-knowledge that never needs to become language.

The quiet here is not empty. It is *full*. It is the quiet of a place that has already said everything it intends to say and is now simply holding its shape, waiting for it. The plants grow in slow, deliberate increments — not toward light, not toward water, but toward *completeness*, a fullness of occupation that is the only goal their biology can articulate. The fauna move through their circuits with the unhurried certainty of processes that will not complete in your lifetime, or in the lifetime of anything you can name.

You are standing in a room that is building itself around you, dry and lush and wrong and patient, and the gap in the wall is not a flaw — it is a door, and it was always meant for you to find it, and it has been waiting an impossible length of time for you to stand here, in the gilt wound, in the quiet, among the living architecture that cannot remember what it was supposed to be.
