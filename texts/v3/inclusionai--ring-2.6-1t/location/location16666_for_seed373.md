# The Verdance at Height

You arrive at the rim and the sound hits first.

It does not rise — it is already there, it has always been there, a wall of living noise that pushes against the skin like atmospheric pressure from the wrong direction. The air is cool, thin, tasting faintly of copper and ozone, and vibrating. Not humming — that word is too orderly. The sound is multithroated. Screaming, chittering, booming, singing, cracking — a frequency-broad roar that seems to come from the ground, the air, the bones of your own skull. You will learn later it has layers. You will never learn where they begin.

---

The structure is a bowl.

Not carved — grown, or perhaps *assembled* so long ago that the distinction no longer matters. A hemispherical cavity roughly two hundred meters across, open at its apex to a sky the color of tarnished silver, cupped into the crown of a mountain ridge whose name has been worn smooth by five thousand years of wind. The elevation is severe: above most cloud, above most breath. Snow dusts the outer rim in permanent lace. But inside the bowl, the air is different — warmer by degrees, saturated with something that is not quite humidity and is not mist, a held breath of moisture the source of which no instrument has ever isolated. There is no river. No lake. No aquifer. Hydrologists who have studied the place file reports that end with ellipses.

The bowl's floor is mineral — a pale, glassy substrate that fractures underfoot in clean hexagonal planes, like a frozen lake that never had water. It is denseite, the old texts call it, though no one knows what language the name derives from. It has been eroded into shallow valleys and ridges, not by rain but by something slower and stranger — a corrosive exhalation from the root systems of the vegetation above, a process that has been ongoing for so long that the floor has taken on the appearance of a relief map of a landscape you can't recognize. Basins without rivers. Ridges without peaks. Smoothings that suggest rainfall that never fell.

---

The vegetation is what makes you doubt your senses.

It is *lush* — you have no other word for it. The density of biomass is staggering, and yet the space between individual organisms is enormous. This is not a jungle in the terrestrial sense, where every surface crawls and tangles. It is the opposite: a savannah of the impossible, where each life-form commands a territory of meters, and between them stretches open, cathedral-bell emptiness through which sound rolls and collides and amplifies.

The dominant growths are called striders by the few expeditions that have catalogued them — though they are not alive in any sense that biology comfortably accommodates. They rise from the glassy floor on single stalks, pale violet or deep arterial red, and branch at their summits into fractal lattices of translucent tubes. Some reach twenty meters. Others — the older ones, identified by erosion scarring on their lower shafts — are over sixty meters tall and wide enough at the crown to shelter a small house. Their lattice-work pulses. Not metaphorically: visibly, in slow waves of bioluminescence that travel downward from the canopy tips to the roots, a phosphorescence that is green-gold and faintly warm to the naked eye. The pulse frequency changes. No one has correlated it with anything.

Between the striders: a lower layer. Mat-grasses, they're sometimes called, though they are not grass. They form dense, low cushions across the mineral floor, each cushion a meter or two across, iridescent blue-green, and warm to the touch. They do not photosynthesize — the light that filters through the strider lattice is insufficient, and the spectrum at this altitude is wrong. Their metabolism is based on something else. Analysis of their tissues reveals molecular arrangements that appear to harvest kinetic energy from vibration — from *sound* — converting acoustic pressure into electrochemical gradients with an efficiency that would overturn several branches of thermodynamics if published without caveat.

There are other plants. Tower-ferns with spiraling fronds that unfurl only during the thermal plumes that rise unpredictably from the bowl floor — thermals with no geothermal explanation. Suspended mosses that drift on air currents at the mid-canopy level, rootless, drifting in slow orbits like the debris of a garden that was flung skyward and never came down. Clinging vines on the strider trunks that exude a resin with refractive indices that bend visible light around them — you walk past and a section of vine simply ceases to be visible, not through camouflage but through local refraction, and the effect persists for minutes after you pass, so that you look back and the vine has moved — or you have — and the space behind it is wrong, a few degrees too rectangular for the forest around it.

Nothing here drinks. Nothing here is watered. The lushness is sustained by mechanisms that operate in the space between chemistry and conjecture.

---

The fauna are abundant. They must be, to produce this sound.

They are small, mostly. Nothing here is large — nothing approaches the scale of the striders, and nothing burrows deep into the mineral floor. The creatures live in the air, in the lattice-work, on the fronds, suspended in the column of the bowl between floor and open sky.

The noisemakers are called kites, though they are not birds. They are membrane-winged, six-limbed, the size of a human hand, and they travel in flocks that number in the thousands. Their wings are rigid, crystalline, and when a flock passes overhead — and they pass constantly, always circling, always reforming — the sound is a percussive crack, a snap like a thousand whips drawn across glass, amplified by the bowl geometry into something that borders on physical force. They leave contrails of fine iridescent dust that settles on everything and is the closest thing this place has to precipitation.

Below the kites, in the mid-strider zone: weavers. Colonies of small, pale organisms that spin thread between the strider lattices, each thread a vibrating filament that hums at a specific frequency. A single weaver's thread is inaudible. A colony of ten thousand is a chord. The colonies are everywhere. The cumulative effect is a sustained, shifting harmonic drone that underlies everything, a tonal bedrock beneath the percussive chaos of the kites and the structural groaning of the striders and the constant, smaller sounds — clicking, trilling, piping, shrieking — of everything else that lives here.

There are others. Burrow-shadows — flat, eel-like creatures that slide across the mineral floor in the gaps between the mat-grasses, moving so fast they cast motion-blurs. Glass beetles that feed on the strider bark and produce sounds of their own, stridulations so high-pitched they approach ultrasound and rattle the fillings in your teeth. Floaters — gas-bag organisms the size of a fist, translucent, drifting at various heights, occasionally popping with a sound like a cork pulled from a bottle, each pop releasing a bloom of spores that drift downward through the lattice in slow, glittering curtains.

---

There is technology here.

You see it first in the striders themselves. The oldest specimens — the ones with the most erosion on their stalks — have embedded in their bases structures that are clearly not organic. Seamless bands of a dark, vitreous material, fused to the mineral substrate, inscribed with patterns that repeat at different scales: the same geometry visible to the naked eye reveals further self-similar geometries under magnification, down to the nanoscale, where current instruments simply lose resolution and cannot say whether the pattern continues or terminates. The striders have grown around these bands. Their lattices incorporate them. Root networks touch them. They are old enough to have become part of the ecology — load-bearing members of the strider's structural system, perhaps, or nutrient channels, or something for which there is no word.

Scattered elsewhere: single buildings. Small. A ring of standing slabs, waist-high, arranged in a geometry that is almost but not quite circular — off by exactly the irrational ratio that appears in the strider-lattice branching patterns. A domed structure, collapsed on one side, its interior walls lined with a material that absorbs all sound. Standing inside it is an experience of absolute, annihilating silence so profound it causes disorientation and, in some visitors, nausea. No echo. No ambient tone. The kites outside are invisible. The drone vanishes. You hear only yourself, and the sound terrifies you because it is the only thing in the world and it is too loud.

A single tower, freestanding, twenty meters tall, built of the same dark vitreous material as the strider-bands but not grown — cut, fitted, polished. Its surface is warm. It emits no sound. It vibrates at a frequency you feel in your sternum. Up close, its proportions are unsettling: the distances between its structural nodes correspond to no standard measurement, but if you pace them out, your stride unconsciously adjusts. You take steps of a length you never normally use, and it feels correct, and wrong, and you cannot stop.

These structures are sparse. Fewer than a dozen in the entire bowl. Some are partially buried under centuries of dust-fall from the kites. Some are cracked, or subsiding into the mineral floor along erosion channels worn by processes that operate on timescales you are not equipped to measure. They are artifacts. They are functional. They have not been maintained. They are not decaying.

---

The light is normal.

This is the detail that unsettles most. The bowl is open to the sky, and the sky is a standard sky — grey-white, cloud-thin, the sun a pale disc not significantly brighter or dimmer than it should be at this altitude. Shadows are sharp. Color rendition is ordinary. The striders' bioluminescence is visible but not dominant — you can see everything, everything is lit, everything is in plain sight, and the sheer *amount* of visual information is oppressive. Every surface has texture. Every organism has detail. The mat-grasses ripple in winds you cannot feel. The kites' crystalline wings flash prismatic light. The floaters glow faintly from within. It is a landscape that gives up nothing to shadow, that hides nothing in darkness, and yet you cannot understand any of it.

The temperature is cool — you'll want a layer, perhaps two. The air at this elevation bites at exposed skin, but inside the bowl the thermal plumes rise unpredictably, warm columns that seem to originate from nowhere, drift upward, and dissipate. Standing in one is like stepping into a different season. The striders' pulse frequency seems to change in these moments. The weavers' chords shift pitch. The kites alter their flight patterns. Everything responds. Nothing explains.

---

The erosion tells you this place has been here a very long time, and that it is not finished.

The mineral floor is scored with channels worn by the slow acidic breath of the strider-roots, but new channels are forming — you can see pale, fresh scarring beside grooves so deep their walls have glazed smooth over centuries. Striders fall. When they do, the sound is immense — a crack like a lightning strike followed by a sustained, resonant boom that rolls across the bowl floor and up the curved walls and back down again, a sound that continues for minutes as the tower-sized structure contacts the ground and shatters along its crystal planes, and the kites erupt in panic-flight, and the weavers' tens-of-thousands-of-chord colony goes dissonant for half an hour before slowly, inevitably, re-tuning to something adjacent. A new strider has already begun growing from the fallen one's scattered spores. You can see it — a green shoot, a hand's length, already threaded with faint luminescence. In a decade it will be a stalk. In a century, a lattice. In five centuries, it will have its own ecosystem of weavers and bark-beetles and root-networks touching the vitreous bands that will, inexplicably, be there.

The built structures show the same long weathering. The ring of slabs tilts slightly more each year. The silence dome has lost a fist-sized chunk of its wall to erosion, and through the gap you can hear — impossibly — a slightly *different* silence, one with the faintest undertone, as if the world behind the dome's acoustic shield has its own drone. The tower stands unchanged.

---

Nothing here requires water. Nothing here should work. Everything here works anyway, with a conviction that borders on aggression, as though the place has decided that physics is a suggestion it has outlived. The lushness is not peaceful. The abundance is not gentle. This is a place in a state of permanent, magnificent crisis — every organism exploiting every other, every sound feeding back into every growth pattern, every artifact integrated into a system it was perhaps designed for or perhaps designed *against*, and no living scholar left who can tell the difference.

The bowl rings. The striders pulse. The kites shriek. The mat-grasses hum beneath your feet and you feel their vibration through your boots and up through your skeleton and into your teeth. The air is full of iridescent dust, of spores, of membrane-winged noise, of light that behaves exactly as it should while everything else refuses to.

You stand at the center and understand why no one has settled here, why no expedition has stayed longer than nine days. Not because of danger — the fauna don't attack, the structures don't threaten, the air is breathable, the temperature is manageable.

It is because the place is *listening to you*, and the sound of it listening is louder than anything you have ever heard, and after the fourth day you begin to suspect that the sound is not coming from the landscape at all.
