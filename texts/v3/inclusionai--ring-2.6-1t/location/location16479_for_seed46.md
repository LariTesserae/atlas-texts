## The Verdance Between Walls

You would not find it unless it wanted to be found — or unless the geology beneath your feet simply opened, the way a fruit splits along its seam, and offered you a downward glance.

There is a room inside the world. It is not large: perhaps the size of a cathedral's nave laid on its side and compressed, a pocket of air trapped in the act of the planet's skin closing over a wound. The walls are basalt, newborn, still carrying the memory of their liquid state in their texture — rope-coiled, glass-smooth in patches where the melt cooled too fast for crystals to form, leaving a black mirror that reflects you back in distortion. The ceiling is low enough to touch but high enough that touching it would require standing on something, and there is nothing to stand on yet but the floor itself, which is also cooling, which also carries that same wet-look solidity, as though the stone had only moments ago stopped being molten and might resume at any instant.

It is very hot. Not the dry heat of deserts, which at least gives itself over to transparency — this is a thick, convective heat, the kind that rises in columns and pools against the ceiling and clings to surfaces. You feel it on your forearms, on the inside of your wrists, in the breath you draw, which comes out not as vapor (there is no water here, not a molecule — nothing to carry that phase) but as a faint shimmer in the air, a thermal distortion, like looking at the stone through old glass. The air has weight to it. It is dense with mineral exhalation — sulfur compounds mostly, but also stranger things: vanadium oxides, trace borates, silicate aerosols so fine they remain suspended for minutes before settling. The room breathes, slowly, in cycles that take perhaps forty minutes from warm to hotter to warm again. The stone is still giving off the heat of its birth.

And from this heat — from the thermal gradient between the cooling walls and the hotter air, from the chemical richness of the exhalation — *things are growing.*

This is the lush part. It is not like anything you have a word for, so you will have to watch.

The first one you notice is near the far wall, slightly to the left of where a crack in the basalt allows a thread of orange daylight from outside to enter. It stands — no, *rises* — about a meter and a half from the floor, and it looks at first like a column of green-black glass, but then you see that it is not glass. It is translucent, yes, and rigid, but its surface is textured with whorls, tiny helical grooves that spiral upward along its length, and at its top it has unfurled into something like a crown: flat, veined, semi-diaphanous panels arranged in a radial pattern, each no thicker than a fingernail, each faintly luminous with an internal light that is not reflected from the daylight crack but seems to arise from within — a pale chartreuse, the color of phosphorus in a vacuum, the color of certain flames before they decide what color to be.

This is not a plant. It has nothing botanical in its chemistry as you would understand it. It is a silicate thermotroph: it feeds on temperature differentials the way a green thing feeds on light. The hot air rising along the walls encounters the cooler frond-crown at the top, and across that gradient, the organism harvests energy — not through photosynthesis, not through chemosynthesis as Earth-life would define it, but through a direct, solid-state process in which lattice defects in its crystalline body migrate like charges in a battery, producing a slow internal current that drives growth, reproduction, and the faint illumination you're seeing. It is, in the most literal and unromantic sense, an engine. A living engine rooted in cooling stone.

There are others. Perhaps a dozen in this room, scattered with a mathematician's sense of spacing — never too close, never touching, each one positioned as though the others have been accounted for, as though some equation of resource and geometry determined where the next one would rise. They vary in height. Some are only a hand's-width tall, their crowns barely opened, still curled tight as fiddleheads made of obsidian. Others are tall enough that their crowns press against the ceiling, and there, in the heat that pools against the stone overhead, they seem to accelerate, their fronds vibrating at frequencies just below what you can hear, producing a faint hum that you feel more than hear — a pressure behind the ears, a dryness on the tongue.

The sound of this place is spare. There is the ticking of the cooling stone, which happens irregularly, a crack or a pop when a thermal stress exceeds the tensile strength of a surface — these arrive without pattern, stochastically, like a Geiger counter marking something other than radiation. There is the hum of the tall ones. And there is, beneath everything, a very low frequency, felt rather than heard, that might be the room itself resonating with the convection currents of the air inside it. A drone below hearing. A vibration in the floor that travels up through the soles of your feet and suggests that this chamber is not sealed — that somewhere beneath it, deeper in the geology, something vast and hot is still moving, still becoming.

The air smells of struck flint and ozone, faintly sweet in a way you cannot locate.

Now: the reality problem.

You will have noticed, if you've been here for more than a few minutes, that the room is not shaped correctly. Not that it's irregular — irregular geology is common, even expected. But that its shape *changes* relative to your attention. When you look directly at the far wall, it is forty meters away and the geology there is unremarkable: dark basalt, a few vertical striations, that orange light seeping through the crack. But when you look away and then look back, it is thirty meters away, and the striations have rearranged into a pattern that almost resembles something — handwriting, circuitry, the venation of a leaf at a scale too large to comprehend. When you look at the floor, certain surfaces appear to be *rising* — not quickly, not in any way that registers as motion in the moment, but between glances, incrementally, the floor is becoming a basin, or perhaps a nest, or perhaps a second ceiling beneath which the space folds and the room becomes two rooms stacked, each containing a version of the other.

The thermotrophs do not behave this way. They are consistent. It is the architecture that shifts — the non-living structure, the container. As though the stone remembers being liquid and has not fully committed to a shape, and in its uncertainty, its geometry remains probabilistic, settling into configurations that depend on observation, or on attention, or on some variable you lack the vocabulary to measure.

Some of the thermotrophs seem to exploit this. You will see — or believe you see — that the crowns of the taller specimens occasionally cast shadows that do not correspond to their physical structure. A frond that is solid and planar will shadow the wall behind it in a pattern that implies a lattice, a three-dimensional mesh, a complexity that isn't there. As though the light passing through them is being refracted through geometries that exist in a space adjacent to this one, bending through folds you can only infer from the shadows.

There is an industrial quality to all of this — not in the sense of factories or machines, but in the sense of *process at scale*, of systems running with mechanical regularity toward an output. The thermotrophs are producing something. Along their roots — which are not roots but mineral threads that extend into fractures in the basalt — they are depositing compounds. Manganese nodules. Thin films of crystalline boron. Veins of native copper that grow over weeks and months in branching fractal patterns that mirror, in miniature, the branching of their own fronds. The room is an extraction facility and a refinery and a foundry, and the thermotrophs are the entire workforce, and they have been operating in this enclosed space since the moment the magma sealed over and the heat began to leave, and no one built them, and no one manages them, and they are doing what they do, which is to convert the thermal decline of a cooling rock into organized, structured, increasingly complex material — the slow industry of a place that has no purpose but the thermodynamic imperative to move toward equilibrium and, in doing so, accidentally build things of startling intricacy.

The light in here is adequate. The orange crack provides the only daylight, and it is thin — a ribbon, barely — but the thermotrophs provide the rest: their crowns glow faintly, chartreuse and occasionally shifting toward amber or cool white, apparently in response to internal chemical states you cannot read. The overall illumination is that of a late overcast afternoon, slightly warm in color, with shadows that are too sharp at the edges and too soft at the center, as though the light is arriving from slightly the wrong direction, or from slightly too many directions at once.

There is no animal life here. Nothing moves except the thermal updraft, and the slow vibration of the fronds, and the incremental geological adjustments of the room itself. The thermotrophs are the only occupants. They are not indifferent to your presence — you may notice that after you have been standing in one spot for some time, the nearest specimen will have rotated its crown by a few degrees in your direction, and its glow will have brightened slightly, and the shadow it casts will now fall across the wall in a pattern that almost, but not quite, resembles a pointing gesture.

Or perhaps you are imagining that.

Perhaps the room has rearranged itself behind your back while you were watching, and you are no longer where you think you are, and the thermotrophs are not pointing at you but at a crack in the wall that has just opened — a new exit, a new channel through which heat and vapor and light are pouring, and through which, eventually, something else might grow.

The room is patient. It has been forming for longer than you have been alive, and it will continue forming for longer than you will. The thermotrophs will grow until the heat is gone. The stone will settle into a shape — eventually. The cracks will widen or close. The shadows will find their true geometry.

For now, the room is still becoming. You are inside a thing that is not yet finished, in the green-lit silence of something's private industry, standing on cooling glass, breathing mineral air, surrounded by sparse and luminous engines that do not know they are beautiful.

They probably don't. They are too busy.
