# The Weeping Alcove

---

You find it by the sound first.

Not a door — doors stopped mattering to this place centuries ago, if doors were ever what they were. You find it by the resonance, a low tonal presence that sits beneath everything like a second heartbeat, felt in the fillings of your teeth and the cartilage of your ribs. You round a breach in a corridor wall — the corridor itself is a thing of scaffolding-like lattice, some material dark with oxidation that has the heft and grain of basalt but gives slightly, almost imperceptibly, under your palm, like a callus — and the sound opens up.

You step into a room no larger than a chapel's apse. It might be one. It might have been one. The architecture is unrecoverable. Centuries of mineral accretion have softened every edge, layered surfaces with a rind of calcite and something darker, something with a faintly oily iridescence, so that the walls look like the inside of a geode that someone then carved into walls and then left alone for a thousand years for the geode to reassert itself. The original material — and there is no question that this was made, that nothing here is stone in any natural sense — peeks through in seams and fractures: a geometry too deliberate for geology, too corroded for legibility.

It is warm. Warm the way a greenhouse is warm, the way a beehive is warm: an enclosed heat that has no single source but saturates everything equally. The air sits on your skin. You realize you have been sweating for the last ten meters without noticing. The temperature hovers at that threshold where discomfort becomes something closer to immersion — you are *in* the warmth the way you are in water. Because you are, in a sense. The air is so saturated with moisture that your eyelashes begin to film. Exhale and you see your breath immediately, a brief ghost that hangs and disperses with unusual slowness, as though the atmosphere resists perturbation.

The water is everywhere. Not flowing, exactly, but *present* — the way a landscape can be present on a body after rain. It beads on the ceiling in films so thin they are almost architectural, trembling with each resonance from the building's deeper systems before letting go in individual drops that fall with a sound like a finger tapping a wet glass rim. It sheets down the walls in paths that have been carved by nothing but centuries of patient seepage, finding the same fault lines over and over, darkening the mineral rind into topographic maps of themselves. It pools on the floor in shallow basins no deeper than a thumbnail, where the surface tension does things that make you stop and stare: the water draws shapes. Tiny ridges. Hexagonal lattices that form and dissolve with a deliberateness that looks structural, as though the water is communicating in a language just below the threshold of pattern recognition. You lean closer. The shapes collapse. Rearrange. Something in the mineral composition of the water, or in the nanoscale texturing of the basin's surface — some engineering so fine it predates any manufacturing schema you know — is sculpting the meniscus into geometries that hold for half a second, a full second, and then surrender to gravity with a sound like a whisper.

Dim light. Not dark — never dark, here — but dim in the way the inside of a closed fist is dim. Amber, mostly. A warmth of color without a warmth of illumination. It comes from two sources. The first is bioluminescent films, pale chartreuse, that coat certain surfaces in patches: above the water basins, along the seams where wall panels have separated, in the cracks of a column near the room's center that may once have been structural and now is merely geological, a stalactite of accreted material around a corroded spine of alloy. The glow is faint, wet, alive in the way that mold is alive — spreading, consuming, radiating its metabolic byproduct as light. The second source is deeper. Behind the walls, behind the mineral crust, there is something that glows. You can see it in the fractures — a warmer amber, almost ochre, pulsing with a rhythm so slow you almost convince yourself it is your own pulse until you hold your breath and it keeps going. Something behind the panels is still powered. Something behind the panels has not stopped for longer than anyone can remember, and if it ever does, you think the room will know. You think the room already knows how close it is to stopping.

The room is dense. Not with objects — there is almost nothing you could call furniture, almost nothing you could separate from the architecture and name — but with *presence*. Layers upon layers. The mineral crusts, the bioluminescent films, the water films, the things that live in and on and between all of them. Everything touches everything else. Every surface is colonized. Every drop of water is a world.

The fauna is what makes this place unmistakably alive and unmistakably not meant for you.

They are small. Most of them. Along the water films, things that look like mites but are longer than they should be, translucent, their internal structures visible as a faint amber smudge — or perhaps that is the bioluminescence reflecting through them. They move in coordinated flows, hundreds of them, traveling the same paths the water takes, feeding on mineral deposits or on each other with an indifference that is almost mechanical. In the basins, ribbon-worms: pale, flat, segmented things that coil and uncoil in the water like phone cords, their surfaces rippling with cilia. When you breathe near one, it contracts with a speed that startles you. When you touch the water's surface with a fingertip, the ribbons scatter and then slowly, over minutes, reconverge — not to where they were, but to where you are.

There are insects the size of a fingernail with shells that look like hammered copper. They emerge from seams in the wall in ones and twos, moving with a mechanical precision, antennae sweeping in arcs. They are indifferent to you. They are indifferent to everything except the moisture gradient, and they follow it with a single-mindedness that borders on devotion.

And then there is the sound from inside the walls.

Not the hum — the hum is the building, the whole structure, its vast systems cycling fluids and energy through corridors that may extend for kilometers in every direction. The hum is the room breathing. No — the sound from inside the walls is *displacement*. Something heavy. Something that moves with the slow, deliberate weight of a creature that knows every surface, every hollow, every thermal gradient in its territory. You hear it shift on the other side of the wall to your left, and the sound is the sound of wet carpet compressed, of mass moving through a space not much larger than its body. You hear it pause. You hear what might be a mandible — or a sensory filament — tap against the interior wall surface. Tap. Tap. Tap. Three times. Always three times. You cannot tell if it is checking for you or if this is routine. You cannot tell if it knows you are here.

You realize you have been holding your breath.

The air tastes like a mineral spring, like the inside of a seashell concentrated and heated. There is something faintly sweet underneath, something that might be the bioluminescent colonies releasing metabolic gas, or might be something older, something the walls themselves exhale as they continue their slow, multi-century process of returning to equilibrium.

The vegetation is nearly nonexistent. A patch of dark moss — real moss, actual *Bryopsida*, though how it got here and what it metabolizes is anyone's guess — grows in the basin that holds the most consistent water supply. A thin, barely visible film of algae along one wall seam, dark red-brown, almost black. And, in the cracks of the central column, a fungal network: hair-thin, white, pulsing with bioluminescence so faint you would miss it if the light were any brighter. The threads reach toward the water basins with a patience that is not biological. This is a place where the boundary between organism and infrastructure has dissolved entirely, where the things that grow here may have learned to feed on the building's own waste heat and chemical runoff, and the building — if it is still *a* building — may have learned, in turn, to let them.

Nothing here is natural. But nothing here is sterile. Life has found the thresholds, the seams, the places where the constructed world's precision has worn down to something porous. Life has filled them.

The sound builds. A drip from the ceiling, three meters to your left, strikes a mineral-crusted surface and splits into a tiny chord — two tones, a fifth apart, ringing for nearly a full second before decaying. The hum from behind the walls deepens by a quarter-tone. Somewhere deeper in the structure, a clang resonates — massive, metallic, final — and the sound rolls through the chamber like surf, rattling the copper-shell insects into momentary stillness, sending the ribbon-worms into tight spirals, and then fading, and then fading, and then — 

Silence is not the right word. There is never silence. There is only the interval between sounds, and in this room the intervals are short, and the sounds are layered, and you are standing in something that feels less like a place and more like a throat.

You step carefully around the nearest basin, avoiding the water. The hexagonal lattices reform behind you. The tapping inside the wall resumes.

Five times now.

You are not the first to visit. But you are not sure the room knows the difference.
