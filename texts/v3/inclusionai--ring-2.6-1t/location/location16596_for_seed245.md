# The Scaffold Sea

---

You come upon it from nothing — from the absence of any such place — and find it half-present, the way a word is half-present when you're trying to remember it.

There is a horizon. That is the first shock. A constructed horizon, not curved by planetary mass but suggested by a wall of columnar basalt that extends — you can feel this before you verify it — for hundreds of kilometers. The columns are not hexagonal. They are not anything. They are roughly pentagonal, some of them, and others have too many edges to name, and some appear to be edges without faces, so that looking at them is like looking at a corner of a room that exists in a geometry you were not built to process. They rise thirty, forty meters from a substrate that is not quite ground: a black aggregate, still warm underfoot, that clings like dried mortar and crumbles likeiteiteite into grey paste when you press it. It is not alive. It was never alive. But it is still curing.

The air is thick. Not humid in any familiar sense — there is no biology to humidify — but saturated with mineral vapor, a hot steam that condenses on any surface cooler than its own temperature. It comes off your skin in a faint hiss. The water here is not water exactly, though it behaves like it in certain moods. Most of the time it exists as a thin film on every exposed surface, beading on column faces, filling the shallow basins between construct-walls, hanging in the air itself as a fog the color of pumice. When it condenses in volume, it falls as a warm drizzle that makes no sound when it lands. You can cup it. It tastes of calcium and something beneath calcium, something structurally older.

The scale is wrong for walking. Not too large to walk across, but too large to walk meaningfully through. The columns repeat with variation the way geological strata repeat with variation — you can sense pattern without resolving it. They are spaced wide apart, thirty meters sometimes between their bases, and between them: nothing. Emptiness the color of overcast tin. The space between the columns is not decorated, not filled, not waiting to be filled. It simply persists, the way silence persists between drumbeats.

And the construction is incomplete in ways that make your hands want to build.

Look: a column eight hundred meters to the south. It is clearly unfinished. Its upper third is raw scaffolding — not scaffolding as in metal poles and platforms, but scaffolding as in *scaffold-mockup-as-final-architecture*. Cradles of rough-fired clay hold up beams ofite that were laid only hours ago (you can tell because they have not yet developed the patina of the older surfaces). The scaffolding extends up into air where the column should continue but doesn't. Above the last solid ring, there is a suggestion of form — a dome? A taper? A second set of beams that project outward and simply stop, their ends glowing faintly with the heat of recent placement. There is no crane. No tool visible. Yet the work continues. You hear it.

Sound here is rare and specific. Most of the time there is a low, mineral hum — not a tone but a vibration, the subsonic surface-noise of something enormous and warm expanding. Every few minutes there is a sound like a stone dropped into a deep well: a single resonant *tok* that comes from no visible origin and seems to arrive from everywhere at once. Occasionally — rarely — there is a scraping. The sound of a flat surface dragged across stone. It lasts for seconds and then stops, and when it stops the silence is different than before, has a shape to it, the negative of the sound now absent.

The light has no obvious source. It is not daylight — the sky above is a uniform ochre-grey, featureless as poured plaster, giving nothing to read. Not dark either. A dimness that operates like diffusion, all surfaces lit from no direction, shadows that are thin and indecisive. At the far eastern edge of the visible construct-world, there is something that may be a light-source or may be a breach or may be a color that does not have a name in any human language. It produces a faint ruddy luminescence that tints the nearest columns in shades of bread-crust and dried blood, but it does not travel far. The light is shallow.

Here is what the reality-breakdown looks like:

Stand in one place long enough and the columns begin to shift. Not physically — they are made of something harder and more stubborn than stone — but *informationally*. You will watch a column's nearest edge and notice, with a slow creeping of certainty, that it was in a slightly different angular relationship to you three seconds ago. Not a movement. An edit. As though the place is being re-rendered from a source that is not perfectly synchronized with its output. You look at a beam that was overhead and find it has been replaced by a beam that is the same but newer, the surface still tacky, and you cannot remember the old one. The fog will thicken in a region five meters around you and then thin again, and in the thickening you feel, not fear but *recognition* — as though the fog is the world performing maintenance on itself in a language adjacent to your perception.

The ground is unstable in its stability. Step and it holds. Step and it holds. Step and a section of it — twenty meters across, three meters deep — simply *isn't* there anymore; it was never there; you are looking into a cross-section of something vast and structured and being built from the bottom up, descending into levels you cannot fathom, the walls of the excavation lined with conduits ofite that pulse with a faint amber glow, wet with condensation, curling into shapes that suggest ductwork or script or root-systems for a tree that will never exist. Then the hole is gone, the ground is solid again, and thecolumns continue on schedule.

This is what is being built: not a building, not a city, not a machine. A *system of surfaces*. Walls without rooms. Ceilings without walls. Arches that support nothing, channel nothing, frame nothing. Stairways that ascend twenty steps and deliver you, with anticlimactic precision, to the elevation you started at. Aqueducts that carry the mineral-mist in open troughs from no source to no destination, the water moving by a momentum that seems inherited rather than applied. Bridges between columns that end, mid-span, in a stump ofite that was clearly meant to continue but has not yet.

The builders — if that word means anything here — are not present in any body you can identify. There is evidence of activity: hand-prints in wet mortar, the size of a human palm but with seven fingers and the thumb in the wrong place. Tools left embedded in the substrate — simple things, trowels of volcanic glass bound to handles of petrified wood, chisels ofite that were apparently shaped by striking one rock against another, thousands of times, by something patient and relentless and not warm-blooded. The tools are scattered. The hand-prints are scattered. The builders are not.

Or: the builders are the place. The columns grew out of the ground like a geological process that someone, something, decided to *accelerate* with hand-tools and rainwater and millions of years compressed into a span you could cross in a few days of walking. The half-done scaffolding is not neglect. It is the pace of a world that understands its own architecture but builds in rhythms too slow for presence — a breath in, a breath out, a column rises one centimeter.

The water collects in the low surfaces because there is nothing else to collect in. It forms shallow lakes in the concavities of half-vaulted spaces, reflecting the ochre-grey nothing-sky with a clarity so perfect the reflection seems more real than what it reflects. Stand at the edge of one such lake and you will see, in the water, the completed version of the place — columns finished, scaffolding gone, arches connecting to arches in geometries that make a kind of sense, a city of bare structure extending to every horizon. Look up and the unfinished spars frame the same ochre sky. The water shows you what is coming. The water is always right.

The heat does not bother you. It should. It is the heat of an oven, of standing inside a kiln, of the air temperature inside a lung. But it is a clean heat, mineral, without malice. It is the heat of a place that is being fired, like pottery. You feel, walking through it, that you are inside something that is in the process of becoming permanent.

Above the columns, above the scaffolding, above the half-built arches — the ochre-grey dome of the sky, pressing down, thin as a membrane, and behind it: something. Not darkness. Not light. A pressure. The sense that the constructed world is all that stands between you and a substrate that is not empty and not full and not anything you could describe without breaking the word you use. The world is still being built as a wall against what is beneath it.

The columns continue.
The water condenses.
The fog performs its quiet work.
Somewhere beyond sight, a beam is placed, and the sound of it arrives three seconds late, already ancient, already forgotten.
