## The Compression

You do not arrive here. You settle.

The descent takes long enough that the concept of *surface* becomes a memory of a memory — something you once knew the word for, the way a word repeated too many times stops being a word and becomes only a shape your mouth makes. The water thickens around you. It does not lighten. At some point the last filament of downwelling solar radiation was consumed miles above, filtered through columns so dense they might as well be earth. Here there is only the kind of dark that has weight. It presses against your skin with the same patience as the water itself.

You reach the bottom. That is not the right word for it. There is no bottom. There is a region of extraordinary *saturation* — a plain of compressed material so dense, so fully occupied, that nothing can penetrate it. Millennia of accumulated sediment have been pressurized into a substance that is neither stone nor silt nor organic deposit but something adjacent to all three. It has no grain. It has no layers you can distinguish with an unaided eye. It is continuous, homogeneous, and absolute in its occupation of space. If you drove a hand into it — and you could, with effort, the cold makes things brittle — you would find resistance everywhere, uniformly, as though the ground were made of frozen breath. In most places, it has been worn to near-flatness. The terrain undulates only slightly: low swells and depressions measured in centimeters, in millimeters, features so subtle they might be read as texture rather than topography. The erosion here is not violent. It is the erosion of *time having nowhere else to go* — water, pressure, and age have worked everything into its most stable, most final arrangement.

And yet.

The turbulence comes without warning. It arrives as a current — not a current like a river's, visible and directional, but a *convulsion* of the water column itself. Pressure shifts. A wall of particulate matter, loosened from some elevated shelf far above, descends as a turbidity flow — a river of suspended sediment moving through the dark like a thought moving through an unconscious mind. The ground shudders. The dense seabed, for all its compression, is not inert: it flexes. Micro-fractures propagate. Fine material, trapped for centuries, is briefly liberated into suspension, and for minutes or hours the water here is *thick* in a way it usually is not — not just dense with pressure but dense with matter, a broth of calcium and silica and the powdered remains of organisms that died before language existed. Then the particles settle again. The seabed compacts. Everything returns to its final form.

The silence between these events is nearly total. Not the silence of absence but the silence of *compression* — the same silence you'd hear if you pressed your ear against a glacier. It is alive in the lowest frequencies: the groan of settled rock adjusting to the weight of ocean, the faint creak of mineral lattices holding under pressures that would crush surface architecture into paste. If you are still — and you must be still here, stillness is the only rational response — you can feel these sounds as vibrations through the substrate, entering your body not through the ears but through whatever is touching the ground.

There is almost nothing growing. Almost. In the rare places where a hard surface has been exposed — a seam of compressed calcium carbonate rising a few centimeters above the surrounding plain, a fragment of ancient basalt too dense to be ground down — microbial films cling in thin, translucent sheets. They are not plants. They are chemosynthetic, deriving energy from mineral reactions at the boundary between sediment and water. Under close inspection they would look like frost on glass, or like the first tentative scribble of something that might, over geological time, become a drawing. They are here. They are sparse. They are the closest thing to green that exists in this place, which is to say they are the closest thing to *any* color, and even that is faint and localized and probably not what you'd think of as life.

The fauna — what there is of it — moves through this landscape with the understanding that presence costs energy and energy is scarce. You might encounter one: a slow, pale organism with a body plan so simple it might predate the concept of bilateral symmetry. It drifts centimeters above the seabed on vestigial fins or cilia, filtering detritus from the water. It does not react to you. It has no reason to react to anything; the world here changes on timescales that are not its timescales, and its response to perturbation is to become quieter. During turbidity events, these creatures burrow — short, clumsy burrows into the compressed surface, disappearing into a medium that barely accepts them, and they wait. The wait may last hours. It may last years. There is no meaningful difference here.

The scale of this place is deceptive. Standing on the seabed — if standing is the word for the laborious splay of limbs required to maintain position against the current — you can see... nothing. Not in the way that you see nothing in a dark room, where the darkness has edges. Here the darkness has no edges. It extends in every direction beyond the threshold of perception. The water is optically homogeneous, particulate-laden even in calm periods, so there is no far-off glimmer, no ambient haze to suggest depth or distance. You are in a room with no walls, no ceiling, no floor, only the material certainty of ground beneath you and water in all other directions. The regional extent of this plain — this compressed, ancient, abyssal plain — is probably hundreds of kilometers. You could walk in one direction for days and never encounter a feature that would merit a name: no ridge, no canyon, no vent field, no wreck. Only the ground, which is always the same ground, and the water, which is always the same water, and the dark, which is always the same dark.

Temperature: just above freezing. Your body would lose heat slowly, the water being dense and the thermal conductivity of deep-ocean water being what it is. You would not freeze. You would simply, gradually, become cold — and then colder, and then cold would become the only sensation you had, and then even that would fade to a dull awareness that your extremities are no longer yours, and then you would be another presence on the seabed, sparse and still and slowly becoming part of the compression.

There are no structures here. No artifacts. No evidence that anything has ever been arranged or built or deliberately placed. The landscape is untouched not because it has been preserved but because nothing has ever had reason to alter it. It settled into its final form long before anything existed that might disturb it, and it has been settling ever since, and it will continue to settle for longer than any reckoning you can hold in your mind.

On the rare occasion when the turbulence is strong enough to reshape the surface — redistributing a thin layer of sediment, exposing a fresh patch of compressed material, sending a slow avalanche down one of the near-imperceptible gradients — the landscape does not so much change as *reassert* itself. New ground is old ground. The distance between one state and the next is negligible. The world here is at the end of its capacity to change, and yet it still changes, because the ocean above never stops moving, and the planet beneath never stops cooling, and between these two facts, compressed into a density that admits no void, the abyssal plain endures — silent, cold, packed to the limit, ancient beyond the point where age is a meaningful word.

It is nothing. It is everything. It is the place where the world has finally finished becoming itself and has decided, with the patience of stone and the gentleness of deep water, to stay.
