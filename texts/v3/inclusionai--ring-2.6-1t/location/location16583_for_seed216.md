# The Plateau of Grown Silence

---

You do not arrive on the Plateau so much as ascend into it. The approach is a long slope of root-ramp and buttressed trunk, narrowing the sky behind you until the last blue is pinched out and you are enclosed in a living ceiling that might be a mile overhead — or might not be, because distance here has long since lost its courtesy of reliability.

The air is warm and saturated. It clings. It is the air of a lung that has never fully exhaled. Every surface weeps — not rain, not exactly, but condensation born from the plateau's own breathing, water pulled from the thick atmosphere by the sheer biological labor of everything alive. It beads on bark-walls. It threads down the veins of leaf-canopies so vast they function as sky. It pools in the concave basins of grown root-systems that arch overhead like the ribcage of an animal whose species has been extinct for epochs. You walk through this water without noticing it, because there is always more of it five steps ahead, hanging in curtains that part for your body and close behind you.

The ground — if you can call it that — is constructed. All of it. Every surface beneath your feet is plant-matter that was *made*. Floors of interwoven root, compressed over time into something harder than stone but still breathing, still faintly warm. Walls that are fused trunks spiraling upward into gloom. Arches grown from trained wood that sprouted centuries before anyone thought to carve it, then was coaxed into right angles and parabolic curves by hands that understood not tools but *patience* — the slow persuasion of a living thing toward a shape it would not have chosen alone. The construction is everywhere: buttresses, colonnades, archways, chambers, bridges spanning voids between growth-towers whose bases are wider than a village and whose upper reaches vanish into the dim canopy above.

But there is almost nothing *here*. That is the first thing that strikes you, once the initial astonishment of the architecture fades. The spaces between these structures are immense. You pass through a cathedral-nave of grown heartwood, its ceiling lost in amber shadow three hundred feet above, and step through an arch into — nothing. A gap of two hundred feet of open air between this structure and the next one, hovering or rising or looming across the void like the skeleton of a drowned god. No undergrowth. No rubble. No rubble because there has been no decay — or rather, all decay has been metabolized, reclaimed, incorporated. The emptiness is not hostile. It is architectural. It is the negative space of an organism that built with air as deliberately as with wood.

You cross these voids on bridges that are themselves grown — not planks, not rope, but tensile root-systems stretched between tower-trunks, their surfaces ridged and ridged again for grip, damp and slightly spongy underfoot. Some of these bridges are hundreds of feet long and sway with a motion so slow it might be the bridge or might be you.

**The light** is dim and green. Not green as in filtered through leaves, though it is that too, but green the way deep water is green — a saturation, a mood, a color that exists in the air itself. There is no sun visible on the Plateau. What light exists seems to emanate from the growth-structures themselves: a faint bioluminescence in the heartwood of certain walls, a phosphorescent bloom on wet surfaces, the dull amber glow of sap circulating in visible veins beneath translucent bark. You can see. You would not call it bright. Shapes hold themselves at the edge of perception, and the distance between shapes is a darkness that has weight.

**The fauna is abundant and nearly silent.** You see them — movement at the edges, shadows detaching from shadows, forms that are difficult to hold with your eyes because they are partly plant themselves, or because your perception keeps refusing to resolve them into single shapes. There are creatures the size of dogs with too many legs, moving in fluid coordination over the root-floors. There are things like upright stems with sensory filaments that sway and track you as you pass. There are flocks of something — not birds exactly, more like seeds with wings and nervous systems — that pass overhead in complete silence, blotting out the faint green glow of the canopy for a moment before dispersing. Nothing calls. Nothing cries. The Plateau's animals communicate through vibration in the living wood, through chemical signals dissolved in the wet air, through mechanisms so subtle they leave no trace on the human ear. The silence is not empty. The silence is *occupied*.

**The weather** exists, in its way. The atmosphere above the Plateau develops its own microclimate — convections driven by the collective transpiration of the entire living surface. There are periods of heavier condensation, thick white fog that rolls through the colonnades and makes the far towers vanish. There are moments when the air thins and you can see, for a few seconds, the full vertical extent of this place: tier upon tier of grown architecture stacked upward, each level separated by voids, the whole thing rising like a ziggurat of wood and leaf into a sky you cannot reach. Then the mist returns and the architecture swallows itself again.

**And the wrongness.**

You do not notice it at first. That is the nature of it. You walk through an arch and the room beyond is plausible — generous, dark, filled with the smell of wet wood and something sweeter, something like overripe fruit mixed with ozone. You rest. You look back through the arch. The room you just left is the right shape but the wrong size. Or: you count the bridges you have crossed. You remember crossing four. You are certain. But the space you have traversed suggests more, or less, and neither arithmetic matches your memory. You reach for a handhold on a root-wall and the texture under your fingers is the texture you remember from an hour ago, from a different part of the Plateau, but you have not moved backward. You *think* you have not moved backward.

The architecture remembers you. That might be the simplest way to say it. The grown structures of the Plateau are alive, and their aliveness is not like the aliveness of the animals here — dull, vegetal, ambient. It is cognizant in a way that does not map onto cognition as you understand it. The corridors rearrange. Not while you watch. Not in any way you can catch. But you are never quite where you were before, and the doorways you remember lead to rooms that have changed in ways that are almost — *almost* — imperceptible. A wall has thickened. A column has divided into two. A staircase now ascends where you recall a flat floor.

The people who built this place — if *people* is the word — understood something about growth-logic that has been forgotten. They left no tools. No carved tablets. No refuse. Only the Plateau itself, still growing, still building, still remembering, vast beyond comprehension, empty of everything but itself, dimly lit, dripping, warm, and so quiet that the silence has texture.

You can hear the silence if you stop walking. It sounds like wood expanding. It sounds like roots drinking. It sounds like a continent-sized organism breathing in a rhythm just below the threshold of sound, and the Plateau is that organism, and you are inside its ribs, and it does not know you are there, and it does know you are there, and the distinction no longer means what it should.

The water drips from a root-basin above you. It falls six inches. It takes four seconds. You watch it fall. By the time it lands, the arch behind you is different, and you are not surprised.
