## The Woven Height

You hear it before you see it. Before you even know what is hearing.

The sound is not a sound. It is a condition, like pressure, like weather. It sits against your skin. It is the cumulative output of more living voices than you have a frame of reference for — a low continuous roar that is not one roar but ten thousand overlapping, interlocking cadences, so constant that after a moment you stop hearing it as noise and start feeling it as a texture, something you perceive in your sternum and the backs of your knees. It does not fluctuate. It has been at this pitch, roughly, for longer than anything here has had a name for pitch.

There is no sky. Not because the sky is hidden, exactly, but because at this elevation — perhaps four thousand meters, perhaps more, it is hard to tell — the place is roofed. The entire basin of the highland is enclosed by a structure so vast it took it a geological age to build, and it is still building. It is not a hive, though hives are its closest analogue. It is not a reef, though reefs grow the same way, in the same patient faith. It is what happens when fauna — every kind of fauna, everything that crawls and flies and burrows and swarms — spends a hundred thousand years constructing on top of and inside of and around and beneath one another's constructions until the whole thing becomes a single material event with no outside. No natural substrate remains exposed. If bedrock exists beneath this place, it has never seen air in the era of living things. Everything underfoot, overhead, and in every direction is built — compacted, woven, secreted, calcified, bonded by fungal thread and root-fiber and chitinous mortar and the slow chemistry of breath and decay.

The light is low. Not dark in the way of night — there is no sensation of an absent sun — but dark in the way of deep water, or the inside of a cathedral whose windows were never designed. Dimness saturates everything. It comes through in faint blue-green threads where the canopy of growth that has colonized the outer surface of the structure admits some fraction of daylight, and it filters down through successive layers — through living walls that are simultaneously architecture and organism, through chambers whose air is thick with condensation, through passages whose ceilings are furred with moss and lichen and the pale, pulsing threads of fungi that feed on the waste of the builders and their builders' builders. The light never reaches the interior. Nothing reaches the interior except nutrients carried by the bodies that move through it, endlessly.

The place is warm. That warmth is not climate, exactly. It is metabolic. The entire structure generates its own heat — the accumulated respiration of untold biomass, the slow fermentation of stored plant material within the walls, the friction of bodies moving through narrow arterial passages. The temperature is steady and patient, barely changing across the seasons because the mass of the construction absorbs and holds everything. Warm and damp. Condensation beads on every surface. Water moves through the structure not as rivers but as a slow, persistent seepage — drawn upward through capillary action in the porous walls, released as vapor from the breathing walls of the deepest chambers, falling again as a fine, continuous mist that makes the air feel like breathing through a warm cloth. There are no streams. There are pools — shallow, still, warm, gathered in the low points of vaulted chambers, thick with microbial life, their surfaces dimpled with the breathing holes of a thousand small organisms. The water tastes faintly of calcium and something sweet, something fungal, something alive.

And the life — there is no way around the life. It is everything. The fauna is not a fauna that lives here. The fauna IS here, in the way stone is in a mountain. Every centimeter of the constructed surface is occupied. Not infested — occupied, in the way a word occupies a sentence, the way a note occupies a chord. There are beetles the size of your thumbnail armored in iridescent plates moving in columns three-wide along the walls of the main corridors. There are things that are not beetles but serve the same structural purpose — armored, many-legged, packed shoulder to shoulder, their combined movement producing a faint vibration you feel through the floor. Above them, clinging to the ceiling in overlapping sheets, are soft-bodied organisms pale as cartilage, pulsing slowly, extruding filaments that descend into the wall and become part of it. They are builders. They have always been builders.

There are flyers — not birds, not anything you would call birds, but things that fly the way birds fly, in enormous murmurations that move through the interior spaces like weather systems, their wing-beats contributing to the roar, their bodies shedding material as they go: down, chaff, shed casings, all of it falling to be incorporated into the mass below. There are things that live only in the condensation film on the walls, too small to see, collectively loud as surf. There are large presences — deep in the core, in the chambers where the oldest construction is — that you hear but cannot see, that shift and groan and exude warmth and moisture, that seem to be as much architecture as organism, rooted into the walls of their birth-chambers, alive but sessile, growing into the structure they were born into until they and it are indistinguishable.

The vegetation is not separate from any of this. It grows everywhere that light and moisture and a surface coincide, which is everywhere. Mosses, ferns, lichens, thin creeping vines that thread through the construction and stabilize it, thick-rooted plants that have become load-bearing members of archways and buttresses, their wooden tissue indistinguishable from the chewed fiber-and-resin composite the builders use. In places the plant growth is so dense that it forms a second skin over the constructed surface, and in those places the boundary between the animal structure and the plant growth simply ceases to exist. They hold each other up. They hold each other in place. The whole mass breathes.

Nothing here is in a hurry. The dynamic of the place is attuned to durations that dwarf a single life, a single generation, a single species. Corridors widen over centuries as the combined tunneling of a dozen species gradually opens them. Chambers collapse and are rebuilt, not by any plan but by the accumulated instinct of organisms who know how to build because their ancestors built, who build with the same material and the same motions and arrive at forms that rhyme with forms from a million years ago without anyone having passed the information explicitly. The changes are slow. The only urgency belongs to the small, short-lived creatures who rush through the narrow passages, carrying things — food, larvae, building material, spores — and whose individual lives last days. Their movement is a current. It is the closest the place has to a rhythm.

The space is regional in scale. It occupies an entire high basin, rimmed by ridgelines that are themselves colonized — the outer surface of the construction draped over the mountainside like a second geology, thick with growth, ridged and folded, weathered into settled forms that look as natural as any cliff face. From outside, on the ridges above, it might look like a vast growth on the mountain — a bulge, a spreading, something alive and sessile. From inside, it is a world. Corridors branch and merge. Chambers open into cathedrals of compacted organic matter, their vaults hung with curtains of root and silk, their floors soft with accumulated detritus that is not detritus but substrate, food, building material, all at once. There are levels. There are vertical passages that drop deep into the oldest layers where the construction meets the buried remnants of earlier constructions built by earlier fauna now extinct but whose architecture persists, repurposed, inhabited, extended.

Nothing is natural here. Nothing is artificial either. The words don't apply. The entire landscape is a single continuous artifact built by life itself, over a duration so long that the builders have come and gone and come again, and the thing they built has become the world their descendants inherit. It is not a metaphor. It is not like anything. It is a place where everything is made and nothing is manufactured, where density is not crowding but simply the default state of matter, where darkness is not absence but presence — the accumulated shadow of a billion bodies, a billion wings, a billion years of building in the warm, damp, high dark where the sound never stops.
