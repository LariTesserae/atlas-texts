## The Basin of Knocking Stone

---

You hear it before the dark fully reaches you. Before the smell. Before the catch in your breath at the sheer density of *material* pressing in from every direction. You hear it — a wall of sound so consistent and so vast that your body decides, before your mind does, that it must be weather. A storm, maybe. A waterfall the size of a river turned sideways. But it isn't. It's life.

The basin opens below you, or around you — it's hard to say how large it is from the rim because the air itself seems thick here, faintly pearlescent, as though humidity has given the atmosphere a body. You're looking down into something the size of a small region, a shallow bowl of land maybe fifteen kilometers across, ringed by ridgelines dark with mineral staining. No trees crown those ridges. No scrub catches the light. The slopes are bare and smooth-looking, calcareous and ancient, streaked with the pale tracings of long-dried watercourses that return for a few days in certain seasons, or maybe in certain years, or maybe in cycles too long for a human life to catch more than one recurrence of.

There is almost no soil. The ground, where it's exposed, is a kind of living stone —ite -ite -ite, you'd stammer if asked. Calcite. Dolomite. Gypsum. Halite. And between and over and through those minerals, a film: the closest thing this place has to a producer layer. It isn't fungi, not exactly, and it isn't lichen, and it isn't moss. It's a crust. A mineral-metabolizing film, thin as breath in some places and an inch thick in others, dark violet-black where it's wet and pale grey-white where it's dry, and it covers *everything*. The rocks. The ledges. The shells of dead things, which here are many and various. The bones. The mineral spires that rise from the basin floor like a city's worth of ruined chimneys, some no taller than your knee, others thirty feet high, all of them encrusted and dripping in that same living skin.

These spires are how old water escapes the basin's deeper layers. Mineral-saturated solution pushed up through fissures by pressure it took centuries to build, and when it hits air it precipitates — slowly, slowly — another ring, another inch, another crystalline season. The locals who have studied this place (or who have passed through it, or who have simply stopped at its edge and then, for reasons they found difficult to explain, stayed) call them *stacked tears* or *slow candles* or *the standing*. They are, in geological terms, tufa formations of extraordinary age and patience. They have been growing since before your species learned to shape a hand-axe. They will continue long after. Erosion has touched them — wind has softened their lower halves to smooth curves, water has undercut some so they lean into each other, and a few have cracked and fallen and now lie half-buried in the mineral crust like the ribs of an impossible animal.

**Light** here is not a thing you experience so much as a thing you are short of. The basin is deep enough below the ridgeline, and the ridgeline heavy enough with its own mineral overhangs and collapsed shelves, that direct sky-light reaches the floor only in a few shattered columns at certain hours — weak, greenish, filtered through so much atmosphere and particulate that it looks less like illumination and more like suggestion. Most of the time, the basin exists in a state of dimness that your eyes slowly adjust to and then, eventually, stop being sure they've adjusted at all. But it isn't fully dark. You are never fully in the dark here. The fauna see, or feel, or perceive in ways that don't require photons, and some of them generate their own. Here and there, in the thickest clusters of living presence, a faint glow: blue-white, occasionally green, sometimes a color you'd call *warm* if it weren't so clearly inorganic. Biofluorescence? Bioluminescence? It's hard to tell from a distance and harder to confirm up close because the things producing it are small, quick, and *everywhere*.

The **fauna** are what break your understanding of the place. Because with no vegetation — no analogue to vegetation, no green-sustaining base, no photosynthetic engine — you expect emptiness. You expect austerity. Instead you get *saturation*.

They're packed. Not like animals packed into a watering hole during a drought — that's desperation, temporal, a compression that has a beginning and an end. This is permanent. This is *structural*. The basin floor is a living lattice of bodies, shells, movement, and chitin. The ground moves when you step on it — not because it's soft, but because it's *occupied*. Crustacean-things the size of your thumbnail, glossy and dark and segmented into too many parts, cover every flat surface in layered carpets. They flick their tails in unison when disturbed and the sound is like — you've never heard anything like it. The ticking, the rushing, the dry whisper of ten thousand half-gram bodies shifting at once. And beneath them, or among them, or over them: things with wings. Things with too many legs. Things that leave trails of mucus so thick the air smells of copper and low tide.

There are hunters. There must be. You hear them — the sound is layered, and beneath the constant insectile clicking there is a periodic whine, sharp and rising, that makes the smaller creatures go briefly still, a silence that ripples outward from some point in the mass like a dropped stone in water. Then the clicking resumes, louder, as though the whole assemblage collectively flinches and recovers.

The **sound** is, in a word, immense. Not painful, exactly, though after some time your skull begins to ache with it. It's the sound of a system that has had millions of years to perfect the art of making noise with chitin on mineral, with air pushed through spiracles, with limbs striking shell, with mandibles and stridulation-organs doing work that, in this dim world, substitutes for the visual displays you're accustomed to. Everything here communicates through vibration and noise. The basin floor *hums*. The spires carry the sound upward and the ridgeline reflects it back. At certain moments — when some vast coordinated thing happens in the fauna, a migration, a feeding event, a spawning — the sound rises to something your chest can feel more than your ears can process. De deafening is not a metaphor here. It is a condition.

Water does not flow openly here. It arrives as condensation on the upper surfaces of the basin's stone walls, where the cool air meets slightly less cool air, and beads run together into threads that descend and dissolve into the mineral crust before reaching the floor. In some seasons — or cycles, or whatever measures of time operate here — the basin receives rain: brief, heavy, warm-relative-to-the-air events that strike the rim and mostly evaporate before touching the ground, leaving only a brief thickening of the atmosphere and a sudden furious intensification of the fauna's activity. Real standing water exists only in a few places: shallow pools in the lowest depressions, the *kiss-pools* as they might be called, where water collects on a bediteite mineral surface too dense for the crust-film to colonize quickly, and the fauna gathers in tight, shimmering spirals to drink, or to feed on the organisms that drink, or to breed in the temporary abundance. These pools last days, sometimes weeks, then vanish, leaving behind a white mineral scar.

**The strangeness** of this place is hard to locate precisely. Nothing is single. Nothing is simple. The fauna do not divide neatly into categories. Some of them seem to change form over the course of a life-cycle that might be weeks or might be years — you see things here that look like larval forms of things whose adult forms you saw hours ago, and you cannot be sure they're the same species or different ones. Some fauna appear to be colonies — single organisms made of smaller organisms, moving and acting as one, the component parts clicking and chirping at each other in patterns that sound almost like the ambient noise of the basin itself, as though the colony is a micro-ecosystem mimicking the macro one. Other fauna are sessile, rooted to the spires and ledges, filtering the mineral-rich air or feeding on the crust-film with appendages they extend and retract in slow, almost geologic rhythm.

There is a rhythm here, to everything. The fauna surge and pull back. The sound intensifies and dims. The moisture accumulates and vanishes. It is an ecosystem with no plants, no photosynthesis, no solar foundation — and yet it teems so densely and diversely that it puts some of the richest forests and reefs of more illuminated worlds to shame. The energy here comes from below. From chemical gradients in the stone. From heat leaking up through deep fissures. From the slow, patient alchemy of water and mineral and time. The entire food web is an inverted tower built on geology.

You do not stay long in the Basin of Knocking Stone. Not because it is dangerous, though it is dangerous — the density of life means the density of predation, and the things that hunt here are fast and coordinated and soundless in ways the rest are not, moving through the clicking horde like knives through cloth. You don't stay because the sound gets into you. After an hour your thoughts begin to have rhythm. After two hours your breathing matches the clicking. After three you realize you have stopped walking and are standing still, eyes half-closed in the dim, and something in your body is *listening* with an attention so deep it no longer feels like yours.

The basin is old. It has been old for longer than you can mean when you say the word. And you, standing in it, are not so much a visitor as a brief disturbance in something that was perfectly settled before you arrived and will be perfectly settled long after you leave, clicking and clicking and clicking in the mineral dark.
