## The Throat

You find it by sound — long before you could ever find it by sight. A roaring that is not weather, not water, not any single engine but the convergence of hundreds into a single unbroken note pressed into the meat of your skull. It does not crescendo. It does not fade. It is the condition of being here, the way pressure is the condition of being deep underwater, so constant you forget it for minutes at a time and then your body remembers and your ribs ache.

The place is a hollow. Not a cave — someone made it. Someone made all of this. The walls are plate steel and riveted iron, curved inward like the interior of a throat, and they are webbed with pipework so dense the surface looks organic, like coral or the inside of a lung. Grated walkways are bolted to the wall in spiraling switchbacks that disappear upward into fog and downward into dark. There are catwalks hung beneath overhead conduits. There are trays and troughs and channels cut into every horizontal surface to carry water — always water — which is everywhere, sliding down the cold outer skin of the walls, weeping from flange-joints and valve-stems, beading on surfaces that the machinery beneath has warmed and the altitude air has cooled. The air is so wet it has weight. Droplets gather on your eyelashes faster than you can blink. Condensation sheets off rivet-heads in slow, cold tears.

It is cold. Not the clean cold of open sky but a soaked, penetrating cold, the kind that enters through the soles of your boots and stays. The altitude is what does it — this chamber is set high, impossibly high, into the flank of something vast. Through grates and open seams in the upper wall you can glimpse the outside: grey distance, wind, the suggestion of a sky so overcast it has no features. But the chamber itself holds its atmosphere like a closed fist. The fog is manufactured. The machinery below radiates heat that lifts the wet air into a permanent low cloud just beneath the ceiling, and from within that cloud the condensation falls in an endless, soft, maddening drizzle.

The light lives inside the fog. You see it before you see what makes it: a dull amber glow along the lower walls — the heat-glow of pipework, of combustion chambers, of processes too large and too enclosed to name. In the fog this light diffuses into something without source, so the whole space seems to be illuminated from within, like the inside of a cloud that is on fire very slowly. Nothing casts a hard shadow. Everything is soft edge and suggestion. A catwalk appears twenty feet away and you cannot tell whether it is solid until you are already on it, stepping through mist that clings to the grating like gauze.

Then you see them.

They are on every surface. Not scattered — *dense*. Packed. Layered. The riveted walls are furred with them. They hang from the undersides of grates in curtains. They crawl across the catwalks in such numbers that the grating disappears beneath them, a shell of chitin and motion over metal. They are not insects — they are too large, too various, too deliberate. Some are the size of your hand, armoured, with too many jointed limbs. Some are longer, pale, translucent in places where you can see the dim industrial glow pass through them like light through frosted glass. Others are heavy-bodied and low to the ground, moving in clots over the wet flooring, their surfaces slick and iridescent. You cannot count them. You cannot look anywhere without seeing them. They coat the pipes and the conduits and the housing of every machine. They have colonized the water channels. They drink from the weeping walls. They feed on the condensation, on each other, on heat, on vibration. Some of them hum at the same frequency as the machinery and you cannot tell if they are responding to it or driving it — or if the distinction even means anything here.

Their sound is folded into the roar. The roar is everything at once: the engines below, the turbines you can feel more than hear through the soles of your boots, the hiss and crackle of steam vents, the wet click of chitin on metal, the aggregate buzz of ten thousand creatures vibrating their bodies in frequencies just below what you can hear but inside what you can feel. It is a wall. It has been this loud for so long that the sound has become part of the architecture — you stop hearing it the way you stop feeling your clothes on your skin, and then, in the rare moment when some deeper frequency pulses through the floor and the whole chamber shifts one half-degree in its resonance, you *feel* the sound as a thing that was always there.

And nothing changes. 

That is the thing you notice last, and it is the thing that should frighten you most. The machines run at the same output. The water weeps at the same rate. The fog hangs at the same density. The creatures move with the same patterns they moved in an hour ago, a day ago — you have no way of knowing how long ago, because nothing about this place marks the passage of hours or days. There is no shift in the light. The roar does not intensify or recede. The water does not pool or drain; it moves in steady-state rivulets that have carved no grooves because they have never needed to, the metal beneath them already shaped for them, already expecting them, as if the place was built to this water and the water was built to this place and they arrived simultaneously. The erosion is zero not because the place resists weathering but because weathering requires time, and time — at least time as change — does not function here. The place is in equilibrium the way a held breath is in equilibrium: everything tensed, nothing moving, waiting for a release that is also a collapse.

The construction looks new. Not freshly built — *new*. The pipe-joints still have their seam-marks. The plating has no patina, only the thin bloom of condensation. The walkways show no wear. It is as if the whole place was assembled in a single moment and has existed in that moment ever since, an embryo that hatched into its final form and has remained in that form forever, still technically becoming, still technically arriving, the way a sound at constant volume is technically still starting.

This is The Throat.

It is a room the size of a cathedral, built into the bones of something much higher, somewhere the air is thin and the rain never stops falling from clouds that are also made of machine-heat and water. It is full — full of pipe and iron and water and chitin and vibration and dark light. It is so dense with presence that you feel you are walking through something, pushing your way through a thicket of living sound and wet metal the way you push through undergrowth in a forest that has no trees. And there is no exit you can identify, only more spiral walkways descending into more fog, more roaring, more of them — creatures on creatures on creatures, layer on layer, covering every inch of a place that was never meant to be empty but was never meant for you either.

You are inside something that is alive and not alive. That is built and is growing. That sounds like industry and breathes like an animal. That does not change because it does not need to, because it has already become everything it was going to become and is holding that shape the way a fist holds a stone: tight, and without mercy, and without end.
