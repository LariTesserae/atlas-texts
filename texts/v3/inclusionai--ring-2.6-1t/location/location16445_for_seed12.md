## The Drowned Plateau

You feel the altitude first — a lightness in the chest, a thinning of the air that never quite becomes cold because the warmth comes from below. The plateau sits high above whatever ancient datum once defined its floor, a vast table of dark volcanic stone that slopes gently in every direction toward depressions the water has claimed. It is not an ocean. It is something quieter: an inland sea of modest depth spread across a still-forming world, as if someone poured a basin and forgot to stop.

---

**The Stone**

The basalt is young. You can tell by the way it looks unfinished — not rough exactly, but unresolved. Columns half-formed, their hexagonal outlines interrupted where the flow cooled too quickly or too slowly. Surfaces that still release heat if you press your palm flat against them, even after whatever eruption or uplift brought them into being. The stone is dark gray tending toward black when wet, and in the dim light it gleams with a permanent dampness. Erosion has barely begun its patient work; edges are still sharp, clefts still narrow, drainage channels only suggested by the angle of water flow. The land is embryonic, and it knows it. Given ten thousand years it will be unrecognizable. It has, for now, only this one shape.

**The Water**

Warm. Almost persistently, unnervingly warm — fed by geothermal springs beneath the plateau and by a sky full of humid air that rarely condenses into anything more dramatic than the perpetual haze. The water sits in the shallow bowls and channels of the basalt at depths ranging from ankle-height to perhaps three or four meters at the lowest points. It is clear near the surface where light penetrates, green-brown with suspended minerals and the dense life of algae. In the deeper basins it goes dark and quiet, and the bottom disappears.

The water moves slowly. There are tides, but they operate on a rhythm so long you might mistake them for the water simply deciding to go somewhere else. Over the course of a week a shallow channel may deepen by half a meter, then recede again. The waterline is a negotiable border, and everything — life, construction, habit — has been built around this negotiation.

There is an unease to the water that the settlers have learned not to name. In certain basins, submerged objects behave strangely. A stone dropped into still water may float for several seconds before reluctantly sinking. A length of weighted cord hung beneath the surface sometimes appears to curve upward. And on the quietest days, when the air is thick and the haze compresses all distance into a soft grey envelope, shapes move beneath the surface that correspond to nothing above — shadows of things in the wrong places, or the wrong shapes, drifting with slow deliberation before vanishing into the murk.

**The Air and Light**

The sky is a low ceiling. Not clouds exactly — a permanent overcast so uniform it might be painted. The light that penetrates it is diffuse, directionless, and dim. There are no shadows to speak of; everything exists in the same even grey-gold twilight that neither brightens nor darkens meaningfully across the hours. At what would be midday the world looks the same as at what would be late afternoon, and the people of the plateau do not divide their days by the sun. They divide them by temperature — the brief warmth-spike that comes sometime after the midpoint of waking hours, and the cooler period that follows it — and by the subtle shifting of the water.

The air is dense with moisture. It coats surfaces with a thin film, beads on skin, carries warmth against the body. It smells of wet mineral stone, of something vegetal and faintly sweet — not decay, but growth. At night (defined by a dimming so subtle you might miss it if you blink) the air cools just enough to produce a mist that rises from the water's surface and hangs at knee-height, turning the lower world into a soft white nothing while the upper world stays in its grey.

**The Growth**

Vegetation on the plateau is abundant but strange in its distribution. The shallows are carpeted in algal mats that shift through colors — green, violet, a coppery bronze — depending on the mineral content of the water at any given spot. These mats are thick enough to walk on in places, springy and warm underfoot, and they release small bubbles constantly, a quiet fizzing that is one of the landscape's steadiest sounds.

Taller growths rise from the shallows: stalked things, not quite kelp, not quite reed, with broad translucent leaves that orient not toward the light but toward warmth and vibration. They sway without wind, leaning toward heat sources — thermal vents, warm stone, the bodies of large animals. In the margins where the water thins to a film, true land-plants have established themselves: dense creeping mosses, low mats of succulent-like vegetation with waxy leaves that store water against the periodic deepening of their habitat. A few squat trees with wide root-bases grip the exposed basalt ridges, their trunks swollen with stored moisture, their canopies sparse but broad — designed, it seems, to catch every photon in the dim air.

The overall impression is of a biosphere that is only a few evolutionary steps past the first tentative colonization. Life arrived here, or began here, and has not yet filled every possibility. There is room. There is space. The ecology is dense where it exists but has not yet spread to every corner. The growth is lush but not oppressive — it has the eager quality of something still becoming established, still experimenting.

**The Creatures**

Large fauna are rare. You may go hours or days without seeing movement that isn't plant or water. But the few creatures here are significant. The grazers are broad, low-slung animals — dense-bodied, almost amphibious, with wide splayed feet and muzzles adapted for cropping algal mats. They move slowly, deliberately, spending entire days traversing a single meadow of growth before moving on. Their hides are slick and dark, and their eyes are small and placed high on the skull, adapted to seeing above water while the body remains mostly submerged.

The largest beings are the drifters — immense filter-feeders that occupy the deeper channels. They are slow beyond reckoning. A drift across a single basin may take weeks. Their bodies, encrusted with the same algae that covers everything else here, eventually become small ecosystems in their own right: small organisms nesting in their crevices, birds (rare, but present) perching on their broad backs. They surface infrequently, expelling a long, warm breath with a sound like a cracked gate opening, and then descend again.

Smaller things exist: translucent arthropod-analogues that skate across the surface tension in colonies, forming rafts that drift and reform. Insects — if that word applies here — that emerge in brief clouds during the warmth-spike and vanish again. Larval forms in the water, innumerable, nearly invisible.

Sound from fauna is rare. The groan of a drifting leviathan. The soft splash of a grazer stepping off a bank. The thin, high call of something unseen moving through the haze at the edge of vision.

**The Settlements**

The builders found the ridges. Where the basalt rises in shallow arcs just above the usual waterline — a matter of a few hand-spans — they have constructed. The settlements are small, dense, and organized with a practical logic that has no word for decoration but produces something close to beauty through sheer accumulation of functional detail.

Walls are built from the dark stone, rough-cut with harder stone tools and bound with a mud-compite that dries harder than the stone itself. Roofs are layered — woven reed over wooden frame over packed earth, designed to shed water that pools too high. There are no straight lines; the builders work with the basalt's natural fractures and slopes, fitting their structures into the landscape rather than against it. The effect is of towns that grew out of the rock as naturally as the moss.

Water runs through the towns as it runs through everything here. Channels are cut between buildings, wide enough to wade through, and the residents move through their daily lives ankle- or knee-deep in warm water as a matter of course. Simple rafts and carved canoes — made from the swollen trunks of the ridge-trees — are the primary vehicles. There are docks: lashed-together platforms of wood and fiber extending out over the shallows where deeper channels allow for the approach of boats.

The technology is entirely pre-metal or early-metal. Stone tools dominate: basalt blades, granite pounders, obsidian-tipped implements for the few trades that require a sharp edge. Ceramics are well-developed — fired clay vessels for storage, cooking, and the slow preparation of foods that require immersion in hot water. Weaving is advanced: textiles from the reeds and aquatic fibers produce fabrics that are dense, water-resistant, and patterned in geometric designs that may encode information the way writing does elsewhere. There is no written language that a visitor would recognize. There is oral tradition, rhythmic and repetitive, and a system of carved marks on stone that serves something between record-keeping and art.

**The Strangeness**

The plateau resists full comprehension. Not through hostility or danger, but through a quality of *almost* — as if the place is one step to the left of where your mind expects it to be.

The water's behavior, as noted, is not entirely consistent with simple physics. Objects lost beneath the surface sometimes reappear in unexpected places — not immediately, but days later, in a different basin, resting on a ledge where they have no reason to be. Sounds travel through the water with unusual clarity, carrying conversations across distances that should muffle them to nothing. On the quietest nights, when the mist is thick and the air is perfectly still, some settlers report hearing music that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere — rhythmic, tonal, and faintly familiar, as if it is a memory of music rather than music itself.

The growth patterns of the vegetation sometimes suggest intention. Not intelligence, exactly, but *response*. An algal mat that thickens in the shape of a footfall where someone walked the previous day. Stalks that lean toward a building as if studying it. A patch of moss that grows in a pattern matching the carved marks on a nearby standing stone.

No one here finds this strange. It is simply the place they live. The world is alive in ways that matter to them — not abstractly, not philosophically, but the way a neighbor is alive: present, responsive, sometimes inexplicable, and not fully knowable. They do not try to explain the plateau. They build with it, eat from it, move through it, and leave it room to be itself.

**The Sound**

In the end, what you hear most is the water. A soft, continuous lapping — not urgent, not rhythmic in any simple way, but a constant whispering contact between water and stone. The fizzing of the algal mats releasing their tiny bubbles. The occasional deep groan of cooling rock somewhere beneath you, felt more than heard, a subsonic pressure in the chest. A distant call from a drifting creature. The soft creak of waterlogged wood at a dock. Wind, when it passes, is a long exhalation that moves across the surface of the water like a thought completing itself, and then is gone.

It is a quiet place. Not empty. Full of presence, but presence that speaks softly, moves slowly, and does not rush to announce itself.

The plateau endures. It is still becoming. It has time.
