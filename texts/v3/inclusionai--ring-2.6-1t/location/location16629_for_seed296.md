# The Tablelands of Qerreth

You arrive by climbing for hours along a road that was never really a road — a scar worn into the flank of the old basalt by generations of cart wheels and pack-animal hooves and now by the groaning pneumatic drays that haul loads ofite up from below. The switchbacks end abruptly. One moment you are gripping exposed root and cold stone, and the next you step onto a surface so flat and so vast that the world simply *stops going up.*

The Tableland.

It stretches out before you like the palm of an enormous hand laid flat against the sky — kilometers of gently undulating stone, planed to an almost obscene smoothness by two hundred million years of erosion. The edges of the old mountains still poke through in places: the stubs of dark ridges rising only a few meters above the plateau surface, rounded like the knuckles of a sleeping fist. Here and there a mesa stands alone, its sides sheer but shallow, a remnant with its head sheared off at the same universal height as everything else, as though the landscape were once a body and something slowly, patiently pressed it into the ground.

The altitude is merciless. The air is thin and tastes of mineral cold. Your lungs work. Your head hums. But the dampness — that is the thing you notice second, after the sheer *flatness* — the dampness is everywhere. Not rain, not exactly. Fog. Condensation that forms on every surface before dawn and does not fully burn off until midday, leaving everything perpetually slick, beaded, glistening. There is a river, or what passes for one: a braided network of shallow channels that appear and shift across the plateau's surface depending on the season, fed by snowmelt from peaks far above and visible only as a faint blue line on the horizon. Water pools in the low places — shallow lakes that are more mineral than fresh, more mirror than water, and in which the sky reflects with a clarity so perfect it is difficult to tell up from down.

And the *vegetation*. It should not be this lush at this height and this cold. That is the first thing the geologists said when they came, a hundred years ago, before the drill-heads and the foundries. That this shouldn't exist. The plants here grow low and wide — vast mats of moss and liverwort that cover the stone in undulating carpets of green so deep and so dense they look almost black in the dim light. Ferns unfurl in fractal spirals from cracks in the basalt, some of them waist-high, their fronds leathery and dark with moisture. There are broad-leafed things with no proper name — the botanists assigned provisional designations and then gave up and started calling them *plateau laurel* and *fog-bloom* and *that creeping red nonsense.* They grow in such density that the stone beneath is rarely visible except where the ground has been cleared for construction or the road has been cut. In places the vegetation forms a kind of sphagnum hummock-land, soft and treacherous, and in the dim twilight of an overcast afternoon the whole surface of the plateau seems to *breathe* with it.

The sky above is almost always clouded — not stormy, but a persistent low overcast, grey-white and featureless, pressing down. The light is perpetual dusk. Even at noon the sun is a pale disc behind gauze, throwing no shadows, bleaching no colors. Everything exists in a state of muted saturation: the greens are deep but never vivid, the stone is charcoal, the water is leaden. At twilight — which is not so much a transition as a slow dimming of an already dim thing — the plateau turns the color of iron, and then of ink, and then of something that is not quite darkness but not quite anything at all.

---

The town is called **Kethrine**.

It was founded forty years ago when the first drills broke through into the sub-plateau mineral strata and found seams ofite — a dense, dark crystalline material that burns hot and clean and has made the city below rich and the town above *necessary.* Kethrine sits at the plateau's northern edge, where the road descends into the lowlands, and it looks — from a distance — like a growth on the stone. A cluster of squat industrial buildings in dark stone and riveted iron, their roofs low-pitched against the wind, their walls thick because the cold is not the kind you can ignore. Steam rises from vents in the streets — not constantly, but in intermittent plumes, hissing and dissipating into the fog. The town's infrastructure is visible everywhere: pipes run along the sides of buildings and across the faces of the old ridges like veins in a forearm, carrying heat andite-fumes and condensate from the extraction sites down below. There is a rail spur — narrow-gauge, industrial — that terminates at a loading platform where the drays are loaded and sent down the mountain. The sound of it is a low, rhythmic clanking that underlies everything: the engines idling, the conveyor belts turning, the dull percussion ofite being cracked from its matrix.

The town is dense in the way old mining towns are dense — not planned so much as *accumulated,* built in layers as need demanded, each generation's construction leaning against or on top of the last. Upper stories are sometimes cantilevered over streets so narrow two people cannot pass without turning sideways. In some alleys the buildings are close enough to touch at the eaves, and the stone between them is slick with condensation and furred with moss. The residents — a few thousand, mostly miners and engineers and support workers and their families — have adapted to the altitude and the damp in the ways people always do: they move slowly, they drink hot things, they keep their windows sealed against the fog. There is a school, a medical hall with an oxygen tent for newcomers, a canteen that serves a thick stew of root vegetables and dried meat that is more fuel than food. The social life orbits the town's one public house — the *Flatline* — which is warm and loud in the way that small warm places are loud, full of the sound of voices competing with the hiss of the stove.

---

The fauna is sparse. You see it in glimpses: a bird now and then, something small and grey and fast that nests in the rock crevices and eats insects you rarely see. A fox-like thing with short legs and dense fur, observed at dusk picking its way along the edge of the mesa with a kind of judicial patience. Mostly, though, the animal life of the Tablelands is hidden or subterranean or nocturnal — and the dynamism of the ecosystem is in the *other* processes: the way the vegetation grows and dies and regrows in cycles that don't quite match the seasons, the way the mineral seepage from the extraction sites changes the chemistry of the shallow lakes and the moss responds by shifting color — from deep green to a bruised ochre — across whole hectares in the space of a week. The insects are numerous: small, dark, persistent midges that breed in the standing water and form clouds so thick near the lakes that they look like mist. The birds come for the midges. Something else comes for the birds, but no one has seen it clearly. There are marks in the mud near the river-channels — three-toed prints, deeply impressed, that appear after heavy condensation nights and are gone by morning, either washed away or *retrieved.*

---

The strangeness is cumulative rather than dramatic.

It starts with the stone. The basalt of the Tablelands is old beyond reckoning, and in places it has a texture that is unsettlingly smooth — not water-worn so much as *worn by something with intention.* There are grooves in the stone in certain areas that are too regular to be natural and too deep to be the work of any tool the miners have identified. The geologists say they are tectonic. The miners say they are something else. Everyone agrees, without discussing it, that you should not walk in the old grooves after dark.

The fog has texture. This is not something anyone can prove, but the residents of Kethrine know it in their bodies: on some mornings the condensation is heavy and sluggish and clings, and on others it is light and dry and moves *through* the streets like something with errands. The old-timers say the fog remembers things — that if you stand in it long enough, you begin to feel the shape of what was here before the stone was here, a topography that is gone but not forgotten, still pressing up through the surface like a memory trying to become real.

And the lakes. The shallow mineral lakes of the Tableland reflect the sky with a fidelity that should not be possible — you look down into them and see not your own face but *the sky as it was,* displaced by seconds or minutes, so that the reflected clouds have moved slightly from where the real clouds are and you cannot tell which sky is the present one. The miners who work near the lakes say that sometimes, on very still days, the reflection does not show the sky at all but shows the plateau as it was — bare stone, unweathered, sharp-edged, *young* — and for a moment you see what the world looked like before the erosion had done its patient work and the mountains were still mountains and the air was thin and empty and *waiting.*

Then the wind shifts, and the image is gone, and you are standing on the flat top of the world in the dim grey light with the smell ofite in your nostrils and the sound of the conveyor belts in your ears and the feeling — which you cannot shake, which you never fully shake — that the landscape is not a place but a *process,* something that is still happening, slowly, beneath your feet, beneath the moss and the stone and the thin cold air, and that you are standing on the surface of something that has not yet finished becoming what it is going to be.
