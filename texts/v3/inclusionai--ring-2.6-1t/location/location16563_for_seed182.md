## The Sump of Ten Thousand Pipes

You arrive through a wound in the stone.

Not a door, exactly — the opening was made by a subsidence event maybe forty years ago, when the underside of the Tavaran Plateau split along a fault no one had mapped. The sheared rock still looks surgical: a scar of white and ochre calcite rising in jagged shelves, the edges too fresh for comfort, too clean for water-work. The air pouring out is hot and close, smelling of copper and wet chalk and something faintly vegetal that shouldn't exist this far down. You would call it the smell of a jungle if jungles also smelled like iron filings and hot oil.

The cavern is not enormous. It would fit comfortably inside a cathedral — or rather, it would if a cathedral had been abandoned to flood and then colonized and then industrialized. The ceiling vaults maybe thirty meters overhead, scarred with fresh fracture lines that still weep warm water in slow threads. The light is dim and greenish-amber, the color of old resin, and it comes from everywhere and nowhere: the bioluminescent films that have colonized every wet surface, and the electrical conduit that the settlers threaded through in the early years, now so encrusted with mineral growth and root-matter that you can't always tell where the wiring ends and the cave begins.

The city — no, *settlement* is too modest, but *city* is too grand for what is essentially a hamlet pressed into a single chamber — fills the space the way sediment fills a jar. Structures are built on structures, wedged into fissures, hung from anchor-bolts driven into the ceiling, stacked on ledges that shouldn't bear weight. The architecture is industrial in origin: riveted steel, poured concrete, pipe-sections repurposed as columns and walls. But the vegetation has pushed through every gap with relentless patience. Broad-leafed plants with pale, almost translucent foliage have colonized the upper reaches, hanging in curtains that sway in the convection currents. Their roots are visible, clinging to metal and stone alike, drinking from condensation channels and from the warm trickle of water that runs along the capillary-fine cracks in the cavern walls. The leaves do not photosynthesize — there is not enough light for that — and no one has ever fully agreed on what they *do* metabolize. Some say minerals. Some say the light itself, in wavelengths we can't see. Some say they don't metabolize at all, that they are simply here, the way rust is simply here when the conditions are right.

They are lush. That is the word for them. Lush and wrong, a jungle in the dark, the air thick with the moisture they transpire, the temperature climbing past what the geothermal vents alone could explain. It hovers around thirty-eight degrees Celsius — tolerable if you don't exert yourself, dangerous if you stay too long, and the humidity makes it feel worse. Your clothes will be soaked in minutes. Your instruments will fog. The condensation regime is constant: everything drips, everything beads, everything rusts at a pace that makes maintenance a full-time occupation.

The sound is what unsettles you most, or rather the near-absence of it. There is a baseline hum — the generators, three of them, diesel-thermal units housed in a reinforced alcove near the south wall, their vibrations transmitted through the stone floor and up into your teeth. There is the irregular percussion of water drops falling from the ceiling into pooled surfaces below, each one a small, precise event in a room where every other sound is absorbed by the dense vegetation and the packed construction. Occasionally the stone groans. A settling sound, deep and tectonic, that makes the buildings shiver and the pipe-work rattle. The settlers say it happens about once every six hours. They say the cavern is still opening.

There is almost no fauna. A few arthropods — pale, eyeless, no larger than a fingernail — cluster around the warm seams in the rock. Something flies through the upper canopy of vegetation: a translucent, kite-shaped organism with no visible means of propulsion, drifting on thermal currents with the slow deliberation of a balloon. It is the largest animal anyone has seen. It appears at dusk — if *dusk* is the word for the dimming cycle when the electrical supply is partially rationed and the bioluminescence becomes the dominant light source — and vanishes by morning. No one has touched one. No one has found a dead one.

The rules of this place are geological, ultimately. The erosion is young. The stone is fresh, the features sharp, the columns still angular where they should by rights be rounded by water or time. The cavern was opened less than a century ago by forces still debated — tectonic creep, deep aquifer collapse, something stranger — and everything here is still in its first formation. The buildings sit on surfaces that are still mineralizing. The conduits are being slowly swallowed by calcite deposits. In thirty years, some of the infrastructure will be embedded in new stone. In sixty, it will be gone. The settlement exists in a window of geological opportunity, building into a space that is, by deep-earth standards, *temporary*.

This is the strangest part, and you feel it before you can articulate it. The proportions are right but the *feel* is wrong. The sharp edges are too sharp, the symmetry of some of the natural stone formations too exact. There are alcoves that are clearly not erosion-carved, and there are patterns in the bioluminescent film — viewed from the upper walkways on a still night, when the generators fall to their lowest idle — that almost suggest arrangement. Not language. Not intention. But a quality of *tendency*, as if the cavern is being shaped by something with patience beyond yours, and the settlement, with its packed buildings and thick vegetation and ten thousand sweating pipes, is growing into a pattern it does not yet recognize.

The pace is glacial. A building takes a decade. A garden takes longer. Children born here have never seen the surface. The water level in the lowest channels rises a centimeter a year. A new crack appeared in the western wall seven years ago; it is now three meters long, weeping warm water, and someone has built a pipe-conduit along its edge to collect the mineral-rich flow for use in the settlement's water system. Someone has planted things in it. The things are growing.

It is quiet here. Hot and quiet and green-lit and dense, a pocket of jungle sealed inside the earth, dreaming slowly in the dark.
