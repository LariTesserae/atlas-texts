## The Hollowing of Aeth-Mara

---

You find it by sound before you find it by sight.

A resonance — deep, percussive, almost tectonic — rising from somewhere below the ridgeline. The air is cold up here. Cold enough that each exhale becomes architecture: a brief translucent scaffold that hangs for a moment before dissolving. The elevation presses against your ears. The world is thin. The sky is the color of unpolished steel, and the wind moves across it in long, low trajectories, like something being dragged.

The plateau appears to have been finished long ago. Not by human hands. By time.

The stone beneath your feet has been eroded past the point of recognition — there are no edges, no fractures, no visible memory of the forces that shaped it. The bedrock has been worn down into smooth, flowing undulations, like the surface of a riverbed magnified to a landscape's scale. Ridges the height of a person's waist curve for miles in slow sine waves, and between them, shallow valleys hold pools of condensation-fed water. The water is black with dissolved minerals, still as poured glass, and warm relative to the air — a degree or two above freezing, which here feels almost hospitable.

This is the end-state of a mountain range that forgot what it was trying to become. The erosion has been so thorough, so patient, that the rock has begun the slow process of forgetting it was ever rock.

---

And then there is the town.

It does not announce itself as a town. There is no wall, no gate, no perimeter. You simply begin to notice, as you cross the lowest of the undulating ridges, that the smooth stone is no longer bare. Pores have opened in it — not naturally, not exactly. They are too regular, too symmetrically placed, like the stomata of a leaf scaled up to the size of doorways. Through them you see dim cavities, and in those cavities: structure.

The architecture here is grown, not built. It comes from a species of lignified vine — dark amber, translucent when wet — that has found purchase in the mineralite substrate and exploited every seam, every capillary, every hollow left by ten thousand years of dissolution. The vines have wound themselves into load-bearing arches, into lattice walls, into spiraling columns that rise into the void above and branch at the top into buttresses that connect to neighboring structures. There are no right angles. There are no straight lines. The spaces the vines have formed are organic, flowing, liturgically curved — corridors that widen into chambers that narrow into passages that open onto overlooks above the valley floor.

The town has maybe forty or fifty of these structures. Some are the size of a single room — chambers with seating hollows worn into the vine-walls, with shelves of compressed moss where things have been stored. Others are larger, communal in scale, with vaulted ceilings twenty feet overhead and floors textured with root-tendrils that grip the stone beneath the vine layer. The layout follows a logic that is legible but not comfortable: spiraling paths between structures, bridges that cross at odd angles, dead-ends that feel intentional rather than abandoned. This is urban planning performed by biology — a mycelial logic, optimized not for efficiency but for connection.

Because the vines connect everything. Above and below. You can hear it if you press your back against the wall of one of the chambers: a slow, wet clicking, like knitting needles at work in another room, and it is coming from inside the walls. The vascular tissue of the town is active. Sap moves through it. Nutrients shuttle between structure and substrate. The town is metabolizing.

---

The vegetation is beyond lush. It is unapologetic.

Where the stone is shallow enough, and the condensation pools have deposited enough mineral wealth, the plant life has colonized everything. Ferns the color of tarnished bronze unfurl from the joints of the vine-structures, their fronds five or six feet long and heavy with dew. Mosses have formed carpets so dense and so layered that the distinction between floor and organism has become philosophically unclear. You step on moss and the moss gives way, slightly, and then pushes back — not aggressively, but with a firm resistance, like pressing into a muscle.

Some of the greenery is bioluminescent. Not dramatically — not enough to light your way — but enough that if you sit still in one of the vine-chambers and let your eyes adjust, you begin to notice a faint green-gold emanation from the walls, a byproduct of chemical processes you can smell: sweet, fungal, faintly ammoniac, like a cellar full of ripe melons left too long in the rain.

The most striking growths are the aerophytes. They hang from the vine-structures in clusters — translucent, bladder-like organisms filled with gas they produce internally. They shift color with the humidity, moving from pale grey in dry spells to a deep viridian when the condensation rises. When the wind moves through the town, the aerophytes chime against each other in complex, arrhythmic patterns. It is a sound like a field of glass bells played by an unskilled hand: vast, slightly overwhelming, and strangely beautiful.

This is one source of the noise. The wind itself is another — funneled through the density of structures, it moans in shifting pitch, sometimes harmonic, sometimes dissonant. The condensation water, draining from the smooth rock surfaces, falls through arrangements of leaves and vine and stone in a hundred different locations, creating a perpetual, overlapping percussion. And beneath it all, the town: alive, clicking, dripping, breathing.

It is never quiet here. It is never meant to be.

---

There is a strangeness to this place that resists description but insists on being felt.

The light is ordinary — grey daylight from the steel sky, diffused through the cloud layer that sits perpetually at the altitude of the plateau's upper edge. But shadows here do not behave as they should. In the vine-chambers, particularly deep ones, the shadows seem to persist after their source has moved, hanging for seconds or minutes like afterimages in peripheral vision. The bioluminescent mosses cast light upward, which is expected, but also laterally, which is not — the moss-light seems to choose its direction, brightening on the side facing empty space as though it is showing you something that isn't there yet.

The water in the condensation pools is still in a way that exceeds calm. A pebble dropped into one will produce ripples that spread outward in perfect circles — for a moment — and then the circles distort, elongating, pulling toward a point that is not the center. As though the pool has a preference. As though it is listening, and the ripples are tilting toward the thing they're listening for.

The fauna here is almost nonexistent. You will see, perhaps, a single creature in the hours you spend in Aeth-Mara. It is small — the size of a rat, or a large beetle, or something that exists between those two ideas. It moves with a speed that seems to violate its size: one frame here, one frame fifty feet away. It is pale, almost translucent, and it carries itself with an apparent purpose that you can never follow. You will never see where it goes.

The residents — if they exist — have never been seen. The vine-chambers show signs of use: smoothed seating surfaces, compressed moss-beds, containers fashioned from hardened resin. But there is no residue on them. No food remains. No waste. The town is maintained by a biology that is not entirely a biology, used by inhabitants who are either invisible, departed, or not yet arrived.

---

There are moments, standing at the edge of the town where the vine growth thins and the bare stone resumes, when you can feel the erosion beneath you. Not as instability — the stone is solid, ancient, profoundly settled — but as a kind of deep motion. Tectonic slowness. The plateau is still descending, still being worn flat, still approaching a final condition that may be a surface below sea level, or may be nothing at all. The town grows on this motion. Its roots feel the settling and respond, reinforcing, weaving, building complexity faster than the stone can lose it. It is a negotiation between dissolution and construction, and it has been ongoing for so long that neither side remembers what it started from.

The cold is not hostile here. It is atmospheric. It is the temperature of patience. It makes the air sharp and clean and fills your lungs with a clarity that borders on sorrow. You breathe and feel the breath go all the way down, and for a moment you understand what it means to be a process in a system that has almost finished running.

Above the aerophytes, above the vine-canopy, the steel sky moves without visible wind. Clouds pass. Light shifts. The town hums.

The water drips. The stone settles. Nothing is built here. Everything grows.

And the sound — the sound never stops.
