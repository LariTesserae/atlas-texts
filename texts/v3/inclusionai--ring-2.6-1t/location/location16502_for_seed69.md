# The Sump of Kael Varn

---

There is no sky here. Not the way you'd recognize one. Above the plateau the atmosphere thickens into a permanent inversion layer — warm, wet, opaque as poured clay — and the sun, if it still exists, is a rumor. What light might filter down is consumed by the sheer vertical density of the biomass: layer upon layer of canopy, vine, epiphyte, moss, fungal curtain, and spore-film until the last photon is devoured somewhere around forty meters up, leaving everything below in a darkness so consistent it feels geological. You don't see this place. You *inhabit* it. You feel it on your skin, in your ears, in the pressure behind your teeth.

The ground is soft. That's the first thing — before sound, before smell, before the dark fully settles — the ground yields. It is not mud exactly, though mud is in it. It is a living mat: roots, peat, decomposing vegetation, fungal mycelium so dense it has its own texture, almost like felt. Step on it and it exhales a breath of warm, mineral-rich air. Step wrong and you're ankle-deep in black water that has been sitting for centuries, leaching iron and manganese from the volcanic substrate below, staining everything it touches a rust-orange that never fully washes out. The water table is inches below the surface everywhere. In the low basins it *is* the surface — vast shallow lakes, no more than a foot or two deep, so still they look like poured obsidian, reflecting nothing because there is nothing to reflect.

And the sound. Gods, the sound.

It begins as a low hum — subsonic, felt in the chest before it reaches the ears — and builds from there. The water is always moving, even when it appears still: thermocline currents in the black lakes, capillary creep through the root-mat, the slow gravitational seep of the plateau's edges where waterfalls pour over vertical rock faces into unseen gorges below. You hear them before you see them — these falls — as a roar that rises and falls with the density of the vapor they carry. The vegetation adds its own layer: the creak and groan of trunks slowly reshaping themselves in the permanent damp, the pop and snap of gas bubbles released from submerged peat, the ceaseless, papery rustle of spore-bodies opening and closing in response to barometric shifts you can't feel but they can.

The fauna has learned to navigate by sound, and the fauna is *loud*. There are things here — large things — that communicate in infrasonic pulses that travel through the ground itself. You feel them as vibrations in your ribcage: a slow, rhythmic thudding, almost like a heartbeat, but arrhythmic, polyrhythmic, dozens of overlapping cardiac patterns none of which are your own. Closer, in the canopy, there are creatures with vocal sacs the size of melons that emit calls in frequencies that seem to shift pitch depending on how much water is in the air between you and them — so that the sound you hear is not the sound they made but the sound the atmosphere *delivers*, and it is never the same twice. Frogs — enormous frogs, the size of a human head, with vocalizations like short-wave radio static — chorus from the waterlogged bases of trees in densities so great their combined noise becomes a single, textured roar, a wall of amphibian argument that never ceases.

And then there are the machines.

You come upon them slowly, the way you come upon everything here: by sound first, then by touch, then by the faintest scent of ozone and heated metal that means *something human was here, or something that learned from humans*. A turbine, half-swallowed by a strangler fig, its blades furred with moss and lichen, still turning — impossibly, slowly — driven not by steam or combustion but by the updraft that rises from the geothermal vents threading the plateau's underside. The vents exhale warm, sulfur-scented air through fissures in the rock, and the old industrial infrastructure — pipes, conduits, heat exchangers — has become a kind of skeleton for the ecosystem. Roots thread through cable conduit. Fungal mats colonize the flat surfaces of generator housings. A concrete cooling tower, cracked and listing, has become a waterfall in miniature, its interior walls green with algae that glows — faintly, almost imperceptibly — a pale blue-green that comes and goes with the spore cycle, so that if you sit still long enough you begin to see afterimages: ghostly traces of color in the absolute dark, pulsing in time with the venting of the geothermal steam.

There are more structures. Faint grids of metal in the root-mat, detectable only when you step over them and feel the resistance underfoot — old flooring, old foundations, the ghosts of buildings. A control panel, mostly buried, with a single indicator light that has somehow survived centuries of darkness and humidity, blinking once every forty-seven seconds with a faint amber glow so weak you can only see it if you cup your hands around your eyes and look at the exact spot from the exact angle. It means nothing now. Nothing in this place serves its original purpose. But the machines still run, the roots still follow the cable paths, the water still flows through the old plumbing, and if you press your ear to any pipe you will hear it: a sound like a heartbeat. A pump. Moving water. Something still *working*, in the dark, beneath the weight of a jungle that doesn't know what it's built on top of.

The scale is what unsettles most. The plateau extends beyond what you can intuit. In the darkness, distance becomes acoustic: you can hear a waterfall three kilometers away as clearly as a voice beside you, because the dense, moisture-heavy air carries sound with a fidelity that is almost cruel. Sounds arrive from everywhere, layered, overlapping, and you cannot build a mental map from them because the plateau generates its own weather — warm thermals rise from the geothermal zones, collide with the cool, saturated air descending from the inversion layer, and produce micro-storms that roll across the surface like slow surf: sudden drops in pressure, sudden surges of humidity, bursts of sound as old-growth trees creak and sway under wind that moves in unpredictable directions. You can feel the weather on your face even in total darkness: a cool spot, then a warm spot, then moisture so fine it is almost fog, then a momentary dry pocket through which your sweat evaporates with startling speed before the wet returns.

The fauna is everywhere. Not just the frogs and the unseen, infrasonic giants. Insects — vast clouds of them, but insects the size of small birds, their wings producing a sound like helicopter rotors, navigating by vibration-sense and infrared. Blind fish in the underground waterways, translucent, with lateral line systems so sensitive they react to the footsteps of creatures walking ten meters above. Something that lives in the canopy and you never see but you hear every few minutes: a sound like a door slamming, resonant and startling, and then nothing. Small creatures that have learned to shelter in the old ventilation ducts, and you hear them moving through the walls, their feet ticking across metal in patterns that are almost, but not quite, the rhythm of something mechanical still operating.

And the water. Always the water. It does not just flow here — it *permeates*. Everything is damp. Everything is alive with moisture. The air is so saturated that condensation forms on every surface, including your skin, and you are never fully dry, never fully warm, but also never cold — the plateau exists in a narrow thermal band, warm and tropical despite its elevation, because the geothermal heat from below and the insulation of the cloud layer above create a pocket of climate that is utterly its own. Rain falls, sometimes, though the water that comes from above is almost indistinguishable from the water that rises as mist from below. The drops are warm. They smell of sulfur and iron and something sweet and organic — the breath of ten million plants decomposing in the wet.

The weirdness is not dramatic. It is cumulative. It is the moment you realize the roots have grown into patterns that echo the old electrical schematics painted on the conduit walls. It is the frogs that synchronize their calls with the rhythm of the geothermal pump. It is the faint phosphorescence that follows the paths of the old wiring, as if the mycelium learned where the current flowed and now marks those lines in dim, living light. It is the sound — always beneath everything, beneath the frogs and the wind and the creaking trees — of something that sounds almost like machines and sounds almost like breathing and may be either or both.

You do not see this place.

You feel it pressing against you from all sides, warm and wet and loud and alive, black and vast, humming with the work of systems that no longer need anyone to explain what they're for.
