# The Lattice Ward

You hear it before you see it — a low, resonant hum that isn't quite mechanical and isn't quite wind. It lives in the space between, vibrating through the walls, which are not walls exactly but the ridged flank of a root column three stories tall, its surface furred with pale lichen that pulses faintly when the humidity shifts. The air is cool on your skin, carrying the mineral sweetness of condensation and something behind it — copper, perhaps, or ozone — the signature of the old transformers bolted into the root-mass overhead.

The Lattice Ward is a settlement the size of a market town's central quarter, tucked inside what was once (or still is — the distinction has blurred) a single organism. The young growths that arch overhead in long, catenary curves are lignified enough to bear weight, and someone — generations ago — began routing pipe and cable along them. Now the infrastructure has the density of a real city: conduit bundles the color of old bone spiral up root trunks, junction boxes are riveted to bark, and electromagnetic transformers — squat, oil-cooled, and older than anyone's memory — sit in cradle-welds cut into living wood. The wood has grown around them. The transformers have grown into the wood. Feedbacks loop through both.

The spacing is what gets you. The Ward is not cramped. Between each great column-root there are wide avenues, thirty or forty paces of open air where mist collects in thin veils and drifts at ankle height. The ground is soft — a compressed mat of fibrous detritus, perpetually damp, with small rivulets threading through it in channels that the residents have lined with flattened sheet metal. The water collects in bulbous cisterns grown — or grafted — into the roots themselves, their translucent membranes showing the level within. When it rains, which it does in brief, cool curtains twice a day, every surface streams and the hum of the transformers rises in pitch, as if the organism is drinking.

Fauna is scarce. A few species remain: a pale beetle the size of a thumbnail that navigates by the electromagnetic fields of the conduits, nesting in the junction boxes; a long-legged wading bird that picks through the detritus mat at dawn and vanishes by midday; somewhere deeper in, something larger that you hear more often than see — a slow, arrhythmic thrumming that vibrates the puddles. No one speaks of it much. The industrial catalogues list it as "ambient vibration source, unclassified."

The light is grey-white and even, filtering down through the canopy of young wood where it has been supplemented — not replaced — by banks of phosphor-coated panels powered by the transformers. The combined effect is daylight-ish, permanent, without the drama of sun angle or shadow. At night the panels dim to a warm ochre, and then you can see the bioluminescence: faint green-gold threads running through the inner surfaces of the root columns, the plant's own vascular system glowing faintly like circuitry, like a nervous system carrying signals too slow for the eye at full brightness. If you stand still long enough, you begin to see the pulses — slow, rhythmic, about the speed of a resting heartbeat.

Infrastructure is layered. First generation: the cut-and-rivet work, hand-forged brackets, wrought-iron clamps biting into living tissue, now so corroded they've become part of the mineral substrate. Second generation: the electrification, with its oil-cooled transformers and copper bus-bars strung along the ribs of the arches. Third generation — still partial, still being installed in the outer wards — is solid-state, low-voltage, networked into the bioluminescent system, reading the plant's chemical state and adjusting the light panels accordingly. The three coexist. Technicians maintain all three. They argue about which layer the plant responds to best. No one has settled it.

The air moves slowly. There is a ventilation rhythm — some say it's the plant itself expanding and contracting with moisture uptake, others say it's pressure differentials driven by the heat of the transformers — but either way, there is a breath. Cool air enters through fissures in the eastern root wall, warm air exits through vents in the canopy high above. The mist thickens and thins on this cycle. Sound carries differently in each phase: in the inflow, voices carry far; in the outflow, the place is almost perfectly quiet, and you hear only the tick of condensed water drops falling from leaf-edge to leaf-edge and the deep, sub-audible murmur of the transformers.

Everything is weathered. Everything is settled. The Lattice Ward has existed long enough that its maintenance rituals feel more like tending than engineering — scouring corrosion from contacts, pruning root-growth away from cable runs, replacing phosphor panels that have dimmed. The plant regrows what is cut. The machines outlast their makers and are rebuilt from salvaged parts. The cisterns fill and drain. The beetles navigate their invisible grids. The thrumming in the deep continues.

There is something unusual here, but nothing that announces itself as strange. Only when you've been inside for a while do you notice: the root-columns lean toward each other slightly, not randomly — they create a geometry that isn't quite natural branching, and the conduit paths between them follow the same angles, as if the infrastructure and the organism arrived at the same solution independently. Or perhaps not independently. The engineers' notes, copied and recopied over decades, contain a single recurring annotation in different hands: *the lattice corrects*.

No one knows what it corrects toward. The Ward works. The water flows. The light stays even. The hum remains.
