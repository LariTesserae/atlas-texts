# The Soundwood Hollow

---

There is no light here. Not darkness as you know it — not the absence that comes when you close your eyes or step indoors at night. This is a darkness that predates the concept. No sun has ever reached the basin floor. No starlight leaks down the walls. The air is cool, faintly metallic, and it has not moved quickly in a very long time.

You enter through a throat.

A narrow fissure in the rock, barely wider than your shoulders, descending at a shallow angle through pale stone that is not quite stone — closer toite, calcified and layered, like the inside of a shell magnified to geological scale. The walls are slick with mineral breath. After perhaps ten minutes of downward walking, the fissure opens without announcement into the Hollow.

And the sound hits you.

It is enormous. Not aggressive, not violent — it is simply *present at a scale that your body does not expect from silence-made-noise.* A low, continuous tonal drone fills the basin, rising and falling in cycles too long to time with your breathing. It hums in the range you feel more than hear, in the sternum, the teeth, the fluid of the inner ear. Beneath it — layered, almost polyphonic — are drier sounds: creaking, groaning, occasional sharp taps like woodpeckers you've never seen striking wood you cannot see. Things flex. Things swell. Things exhale.

There is no wind in the conventional sense. Air moves through the Hollow in slow convection cycles, warm gas rising from mineral vents far below, cooler air settling back down along the walls. But this movement, patient as geology, is enough. It passes through the city — and it *is* a city — and the city sings.

---

The architecture is alive. That is the only way to say it plainly.

In the beginning, someone might have planted something, or something might have grown on its own. The distinction no longer matters after the epochs that have passed. What stands now — what has stood for longer than any language has a word for — is an urban form composed entirely of organisms that are neither tree nor fungus nor anything with a Latin name you would recognize. They are the color of wet bone and deep charcoal. Their trunks — if trunks they are — rise in slow spirals, sometimes branching into archways, sometimes narrowing into columns that support vaulted ceilings forty meters overhead. The vaults are living canopies, dense lattices of interlocking limbs through which no light passes (there is none to pass) and which serve as acoustic chambers, amplifying and shaping the sounds of the Hollow into something almost structured. Almost musical. Almost deliberate.

The wood is not hard the way oak is hard. It is dense and slightly yielding, like the firm flesh of a fruit. It is warm to the touch — not warm as in room-temperature, but warm as in *generating heat,* a slow metabolic warmth that seeps from every surface. In some of the older structures, the grain has compressed into patterns resembling carved script, though no one ever carved them. Growth rings accumulate at the rate of perhaps one per century, and each ring is slightly different in density, creating a material with directional resonance — tuned, by age alone, to vibrate at certain frequencies. The city hums because it is made of instruments that have been growing for millennia.

There are streets, if you want to call them that — wide, curving channels between the great trunks, their floors covered in a soft, fibrous mat of root-mass that cushions each footstep. The streets do not go in straight lines. They spiral. They meander. They sometimes end in circular plazas where five or six enormous boles converge, their bases swollen and fused, forming natural amphitheaters where the sound gathers and intensifies. Stand in one of these plazas and press your hand to the bark and you will feel it: a pulse, slow, like a heartbeat taken from a body the size of a district. Once per revolution. Once per many hours.

The buildings — homes, halls, gathering spaces, whatever they are — are not hollowed out of the wood. They are grown into shape. Walls are living tissue, a few centimeters thick, semi-translucent to no light but still organized into tissue layers that function like membranes. Corridors constrict and dilate over weeks, months, generations. Doorways slowly reshape themselves. A room you entered comfortably may, over the course of a decade, narrow to a passage requiring you to turn sideways. The wood remembers pressure. It responds to contact, growing thicker where it is touched most often, reinforcing the frames where bodies pass through. In this way the city is a palimpsest of its inhabitants — or of whatever passed through, across the uncountable years that shaped it into what it is.

There are no doors in the human sense. There are openings, bridged by strands of tissue thin as silk, which part when you press and slowly close again behind you, sealing with a faint adhesive warmth, then remodeling themselves shut over the following months.

---

The scale is disorienting. From a distance — from the fissure's mouth, looking down into the basin — the city appears small, miniature, like a model of itself. The structures are tall but narrow. The streets are fine threads. It looks like a terrarium, a diorama, a toy. But the distance is deceiving. The basin is a pocket: perhaps two kilometers across, a shallow bowl maybe three hundred meters deep, walled by theite and sealed at the top by an unbroken ceiling of fused root and mineral. Everything is close. Everything is intimate. The loudness is loud precisely because the space is small and the resonators are vast. Sound bounces off living walls and returns changed, harmonically enriched, sustained. A single groan of expanding wood can echo for minutes, layered upon itself, accumulating complexity like a chord that never resolves.

And there is so much empty space — paradoxically. The structures are enormous but sparse. Between the great trunks and halls, the basin floor is open, clear, almost bare. Pale mineral dust in thin, undisturbed layers. Root networks visible through gaps in the floor like the exposed anatomy of a buried giant. You can see across most of the basin from any single point, and the effect is of standing inside a cathedral organ — ranks of pipes receding into darkness, each one alive, each one contributing its note to the chord that has been sounding, without ceasing, for longer than anything in your experience of time.

The air smells of ozone and old paper and something sweetly vegetal that you cannot place — not rot, not flower, not sap, but something that belongs to a metabolism with no analogy in surface-world chemistry. These organisms do not photosynthesize. There is no light to photosynthesize. They draw energy from vibration. The sound passes through them and they drink it. Their roots are deep in the mineral substrate, where thermal gradients and piezoelectric effects in compressed crystal lattices generate faint electrical potentials. The wood is a transducer. It converts mechanical energy — sound, pressure, vibration — into chemical energy, and it has been doing so for so long that the system has reached equilibrium, a self-sustaining loop: the city creates the sound, the sound feeds the city, the city grows, reshaping itself, altering the sound, which alters the growth, which alters the sound.

It is alive. It is a single, distributed organism, or perhaps a colony of organisms, and it is the city, and the city is its body, and it sings because singing is how it eats.

---

There is no fauna. No insects. No birds. No scuttling things in the root-mat. When you place your ear against the trunk of one of the great boles and listen, you hear nothing that resembles an animal. You hear the slow flexion of living tissue, the creak of cellulose fibers under tension, the faint whistle of air moving through internal channels — resonant cavities formed by the organism's own growth, tuned, perhaps accidentally, perhaps not, to frequencies that feel like language.

Months. Decades. The wood thickens by fractions of a millimeter. A corridor shifts its angle by degrees. A plaza slowly, slowly fills with new growth as an opening above constricts and redirects a convection current. On the longest timescales, the Hollow changes. On the scale of a visit — a lifetime — it seems eternal. Unmoved. A monument to patience and self-sustaining complexity.

The temperature stays cool. Around you, it hovers at something you might call autumnal — the air of an early morning before anyone is awake. The metabolic warmth of the wood provides gentle local gradients, so that leaning against a trunk feels like leaning against a sun-warmed wall in a season that no longer exists. The air near the floor is cooler, heavier, mineral-laden. Near the canopy-vaults it is slightly warmer, humid, faintly sweet.

There is a room deep in the central district — if "district" is the right word for a cluster of seven enormous trunks growing in tight formation — where the acoustics are so precise that a whisper at the center is amplified to a volume that fills the entire basin. People who have entered this room (and who have come, and gone, and come again, because the fissure opens and closes and opens again on timescales no cartographer has learned to predict) report that the amplification is not merely acoustic. That the sound seems, in that room, to acquire intention. That the harmonics suggest pattern. That the groaning wood sounds like a voice just below the threshold of comprehension.

Perhaps it is. Perhaps it was, once, when the builders — the planters, the shapers, whoever first coaxed growth into geometry in this lightless hollow — were still present to listen. Perhaps the language died with them, or they dissolved into the wood, or they walked back out through the throat and sealed it behind them.

What remains is the city. What remains is the sound. What remains is the oldest living room in the world, humming to itself in the dark, patient as stone, alive as anything has ever been.
