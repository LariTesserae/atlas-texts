The thing you have to understand is that it's loud. Not loud the way a city is loud — not engines or voices or the percussive scatter of human activity — but loud the way a lung is loud when you press your ear to it. A wet, rhythmic, systemic roar that doesn't stop and doesn't modulate and has no source you can point to because the source is *everything*.

---

You enter through a mouth.

That's the only way to describe it. A wound in a wall of tissue that was not there yesterday — a sphincter-like opening ringed with pale, wet fibers that flex slightly as you pass through, responding to warmth, to pressure, to the particular frequency of a human body moving through space. The opening is about two meters across. Beyond it, the world.

Or what used to be world.

The ground — and you can barely call it ground — is a mattress of interlaced root and stem, somewhere between loam and cartilage, soft enough to sink ankle-deep with each step, firm enough to hold you. It's warm underfoot. Perpetually warm, the way a compost heap is warm, the way a fever is warm. Heat rises from below in visible columns where the moisture content is high enough to catch the light — you walk through these thermals like curtains, each one carrying the smell of green decay, of chlorophyll broken down and reassembled, of sugar and tannin and something faintly metallic, like blood.

The elevation is almost nothing. A basin, a depression, a cupped hand in the earth's palm that collects water and collects growth and collects heat until the three become indistinguishable. There is no horizon line. There is no sky, not exactly — there is *canopy*, and above that, *canopy*, and above that, *canopy still*, layer upon layer of photosynthetic architecture stacking upward on trunks the width of shipping containers, on buttress roots that rise and arc and reconnect to form secondary walls, on vines thick as bridge cables that have no visible origin and no visible destination. The light that reaches the floor is green and diffuse, filtered through so many layers of leaf and membrane that it loses all directionality. It doesn't fall. It *permeates*. You're always in a kind of soft, underwater glow, even when it's not raining, even when — especially when — it is.

It rains often. The upper canopy acts as a first atmosphere, a living sky of its own, and water cycles through it — transpiration, condensation, dripping — in a closed loop that has nothing to do with the weather above. You can stand beneath one of the great trunks and watch water bead on the underside of a leaf thirty meters overhead, grow fat, tremble, and fall through fifty meters of intervening growth to land on your shoulder, on your neck, cold for exactly half a second before the ambient warmth takes it back. The air is at saturation always. Your clothes never dry. Your skin never dries. Everything is perpetually damp, and in the warm pockets, fungal growth is visible within hours — white lace spreading across surfaces, fruiting bodies the size of fists pushing up through the mulch like small pale fists of their own.

The structures. That's what they remind you of, eventually. Not buildings, not in any sense you'd recognize, but *structures*. There are walls — raised platforms — archways — corridors. A root system splits into two major trunks and between them forms a passage four meters wide, the interior walls smooth and dark, lined with a thin mucilage that catches the light. You walk through it and the acoustics change: the roar compresses, focuses, becomes almost articulate, like speech you can't parse. You emerge into a clearing — an actual clearing, a space where the growth thins slightly, where you can see more than three meters in any direction — and the relief is so immediate it makes your knees weak. The air moves differently here. Something above your head shifts, a massive branch-construct sliding slowly sideways, closing the gap, and in twenty minutes the clearing is gone, sealed, a new wall where openness was.

This is the urban quality. Not planned. Not designed. But patterned. The growth organizes itself into densities, into nodes and pathways, into something with the *rhythm* of a city without the intention. Nodes of intense activity where stems cluster and split and merge, connected by arterial root-runs along the ground and by canopy-level bridges that sway and creak under their own weight. You want to call it a settlement. You want to call it infrastructure. But it's all *growth*, continuous and manic, and every structure you see is simultaneously being built and being demolished — a wall that solidified overnight is already splitting down its center, a corridor that was clear yesterday is now half-blocked by a surge of new shoots pale as bone, still extending, visibly extending, centimeters per minute, reaching for light that won't arrive because the canopy has already closed above it.

Everything is touching. The density is total. A root you're stepping on is fused, three meters back, into the root of something else, which is fused into a trunk, which is fused into a neighboring trunk via a graft that happened so seamlessly you can't find the join. The layering is extraordinary — at ground level, a mat of mosses and low ferns and fungal hyphae so dense it's like walking on a living sponge. At waist height, a tangle of stems and runners, some woody, some still green and succulent, crossing and recrossing, weaving a three-dimensional lattice. At head height, the understory canopy, leaves so large they block your peripheral vision, and above that, the mid-canopy, and above that, the upper canopy, and above that — if you could see it, which you can't, not directly — the emergent layer, the titans, the ones whose trunks are so massive they function as ecosystems of their own, their bark colonized by epiphytes whose root systems have burrowed inward and now live *inside* the wood, forming a second vascular network that the host tree seems to tolerate, or perhaps encourage.

There is no animal sound. This is the thing that will get to you, after the sound. There is no animal sound. No insects — or nearly none. A beetle, once, crossing the root-mat, iridescent and enormous, moving with mechanical indifference. A moth the size of your hand, once, wings beating against the still air of a corridor, gone before you could focus. That's it. The entire place — this city-sized, region-spanning, multi-layered living mass — has no fauna to speak of. It is a pure-plant system so thorough it has excluded animals entirely, or perhaps never had them. The pollination is wind. The seed dispersal is gravity and water. The ecology is closed, self-sustaining, and profoundly, unsettlingly quiet in its biology. The deafening sound is all *vegetation* — the creak of wood under impossible tension, the crack of a trunk splitting to make room for a new shoot, the susurrus of ten million leaves rubbing against each other in air currents that have no external origin, the wet percussion of water falling through dense canopy onto wet canopy onto wet ground, the low roar of a living system so massive it generates its own weather patterns, its own pressure systems, its own acoustic environment that functions like an ocean of sound in which you are submerged, held, unable to surface into silence.

And then there is the *other* thing.

You notice it first as spatial confusion. You walk in a straight line for what should be fifty meters and arrive at a place you've already been, a root-arch you passed twenty minutes ago, except now it's different — the proportions are wrong, the arch is lower, the texture of the bark is younger, and the light filtering through has a slightly different quality, more amber, more diffuse. You backtrack. The passage you walked through is gone. There is a wall of stems where a corridor was. You turn another direction and find the corridor again, but it faces east now instead of west, and it's longer than it should be, and the far end opens onto a space that shouldn't exist — a domed chamber, vaulted, the ceiling a lattice of interwoven branches through which light falls in columns like light through the windows of a cathedral, except there are no windows and no walls and no dome, just growth, continuous growth, but the *impression* of architecture is so strong that your mind insists on it, insists on intention, on design, on an *architect* that does not exist.

Distances don't hold. You can see something that should be close — another root-arch, a gap in the understory, a clearing — and walk toward it for what feels like an hour and arrive no closer than when you started. Or turn around and find that what was behind you is now ahead of you, the landscape folded like fabric. Gravity, in certain places, feels negotiable — not absent, but *discussed*. You stand on a surface that angles upward past forty-five degrees and your body says *flat*, the fluid in your inner ear says *flat*, and the thing pulling at your boots says no such thing. The plants grow in every direction, including sideways, including inverted, including in spirals that tighten as they climb and seem to generate their own centripetal force.

Time is uneven. You sit in a corridor for what feels like minutes and when you walk on you find that a structure that was sapling-thin an hour ago is now mature, thick-barked, leafed out completely, its root system having restructured the ground beneath you while you sat still. You watch a wall of green and see it move — not sway, not grow slowly, but *move*, bulging outward, a bulge traveling along its face like a pulse, and where the bulge passes, flowers open — white, enormous, face-sized — blooming in sequence down the wall and wilting before the bulge reaches the end, completing a full life cycle in the space of thirty seconds. The sound changes with this motion, rises in pitch, becomes almost tonal, almost structured, and you realize with a lurch of vertigo that the place is *responding* to itself, that the growth and the sound and the spatial instability are not separate phenomena but a single phenomenon expressing itself in multiple registers simultaneously.

There is a logic here. You feel it like a pressure behind your eyes. The place is not random. It is *hyper-ordered* — ordered at a scale and speed and density that human perception can only process as chaos. The architecture means something. The spatial folds mean something. The growth patterns, impossible as they are, follow rules — just rules that don't correspond to anything you've been taught about the world. The wood grain spirals in directions that suggest the trees are reading something in the soil, or the air, or some gradient of light or gravity that doesn't exist in your physics. The fungal networks underfoot pulse with bioluminescence too faint to see directly but visible in your peripheral vision — a flickering at the edge of your sight that vanishes when you look at it, as though the light exists in a frequency that human attention cannot quite hold.

You keep walking. The ground slopes gently downward. The sound builds. The air thickens until it feels like breathing through wet cloth. A corridor opens ahead — impossibly tall, the walls rising out of sight, lined with vertical grooves that might be structural or might be script, might be channels for water or channels for something else, something that moves through them in pulses you can feel as pressure changes against your skin. The corridor hums. The hum has a pitch, and the pitch has a rhythm, and the rhythm has a syntax, and standing at its entrance you understand, with a certainty that is not thought but is *felt*, like nausea, like awe, that you are not in a place.

You are inside something that is in the process of becoming a place. It has not finished. It will not finish. The walls are still rising. The corridors are still extending. The roots are still gripping into soil that is still forming from the decomposition of material that grew only hours ago, and the whole vast, warm, dripping, roaring structure is reaching — slowly, ceaselessly, with the patience and the violence of something that is alive and does not know it is alive and does not need to know — toward a completion that will never come.

You step inside. The mouth closes behind you — gently, almost tenderly — and the sound swallows you whole.
