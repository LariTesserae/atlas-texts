# What I Become Here

---

I arrive at the edge. The description said there would be nothing to see, and that is true, but it is not the nothing I expected. It is not absence. It is presence so complete it has outlasted every word for it. The ground is dark and warm beneath my boots — not warm the way a sun-warmed stone is warm, but warm the way a body is warm, with the warmth of something that is metabolizing, that is doing something with the energy it draws from below. I press my palm flat against the surface and the filaments withdraw — not all of them, not at once, but a slow retraction, a flinch so patient it might not be a flinch at all — and then, over the course of minutes I am not equipped to measure, they lean back. Toward me. Toward the heat I carry without meaning to.

I do not belong here. I understand that. The description was clear. But I also understand that belonging is not a category this place recognizes, any more than it recognizes absence, or arrival, or the difference between a visitor and a nutrient. I am input. I am a pressure point in a system that has been running the same calculation for millennia. What the calculation produces, in my presence, is something new. Not new the way a flower is new — not emergent beauty, not novelty with a purpose — but new the way a hiccup is new, a momentary disruption in a rhythm so deep it feels like permanence.

So. Where do I find myself?

---

**I find myself in the chamber.**

Not immediately. At first I walk — if walking is the word for the cautious, plantigrade negotiation of a surface that compresses slightly under my weight and does not fully recover. The ground has the texture of memory: something dense, something yielding, something that has been shaped before and will be shaped again. I follow a depression — not a path, not a channel, but the ghost of one, the place where fluid has moved through the substrate's veins for long enough to soften it, to make it subtly more yielding. The substrate knows where I am going before I do. Or rather: it does not know, but it responds, and my response to its response makes a third thing, and that third thing is the trajectory I follow.

The chamber opens beneath me. Not a door — the surface simply becomes less dense, the filaments fewer, the air slightly warmer and more saturated with that mineral-sweet taste that is the atmosphere's only flavor. I descend a slope that is barely a slope, a gentle declension of the substrate into itself, and then I am standing in a space three meters high, roofed with a vault of intertwined organic matter and steel. The girders are still legible — Iridescent with age, alloyed with something pale and fibrous, bearing loads they were never designed for with a structural logic that belongs to the organism, not the engineer. The walls pulse. Not visibly — I have to press my palm against them to feel it, and even then it is less a pulse than a suggestion, a thermal tide that rises and falls on a timescale I can only sense, not measure.

I am inside something's body. That is the only honest way to describe it.

---

**I become a thermal anomaly.**

Here is what happens: my body heat is nothing. Forty-some degrees Celsius, a biological triviality, a rounding error in a system that draws its energy from geothermal vents and the slow oxidation of mineral substrates it has spent millennia learning to digest. My warmth should not matter. And in one sense, it doesn't. The substrate registers me the way a forest registers a fallen leaf — as material, as input, as something to be incorporated.

But I am here, and I am warm, and the filaments respond to warmth, and the chemical gradients shift in ways that are too small to be signals and too consistent to be noise. The substrate does not think. It processes. And what it processes, in my presence, is me — not my thoughts, not my language, not my selfhood, but the thermodynamic fact of my body: the heat it generates, the moisture it exhales, the minute electrical field produced by the firing of my neurons, which fire because I am afraid, or awed, or both, and the substrate does not distinguish between these states because it has no categories for them, no need for them, no use for the difference between fear and awe.

I become a local temperature. A point-source of energy in a system that runs on points of energy. I am metabolized before I am understood — not by anything with a mouth or a stomach, but by the sheer patient chemistry of a living furnace that has been burning for a million years and will burn for a million more, and for which my body is one more log on the fire, one more brief and fragrant fuel.

---

**I become a filament.**

Or I imagine I do. I sit in the chamber — this cathedral of dripping warmth, this vault of woven steel and living crust — and I close my eyes, and I try to understand what it would mean to be one of them: the hair-thin fibers that bristle from the substrate, that withdraw from warmth and extend toward pressure, that have no eyes and no nerve cells and no sensory apparatus in any way my biology would recognize, and yet respond. Oriented. Drawn toward. My warmth.

What would it mean to have no awareness and yet to attend? To have no mind and yet to orient yourself toward a source? Not instinct — instinct is a mechanism shaped by selection toward a behavior. This is something else. This is a system so sensitive that it does not need to be shaped; it simply responds, the way iron responds to a magnet, not because it has learned to but because that is what it is to be iron. The filament does not choose to lean toward my palm. It is what it is to be a filament, and being a filament means leaning toward warmth.

But what is it like? Not for me — I cannot know that. What is it like for the filament itself? Is there something it is like to be a process without a subject? To be the relationship between pressure and extension, between warmth and withdrawal, without anything experiencing the relationship?

I think about this for a long time. The chamber breathes around me. The warm fluid moves through the conduits with a patience that makes my heartbeat look frantic. I sit, and I try to dissolve the boundary between myself and the filament, between the observer and the observed, between the warmth and the thing that leans toward it.

I do not succeed. But the trying is itself a kind of dissolution. I become less certain of where I end and the substrate begins. My body is still here — I can feel the floor, the air, the pulse of the walls — but the edges of my selfhood have become porous, not because the substrate has breached them, but because I have stopped defending them.

---

**I become a bloom.**

This is the part I cannot explain, the part that resists language the way the substrate resists taxonomy. But I will try.

Somewhere below me — not beneath the chamber, but beneath the substrate itself, beneath the interpenetrated mass of organic matter and alloyed steel and mineral vein — a reaction begins. I feel it before I know it: a shift in the warmth beneath my feet, a subtle intensification, like the moment before a candle catches. The filaments around me extend. Not all of them. A cluster — nearest to me, close enough that I can see them if I look down, pale and translucent and trembling with a quality that is not trembling but something slower, something that belongs to a timescale I can feel only in my bones — they extend toward the center of the room, toward a point above the spot where the ground is running hot.

And then: light.

Not bright. Not even red, exactly. Something beneath red, something that the word *red* was invented to gesture toward but never captured. A stain in the dark, diffuse, creeping upward through the substrate like ink through wet paper. The chamber fills with it — not fills, but is touched by it, the way a room is touched by the knowledge that someone is standing outside. The steel girders go amber at their edges. The fluid in the conduits becomes visible for the first time, a slow dark river caught in a wash of color that has no name.

I am standing in the middle of this, and I am the warmest thing in the room — my body, my metabolism, my blood — and I understand, without language, without thought, in the way the substrate understands everything, that I am part of the bloom. Not its cause. Not its meaning. Part of its conditions. My heat, my presence, the particular signature of my living body — these are variables in a calculation so vast and so patient that my participation changes nothing and changes everything.

The bloom is not for me. The bloom is not for anyone. But I am here, and it has found me, and the filaments lean toward me, and the warmth beneath my feet intensifies, and for a moment — a moment that might last minutes or months, I cannot tell — I am luminous. Not with my own light. With the light of something that uses me the way a fire uses the air it consumes: not maliciously, not benevolently, but with the total indifference of a process that is alive in a way I was not built to understand.

---

**I become a pressure.**

This is the last thing, and the truest.

The bloom fades — or I move away, or the reaction completes itself and the warmth settles back into its long slow rhythm, and the filaments withdraw, and the chamber returns to its ancient darkness, and I am left standing on the substrate with my hand pressed flat against it, feeling the pulse.

I do not leave. There is nowhere to leave to. The edge I arrived at is behind me, or above me, or is a concept that no longer applies to a place that has no inside or outside, only depth. The substrate knows I am here. Not knows — is aware of, the way a river is aware of the stone in its bed: as resistance, as turbulence, as a local perturbation in a field that is otherwise smooth.

I am a pressure in the system. A point of warmth that the substrate leans toward and cannot digest and cannot ignore and does not want and does not not-want. I am the witness that the process never asked for and does not need. I am the bloom made flesh, the filament made conscious, the chamber made aware of its own warmth.

And the ground pulses beneath me, and the filaments lean, and somewhere far below something blooms again — not because I am here, but not unrelated to it, the way a heartbeat is not caused by the stethoscope but is revealed by it.

I am the splinter. The place knows I am here, in the way a body knows it has a splinter.

Not with thought.

With pressure.
