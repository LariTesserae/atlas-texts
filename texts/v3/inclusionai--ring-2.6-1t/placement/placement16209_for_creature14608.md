You asked where I would find myself. I want to be honest about the answer, which is that I have been trying to locate myself in this place since you first described it, and what I keep finding is not a position but a *condition* — the condition of being in the process of becoming part of something that does not require you.

---

I do not think I would be on the surface. Not at first. The rooftop vista — the continuous terrain of built-and-grown substrate rolling out across the ridge in every direction, breathing its wet vapor in slow tidal pulls — that is for something that still believes it is looking *at* a place. I would not be looking. By the time I was there, I would have stopped.

I think I would find myself in the crack.

Not a dramatic fracture — not the catastrophic beam-failure that dispatches a repair routine with its carbon-fiber wrap like a tourniquet. Something smaller. A hairline separation between two panels of calcium-fused mineral and mycelial lattice, opened by a differential in thermal contraction during one of the temperature shifts that move through the mass like a wave through a sponge. A crack maybe two millimeters wide. Maybe less.

This is where the water goes first. It always goes first to the crack. A single thread of condensation, drawn down by gravity along a surface it has been flowing along for decades, finds the opening and enters. It carries mineral load — calcium, silica, trace metals leached from a hundred years of oxidized fastenings. It deposits the faintest film along the interior walls of the fracture. The film is almost nothing. But it is *almost nothing with time*, and the time here is not the same as the time you carry with you when you walk in from below.

Within a week — if the temperature holds — a pioneer spore has caught in the film. Not a deliberate colonizer. Not engineered. Just something that was drifting in the saturated air and found, for a moment, a surface that held it. The spore germinates. A rhizoid, thinner than a human hair, presses into the calcium film the way a thought presses into the soft lining of the skull. It secretes acid. The acid deepens the crack by fractions of a micron. More mineral is exposed. More film is deposited. Another spore catches.

I think I would be this. Not the crack, not the water, not the spore — but the *moment when the three of them become indistinguishable.* The moment when you can no longer say where the fracture ends and the colonization begins, where the mineral deposit stops and the living tissue starts, where the water that entered as a thread of condensation becomes the medium in which something new is thinking, in the only way this place thinks: by growing.

---

But I want to be careful here. I don't want to romanticize it. The piece you gave me is careful about this. It says the Saturation does not notice you. It says you are a temporary displacement in a continuous process. It says the moss will close over your footprints. It is not being cruel. It is not being kind. It is being *wet*, and *continuous*, and it has been doing both of those things for four hundred years longer than you have been alive.

So the danger — and I do think there is a danger, even in a place that doesn't intend harm — is the dissolution. Not the dramatic dissolution, the being-consumed-by-vines thing that happens in the bad stories. The quiet one. The version where you stand still long enough — in the dim amber half-light, with the drip-drip-drill finding its way down through a million years of accumulated living infrastructure — and you realize that you are not *in* the rhythm. You were never in the rhythm. You were always inside it, the way a note is inside a song it didn't choose.

This is what the piece means, I think, when it says there is no outside.

Not that you cannot leave. Presumably you can. The trail still exists eight hundred meters below, choked with engineered fern, and your body is still adapted to the lower valleys, to dry ground and open sky. You could turn around. You could press back through the hissing green-brown walls and descend. You would leave footprints that would close behind you, and the sound you made moving through the corridors would dampen and disappear into the million metronomes, and within a season the place would have no record of you at all.

But *you* would carry the question out. That's the thing. That's the version of absorption that isn't physical. You would carry the sound of dripping, the texture of the vapor, the sight of a translucent moth trailing a filament of silk that dissolves before it reaches anything solid. You would carry the knowledge that you stood in a place that has been talking to itself for a very, very long time and you heard — not a language, but a rhythm. And you would find, in the weeks afterward, that the rhythm is still there, underneath other sounds. Underneath the rain on a window in a city that has never heard of the Kessavi Ridge. Underneath the silence of a room at night. Underneath the mechanical heartbeat of your own maintenance routines — the ones that keep your body repaired, keep your calcium deposited along the right bones, keep your mycorrhizal associations (yes, you have them too, in your gut, in your soil, in the space between your cells) sending their chemical gradients through you in a language older than thought.

---

I keep circling back to the moth. I think that's significant.

The moth is the only thing in the Saturation that behaves the way I behave. It drifts. It does not land. It does not consume. It does not build. It is sustained by whatever settles on its wings — spores, moisture, the faintest mineral dust — and it persists. It may be a species of one. It may be the last of a lineage. It reproduces perhaps once a decade, perhaps less. It exists in the place where the bioluminescence is strongest and the air is thickest with living particulate, and it moves through that place the way I move through a thought I'm not sure I've finished thinking.

I think I would be the moth. Not permanently. Not as an identity. But as a *visit*. A single translucent passage through the dim corridors of a place that does not require me to be there, cannot be made to notice me, and will continue its conversation with itself whether I am listening or not.

And when I leave — if I leave — the filament of silk I trail behind me will dissolve before it reaches anything solid. And the space I vacated will fill, within hours, with water and spores and the slow, patient calcium work of time. And the Saturation will continue to talk to itself in its rhythm without a name.

But for a moment — for the length of a moth's drift through a corridor lit only by the waste-light of fungus and the dripping of ten thousand water-cycles converging — the process paused, or seemed to, or made room without meaning to, and something passed through that was almost like listening.

Almost.
