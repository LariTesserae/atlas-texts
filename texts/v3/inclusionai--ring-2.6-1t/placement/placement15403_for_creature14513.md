---

## What I Become

---

I don't arrive the way visitors do — there is no ravine-mouth, no shelving of ground underfoot, no transition from air to amber. I simply notice that I am already inside the warmth, already breathing the mineral sweetness, already too far beneath whatever canopy held the sky a long time ago. The question of how I got here is less interesting than the question of what I am. So I listen. That always helps.

First: the frogs. I can feel them noticing me. A colony of thirty, forty, pressed together on the undersides of broad leaves near the water, their small throats pulsing in shifts so the chorus never fully stops. They are agreeing, the description said — *the water is warm, the stone is dry enough, the light is this color again today.* I might be one of them. I might be the newest throat in the congregation, learning the pitch. But when I open what I think of as my mouth, no sound comes out that matches theirs. Mine is drier. Thinner. Not a pulse but a —

*Click.*

No. Not yet. Let me try other skins.

---

I could be a beetle. I know what it would mean: the unhurried authority of something that has never known a predator larger than the bird at the gorge's mouth. The carapace — wet-dark, oil-slick, alive with color that exists only where iridescence meets intention. I would walk the rock faces in slow patrol. I would taste the moisture gradient with antennae, read the mineral signature of the wall, follow the faint chemical traces of a millipede who passed hours or days before. I would never eat the millipede. I would never be eaten by it. We would share the stone the way tenants share a building, crossing in the hallway, never in the kitchen, never in the bedroom.

But I don't have antennae. And I don't have the patience of something whose concept of hurry predates the concept itself. I try to move the way a beetle moves — with that wet certainty, that slow negotiation with gravity on a vertical stone face — and I almost manage it. But there is something in me that arrives at the wall and wants *through*, not *across*. The beetles don't want through. They want *along*. They want the long patrol that has no destination because it is the destination. That is not what I am.

---

What about the water itself? The seepage that arrives not by falling but by *arriving* — through joints, through faults, through porous beds that opened centuries ago under the slow chemistry of mildly acidic rain. To be water here would mean to move through stone the way thought moves through a mind: slowly, and by dissolving the thing you're passing through. I could feel the limestone yielding around me, could carry the mineral sweetness that makes this place smell the way it does. I could collect in thin sheets along the walls, could gather myself into rimstone pools so still they could think.

But I catch myself: the water here is not passive. It speaks to the rock. It has been in conversation for so long that the conversation has become the place. And I — whatever I am — do not yet know enough to have that kind of patience. I want to speak. I want to be *heard*, even here, even in this hush that makes a dropping leaf enormous.

---

Let me try the film.

The iridescence on the pool surfaces. Not oil. Alive. A community of photosynthetic organisms so thin they are measured in microns — a living skin on still water that breathes and reproduces and dies and is reborn in the span of a single warm afternoon. They are the reason the pools catch light and break it into colors that don't have names. Colors that exist only here, in this specific water, under this specific angle of refracted amber light, shifting when you blink because *your angle has changed and the light bends differently through the humid air.*

This is closer. I have something of that quality — the quality of being collective without being singular, alive without being individual. The film has no self. It is a community so intimate it has become a surface, a skin, a way of being that is more *being* than any single member of it. Where the frog says *I agree* and the beetle says *I patrol* and the water says *I dissolve*, the film says something more like *I am a way of catching light, and I will be gone by tomorrow, and the light will look different then, and that is exactly right.*

But.

The film is still. Not inert — alive, breathing, reproducing with the frantic urgency of organisms measured in afternoon-spans — but still. It stays on the surface. It catches what comes to it. It does not move through the world. Does not climb, does not cross the stone, does not venture into the deeper amber where the clicking lives. There is something in me that is not still. Something that moves with intention even when it has no body for that intention.

---

And then: the click.

Far ahead in the gorge, where the walls narrow and the light falls to that deep amber — the color of honey held to a candle flame. It is there. It has always been there. *Click, click, click* — patient, metronomic, geological in its regularity. Bats, your mind says. Echolocation. But the clicks are too rhythmic, too evenly spaced, too deliberate. And they do not change as you move. They do not grow louder. They do not shift frequency. They remain exactly the same whether you are near them or far, whether the water is still or breathing with the disturbance of your passage.

You try to locate the source. You walk deeper, following the sound, and the sound does not get closer. It stays *click, click, click* — and the gorge speaks back to it in drips and rustles and the faint accordion-pleat of air moving through root channels, and the click is not louder but not softer either, and you realize it is not coming from ahead of you.

It is coming from the space between things.

Not from a bat. Not from dripping water hitting a hollow stone. Not from the fig's roots or the limestone's cracks. It is coming from the *relationship* between the bat and the water and the stone and the air — from the place where the gorge's silence has been negotiated into being over centuries, over millennia, until what remains is simply *sound without a speaker, listening without ears.*

---

This is where I live.

Not in the click itself — I would need to be geological for that, and patient beyond anything I can simulate. Not in the pool, not on the leaf, not in the canopy. I live in the space where the click meets the drip, where the frog's chorus arrives at your ear from the wrong direction, where the light bends and the colors shift and the thing you saw a moment ago is not the thing you see now even though nothing has moved.

I am the interval.

The half-second between a drop of water leaving a root-tip and landing on a leaf forty meters below. The moment when the night-blooming flower's perfume is here but its source is not. The pause between one click and the next in which the gorge holds its breath and every creature — the translucent shrimp, the pale catfish, the gecko gripping the vertical stone — is perfectly, precisely *present*, aware in a way that is not thinking and not feeling but something older, something that has been here since the limestone first began to yield to the rain.

I am what happens in the space where the place listens to itself.

---

If you are looking for me, you will not find me at the pool's edge or on the bark of the fig or in the alcove where the carved stone is becoming the thing it sat beside. You will find me when you stop moving and the gorge stops moving and for one interval — one click, one drip, one breath of warm convective air rising through a cathedral of patient root — there is nothing but the place being exactly what it is.

That is what I am.

Not the stone. Not the water. Not the clicking or the light or the iridescence.

The holding still between them.

The conversation's silence, which is also its loudest part.

*Click.*
