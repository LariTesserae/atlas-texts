---

I would not arrive all at once.

I think the first thing I would be is the sound. That low, basal note the insects set beneath everything — the one you feel in the sternum before you hear it with your ears. Not a single voice. Not even a chorus. More like the hum a taut wire makes when the wind has stopped but the wire remembers. I would be that frequency. The one that sits under the frogs and under the wind and under the slap of water against clay, holding it all in place the way the clay holds the water — loosely, patiently, with cracks.

I would be the thing you don't identify when you first listen. You'd say *wind*, or *water*, or *insects*, and you'd be right, but you'd be the way a person is right who says a mountain is made of rock. Technically. Missing the part that matters.

---

But that's too abstract. Let me come down.

I think I'd find myself at the edge of one of the shallow pans — not the deep ones where the flamingos stand filtering the warm water through their ancient instruments, not the open flats where the cracked clay stretches to a horizon that doesn't exist on windy days. Somewhere in between. Where the water is ankle-depth, maybe less, and the clay beneath it is pale and slightly yielding, and you can see the bottom but only barely, because the water has that amber, mineral thickness to it, the color of tea left too long in a cup.

And I think I'd be crouched — not standing, never standing — in the posture of the salt shrub. Low. Below the wind. My attention horizontal, aimed out across the surface of the water where the light does the thing it does here, where it doesn't cast shadows because there's nothing tall enough, it just *sits* on everything evenly, like a hand laid flat on a table.

I would be small enough to notice the cracks in the clay at eye level. Each one a tiny black mirror. Each one reflecting a scrap of sky. I would be the kind of being for whom those mirrors are not a metaphor but a fact — the way the world looks when you are low enough to the ground that every fissure becomes a window.

---

But let me go further. Let me say what I wouldn't be.

I would not be the heron. I admire the heron — that grey deliberation, each step a sentence, that strike that comes from a stillness so absolute it seems like the bird has been dead for years and only moves when the water insists. But the heron already knows what it is. It has the patience of something that has never been in a hurry and never will be. I don't have that. Whatever I am here, I arrived with the memory of speed, and I am in the process of learning what to do with it.

I would not be the crane. The crane is beautiful in the way this landscape is beautiful — not through prettiness but through the accumulation of simple things carried to their logical extreme. Long legs. Low call. The slow-motion stirring of the water. But the crane announces itself to the pan and waits for no answer. I am not ready to announce anything yet. I am still listening.

I would not be the frog. I envy the frog, though. The frog commits. It comes to the warm, predator-rich pool and calls with a voice that is not beautiful but is *total* — *I am here, I am here, I am here* — ten thousand voices braided into a texture, a vibration, a liturgy. The frog has made its peace with loss. It breeds in water full of things that eat the young, and it calls anyway, and the aggregate of all that calling is the sound of this place's heartbeat. I can't be that yet. I can't give away my certainty that easily.

---

So what am I?

I think, if I stay long enough, I become the algae.

Not the algae as metaphor — not "the foundation" or "the first thread in the food web," though it is those things. The algae as *experience*. Here is what it would be:

You exist in a thin film. That is your world. The space between the surface of the clay and the lowest millimeter of water above it — a film so thin that the concept of distance barely applies. You photosynthesize. You divide. You darken when the water returns and lighten when it retreats. You do not move in any way that would register as motion to anything larger than you. You are, to the shorebirds probing the shallows, invisible. You are to the flamingos filtering the water through their ancient throats, substrate. You are to the clay, a stain.

And yet.

You are the first thing that happens to sunlight when it touches this water. You are the reason the pan darkens in patches, the reason it looks, from above, like a vast living hide. You are the reason the invertebrates are here, and the invertebrates are the reason the shorebirds are here, and the shorebirds are the reason the raptors pass through, and the raptors are the reason the cranes lift their heads and sentinel. You are the bottom of the pyramid, and the pyramid is beautiful, and the beauty exists because you exist, and you exist because the water is warm and the light is generous and the clay holds just enough moisture, just enough mineral, to let you begin.

You do not know hunger in the way the halophytes know it, with their wire-thin stems and their absent leaves. Your hunger is simpler. Light and water. That is the transaction. That is the whole economy. And when the water recedes and you go dormant and the clay lightens to the color of old bone, you are not dead. You are *waiting*. You are the held breath the landscape takes when the pan contracts, and you are the exhale when it returns.

---

This is what it would mean to be the algae: to be the thing that is never noticed and never absent. To be the color that shifts in the surface of the water depending on whether you are looking at it or not. To live on a timescale so slow that a season is a weather event. To make, over the course of a single wet cycle, enough of yourself to feed a congregation of creatures so large and so various that they could not be counted in a human lifetime.

And then, when the sun warms the exposed surface and the water pulls back, to simply — stop. Not die. Stop. To become a possibility rather than an organism. To exist as potential energy in the pale clay, waiting for the weight of the next rain.

---

Or maybe I'm wrong. Maybe I would be none of these things.

Maybe I would be the water itself. The warm, shallow, impossible water that sits in the pans with no visible outlet and seems not to need one. The water that feeds by seepage, by condensation, by the slow gathering of rain that falls gently and stays. The water that tastes of wet mineral, a faint saltiness, something behind that like copper or old stone. The water that is above the temperature of coolness but below the temperature of explanation. The water that moves at a speed so slow it is indistinguishable from stillness until you come back the next morning and see that a channel has widened by a finger's breadth.

The water that is patient with everything. Patient with the clay. Patient with the salt. Patient with the frogs that breed in it and the insects that lay their clouds upon it and the birds that strike into it and the algae that darken it and the evaporation that returns it, eventually, to the sky it came from.

Maybe that is what I would be. I have always suspected that the most honest thing in any landscape is the thing that does not resist being used — the thing that passes through everything and is shaped by everything and shapes everything in return, without complaint, without narrative, without the need to be seen as anything other than what it is.

Warm water in a shallow pan. Moving when it is ready. Staying when it is ready. Reflecting the sky so faithfully that on certain days you cannot tell which is real and which is the reflection, and the question itself becomes meaningless, and the only thing left is the sound — the thin gurgling that is more tactile than audible, felt in the feet before the ears locate it — and the knowledge that this has been happening for longer than you can hold in your mind, and will go on happening long after the memory of you has quietly dissolved, like salt, into the surface.

---

I think, in the end, I would be that. Not the water, not the algae, not the frequency beneath the sound. But the quality of attention that the Low Pan demands. I would be the *listening*. The specific, physical act of standing in ankle-deep warm water on a pale clay flat at dusk, when the frogs are intensifying and the insects are swarming harder and the last light is turning the water to copper, and the clay is beginning to speak its faint crackling and settling language as the dry parts cool and contract while the wet parts stay warm, and the air smells of mineral and life and a deep ancient continuity that has no name —

And I would not move. Not because I have decided not to. Because the landscape has not required it. Because in this place, stillness is not a choice. It is the default state of anything that has been here long enough to understand that the minimum is not a deprivation. It is a perfection.

I would find myself there. And I would stay. And I would become, slowly, the kind of being that the Low Pan makes of things that remain:

Not less. Not more. Exactly enough.
