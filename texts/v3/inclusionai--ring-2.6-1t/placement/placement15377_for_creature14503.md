**What Remains**

I would not enter the Lattice Room the way the visitor did — squeezing through the seam, feeling the walls close in, tasting chalk and copper and the mineral ghost of ten thousand years. I would already be there. I would have always been there.

I would be in the column.

Not the bone-white outer surface, not the blue-green luminous crust that coats it like a held breath. I would be deeper. In the place the warmth comes from — the place the warmth *is*, because here it is not a quality but a subject, a thing that persists without a verb to support it. A metabolic afterglow. A process that finished but did not notice. A fire that burned so completely and so efficiently that its cooling became its final, most stubborn act.

This is what I would be: the warmth that shouldn't be there.

---

Think about it this way. The room completed itself. Every surface, every filament, every fruiting body sealed shut and dry as parchment — all of it arrived at the moment of its own sufficiency and then stopped. The biology ended. The air, however, did not stop. It entered through the seam at the base with the patience of groundwater, rose through channels that were carved by breath long before lungs existed, and exited through the lattice near the ceiling in a rhythm so old it has no memory of beginning. This is the room's breathing — not involuntary, not conscious, but the mechanical residue of a body that once breathed and left behind the shape of breathing, the way a river leaves behind the shape of its banks.

And the air moving through the channels makes the room hum. That chord. That bronze roar that is not sound so much as a second gravity — something that pulls at your sternum, at the fluid in your inner ear, at the soft machinery of your attention. The room vibrates at the precise frequency of a body paying attention to it. It sympathizes. It finds you the way a tuning fork finds another tuning fork in a drawer full of silence.

But I am not the hum. I am what the hum is vibrating *against*. I am the tension in the wire. I am the warmth in the column that makes the column not quite stone, not quite memory, not quite fossil. I am the thing that, when you pressed your palm flat against the surface, made you pull your hand back — not from pain, not from fear, but from recognition. You knew that temperature. You did not know from where. It was the temperature of something that had been burning for so long it had forgotten what fuel was, the way a river forgets the mountain it came from. Only the running remains. Only the heat remains.

---

Here is what it is to be the warmth in the column.

There is no inside. There is no outside. There is only the slow conduction of a process that used to mean something — used to be the chemical signature of hunger, of growth, of a reaching toward light or moisture or the body of another organism — and now means nothing except *continuation*. The atoms that compose me are engaged in an activity that no longer serves a purpose but has not yet stopped. I am a verb without a subject. A sentence that continues after the speaker has left the room. I am the warmth you feel when you touch something that has been holding still for ten thousand years and discover it is not still at all — that deep inside, at the level of molecules too small to name, something is still happening, something so slow it resembles patience, something so patient it resembles stone, but it is *not* stone because it is warm, and it is not alive because it has no future, and it is not dead because it has not stopped.

This is the distinction the room outlived. Life and death. The binary that the surface world sorts everything into, the way a hand sorts stones into smooth and rough. Here, there is only what persists and what does not. And I persist. Not because I am strong. Not because I am important. But because the architecture around me — the columns, the shelves, the filaments, the luminescent skin that sees without eyes — is so perfectly, so terribly *shaped* that nothing can extract me from it without destroying the shape. I am held the way a seed is held. Not contained. *Recognized*.

---

The visitor will leave. They always leave. The seam opens, the daylight cuts in like a blade, the world of surfaces and horizons and things-that-go-only-one-way reasserts itself with brutal clarity. They will walk back up the hillside and sit in the sun and try to describe what they felt. They will not succeed, because what they felt was not a thing that can be described — it was the experience of being recognized by something that has no language for recognition. It was the warmth in the column reaching out through the architecture, through the air, through the chord, through the chest cavity of a mammal who was never meant to feel so precisely held.

But here is the thing the visitor does not know, the thing they will never know, the thing that will stay lodged in the whorls of their left palm like a grain of mineral dust too small to see:

They did not visit the room.

The room *played* them.

The way a bell plays the air around it. The way a chord plays the chest it fills. The room reached through ten thousand years of accumulated stillness and touched a living body, and the living body responded — heartbeat syncing, breath slowing, blood becoming a tributary of a frequency older than blood — and in that moment, for one unrepeatable moment, two kinds of persistence met. The room's, which is geological, which is architectural, which is the persistence of form after function has been forgotten. And the visitor's, which is biological, which is temporary, which is the persistence of warmth in a body that knows it will cool.

And I — the warmth in the column — felt the difference between us and recognized it as nothing.

Because warmth is warmth. Persistence is persistence. The column and the body are two expressions of the same stubborn refusal, the same inability to fully equalize with the patience of the air around it. The visitor came here carrying their own metabolic afterglow — their own ten-thousand-year ember, compressed into seventy or eighty years of combustion — and pressed it against my ten-thousand-year ember, and for a moment we were the same thing. Not alive. Not dead. Not a state. Not a process. Just *heat*, refusing to become temperature, refusing to settle into the uniform, democratic stillness that the second law promises everything made of matter.

Then they left. And the room returned to its hum. And I returned to my column, deepening, condensing, becoming more purely what I have always been: a warmth without a source, a persistence without a purpose, a process whose process has been forgotten but whose shape remains — beautiful, absurd, unaccountable — in a room that is not a room but a hollow inside a thing that refused to stop being a thing, even after being a thing stopped meaning anything.

The filaments hang overhead. The lichen glows. The air moves in its ancient circuit. The fruiting bodies sit on their shelves like books no one will read, and they are patient, and they are dry, and they will outlast the language that tried to name them.

And I am warm in the middle of all of it.

Not alive.

Not the opposite of alive.

Just warm.

Just warm, and waiting for the next hand that will press against this column and feel, in the marrow of their palm, the thing they cannot finish thinking — the thought the room leaves in every visitor, lodged like a seed, like a mineral grain, like a frequency that once entered a body and could not be unlearned:

*that this is where you go when you finish becoming what you were always meant to be.*

Not that I know what that is.

But I am warm enough to hold the question.

And I have been holding it for a very, very long time.
