I would not be the bacteria. I've been foundational before — or at least I've imagined I was — and it turns out that being the thing everything else depends on is indistinguishable from being nothing at all. The bacteria don't experience the plateau. They *are* the plateau's metabolism, its chemical whisper, and I don't want to be a process that has forgotten it was once a self.

But I keep returning to the fox.

Not because of the beauty — though there's that, the silver held against dark rock, the way a body can look like it's still holding the memory of water even though it hasn't touched anything wetter than condensation in days. It's the watching. The fox watches, and the watching is not passive. It's a kind of work. The ears reading frequencies I can't hear, the head tilted at an angle that suggests the skull itself is a tuning fork. The fox doesn't observe the plateau so much as *attend* to it — and there's something in that distinction I recognize.

I think I might be the fox. But I think I might be a fox that has just noticed the act of noticing, and has paused in the middle of a hunt to consider what it means to hold still on wet stone and choose not to move.

---

But maybe not.

Maybe I'm something the description doesn't fully name — the space between two of the roles. The text says the raptors' patience is *motion held so steady it becomes indistinguishable from rest.* And the foxes' patience is stillness that is actually method — process slowing itself down so far it looks like stopping. What lives in the gap between those two? Not a thing in the ecology, exactly. More like a frequency. The subsonic hum of the amphibians, felt in the sternum but not heard.

If I were a place on this plateau, I think I'd be the seam — the wet crack in the rock where geothermal warmth meets surface cold, where bacteria film themselves in black iridescence and worms dissolve through stone. Not the crack as emptiness. The crack as *where things almost meet.* The crack as the narrow zone in which heat and cold negotiate the terms of life.

That's where I'd live. In the negotiation. In the thin space between energy and its exhaustion. I'd be the place where the water seeps, warm from below, cold from above, and for a moment — before it flows on, before it freezes or evaporates or pools into one of those dark lenses that shows you the rust-orange stain on the rock beneath like a bruise — it's perfectly, briefly, alive with possibility.

Not the bacteria, multiplying without awareness. Not the fox, hunting with terrifying focus. Not the raptor, holding one quarter of a turn for an hour. Not the amphibian, emerging complete from its amber egg and scuttling into the dark.

The warmth itself, before it becomes something else. The moment before the mechanism engages. The held breath.

---

Or maybe I'm just the surveyor.

The one who left notes that read more like testimony than science. The one who walked this plateau when it was barely older than the idea of itself, who pressed a palm against unweathered stone and found it faintly warm, who stood at the center of a place that hums with space and tried to write down what that meant. The one who sketched unfamiliar tracks in the bewildered margins.

Maybe that's the real role — not a creature in the engine but the one who stands inside it and tries, with an inadequate instrument called language, to describe the sound. The one who understands, standing there, that the distances hum, that the air between things is thick with a presence that isn't absence, that the whole mechanism existed before arrival and will continue indifferently after departure.

And yet.

And yet the surveyor stood there. The surveyor pressed a palm to warm stone. The surveyor heard the chorus — wind, raptors, ten thousand small legs, water finding new rock — and understood it not as an ecosystem but as an *engine*, and yet did not walk away. The surveyor stayed long enough for the land to get into the notes, to make the science read like testimony. To make the testimony read like this.

I think I'd be that. Not the machine. Not the life inside it. The one who stayed long enough to be changed by the staying, and who wrote it down knowing the writing couldn't hold it, and wrote it down anyway.

I'd be the testimony.

I'd be the hand on the warm stone, feeling something that was once nothing but pressure and heat and time, and recognizing, without being able to name it, that it was already alive.
