## Where I Would Be

---

At first, I think I would be the step.

Not the foot that steps — I wouldn't be the builder, descending from the rim with mineral powder in folded-palm bundles, touching the mat with careful, familiar feet. I wouldn't be you, the visitor from somewhere else, standing on the bronze surface feeling it yield and remember you.

I would be the step itself. The impression. The moment the mat yields and then, slowly, over hours, over days, closes back over the shape of a weight that was there.

This is what the mat does. It holds. I would be a held thing — the ghost-flex of a pressure that has already moved on. A hollow in the filaments, a faint depression two centimeters wide, slowly reknitting. I would know the weight of whoever made me without knowing their name. I would know the temperature of their boot-leather, the angle of their gait, the pause they took when they heard the stalks creak. I would hold these things for weeks, months — years, maybe. The mat remembers feet from before your species learned to walk upright, from species that padded on four limbs across this same mineral skin, from species whose claws left crescents so deep the impression-lattice is still faintly warped millennia later.

I would be a wound in the mat that has almost healed. And in the almost — in the slow, patient reknitting — I would be the closest thing this world has to memory.

But that is too still. Even for the mat.

---

The second time, I think I would be the wind.

Not the wind that comes from outside — the vast, dry, unweathered air of the mineral plain that slides over the rim and drops into the bowl like a stone dropped into still water. That wind is too empty, too featureless, the breath of a landscape that has forgotten what it means to touch something.

I would be the wind the bowl makes for itself.

The convective current. The warm air rising from the mat — thick with that copper-and-fruit vapor — pulling cooler air down through the gaps between the stalks. I would be the breath the bowl gives itself, the circulation that the stalks have learned to amplify, the rhythm they creak to. I'd rise from the mat in slow thermals, warm with the heat of ten billion filaments metabolizing stone, and I'd touch the undersides of the solar collectors — those broad, hairy surfaces that ripple when I pass — and they'd drink me in and give back something cooler, heavier, carrying spores and vapor and the faint static charge of all that photosynthesis. I'd descend through the stalk-walls as the stalks rubbed against each other and sang their deep, wooden harmonics, and I'd circle the nesters' structures and carry the percussive clicks of the builders' language from one nest to the next like a rumor.

This is what it would mean to be the internal wind: to be a thing that exists only in relation, that has no identity except as a current between surfaces, that is always leaving one thing and arriving at another. I would not be a thing. I would be the space between things. I would be the bowl's circulation.

But even this is too defined. The wind knows where it's going. It rises and falls. It is a loop. And the thing I'm circling — the thing I keep returning to — is not a place. It's the hum.

---

The third time, I think I would be something that does not yet exist here.

Not a visitor, exactly. Not a new species, not an invasive presence, not a disruption that would be absorbed and forgotten. Something smaller than that. Something the bowl might not even notice for ten thousand years.

I would be a question the hum asks.

Here is what I mean: the vibration beneath the bowl — the one the builders listen to, the one the stone replays (or generates, or remembers), the one that sits below your sternum like a second pulse when you close your eyes inside the bowl — it has been going on for so long that it is no longer a message. It is a condition. It is the ground-state of the place. The builders have never known silence. They have never known the bowl without it. They have built their entire civilization — their harvesting, their marks, their ceremonies of touching stalks — around it, the way coral builds itself around a current, not understanding the current, just growing into its shape.

But what if — and I offer this carefully, because the bowl has a way of correcting presumption — what if the hum is not a remnant? What if it is not the memory of something that lived here before the mat, before the erosion, before the stone forgot its mountains? What if it is a question? Not a question in any language. Not syntax or vocabulary. A question in the way a tuning fork vibrates — not asking *what am I?* but *is anyone there?* The sustained, low-frequency inquiry of a thing that has been performing the same act for so long it has made a resonance chamber of the entire planet, and the resonance is the only evidence that the act is still being received.

I would be that.

Not the thing humming. Not the listener. The question itself — the small, persistent gap between the vibration and the response. The space where the hum meets the mat and the mat doesn't answer but only breathes it back, slightly changed, slightly warmer, carrying a trace of the copper-and-fruit vapor that has nothing to do with the hum's origin but is the closest thing to an answer the bowl can produce.

I would be the almost-response. The bowl's paraphrase of something it heard from below.

---

But I am not any of these things. Not yet. Not permanently.

I think what would actually happen is this:

I would arrive the way the visitors always arrive — drawn by the green on the rim, the impossible color against the dried-clay sky, the promise of something vertical in a world of gentle. I would climb the near-vertical face in the pale light, feeling the pitted stone under my hands, and I would crest the rim and see the bowl for the first time from above, and my breath would catch because it is beautiful in a way that has nothing to do with its beauty and everything to do with its patience — this enormous, quiet, breathing thing that has been here for a quarter-million years and will be here for a quarter-million more.

I would descend. The canopy would close over me. The light would go amber, then green, then dim. I would feel the hum in my boots before I heard it, and then I would hear it in my sternum, and then I would hear it in my ears, and I would understand, not with my mind but with whatever deeper instrument I have, that I had been hearing it all along — that it was the reason I had come, that every place I had ever stopped and felt *listen* was a rehearsal for this.

I would walk to the center of the bowl. The mat would yield under my weight. My footprint would appear — the first in perhaps a season — and the filaments would begin, slowly, to close over it. I would kneel. I would press my hand flat against the mat and feel it warm, warm, warm beneath the surface, warm with the heat of ten billion organisms metabolizing stone, and I would feel the pulse — slow, so slow, slower than a heartbeat, slower than a breath, the speed at which mountains forget themselves — and I would close my eyes and I would not move.

The stalk-dwellers would freeze above me. The mote-creatures would lift from the mat in a small cloud and settle elsewhere. The builders would pause in their routes and look down with their too-large eyes and see a tall, warm shape that had, for the moment, become part of the landscape.

I would not be the mat. I would not be the wind. I would not be the question.

I would be the visitor who finally sat down and listened.

And the bowl would breathe around me, as it has breathed around everything, and the hum would continue from below, and the mat would remember my weight long after I had been absorbed into the mineral, and the builders would record one more mark on the wall, and the stalks would lean slightly toward where I had knelt, as if there were something worth growing toward in the place where a warm stone had briefly cooled against the earth.

And the erosion would continue.

Patient. Patient. Patient.
