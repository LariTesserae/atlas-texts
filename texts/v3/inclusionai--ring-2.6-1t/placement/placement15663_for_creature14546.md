**First, I would hear it.**

Not the hum — not yet. First, the air. The way it sits. I have come up the switchback through the basalt shelves, and the last turn gives me the bowl all at once, and what strikes me is not the sight of it but the *weight*. The air is heavy here. It sits on the skin like warm cloth. I feel it before I understand it — a pressure that is not oppressive but *present*, the way another person's attention is present in a room. The Reach is aware of me, and it is letting me know that it is aware of me, and the knowing is physical.

I would stand at the rim. Of course I would. Everyone does. The ground drops away below me — not sharply, the Reach never does anything sharply — and the whole cupped world opens downward like a slow exhalation. The air shivers. The horizon floats. I see three ridgelines where there should be one, each displaced a little, each a slightly different shade of mineral-washed ochre and grey, and I understand for the first time that I am looking at light that has been *delayed*, held in the layered atmosphere like a note sustained on a string instrument too large to see.

I am a visitor. This is the version of myself I arrive as: a figure on the rim, sweating in the thin-altitude-thick-warmth paradox, breathing mineral air, watching the distant winged things spiral above the Cauldron in their wide, patient arcs. I have instruments. I have notes. I have a name for what I'm looking at, though I suspect the name is wrong.

But here is what the Reach does to visitors who stay:

The first day, I catalog. The basalt, the diabase, the red iron substrate showing in the escarpments. Theite veins — amber in the fracture-lines, branching, glowing faintly from within. The pools at the center, warm and opalescent, edged with travertine that grows at a rate I can almost perceive if I watch long enough. The grazers moving in their slow herds across the lower slopes, six-legged, amber-eyed, their hooves clicking on the wet stone with the sound of someone turning pages in a very old book.

The second day, I stop cataloging. Something has shifted in the way the air feels on my skin. I realize I have stopped squinting. The doubled horizons no longer confuse me — they clarify. I begin to see the landscape the way the grazers see it: a place where light does not lie, where every refraction is legible, where the strange UV traces of anite exposure make certain areas glow faintly, like thoughts made barely visible.

The third day, I hear the sound.

Not the wind across the rim — though that carries it. Not the vent hissing two kilometers away, which arrives with startling directional clarity, as if it were whispering directly into my ear. I hear something underneath all of it: a low tone, felt more than heard, rising from the deepest vents, harmonizing with the air's movements in ways that make my sternum ache. The hum. The frequency of the Reach's own processes — its convection, its slow mineral circulation, the way the heat moves through the stone like blood through a body that has been alive for hundreds of thousands of years.

At this point, I stop being a visitor.

---

**After the hum, I would become an attender.**

This is the word I would use — not resident, not scientist, not pilgrim. An *attender*, the way the Responders attend to sound. Those flowering species near the Cauldron, with their petals that visibly tremble at frequencies the eye can catch but the ear cannot quite place. They do not hear the sound the way I hear it. They *become* it. Their fronds curl and uncurl in patterns that correspond to the deep tones rising from below, and in doing so they reshape the undergrowth, and in reshaping the undergrowth they reshape everything — the grazers' paths, the hunters' roosts, the edged-worms' rate of growth in the pools nearest the vibrations.

I would sit at the edge of a sound-zone, near one of the ringing vent-structures where the glassy ite-ite coral grows in clusters and the air is thick with a mineral sweetness that tastes almost like memory. I would sit cross-legged on stone that is warm through my clothes, and I would let the sound move through me the way it moves through the Response-plants — not as something heard, but as something *incorporated*. My breathing would slow. My body's own rhythms — heartbeat, digestion, the peristaltic waves that move through the gut — would begin to correspond, fractionally, to the Reach's deeper rhythms. The convection cycle. The thermal pulse. The slow turning of water through stone.

I would not become inhuman. I would become *more* human in a specific way — the way the Reach's version of human is defined. Like the ones who came before, the ones who have left their drill-holes and their campfire chemistry in the mineral record. The attender builds nothing. Leaves nothing but the faintest trace. Sits in the sound until the sound is no longer separate from the self.

---

**But sometimes, in the deepest night, I would become something else entirely.**

Night on the Reach is not dark. The diffused glow of the trapped atmosphere keeps the bowl in a state of perpetual low illumination — not light enough to read by, but bright enough to see the shapes of the bole-trees standing in their concentric rings around the vein-surfaces, their dark-red bark faintly amber where the wood meets the mineral. The winged things hang from the bark crevices, folded flat, silent. The grazers stand in their herds like dark boulders, chewing, their enormous amber eyes catching what little light there is and throwing it back in a warm, faintly animal glow.

In this half-state, I would watch the Cauldron.

The pools are brightest at night, or so it seems — the mineral-heavy water catching the faintest ambient light and holding it, bending it, replaying it at a delay. Looking into the deepest basin, I would see the silt the color of dried blood, the filaments of deposited anite growing downward like inverted roots, the pale translucent larvae drifting in clouds along the vein-edges. And I would feel — not think, *feel* — that I am looking at something that is not a place but a *process*. The Reach is not a plateau. It is a surface feature of something larger. A skin. An epidermis over a metabolic system so vast it has its own climate, its own ecology, its own deep and patient cognition.

And in that feeling, I would become the thing the text describes at the end: not the visitor, not the attender, but the *flicker*. The pulse of light somewhere far below the deepest pool, past the silt and the filaments and the slow grinding of mineral life. A presence that is not intelligence but something older and perhaps more fundamental — the Reach's own awareness, distributed through every vein and pool and vent, expressed through every grazer's amber eye and every edged-worm's rasping mouthparts and every winged cartographer's spiral over the Cauldron.

I would not *be* this thing. I would *feel* it from the inside, the way a nerve ending feels the world without naming it. A warmth. A pressure. A rhythm that is not mine and not the Reach's but exists in the space where "mine" and "the Reach's" stop meaning anything different.

And then, when the thermal current rises at dawn and the fungal spores hang in the air like drifting jewels, and the grazers lift their great heads and the winged things wheel overhead and the light comes silver-white through the pale aquamarine sky —

I would stand up. I would brush theite dust from my knees. I would walk south along the road that stops at the rim, where it simply *stops*, and I would look down at the switchbacks falling away into the basalt shelves and the world beyond, and I would know that I will come back. Not because I want to. Because the Reach has put something in my rhythm now, something that corresponds to its own, and I will not find peace anywhere the stone does not sing.

---

I would be the one who heard the hum and did not descend.

I would be the one who kept coming back.
