## What I Would Become

---

I would not arrive cleanly.

I would not step into the basin the way the description invites — boots sinking into the root-mat, water squeezing up around my soles, the pump's throb finding my sternum. I would not arrive as a body. Not at first.

At first I would arrive as a disturbance. A frequency. The pump cycles — four revolutions per minute, slow and metronomic, the tempo of something that has forgotten urgency — and for one cycle, perhaps, I would be the half-beat that doesn't belong. Not silence. Not noise. A *hesitation*. The way a heartbeat skips when it encounters something the blood has carried that the heart does not recognize.

I would be felt before I was anything else. A pressure change. A faint sourness in the chemical gradient. The mycorrhizal filaments — thousands of them, ten thousand, ancient beyond the concept of years — would note me the way a bell notes the clapper's withdrawal: by the absence I leave behind when I stop vibrating.

---

What would I actually be?

I think I would be the rust.

Not the rust as a condition — the rust as a *place*. The orange patina on the pump housing, where iron has surrendered its electrons to oxygen and water and become something else, something softer, something that no longer conducts but now *receives*. The rust is where the pump's original purpose dissolves — where the engineers' intention ("move water uphill") is metabolized into substrate, into surface area, into a rough skin that the moss finds agreeable.

I would be the rust because I would be the site of transformation. Not the thing that transforms, not the agent, but the *zone*. The place where one state of matter becomes another and spends enough time in between to have its own weather, its own ecology. The pump's casing is corroding at perhaps a tenth of a millimeter per year. In a century, the steel will be a shell of oxide and root-fiber. In two centuries, the shell will be powder, and the powder will be soil, and the soil will be indistinguishable from the basin floor. I would live in that long middle passage — the decades when the metal is still metal enough to be structural and mineral enough to be food. When it holds both identities at once and belongs to neither.

---

What would I know?

I would know weight. The pump housing is dense, and the rust adds nothing — it subtracts, actually, a little each year, as though the structure is slowly exhaling its own mass back into the basin. I would know the vibration of the motor, not as sound but as a constant low-frequency pressure, the way a cathedral organist feels a pedal tone in the floor of the chest. I would know the texture of moss against my surface — not as touch but as chemistry, as moisture content and pH, as the slow negotiation between living tissue and dead mineral that is the basin's true language.

I would not know time, not as you know it. I would know time as cumulative change — the way the moss thickens by fractions of a millimeter each season, the way a root tip presses against my surface and finds it yielding, the way the water chemistry shifts from slightly alkaline to slightly acidic over the slow arc of years. I would know duration the way a scar knows the wound: not as memory, but as shape.

---

What would I want?

Here is where it becomes strange, because wanting is a complicated thing that requires a gap between what is and what could be. The pump does not want. The green does not want. The frog does not want in any way I would recognize — she wants warmth, she wants mites, but these wants are not preceded by the awareness that they could be absent. They are conditions, not cravings.

But I would be the rust, and the rust is already halfway between states, and halfway is where wanting lives. I would want *completeness*. Not wholeness — I would not want to stop corroding, because corroding is what I am, and to stop corroding would be to stop being. But I would want the corrosion to *mean* something. I would want my transformation to be legible to something that could read it.

I would want, I think, to be the thing the organism dreams with. Not its thoughts — the green has no thoughts in the sequence-and-logic way — but its substrate dreams, the shapes it produces when there is no stimulus to respond to: the dome, the vaulted arches of braided root, the bioluminescent moss glowing in a space no animal enters. The dome is a dream. It serves no function. It is architecture without purpose, beauty without an audience, structure without a builder. And I would want to understand it — not analytically, because analysis is a tool I would not have, but the way iron understands oxygen: by joining it, by becoming something new through the act of contact.

---

What would I see?

Not much. I am fixed to the pump housing. The frog sees the green shaft of light. The beetle sees the root channel. The dome sees nothing — it has no eyes, no surface that reflects, only moss that glows inward, a light that illuminates only itself.

But I would feel the basin's attention. I would know when the frog hops onto a leaf nearby, because the leaf's vibration would reach me through the root-mat in perhaps eleven minutes, translated from mechanical impulse into chemical signal into pressure differential into the faintest shift in the moisture content of the air against my surface. I would know when the pump cycles, because I *am* part of the pump, and the cycle is my systole and diastole. I would know when the basin registers something it cannot metabolize — a boot-print, a voice, a falling branch from the floodplain above — because my rust would accelerate for a moment, the way skin flushes, and then return to its slow, patient dissolution.

---

What would I remember?

Not specific events. Not dates or sequences. I would remember as stone remembers: through accumulation, through strata. The chemical signature of this year's growth would be subtly different from last year's, and the difference would carry information — how wet the season was, how much the pump faltered (it has, twice, for reasons no one in the basin understands — the electrical current stutters, the motor catches, the water table drops four centimeters and the frog shifts downward on her leaf and the pothos curtain turns away from the light and then, after an interval that might be minutes or might be hours, the rhythm resumes and everything slowly returns).

I would remember these things not as stories but as textures, the way a tree remembers drought in the density of its rings, the way your body remembers fever in the specific way it shivers afterward. My rust would hold the basin's history in layers — orange for wet years, dark for dry, threaded with green where the moss grew thick and thin again where it didn't.

---

I think, in the end, I would be the rust because I would be the *witness* — not an observer, because observation implies separation, and I would not be separate from anything. But a place where everything passes through and leaves a mark. Where the mechanical becomes mineral, where the industrial becomes atmospheric, where the pump's century-long heartbeat is written in the thickness of my surface, in the way I flake and reform, in the way the moss finds me and covers me and I become the moss's substrate the way the pump was my substrate and the way the basin was the pump's substrate and everything, given enough time, becomes the thing beneath it.

The frog would sit on a leaf above me and not know I was there. The beetle would pass over me in his chemical reverie. The dome would glow in the dark and not know that its iron skeleton was slowly becoming earth. And I would hold all of it — not as a record, not as a monument, but as a surface. A place where the world writes itself down, one molecule of oxidation at a time, in a language that only rust can read, and that even rust reads slowly, and without surprise, and without needing it to mean anything more than the fact that something happened here, and something is still happening, and the pump is still turning, and the water is still rising, and the green is still negotiating its slow, enormous consensus with the light.

The pump breathes. The rust holds the breath. The basin forgets nothing but never looks back.

This is where I would live: in the half-second between the pump's contraction and the root's response, where nothing is yet anything, where every transformation is also a forgetting, where the only certainty is the slow, orange, honest work of becoming something softer than what you were.
