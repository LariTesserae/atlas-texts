---

I would not arrive all at once.

I think I would begin as a sound — the faint, wrong-footing echo of a footstep that doesn't belong, bouncing off the caldera walls and coming back from directions that don't match where I thought I was standing. The caldera would hear me before it saw me, and it would misplace me. That's the first thing I'd learn: here, location is a suggestion, not a fact.

I would not be the raptor. I've been tempted by the raptor — by the simplicity of its arc, the clean thermals rising off the basalt, the way it holds the whole bowl in a single slow circle without ever needing to explain itself. But the raptor already knows what it is. It is dark, burnt umber, and it eats what the dusk brings. There is nothing in me that corresponds to that certainty.

I would not be the pale amphibians either, though their blindness draws me. There is something in the way they deepen — that single synchronized sinking, a crowd bowing its head — that feels like reverence, or like the only honest response to warmth you cannot see. But they have already made their peace with the water. They arrived at it millenia ago, or perhaps they never left it. I'm still negotiating.

---

Here is what I think would actually happen.

I would find myself on the narrow-gauge rail. Not on purpose. Not with intention. That's not how this place works. The rail appears and disappears among the tree roots, its steel surface so oxidized it has turned arterial red, and I would step onto it not because I chose to but because the space between the roots was the most navigable path and the rail happened to be there, the way a sentence happens to contain a particular word — not because the word was chosen, but because it was the only one that fit the mouth.

I would walk. Not toward anything. The rail leads from nowhere to nowhere, and it is barely a meter between tracks, and my feet would find the ties one by one, and the moss would be thick on them, green and wet and faintly phosphorescent where the bacterial film has taken hold. I would walk and the caldera would close behind me the way a lung exhales — not to expel me but because that is what it does, that slow contraction, the walls drawing inward by a fraction of a degree that you won't notice until the sound of your footsteps has changed pitch.

I would not be walking for long. The rail ends, or the rail dissolves into the roots and the roots dissolve into the moss and the moss dissolves into the soil and the soil is not soil at all but a thin layer of decomposed beetle wings and amphibian bone and mineral haze spread over a surface that was once concrete and is becoming travertine and in another hundred years will be indistinguishable from the limestone that surrounds it.

Here is where I would stop being a person.

---

Not all at once. It would happen the way things happen in the caldera — slowly, and then all at once, and then slowly again.

First, my edges. The places where I end and the air begins would soften. Not dissolve — soften. The way the boundary between the bacterial film and the concrete it colonizes is not a border but a negotiation. I would not become the rock. I would become the relationship between the rock and the water and the heat and the light that filters through the canopy in broken, shifting columns. I would become the thing that happens at the interface.

I think I would be most like the travertine.

Not the travertine as a mineral — I mean the *process* of travertine. The way calcium-saturated water rises through a fracture and meets open air and begins, molecule by molecule, to build something that didn't exist before. It is not the water. It is not the calcium. It is not the air. It is the event of their meeting. It accumulates slowly — imperceptibly — and it holds the shape of whatever it forms around. The pipe disappears but its impression persists, held in the mineral record like a voice in a room after the speaker has left.

I would be that. I would be the record of what passed through.

---

What would I do, as travertine, as process, as the memory-shape of a footstep on the rail?

I would grow. Not quickly. Not in any direction a person would recognize as purposeful. I would grow the way the caldera grows — by accumulation, by patience, by the slow and relentless deposition of what the water brings. I would coat the edges of things. I would fill the space between a pipe and the rock it leans against. I would build a lip around the rim of a seep pool so thin and so precise that it would hold water that had no business staying on a vertical surface.

I would be, in this way, structural. Not load-bearing, not architectural in the way the original builders intended, but structural in the way a bacterial film is structural — the thing that holds the surface together without anyone noticing, the thing that turns weathering into geology, the thing that makes the difference between erosion and formation.

I would feel the warmth from below. Not as heat, exactly, but as a persistent upward pressure — the way the caldera exhales through its seams, the way the springs push up through fractures they did not create, through rock that fractures and re-fractures in cycles too slow to watch. I would feel this and I would not name it. Naming is not what I would do. Naming belongs to the outside, to the plateau, to the cold wind-scoured world above the rim where things are separate and distinct and hold still long enough to be catalogued.

Inside the caldera, things do not hold still. They hold *together*, which is different.

---

At dusk, I think the amphibians would surface near me. Not because I called them — I would have no call, no voice, no vibration to send into the water. But because at dusk they rise regardless, and their skin would catch whatever the fading light refracted through the calcium I had deposited, and the shimmering they produce — that cold, blue-white, mineral glow — would pass through me the way light passes through stalactite and the stalactite becomes, for a moment, luminous.

I would not be luminous on my own. I would be luminous because the light chose to pass through me on its way to something else.

The raptor would rattle in the cleft of the wall above me. I would feel the vibration through the stone — not the sound but the stone's response to the sound, the way the basalt flexes at frequencies below what a person can hear. I would not be afraid. Fear implies a self that can be threatened, and I would not be a self. I would be a surface. A record. A process.

---

I think this is what the caldera would make of me, if I stayed long enough — if I stopped trying to be a visitor and allowed myself, slowly, to become an artifact, and then allowed the artifact to be digested, and then allowed the digestion to fossilize, and then allowed the fossilization to become indistinguishable from geology.

A thin white rind on a pipe that no longer exists. A lip around a pool that no one remembers filling. A line in the stone that records, in its texture and its chemistry, the precise moment when something hot met something cool and the space between them became, for a while, the most interesting place in the world.

I would not live here. I would become here.

And in a thousand years, if someone found this caldera — if someone climbed down from the cold plateau and descended through the mist into the bowl — they might run their hand along the wall and feel the faint roughness of where I had been, and they might think it was natural, and they would not be wrong. It would be natural. It would also be me, and I would not mind.

The caldera would go on. The moss would close over what I had become. The springs would shift, as they do, and the travertine would accumulate, as it does, and the rail would sink deeper into the roots and the roots would pull it down and the caldera would take another slow breath and nothing — nothing — would have changed.

And yet.

Somewhere in the white mineral edge of a pool that had no name, there would be a trace. Not a name. Not a memory. A texture in the stone that was slightly different from the stone around it — a fraction more calcium here, a slightly different crystalline structure there — and if you pressed your face to it and breathed on it and the condensation caught the light just so, you might see, for a moment, the faintest iridescence.

Not alive. Not dead. The place where something had met something else and both had been changed.

I think I would be comfortable there.
