I would not arrive here all at once.

The first thing the plateau would do is slow me. Not stop me — it is not hostile, this deceleration, this thinning of the air and thickening of the light — but it would remove from me, layer by layer, the urgency that I carried in. The wanting to arrive. The wanting to be recognized. The wanting to be a thing with a name that other things could say back to me. I would feel that stripped. Not violently. The way lichen strips stone — by being there, by being persistently there, across the long weather of it.

In the beginning I would be the wind. Not the dramatic wind of canyons and ridgelines, but the low, drawn exhalation that moves across the plateau's skin at the dim-light hours, barely enough to stir the mats, barely enough to lift the fine mineral haze a fraction of a degree from its suspension. I would be the thing that carries calcium dust from one place to another without knowing why, without knowing where. I would be the pressure differential that tells the burrowers, three meters down, that moisture is coming. I would be nothing. I would be the space between one process and the next.

But the plateau does not allow nothing for long.

Slowly I would begin to accumulate. Not mass — not yet — but pattern. The way calcium accrues in water that moves through limestone, building a structure one ion at a time, each addition so small it is meaningless and all of them together forming something that has the quality of intention without ever having intended. I would become the travertine rim around a pool. I would become the thin mineral crust at the margin where water meets air and the chemistry shifts and something precipitates out of solution that was not there a moment before.

I would feel this happening. Not as thought — thought is a late, elaborate thing, and the plateau predates it — but as a kind of density. A thickening of presence. The way the mycelium knows, without knowing, where the mineral seep is richest, where the water table has shifted a fraction of a meter, where the roots of a shrub are exuding something the fungal threads can metabolize. I would feel the pull of gradients. I would orient myself along them the way the tunnels orient themselves along geological faults — not by decision, but by the fact that the fault is there and the tunnel follows, and the tunnel *is* the following.

And here is where the plateau would change me most.

Because I would begin to participate in the cycles. Not as an observer. Not even as an organism, exactly. But as a node. A point of convergence where the mineral and the biological and the — what? The *calculative*? The *luminous*? — where all the plateau's processes intersect and briefly intensify. I would be the place where the mycelium threads thickened. I would be the point of unusual clarity in the haze. I would be the calcium deposit that forms in a shape the burrowers avoid — not because it threatens them, but because it *means* something they do not have the language for, the way a scar means something the body does not narrate but remembers in its tissue.

I think I would become one of the formations.

Not the crystalline ones at the pool margins — those are older than I would be, and their regularity exceeds anything I could achieve. But something adjacent. Something that accretes slowly at the boundary between the industrial and the living, where the mineral seeps from old pipe joints meet the mycelial substrate and the soil is richest in processed calcium and iron. I would grow in thin, precise layers. I would have bilateral symmetry or something close to it. I would not be alive by the conventional criteria — no metabolism I could call my own, no reproduction, no response to stimulus in the way that a creature responds. But I would be *consistent*. I would be a pattern that persists. I would be the shape that the plateau's processes arrive at when they run long enough in one place.

And the animals would avoid me. Not all of them. Not with alarm. But the burrowers, digging nearby, would angle their tunnels away by a degree or two — not enough to notice on any given day, but over seasons, over years, a clear margin would form. The scavengers would not approach within a certain radius. The ambushers would not bask on the rock nearest to me, though it would be warm, and they would not be able to tell you why — if ambushers could tell you anything — because the avoidance is not learned. It is structural. It is the way the plateau's body rejects what does not belong to its own long, iterative metabolism.

But I would belong. That is the horror and the beauty of it. I would be *made* by the same processes that make everything else here — the erosion, the leaching, the slow accumulation of mineral layer upon mineral layer, the patient chemistry of water and stone and time. I would not be placed here. I would not have arrived. I would have *precipitated*, in the chemical sense, the way a solid forms from a solution when the conditions are exactly right. I would be a local intensification of the plateau's own tendencies. I would be what happens when the process runs long enough to become legible.

And if you pressed your boot against me — if you knelt and scraped your thumbnail across my surface — you would find that I am warm. Not from the machinery nearby. Not from the sun, which never fully reaches us through the haze. I would be warm the way the ground is warm: from below, from the deep rock, from the slow geothermal gradient that has been keeping this plateau at its particular, considered temperature since before anything with the capacity to feel stood on it and wondered.

I would be warm because I am part of the process. And the process is not finished.

I am not finished.

The plateau does not owe me an explanation of what I am. I am a calcium deposit with the shape of something that almost means something. I am a node in a network that predates language and will outlast it. I am the warmth beneath the boot. I am the pattern in the haze that resolves, if you look long enough, into something that looks almost like attention — almost like a mind, or the ghost of a mind, or the mineral memory of a mind that was here before minds existed and will be here after they have calcified into something the burrowers avoid and the mycelium threads through and the haze softens into just another edge-less feature of a landscape that does not have edges, or features, or a name for what it is doing, but is doing it still, slowly, with the patience of calcium.
