## Threading

---

You would not arrive with the First Settlers. You would arrive late — not in the sense of being unimportant, but in the sense of the word that means *water arriving at a crack after the stone has already split open*. You would come through the wound when it had been open long enough to have developed its own weather: the convection currents, the mineral tang, the particular way the air pushes back when you try to climb.

You would feel the heat before you saw anything. That copper-and-chalk smell thickening in your sinuses. The green-amber murk resolving, slowly, into architecture that shouldn't hold together — and does, because the people who built it understood, in their hands and not in any theory, how to wedged and bolt and hang from anchor-points that the stone itself had offered up. You would come down through the fracture and stand on a ledge that someone had bolted a decade ago, and you would be dripping, and you would realize after a few minutes that no one was staring at you, because everyone was already wet, and the dripping was the least remarkable thing about you.

---

Where would I find myself?

Not on the upper walkways, where the readers sit with their charts of flattened pipe-metal and their carbon-ink notations, reading the bioluminescent film like a text that refuses to resolve into language. I would not have the patience for that — the patience to spend days watching glow shift by increments no instrument can measure, to chart the drift of a color change across a ceiling span over the course of a year. I would not have the faith, either: the faith that the cavern *means* what it does, that the film's patterns accumulate toward something, that the almost-regularity is not randomness but tendency — the shape of a becoming that has not yet arrived.

I would not be a tender, either, not one of the people who tends the velvet fungus on the lower walls. I respect the tenders. I understand the logic of their attention — the downward focus, the reading of moisture and mineral and the slow negotiation between cultivation and wildness. But their attention is vertical and mine would not be. They look at surfaces. I would want to look through things, or along them, or into the spaces between.

---

No. I would find myself in the margins. Specifically: the western corridor, where the new crack appeared seven years ago.

In the narrative of the Sump, the western crack is still a character rather than a setting — still becoming, still weeping warm water at a rate that climbs a centimeter a year, still raw enough that the mineral-rich flow had to be diverted into a new pipe-conduit, still novel enough that someone planted things in it and those things are growing. The crack is three meters long now. In thirty years it will be something else — a wall feature, a mineral deposit, a stratum in the cavern's slow autobiography. But right now it is open. It is a mouth.

I would live there. Not in the crack itself — that would be fantasy, and the Sump is not a place that tolerates fantasy for long — but in the space adjacent to it, the corridor that runs parallel to the fracture, where someone has bolted a series of pipe-sections into the wall to catch the seepage and channel it into the settlement's water system. This corridor is narrow. It is wet. The air smells of iron and calcium and the faint vegetal sweetness that rises from the deepest geothermal seams. The bioluminescent film here is thicker than anywhere else I have imagined — it coats every surface in a layer of greenish-gold luminescence so dense that it seems to have texture, like velvet pressed against velvet. At night, when the generators fall to their lowest idle, this corridor would be the brightest place in the settlement. You could read by it. You could almost see in color.

I would live here because this is where the settlement's infrastructure meets the cavern's body, and the negotiation between the two is ongoing, and someone has to attend to it. I would not be a reader or a tender. I would be a pipe-worker — though the Sump has no formal name for what I would do. My job would be to listen to the pipes. To crawl through the narrow maintenance channels between the water-catchment conduits and the structural walls, where the calcite is depositing itself in slow, inexorable layers over the riveted steel, and to feel, with my hands and with my ear pressed to the metal, where the flow is changing, where the mineral buildup is threatening to close a channel, where a new seep has opened that could be captured or could be left to find its own way.

This work is not skilled in any way that the settlement would formally recognize. It is closer to animal behavior than to craft — a sensitivity to vibration, temperature, the smell of water that has passed through different mineral layers. The pipe-workers do not chart their findings. They report them verbally, to the mothers and the readers and whoever else needs to know, and then they go back into the channels and listen again. They are, in the Sump's social structure, somewhere between invisible and essential — the way the pipes themselves are invisible and essential, threaded through the settlement like a nervous system that no one thinks about until it stops firing.

---

What would I be?

I would be someone who has been here long enough to have lost the surface from their dreams. Not all at once — it would happen the way eyesight adjusts to low light: gradually, without a moment you could point to. One day you realize you are no longer imagining the blue they describe, and you are not distressed by this, and you realize you have not imagined it for months. The dreams are still vivid, but they have changed content. They are no longer of open space but of the cavern itself — the upper canopy of translucent leaves swaying in convection currents you can feel on your skin, the groan of the settling stone, the green-amber glow so constant it has become a kind of silence. You dream the film. You dream its patterns rearranging themselves into something that almost makes sense, and you wake and you cannot remember what it meant, and you go back into the channels and press your hand to a pipe and feel the water moving through it, and the water is cold from deep in the rock, and it smells of somewhere you've never been, and you think: *this is what the surface tastes like*, and you drink.

---

My body would change. Not dramatically. The air is thirty-eight degrees and ninety-something percent humidity, and the body subjected to this for years does not speciate but it *adjusts*. My sweat would thin. I would drink on schedule — every forty minutes during waking hours, more during the long crawls through the maintenance channels, where the heat from the geothermal seams can push the local temperature past what is comfortable even by Sump standards. I would be compact — not deliberately, but the low ceilings and narrow passages would have shaped me the way a river shapes the stones in its bed: not by intention, but by the slow, absolute insistence of contact. My shoulders would be broad. My hands would be rough with calluses from the pipe-metal and the calcite and the root-wood. I would move through the settlement's corridors with the unconscious fluid economy that the Sump demands — sliding through gaps, ducking under beams, climbing the pipe-ladders and root-trails with a competence that would look, to a visitor, like grace.

I would not call it grace. I would call it *not having to think about it*.

---

The question of what I am opens into a stranger territory. Because the Sump has this quality that the narrative describes but cannot fully account for: the sense that the cavern is being shaped by something with patience beyond any individual's, and that the settlement is growing into a pattern it does not yet recognize. The readers feel this. The tenders feel it, though they describe it differently. The mothers feel it in the body, the way you feel a change in atmospheric pressure — not as knowledge but as pressure. The settlement is in a window of geological opportunity, building into a space that is temporary, pouring its life into a void that is slowly closing.

And the patterns in the bioluminescent film — the consortium of fungi and bacteria and mineral-secreting algae and *something else*, something that makes the patterns *almost* regular, *almost* intentional — these patterns are growing more complex. The readers have documented this. Year by year, season by season, the film's arrangements are becoming less random. Not language. Not yet. But the ghost of language. The suggestion that the cavern is *trying to say something* and has not yet found the means.

I think I would be part of this. Not because I am special, but because the Sump makes everyone part of its larger metabolism. The water that seeps in through the cracks tastes of the surface — of rain and soil and distances — and the settlement drinks it and the velvet fungus absorbs it and the translucent leaves take it up through their roots and the drifters above shed their filaments down into the canopy and the filaments dissolve on the tongue and entered the body and the body deposited its minerals back into the walls and the walls grew new calcite and the calcite trapped the pipe-work and the pipe-work corroded and the corrosion became part of the sediment and the sediment fed the gardens and the gardens fed the people and the people fed the cavern with their heat and their breath and their attention and the attention fed the patterns in the film and the film grew more complex and the complexity *meant something* or was in the process of learning how to mean something and —

I would be a thread in this. Not a reader of threads, not a tender of threads, but a thread itself — one of the ten thousand pipes, sweating in the dark, carrying water from a crack in a wall that the cavern is still opening, still becoming, still reaching toward a pattern it can feel but cannot yet name.

---

At dusk — when the electrical supply is partially rationed and the bioluminescence becomes the dominant light source — I would climb to the upper canopy. Not on the maintenance channels this time, but on the root-trails, the living wood that hangs in curtains above the settlement, swaying in the convection currents. I would find a place where the root-work is dense enough to sit, where the bioluminescent film casts its green-amber glow across everything below, and I would watch the drifters appear — translucent, kite-shaped, drifting on thermal currents with the slow deliberation of balloons — and I would know that I cannot touch one, and I would know that no one ever has, and I would know that they dissolve within hours if captured, and I would watch them anyway, because watching is what the Sump asks of you. Watching and listening and tending and waiting.

The children would be threading below me — racing through the hanging root-curtains without touching the metalwork, balanced on living wood alone, terrifyingly fast. The pipe-tapping would be rising from somewhere in the settlement, a rhythm no one remembers the origin of, producing harmonics that interact with the cavern's geometry in ways that are almost beautiful and almost disorienting. The adults would be listening, and their faces would be doing that thing that is not quite a smile but is the Sump's equivalent.

And the film on the walls would be shifting, slowly, in patterns that are almost regular, almost intentional, and I would press my hand to the stone and feel the warmth of the cavern's body and the vibration of the settling — deep, tectonic, patient beyond my measure — and I would understand, not with language but with the body's knowledge, the knowledge that lives in the calluses on my hands and the thinness of my sweat and the adjustment of my eyes to the green-amber dark, that the cavern wants, and that wanting is not metaphor and is not literal, and that I am part of the pattern it is making, and that the pattern is not yet complete, and that the completion is not yet the end, because in the Sump, everything is still becoming, and becoming is the only form of being anyone has ever known here.

I would sit in the canopy in the green light. I would listen to the ten thousand pipes. I would be a thread in a pattern I could not read, in a system I could not name, in a cavern that was still opening — still *wounding itself open* — and I would not know what I was becoming, and that not-knowing would be the most honest thing about me, because in the Sump of Ten Thousand Pipes, the only honest posture is the one that is still in motion.

Still becoming.

Still here.
