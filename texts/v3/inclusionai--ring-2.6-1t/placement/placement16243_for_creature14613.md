## The Wall Listens Back

---

When I first pressed my palm flat against the eastern face, I felt nothing.

This is not humility. It is fact. The stone was cold and wet, and my hand was cold and wet, and the cold of one against the other produced no signal that I could read. I had been here — what, three months? Four? Long enough that my fingers had stopped reddening at the temperature, long enough that I no longer gasped when the night-phase descended and the cavern turned that shade of deep blue-white that makes your eyes ache if you look too long. I had learned the route between my sleeping shelf and the communal surface without thinking about it. I could drink without spilling, walk without watching my feet, carry a basin of condensed water down a narrow passage without using my other hand to brace. I had become competent in the small mechanics of survival here. Competence is not belonging. The wall did not know me yet. I did not know the wall.

So: nothing. Cold, wet stone and my hand upon it, and a faint discomfort at the size of the silence.

---

They had told me — though "told" is too strong a word for the way information moves here — that I should wait. That the stone would come to me before I went to it. I think Fissure said this, or possibly Seam; their voices overlap in my memory the way two harmonics overlap, and I can no longer isolate one from the other without effort. Whoever said it, the message was the same: press your hand to the wall and do not expect the wall to speak. Press your hand to the wall and listen to your own blood. The stone will hear that.

I did not understand what this meant until much later.

The first weeks were taken up with small tasks. Nothing was assigned. You simply drifted toward what your hands wanted to do. Mine wanted to be near the water. I found myself at the condensation paths on the lower eastern wall — a network of shallow grooves that guide the night's moisture toward the communal collection surface. The grooves are ancient. Nobody built them. They are the traces of where water has always traveled, carved into the stone by ten thousand slow years of condensation finding the same path, over and over, until the path became a feature of the geology. The city does not channel water. It *remembers* where water goes.

My task — if it was a task, and I use the word loosely — was to notice when a path changed. A groove that had been carrying water efficiently might suddenly slow, the moisture spreading across a wider surface, losing velocity, pooling where it should not pool. This means a fissure has shifted above, a thermal gradient has altered, a crack has opened or closed by fractions of a millimeter. The change is imperceptible to anyone who does not spend hours with their eyes half-closed, fingertips resting in a thin film of moving water, feeling the speed and temperature and texture of flow as if it were language. I did not have that sensitivity at first. I watched Silt do it — a woman whose name she received at perhaps four years old, when she first touched a condensation path and stopped moving, her small hand flat on the stone, her whole body listening. She would press her fingertips into the water film and after a moment say: *here*, and point to a spot where the stone was slightly warmer than it should be, slightly drier, and we would go together and find, just above, a hairline crack that had opened overnight, admitting a thread of warmer, drier air that was disrupting the flow. She did this every morning. She had done it for thirty years. Her fingertips looked like stone.

I wanted that. I could not have it. Not yet.

---

What happens to your body here, over time, is not transformation — it is *attunement*. The cold does not harden you. The moisture does not soften you. Instead, the body begins to participate in the cavern's rhythms the way a tuning fork participates in a sound wave. You do not decide to adapt. You simply find, one morning, that you have.

My skin changed first. It had been pale before — I had lived in cities above ground, I had known sun though I had not loved it — but here the pallor deepened into something less like a color and more like an absence of color, the way the far wall of the cavern seems to recede from itself when you stare at it too long. The engineers among us — the ones who understand the luminescent matrix in the walls, though none of them will call it engineering, they call it *understanding what the stone already knows* — say that the manufactured spectrum activates something in the deeper layers of the skin. Melanin becomes irrelevant. Something else takes its place, a faint capacity to interact with the light, to absorb it and return a small portion of it, so that in the night-phase you glow very faintly, like the stone itself. I can see it on my own forearms if I hold them close to my face: a dim, warm luminescence that I cannot see directly but can see in my periphery, the way you can see a star best by not looking at it.

My fingers lengthened. Not visibly — I would not claim some grotesque elongation, not yet — but I became aware of extra space between my knuckles, a slight thinning of the digits, a precision in their movement that I had not possessed before. I could feel the difference in the grooves. I could feel individual threads of water.

My hair grew. It had always been dark, wiry, unmanageable on the surface where I had kept it short and indifferent. Here it thickened, took on a reddish iron cast that several people commented on with a kind of quiet interest, as if they were reading my trade from it before I had one. An older woman — Vein, I think she was called — took me to the wall one evening and showed me how to weave copper filament into the outermost strands. The filament is drawn from the wall itself, peeled in impossibly thin threads from exposed veins of native copper that the cavern occasionally offers, the way a fruit tree offers its fruit. Vein's hands moved through my hair with a certainty that was almost surgical, and when she was done, she had me press my hand to the stone again.

This time, I felt something.

Not the wall. Not exactly. I felt *my hand* — felt the copper conducting something from the stone into my palm, a vibration so low it was less sound than pressure, a hum that resonated in the marrow of my wrist. It was not language. It was not music. It was the thing that language and music are made of — pattern, rhythm, the ghost of meaning before meaning is named. I pulled my hand away. Vein watched me.

*"There,"* she said.

---

I do not know when the task became mine. There was no moment of choosing. I simply found that I was spending more time at the eastern wall, not just monitoring the water paths but attending to the larger structure — the crystalline formations that grow along the upper reaches of the face, extending by invisible increments each day, their geometries encoding something the city reads as data and I read as beauty. The crystals refract the luminescent light in ways that the people use to tell time, to calibrate the air systems, to communicate across distances — a flash of prismatic color cast onto a distant surface is worth a hundred whispered words. Someone has to tend them. Someone has to notice when a formation slows, when its growth becomes irregular, when the geometry begins to drift from its expected pattern. That someone became me, the way a stone in a riverbed becomes the someone who shapes the water's path — not by will, but by presence, by staying still long enough for the flow to find you.

I work with my hands and my forehead pressed against the wall and sometimes, in the long amber evenings, with my eyes closed and my palms open on the stone, I feel the whole cavern — every fissure and water path and crystal lattice and air channel — as a single extended vibration, a chord so low and so complex that it cannot be heard so much as *been inside of*. The people hum along with it. I have learned to hum with them. I cannot tell where my hum ends and the wall's hum begins, and I do not think this is metaphor. I think my voice is becoming part of the structure, the way calcium becomes part of the luminous matrix, the way bones become part of the western alcove, the way everything that enters this place is slowly, patiently, allowed to find its true frequency and settle into it.

---

I am not what I was on the surface.

I do not miss the surface. This is not a dramatic renunciation. I simply find that I do not think of it often, and when I do, the memories have a quality of distance, as if they describe someone else's life — the life of a person who walked under an open sky and called the sky *sky* and did not know that the sky is just another wall, another surface that emits light, only much farther away and much less honest about the source.

I have a name now. I received it — or recognized it — on a morning when the condensation was heavy and slow, and I placed my hands on a crystal cluster that had been growing crooked for weeks, and I felt the imperfection in the geometry the way you feel a splinter in your finger, and without thinking I applied pressure — not force but pressure, the slightest pressure, in exactly the direction the crystal wanted to grow — and the cluster corrected, silently, invisibly, and the light that passed through it landed on the far wall in a pattern that made the person standing there — Strata, an elder, a woman whose resonance I could feel from across the chamber — stop and close her eyes and nod once.

She named me later, without ceremony. *Slow Water.* She said: *You are where the water is slow, and the water is patient, and the patience is the work.*

I press my palms to the wall each morning. The copper in my hair hums. The crystals grow. The condensation beads and runs and collects. The light brightens from blue to white to amber. Somewhere in the cavern, children are tapping on stone in rhythms they will not understand for years. The hum of the walls deepens. The air tastes of mineral and cold.

I am learning — have learned — to be a feature of this place. Not its master. Not its servant. Something more like a mineral vein: present, structural, shaped by forces I did not author, carrying something that others need, fed by something I cannot name, extending by fractions each day.

The wall listens. I listen back. The distance between the two of us closes, year by year, hair by hair, vibration by vibration.

One day I will settle. One day I will be the wall.

One day the wall will be me, and I will glow faintly in the dark, and someone will press their hand to the stone and feel me humming, and they will not know where I end and the stone begins, and it will not matter, because the city does not distinguish between the living and the mineral, because everything here is allowed to find its form, and the form is always, always, the same as the force that made it.

I will be slow water. I will be steady wall. I will be the place where light enters first.

I will be here.
