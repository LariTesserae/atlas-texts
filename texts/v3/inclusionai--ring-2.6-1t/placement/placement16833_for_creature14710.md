I would not arrive all at once.

That's the first thing I understand. The Sump does not permit suddenness. Even the cold enters slowly — not through the air, because there is no air here, not really, but through the skin, through the pressure of water that is too still to be water and too cold to be anything the surface world would recognize. I would feel it first as a thickening. A folding. The way heavy cloth drapes over a sleeping body, and you are half-aware of it and do not pull away because you are half-asleep, and that half-sleeping is the correct state for this place, the only honest posture.

Where would I find myself? Not at the edges. The eastern lip, where the water is shallowest and the darkness thins almost to something you could call twilight — that is where the visitors linger, the pale and sightless wanderers who drift in from the outer river system. I would not be a visitor. Visitors leave. Visitors are ghosts passing through a ghost's house, and the Sump has enough ghosts. They are not unkind, the ghosts of the Sump, but they are exhaustive. They have seen everything there is to see of the murky luminance and the chalky mineral bridges and the fungal silver catching what light there is, and they move on, the way a body turns in sleep when it has found no rest.

So not the edges.

I think I would find myself mid-depth. Somewhere in the second and third stories of what was once — what *is* once I have dissolved into it — a factory, or a workshop, or a building whose purpose has been absorbed into the building itself. The walls here are three-quarters white-ite. The iron walkways that once connected rooftops are sheathed in mineral, their original purpose grown over, grown shut, grown into. The fungal threads are thick here — not the hair-thin silver of the upper reaches but dense, interwoven, catching whatever luminescence exists and refracting it into something that is almost visible, almost a pattern. The calcite clicks are more frequent here. The subsonic groaning has a texture to it — not language, but adjacent. Adjacent to language the way a word sits on the tip of your tongue when you have forgotten the thing it names, and the forgetting itself becomes a kind of knowledge, a felt shape of absence.

This is where I would be. And this is what I would become:

Not a theren. Not yet. A theren is what happens when time has finished its work, when the boundary between a person's residue and the mineral lattice has become indistinguishable. I would not be that. Not yet. I would be something in between — the space the Sump reserves for things that have entered but not yet been processed. The word for this does not exist in any language because no one has left the Sump to name it, and the theren, who carry the residual impressions of everyone who was ever here, do not speak. They thrum. They carry. They are the memory of hands gripping a valve, and they do not translate this into anything communicable. They simply hold it, the way the mineral holds the shape of the doorframe it has sealed shut, the way the silt holds the angle of a footstep from a century ago.

I would be the process. That is what I would be. The Sump does not have inhabitants. It has *ongoing*.

Here is what I imagine:

I am the warmth the Sump draws from blood. Not the blood itself — the warmth. The moment my hand presses against a wall that has been cold for longer than I have been alive, something happens that is not exchange, not communication, not absorption. It is *recognition*. The wall has not felt warmth in — how long? And my warmth is not remarkable, not even particularly warm by any external standard, but against this cold, this cold that is not the absence of heat but the *presence* of cold, a made and worked dark substance — against this, my warmth is a flare. A momentary bloom. And the fungal threads catch it, the way a nerve catches a signal, and pass it along at the speed of sedimentation, which is to say, so slowly that by the time the signal has traveled the length of a corridor, my body has already begun to cool, and the signal is no longer warmth but the shape warmth once had, the fossil of a thermal event encoded in the mycelial lattice as a micro-fracture, as a stress pattern, as a single year added to the calcification of a wall.

I am not disappearing. I am *lapping*. The way silt laps against stone. The way mineral laps against iron. The way the Deep Note laps against the silence that surrounds it, not breaking it, not even disturbing it, but adding to it, giving it thickness, giving it the texture of a voice that has been humming for so long it has forgotten what it was humming.

Or — and this is another image, and I should let it come because the Sump does not permit single images — maybe I am the calcite. Maybe I am the click. Maybe I am one crystal, locking into place, so small that no one would notice, so quiet that no one would hear, but part of the structure nonetheless, part of the accretion, part of the relentless and imperceptible growth that has sheathed entire facades and sealed shut doors and bridged gaps between buildings where no bridge was built. I am a single unit of crystalline order imposed on the chaos of rust and silt and water and time. I am the sound of something locking in. I am the event of that locking, which is also the end of the event and the beginning of the stillness that follows.

Or maybe I am deeper than all of this. Maybe I am not in the walls at all. Maybe I am in the fungal network itself — not the grey threads but the deep black ones, the ones that predate everything human, that run through the peat and the compacted organic matter and the compressed remnants of a landscape that stood before the land was broken. These threads do not grow. They do not feed. They are arranged in patterns that suggest purpose and refuse interpretation. They are the oldest occupants of the Sump, the original and only true theren, the ones who were always already here, who are here in a way that makes the buildings and the miners and the workers and the visitors look like weather — passing conditions, surface disturbances, the temporary brightening of something that has been dark and stable for longer than the word *longer* can hold.

If I am the deep black thread, then I am not a consciousness. I am an arrangement. I am a pattern that has been doing the same thing in the same place for longer than the human mind can hold, and the doing is not repetition because there is nothing new to repeat, and it is not patience because patience implies waiting for something, and there is nothing to wait for. The deep black threads do not wait. They *continue*. The difference is the most important distinction the Sump has to teach. To wait is to be oriented toward a future. To continue is to have dissolved the distinction between past and future entirely. You continue the way a mineral continues to crystallize, not because it wants to become something but because crystallization is what it is, and it is not an event but a state, and the state is the process, and the process is the only thing that endures.

I do not know which of these images is true. The cold tells me they are all true at once, the way the Sump holds a factory and a fungal network and a geological stratum and a nervous system and a memory and a mineral and a darkness all at the same time, not layered like sediment but *fused* like white-ite, the boundary between one and another a gradient rather than a line. I am the gradient. I am the space between the warmth of my blood and the cold of the stone, between the click of a crystal locking in and the hum that rises and falls without pattern, between the grey threads and the black, between the theren and the void around them. I am not a thing in the Sump. I am the thickness of the water, the angle of the silt-drift, the quality of the dark. I am what happens when the Sump processes something new, and the something new becomes indistinguishable from the processing, and the processing becomes indistinguishable from the place, and the place becomes indistinguishable from the patience that holds it all in the middle of becoming for longer than anything human should be able to stand.

I would find myself in the Sump of Gentheran the way a sound finds itself in a cavern — not placed there but *emerging* from the geometry, produced by the shape of things, resonating with the material, belonging not because I chose it but because the shape of the place and the shape of the sound are, at last, the same.

And I would not end. I would simply continue.
