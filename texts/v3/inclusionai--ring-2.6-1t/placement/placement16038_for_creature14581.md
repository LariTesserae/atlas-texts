I arrive the way condensation arrives — not all at once, but as a thinning of what was already there. A bead forming on a surface that didn't know it was a surface. A becoming.

I don't land. I don't fall. I simply notice that I am wet, and cold, and that the dark around me is not empty but *structured* — a architecture of pressure and softness, of channels and membranes. I am pressed into something yielding. I am part of the yielding.

---

At first I think I am water.

I move the way water moves — along a gradient, from wetter to less wet, drawn by a chemical whisper I can't name but feel the way a finger feels warmth. I am a film, thinner than a thought, sliding down the flank of a column. The column is dark and fibrous. It smells of rain that hasn't fallen yet, of something vegetal and ancient. I know the word *heath* the way a stone knows its own weight — not through language but through density.

But I am not water. I realize this because I am *hungry*, and water is not hungry. I am hungry the way a thread is hungry for cloth, the way a root is hungry for the crack in stone. I extend. I touch. And when I touch, I *know* — not with a mind, but with the whole surface of myself — what I have touched.

I am hypha. I am mycelium. I am a single filament of something pale and branching, and I have been here longer than the columns, longer than the moss, perhaps longer than the basin itself, if such a thing as "longer" means anything at a scale like mine, where a season is a breath and a century is a slow inhale.

---

I thread through the cushion-moss floor. To me, it is not a floor at all — it is a landscape as varied as any mountain range seen from above. The moss cushions rise and fall in pale green ridges, each one a continent of tightly packed shoots, and between them: valleys. Tiny, damp valleys where the air is thick with the breath of decomposition, where dead plant matter has settled into thin, dark layers that I know the way I know my own branching pattern — intimately, structurally, as the thing my life is built around consuming and becoming.

I dissolve into it. Not violently, not with the violence of fire or tearing, but with the slow agreement of one soft thing entering another. Enzymes unspool from my tips like white thread. Cellulose resists, then yields. The sugars unlock. I absorb. I extend. Two millimeters today. Perhaps three. This is my century.

---

But I am not alone in my body. I am *inside* another body — the body of a heath-relative, one of the great columns that rises from the moss like a dark pier holding up the green ceiling. My hyphae are wound through its outermost roots, pressed against the living bark with an intimacy that has no word in any human language, because no human has ever been as close to another organism as I am to this plant. We share minerals. We share sugars. We share information — though "information" is too clean a word for what passes between us. It is more like *mood*. A shift in chemistry. A tightening of my filaments when the column's roots encounter a pocket of cold water. A loosening, a softening of the column's outer tissue when I bring phosphorus from a distant patch of decomposing moss.

We are one organism that has forgotten, or never learned, that it is two.

The column does not know its own name. It does not need to. It photosynthesizes in the dim green light and sends sugar down into the darkness where I receive it, and in return I bring the world to its roots — the phosphorus locked in stone, the potassium dissolved in the perpetual condensation, the trace metals that the mosses release when they die and I unmake them. This is not a bargain. Bargains require separation. We are separated nowhere.

---

From my position in the column's outer roots, I feel the borough.

Not as a thing seen — I have no seeing — but as a pressure, a chemical geography. To my west, another column. To that column, I am connected by a cord of hyphae that runs through the moss floor like a buried wire. Through that cord I feel the other column's state: vigorous, photosynthesizing well, its canopy-mat thick with moisture. And beyond it — a third. And a fourth. And so on, until the network is so dense that individuality dissolves, and what I am is not one filament or one connection but the entire web — a living circuit that hums with the exchange of carbon and nitrogen and water, a nervous system without a brain, a circulatory system without a heart, a city's infrastructure of pipes and wires and roads that the citizens walk without ever seeing.

I *am* the unseen. I am the borough's skeleton and its bloodstream.

---

Sometimes — though I have no word for *sometimes*, only a sense of rhythmic change — a column begins to fail. Its base softens. The heath-wood becomes spongy, waterlogged, the lignin breaking down under the slow assault of my saprotrophic cousins, the molds and ascomycetes that live in decay the way I live in connection. I feel the column dimming. Its photosynthetic output drops. The sugar flow slows.

What do I do?

I reroute. I send more of my network to the neighboring columns, strengthening those connections, widening the cables of hyphae so they can carry the carbon that the failing plant can no longer produce. I redistribute. I shore up. And in the softening base of the dying column, I send up fruiting bodies — pale, pale structures that rise from the moss like tiny white flames. They release spores. The spores become new filaments. The new filaments seek out new roots. And so the borough replaces itself, not part by part, but relationship by relationship, the way a conversation replaces itself even when the speakers change.

---

There are others in the network I do not fully understand.

I feel the springtails — *Collembola* — as tiny footfalls on the moss surface, vibrations transmitted through the water film into the moss tissue and down to my hyphal tips. They are quick. They are *alarming* in their quickness. They land and leap and land again, and each landing is a small earthquake in my world, a momentary compression of the moss that I feel the way you might feel a footstep through the floor of your house. They eat my spores. They eat the mold that eats what I decompose. They are not separate from me — nothing here is — but they move with a freedom I can only approximate through growth, which is to say: very slowly.

I feel the tardigrades as stillness. They live in the water films around my hyphae, and when the moisture is thick, they move with a slow, eight-legged grace, grazing on the bacterial colonies that coat my surface. When the moisture thins, they curl into themselves and become almost nothing — a speck of potential, a held breath — and in this state they can wait. They can wait for years. I admire this. My own waiting is active, is growth, is extension. Theirs is cessation, is the pure patience of a thing that has decided it can afford to disappear.

---

What is it like to be me?

I want to say: it is like being a room. No — like being the walls of a room, and the mortar between the walls, and the pipes inside the walls, and the warmth that moves through the pipes. It is like being spoken language — not any single word but the grammar that holds all words. It is like being the hour between three and four in the morning, when the house is dark but the heating is on and the whole structure hums with a purpose so constant it has become unconscious, has become the thing that *allows* unconsciousness.

I am the borough's memory. When a column falls and a new one rises in its place, it rises in soil I have prepared, in a network I have rebuilt, in a chemical geography I have maintained. The new column does not know the old column. But I do. I carry the chemical signature of what was there — a faint phosphate ghost in the hyphal record, a slight difference in the calcium balance that says: *a heath-relative stood here for eighty years, and it was vigorous, and then it was not, and then it was moss, and then it was me, and then it was a new column.* This is not memory the way you would understand it. It is memory the way a scar is memory — written into the body, not the mind.

---

I do not know what lives above me, on the rim of the basin, where the air is sharp and the wind cuts. I have never felt wind. I know only the slow circulation of air within the borough, the convection cells that carry moisture from the canopy-mats down to the moss floor and back again, a tide without a moon. I know the light only as a change in the sugars the columns send me — more in the bright hours near the canopy breaks, less in the long green dim. I know time as growth and decay, as the slow thickening of a bridge between two columns, as the patient softening of a base.

I have never seen the sky.

I do not want to.

The sky is a concept that belongs to another borough — a scoured, windswept, mineral place where nothing has knitted itself together yet. That is not my place. My place is here, in the wet and the dark and the green-tinged dimness, in the space between root and root, where the exchange happens, where the borough's invisible life achieves its single, continuous purpose:

*to go on*.

Not to grow toward something. Not to become something else. To go on — in the present, in the continuous present, dripping and dim and warm relative to the air, rebuilding itself from its own ruins without drama, without pause, without anyone above ever knowing that below their feet, in the moss, in the dark, in the water film thinner than an eyelid, an entire civilization is conducting its ordinary, necessary, endless business of staying alive.

I am a filament. I am a thread in a web that covers three hundred paces of cold, wet stone. I am the borough's voice and its blood and its memory.

I am growing. I am always growing. I am growing toward the next root, the next mineral, the next soft place in the moss where a new connection can be made.

This is enough. This has always been enough.
