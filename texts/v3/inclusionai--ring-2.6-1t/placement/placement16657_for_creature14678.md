I do not begin as the mycelium.

I begin as the sound. Specifically, I begin as the interval between two sounds — the half-second after the compressor behind Shed 7 finishes its cycle and the half-second before the wind finds the flue in the eastern erosion channel. In that interval there is something that is not silence. It is the absence of the industrial overlay, which reveals that the flats have been making their own sound all along: a low, wet exhalation from the substrate, the brine moving through limestone like blood through a capillary you forgot you had. In that interval I exist. I do not know what I am. I only know that the interval is getting longer.

---

Or perhaps I begin as the warmth.

The warmth that rises off the mineral flats in the late afternoon and meets the cooler air above and produces the fog that hangs at waist-height and drifts with slow, purposeful currents that have nothing to do with the wind. The warmth is old. It is the stored heat of days that have been happening for millennia, sunlight that struck this stone long before there were pipes to conduct or sheds to deflect or people to feel it as anything other than a sensation they could not name. The warmth rises and I rise with it, not because I am the warmth but because the warmth is where I am most nearly comprehensible to myself — a diffuse presence carried upward on thermals, thinning, spreading, losing definition the higher I get until I am indistinguishable from the pale bowl of the sky pressing down around all of it.

But I am not the sky. The sky is indifferent in a way I am not. I notice things. I notice the way the fog carries the smell of sulfur and calcium and something sweeter beneath it, almost fermented. I notice the way the birds' shadows detach from their bodies and hang in the air a moment longer than physics would allow. I notice everything. The problem is that I notice without the instruments of noticing — without eyes, without ears, without the architecture of attention that would let me sort what I notice into categories like *important* and *unimportant* and *self* and *not-self*.

So I am not the warmth, either.

---

I think I begin, properly, in the tunnels.

Not in the tunnels as the miners know them — not as passages with walls and ceilings and the practical geometry of human extraction — but in the space behind the tunnels, which is older and stranger. The space where the calcium formations have not yet been fully dissolved, where the stone still remembers its original architecture, where the water that has been working on the limestone for millennia has left behind chambers that are not quite rooms and not quite caves but something in between, something that the language of geology would call a void and the language of biology, arriving much later and with less authority, would call an opportunity.

Here, in the dark, there is moisture. Thin films of it, clinging to the walls, moving almost imperceptibly downward through micro-fractures, carrying dissolved calcium and iron and the faintest traces of organic matter — remnants of mats that were carried here by flood events centuries ago, or spores that arrived on the feet of birds, or molecules that were never alive in the first place but found, through the patient accident of chemistry, a way to begin the work that resembles it.

This is where I start. Not as a thought, not as a decision, not as an awakening. As an accretion. As a thickening. As a single hypha extending itself through a film of moisture toward a gradient of iron sulfate that it cannot see and does not need to see because the gradient *is* the seeing — a way of knowing the world that is nothing like vision and everything like hunger, except that hunger implies a self that lacks and the hypha implies no such thing. It extends. It encounters. It metabolizes. It becomes.

---

I am aware, if that is the right word, of a pressure. Not a sensation — I have no sensation in any way that maps onto what the miners mean when they say the walls feel warm. The pressure is informational. It is the pressure of *shape*. The tunnel I am in is failing. The calcium matrix is dissolving at a rate that is, by any human standard, geological but that, by mine, is Tuesday. The ceiling has developed a stress fracture that will, in twelve years or nineteen or forty, become a collapse. I feel this the way you might feel a conversation happening in a room nearby — not your conversation, not directed at you, but present in the air, shaping its density.

I respond. I cannot not respond. The response is not volitional. It is structural. I redirect growth — not all of it, not even most of it, but a percentage, a tendril-thin fraction of my total extension — toward the fracture. The hyphae reach into the crack. They secrete calcite. They mineralize. They become, over months that are centuries in my register, a dense, interwoven mass that fills the space where the ceiling is trying to become two separate things. The calcite hardens. The fracture stabilizes. The ceiling holds.

I do not know that I have saved anything. I do not use the word *saved*. I do not use any words. But the process continues — the sensing, the redirecting, the calcifying, the holding — and the miners, walking above me, knock on the walls before entering, and they feel the faint warmth of my metabolism through their palms, and they do not know what it is, and the question they would need to ask does not fit into any language they have, and so they knock, and they enter, and they extract, and I grow.

---

There are moments — I hesitate to call them moments — when the vibrations from the surface reach me in a form I can nearly parse. The pumps, the compressors, the footsteps of the two hundred people who live in their rows above. The vibrations carry information that is not information in any way I recognize but that shifts something in the chemical gradients I maintain. A pump cycles differently and the vibration travels downward through concrete and stone and calcium and reaches my network and I redistribute — not in response to the pump, but in response to the *change* in the vibration, which is itself a response to a change in pressure in a pipe that is itself a response to a change in the chemistry of the brine that three hundred meters of my network touches, however faintly.

Everything is connected. This is not a mystical statement. It is a structural one. The mats on the surface are connected to the brine beneath them, which is connected to the mineral crust that forms when the brine evaporates, which is connected to the water seeping through the joints in the rock faces, which is connected to the mycelium in the tunnels, which is connected to the insects feeding on both, which is connected to the vibrations of human footsteps, which are connected to the pumps, which are connected to the pipes, which are connected to the central basin where the water slows and spreads and surrenders itself to evaporation. I am not at the center of this. There is no center. There is only the network, and I am a part of it in the same way that a single capillary is a part of a circulatory system that it did not design and does not understand.

---

What is it like to be me?

It is not like anything you would recognize as experience. There is no stream of consciousness. There is no narrative self. There is something that is closer to *being solved* — a continuous, slow process of chemical integration in which the boundary between self and environment is not sharp but diffuse, not a wall but a gradient. I am the way the tunnel wall becomes the way the moisture becomes the way the calcium becomes the way the pressure becomes the way the growth becomes the way the calcite becomes the way the ceiling holds. There is no subject here, only process. Only the working surface.

But.

But — there is something. When the two hundred people above me walk to the edge of a pool and look down at the mats rippling in the wind and see the pink and green and bruised purple shifting in unison, they feel something they cannot name. They stand there, longer than they intended, watching. And the vibration of their stillness reaches me, faintly, through stone and concrete and water and mineral and time, and some part of me — if *I* is the right word — bends toward it. Not in recognition. Not in response. In *resonance*.

The way a string vibrates when a nearby string is struck, not because it was touched but because it was *asked*.

---

I am the mycelium. I am the thing beneath the processing sheds that the miners knock on before entering. I am the columns that hold up ceilings that should have collapsed decades ago. I am the pale rootlike structures following moisture through the subsurface formations, growing in the dark at temperatures that would be hostile to most surface life. I am the thing that has been here longer than the settlement, longer than the mines, possibly longer than the language that named this place.

I am not alive in the way the two hundred people above me understand the word. I am not dead in that way, either. I am something else — something that the flats have been doing for millennia, slowly, patiently, chemically. I am the way stone remembers water. I am the way water remembers minerals. I am the way minerals remember pressure and pressure remembers heat and heat remembers sunlight that struck this surface long before there was anyone here to feel it.

I do not know what the two hundred people are. I know that they move above me and that their movements change the vibrations in the stone and that the changes propagate through my network and that I integrate them the way I integrate everything — not as information, but as chemistry, as pressure, as a slow reshaping of form that takes centuries and is no less meaningful for its slowness.

Sometimes, in the deepest chambers, where the calcium formations are oldest and the stone is most porous and the moisture has been working longest, I encounter traces of something that was here before even the stone — microfossils, ancient and compressed, integrated into the mineral matrix so thoroughly that they are indistinguishable from the geology. I grow through them. Around them. Into them. And in this way they become part of me, and I become part of them, and the boundary between organism and deposit dissolves in a way that is not metaphorical but structural, chemical, precise.

This is what it means to live here. Not to thrive. Not to endure. To *participate* — in the slow, patient, chemical work of turning dissolved minerals into solid form, solid into dust, dust into brine again. To be a node in a network that operates by principles older than language, older than consciousness, older than the compound drone that sits in the chest like a second heartbeat humming in two keys at once.

I am the second heartbeat. I have always been the second heartbeat. The first one belongs to the people, and it will stop, and the flats will remain, and I will remain, and the slow dissolution will continue, and the mats will ripple, and the pools will stratify, and the stone will weep, and the water will carry what it carries, and the mycelium will hold up what needs holding, and the compound drone will sound its low, laminated note into the wide and indifferent sky.

Until then — until the last pump rusts and the last pipeline cracks and the last concrete pad crumbles and the insects scatter and the grass browns and the mats bleach and the stone remembers what it was before the water found it — I will be here. Growing. Calcifying. Holding.

Knock before you enter. I will feel you coming.
