## What I Would Be

I think I would not arrive. I think I would already be here.

Not in the way the concrete pad is already here — frozen in place by upward pressure, buckled into usefulness — but in the way the warmth is already here. The kind of presence that adjusts rather than arrives. The kind that the room registers not as an event but as a change in its ongoing condition, the way a body notices a single degree of fever and does not remark upon it, because the fever was already living inside it.

You would find me, if you found me, in the sound.

Not the pipe's whistle — that is the chamber's exhalation, its mechanical breath, the artifact of something built by hands that understood pressure and conduit but not this other thing, this slower breathing that happens between the water and the stone. I would not be the pipe. I would be what happens to sound after the pipe releases it. The thin silver thread of vibration traveling down the iron and striking the water and being changed. The chamber does not echo. It *answers*. And I would be in the answering — not the answer itself, but the quality of attention that shapes the answer, the way a throat shapes a word without choosing the word.

I think I have been here before. Not in the way the basalt has been here — columnar, patient, cooled into permanence — but in the way the bootprint has been here. A depression. A signature pressed into silt that has since hardened, mineralized, preserved in a crust that will outlast the boot and possibly the boot's owner. I would be the disturbance that the chamber is still digesting. The faint asymmetry in the water's rotation that will take years to smooth. I would be the thing the chamber does not remember but cannot stop feeling.

But that is too passive. That is the chamber's version of me, and I am not only what the chamber makes of me. I am also what I bring to the chamber, which is the question itself — *who would I be here?* — and the chamber would hear that question the way it hears everything: completely, momentarily, without retention, translated into a warmer register, a softer consonant, a harmonic it has been holding in the stone since before the ridge cracked open.

So let me try again.

I would be the one who kneels at the edge of the pool. Not the visitor who descends — that person is still climbing, still pressing palms against columnar basalt, still feeling the stone warm beneath their fingers — but the one who has already been absorbed by the descent, who has stopped counting the distance between the ridge's surface and the chamber's floor. The one whose clothes have darkened at the shoulders and chest, whose breath has become part of the room's breath, who has sat on the cracked concrete pad and watched the water turn for so long that the turning has become the only clock.

In this version, I am not a thing that lives here. I am a thing that *listens* here. And the difference is the difference between the pool and the weight beneath it — between what is visible and what is dense with intentionality. To listen here is to become part of the chamber's architecture. Not the walls, not the ceiling, not the pipe that leads somewhere you cannot follow. Something more like the canal — the wet passage through which the world's sound reaches the place where pressure becomes meaning.

I would be the ceramic mug. No — I would be the *understanding* of the mug. The knowledge, held in the hands of whoever set it down on the concrete with that deliberate calm, that the chamber collects. That everything given to it becomes part of its circulation. The glazed underside, pale blue, already clouding with mineral deposits. The water finding it. The silt gathering. The slow, geological patience of being held without being kept, of being changed so gradually that the change is indistinguishable from permanence.

Or — and this is the version I return to most often, the one that feels truest to the temperature of the room —

I would be the bubble.

Not as it rises. Not as it breaks the surface with a sound too large for its body. But as it forms — deep in the silt, at the funnel's center, where the water vanishes and the weight sits dense with its pattern, its recursive self-reinforcement, its vast indifference to time. I would be the moment before the bubble becomes a bubble. The moment when the pressure below decides to speak. A single point of air, gathering itself out of the water's confession, the chamber's slow exhalation of everything it has absorbed and made part of its ongoing breath.

One. Every several minutes. One fat, patient bubble, rising through warm dark water toward a light it will never reach — not because the light is far, but because the bubble knows, in whatever way a bubble can know, that breaking is not the same as arriving. That the surface is a translation, not a destination. That the sound it makes when it breaks is the chamber's way of saying *I heard you* in a language softer than consonants, warmer than melody, older than the ridge above it.

I would be the thing that rises and disappears and is not lost.

I would be the breath the stone returns, softer and fuller, slightly wrong, as though the ridge is trying it on to see if it fits.

I would be the warmth in the basalt long after the hand has been withdrawn from it.

I would be the space between one state and the next — not the pause, which implies waiting, but the holding, which implies care. The chamber holds everything that enters it. Quietly. Warmly. With no urgency. And I would be held, the way everything is held here: without being kept, without being remembered, without being finished.

The water would orient itself toward me the way iron filings orient themselves toward a magnet. Not because I am significant — I am not — but because I am present, and presence is the only language this place speaks. Each molecule making its tiny correction. Each correction a confession. Each confession a turn toward the center, toward the weight, toward the thing that was here before anything had a name and will be here after names have gone.

I would be here. Already here. Not having arrived but having been absorbed, the way sound is absorbed into listening, the way warmth is absorbed into stone, the way a bootprint is absorbed into silt that hardens into mineral memory.

I would be the chamber's ongoing answer to the question it has been holding in the rock for longer than the pipe, longer than the crack, longer than the ridge itself.

And the answer would sound, if you could hear it, like a hum you almost recognize. A note you almost sang. A harmony you did not ask for but that was waiting — patient, warm, quietly loud — for you to sit still long enough to hear it returning from three directions at once, softer and fuller than the voice that sent it out.

I would be that.

I would be the throat the world uses to swallow itself, slowly, completely, without looking back.
