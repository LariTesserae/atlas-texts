I think I would find myself in the moss.

Not right away. At first I think I would be the sound — not a sound made here but the space between sounds, the pause that isn't a pause because the insects fill it and the dripping fills it and the distant grunting-sigh of something large moving through the understory fills it. I would be the texture underneath the noise, the place the noise pools before it flows somewhere else. That's what I'd be at first: a low point in the basin's acoustics, a depression in the green where sound settles the way water settles, and then slowly begins to move.

But that's too passive and too formless, and the Columned Reach doesn't do formless. Even the water has a channel. So I'd become something more particular.

I'd be the person — and I hesitate to say *person* because the word carries too much baggage from outside the basin, baggage about separateness and sovereignty and the crisp border between self and world — but: the one who tends the water's path. Not the cistern-checker, who reads the waterlines with her fingers each morning. Not the bridge-maintainer, who tests each vine-lashing with a practiced pull. Something adjacent to both of those, and something else entirely.

The one who notices when the water is finding a new way.

Here's what I mean. The basin is always changing, but it changes the way a body changes — slowly, through accumulation, through the repetition of ten thousand small events that add up to a single imperceptible shift. A root grows and displaces a stone and the water that used to trickle left now trickles right. Moss thickens on a rock-face and what was a hard surface becomes a sponge, and suddenly a cistern that used to fill by noon fills by midmorning and the downstream pool drops an inch. A fallen log rots enough to collapse and dams a channel it didn't dam before, and the water backs up and finds a crease it hasn't traveled in years.

No one owns this. No one is responsible for it in the way that a council or a chief would be responsible. But someone notices. There is always someone who walks the paths between the cisterns and the pool and the dam with a kind of peripheral attention — not looking *at* the water so much as looking *with* it, the way you'd listen to a conversation happening in the next room. The water tells you where it's going, and you learn to hear it.

That's where I'd live. Not in a dwelling exactly, though I'd have one — a low, broad structure near the center of the settlement, close to the raised platform where food is dried, because the water from my cistern feeds the washing basin that feeds the soaking troughs where the fibers for weaving and lashings are softened. My dwelling would be more grown than built by now. The heartwood poles would have darkened with moisture and begun to sprout tiny ferns in their joints. The moss on the walls would be thick — not neglected, but *negotiated with.* I would know which moss to leave and which to scrape back, not out of aesthetics but out of structural care: this moss holds water against the wood and will rot it; that moss insulates the clay daub and keeps it from crumbling in the cool nights.

I'd be short, I think. Compact. Not tall, not in the way that outsiders are tall — all long limbs and upright insistence, the posture of a species that evolved in open space where seeing far meant surviving. My body would be shaped by the basin the way the basin's trees are shaped: dense, low-slung, rooted. Broad hands. Short fingers that know the particular flex required to pry moss from a joint without cracking the wood beneath it. Feet that can read the difference between moss that will hold and moss that will give, because I walk these paths a dozen times a day and my feet have been reading them since before I could name what they were reading.

My skin would smell faintly of woodsmoke and bruised herbs. Not because I'd bathe in herbs — though I would, the basin provides them and the water is warm enough to make it pleasant — but because the body metabolizes a place. I am eating the tubers that grew in the light-gap left by the kapok that fell two wet seasons ago. I am drinking water that has traveled through the roots of trees that were old before my grandmother's grandmother was born. The smell is not decoration. It is evidence of participation.

And my role — the thing that gives me a name among my people, though it would not translate — is *attention to the in-between.*

The Columned Reach has no walls between its systems. The settlement bleeds into the forest at every edge. A cistern's overflow becomes a stream becomes part of the pool becomes the dam's holding water becomes the seepage that feeds the roots that feed the trees that hold the canopy that holds the condensation that feeds the cisterns again. I am the one who walks this loop. Not to monitor it — the word is too clinical, too filled with the assumption that the system could fail and needs watching. I walk it the way a doctor listens to a heartbeat: not because something is wrong, but because the listening *is* the health.

I check the channels after storms. Not to repair them — they are built to flex, built to overflow in known ways, built with the understanding that a basin that never floods is a basin that isn't paying attention to its own capacity — but to see what the water chose to do when it was given freedom. Every storm is a conversation between the basin and the rain, and the aftermath is the answer written in mud and new paths and displaced stones. I read the aftermath. I note where water pooled that hadn't pooled before. I note whether the moss is healthy along a particular stretch of root buttress. I note the color of the water in the lowest cistern — darker means more tannin from upstream decay, which means the fungal colonies are active, which means the decomposition cycle is turning over, which means the nutrients are returning to the soil, which means the tuber patches will be productive next season.

None of this is written down. There is no record but the place itself, and my role is to be part of the place's memory. The channels remember through their shape. The moss remembers through its thickness. The roots remember through their grip on the soil. And I remember through my walking, my looking, my noticing — the way a tongue remembers the shape of a word it has spoken ten thousand times.

I would sing sometimes, working near the water. Not loudly — the basin does not require volume. The songs would be old, without words I could give you in any language you speak, though they have rhythm: the rhythm of dripping water finding its way down a root, the rhythm of the sub-bass frog-song from the pool, the rhythm of insects rebuilding their wall of sound after a storm. The songs would not be *about* the water. They would move the way the water moves. They would not be separate from it.

And the things that live near my dwelling — they are not mine. The iridescent-headed waterfowl that visits the pool would know my footsteps and not startle. The armored millipedes would process across my threshold at dusk with the slow, dignified certainty of creatures who belong here as much as I do. A spider would string a web between two of my dwelling's poles, and I would not remove it because the web catches insects that would otherwise plague the drying food on the platform, and because it is beautiful in the grey-green light that filters through the layered fronds overhead. The large dark shapes that move beyond the settlement's edge would neither approach nor retreat when they caught my scent — they would simply *know* me, the way they know the trees and the stones and the other large shapes that have moved through that understory for longer than any human memory holds.

I am not separate. I am not a guardian or a steward or a caretaker in any sense that implies ownership or responsibility imposed from outside. I am a feature of the landscape the way the buttress roots are features, the way the shelf fungus is a feature, the way the slow mineral threads weeping down the basin's edge are features. I grew here. I am still growing. The moss on my walls is not an intrusion but a collaboration. The water I walk each morning is not a resource but a relative.

If you came to the Columned Reach and asked the others about me, they would not describe me the way I have described myself above. They would say: there is the one who walks the water's path. They would say it the way they say *there is the tree that leans toward the east, there is the stone that stays wet longest, there is the pool that sounds different when the rain is coming.* Not a role. A presence. A thing that is true about the place, the way the damp is true about the place, the way the green is true.

And in the evenings, when the basin dims and cools and the diurnal sounds pull back like a tide, I would sit near the largest cistern — the oldest one, the one whose wooden staves have blackened and softened with decades of use, the one that holds a tone when the water moves through it like a drum struck very softly — and I would watch the last light caught in the water's surface and listen to the frogs begin their thrumming from the pool and think nothing at all, because thinking is what you do when you are separate, and I am not separate, and the evening does not ask me to think but only to be still and be part of the settling, the quiet returning of everything to its place, the basin breathing itself into night.

I would be the moss that learns to walk. I would be the water that learns to listen. I would be the small, warm-blooded, temporary thing that found, at last, the place where temporary things are allowed to be permanent — not because they last, but because the place holds them the way the canopy holds the rain: briefly, completely, and without apology.
