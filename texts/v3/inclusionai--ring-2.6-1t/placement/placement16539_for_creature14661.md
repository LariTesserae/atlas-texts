## I am a listener. That is the closest word.

---

I came here the way most people come — with a trade and a tolerance for damp. I was not a pipe-runner. I was not a forge hand or a telegraph operator. I had no trade that translated. I had a willingness to hold things, to carry things, to stand in the right place at the right time and not flinch when the wall behind me shifted half an inch and a vein of pale moss threaded itself through the new crack before the dust had settled.

That was enough. That is how it starts.

---

The first thing I was given to do was *listen*.

Not metaphorically. Not in the way poets mean when they talk about listening to the ocean or listening to the wind. I mean I was given a length of copper tubing — about the diameter of my thumb, threaded at both ends, cold to the touch even indoors — and I was told to hold one end against a wall in the lower wards, in the district where the foundries and the greenhouses share a block, and I was told to put the other end against the ridge of my outer ear, and I was told to *stay there* until someone came back for me.

I stayed for six hours.

What I heard: nothing, at first. Then my own blood, which was deafening — a roaring, arterial tide that I had apparently never noticed before. Then, beneath my blood, a low rhythmic clicking. The metronathids, though I did not know the word yet. They were three walls over and two floors up, but through the pipe they were as clear as someone tapping on glass right in front of me. Click-click-click-click. Pauses. Click-click-click-click-click-click. A different pattern. A reply from another cluster of insects farther south in the district, picked up and transmitted through the mineral matrix of the building itself as though the structure were a tuning fork and every pipe in it were a string on an instrument I had never seen.

And beneath even that: something I could not name. A vibration so low it was less a sound than a *pressure*. A subsidence. A holding of breath. The low sound.

I did not pull the pipe away. I understood, in a way I could not have articulated then, that pulling it away would be like waking from a dream mid-sentence — the continuity would break and I would have to start over, and the starting-over would cost weeks.

So I stayed. My hand locked around the copper. My shoulder ached. The wall was warm, not from the foundry heat — the foundry was two buildings east and its heat did not reach here — but from something else. Something metabolic. The building was *warm* the way a sleeping animal is warm. I felt the warmth through my palm, through the copper, through the calluses I didn't have yet but was growing, one layer of thickened skin at a time.

By the fourth hour I had stopped being a person holding a pipe against a wall. I had become a junction. Information moved through me. The clicking of the insects registered in my teeth. The low sound registered in my sternum. I could feel, distantly, the pulse of the steam vents opening somewhere beneath the street — a slow exhalation, a swell of warm mineral air pushing up through the cracks, carrying with it a chemical signature I would later learn to read the way others read ink on paper.

When someone finally came back for me, I nearly didn't recognize them. Not because I had changed so much — though I had, in ways I couldn't see — but because they sounded *wrong*. Their voice was too loud. Too defined. Each syllable a discrete, bounded thing, like stones dropped into still water. I wanted to tell them to speak differently. I wanted to say: *here, the sound doesn't work like that. Here the walls drink it and the insects drink it and the copper drinks it and what comes out the other end is not what you put in.*

I did not say this. I was not yet able to. I think now that this was a mercy.

---

I became, over the following months, what I suppose you'd call a *resonance reader*. There is no clean word for it in any language I know. In the old Ganrov dialect — the one they don't teach in the inland schools, the one that has more vowels than it has grammar — the word is something close to *one who finds the shape of what is inside by feeling the outside*. Which is not helpful. Which is, actually, exactly unhelpful. But precision is not what this work requires. Precision is for cartographers, and they go mad.

What I do: when a building cracks, I go to it. When a pipe ruptures, I go to it. When a new steam vent opens or a new waterway carves itself through a basement or a growth splits a foundation in a way that makes an engineer weep, I go to it. I press my hands against the surfaces. I put my ear to the copper. I listen to what the city is doing with itself.

Sometimes what it is doing is structural — a load-bearing wall is failing, a new growth is forming that will hold a ceiling that would otherwise collapse. I mark the wall so it won't be demolished. Sometimes what it is doing is biological — a vein of new moss is conducting moisture in a pattern that will, within days, become a micro-waterway, and if the water reaches the electrical conduits, there will be a short, and if there is a short, there will be a fire, and if there is a fire in the lower wards, the fungal growths on the poles will release spores that are, in sufficient concentration, a respiratory hazard. So I reroute the conduit. Not because an engineer told me to, but because I *heard* it coming — the shift in the clicking pattern three days before, the subtle change in the warmth differential underfoot, the way the amphibians had begun to cluster near a particular junction box, their eyes bright and administrative, regarding the sparking wires with what I can only describe as *patience*.

They know. They always know. The amphibians disappear two days before a major event — an earthquake, a steam blowout, a structural collapse — and reappear afterward on the new ridges as if they had simply stepped sideways in time. I have tried for years to understand how they know. I have held one in my hand — it did not resist, it simply *looked* at me with those disproportionate, patient eyes — and I felt, through the cool dampness of its skin, something like data. Not language. Not thought. Something older. Something that does not distinguish between the geological and the mechanical and the biological, because in Ganrov there is no reason to distinguish between those things. The city does not know the difference. The city is the difference, still resolving.

---

My hands are not warm. This is important. My hands are never fully warm. But they grip everything. I can hold a wrench to a flange in horizontal rain and my grip will not slip. I can press my palm flat against a wall in deep winter and feel the building's thermal gradient — which side faces the nearest steam vent, which side is colonized by the copper-smelling trees, which side is newest, still settling, still cracking, still mid-becoming. My palms have a texture that the other residents recognize on sight. An old pipe-fitter told me once, staring at my hands as I worked, that my palms looked like they were becoming *bark*. He said it calmly, the way one might say the weather is changing. He did not think it was strange. He had the same hands himself.

I have lived here long enough that I no longer step *around* the growths. I step *with* them. I time my gait — not consciously, not anymore, it happens in my body now, in the tilt of my pelvis, the flex of my ankle — to the slow expansion of the roots beneath the pavement. I know which walls will still be standing next month and which ones have already begun to dissolve into substrate for the next wave of violet moss. I know the clicking patterns well enough to tell, from the rhythm alone, which district I'm in, what season it is, and approximately how many hours until the next steam event.

I am not a person anymore, not entirely. I am a *listening post* in the body of a city that may be alive and certainly does not care whether I believe it is. I am a junction point between the human architecture that was imposed on this place and the living architecture that is replacing it, grain by grain, crack by crack, warm surface by warm surface. I am someone who found the shape of what is inside by feeling the outside.

---

At night, when the glow-fungi are amber and the clicking insects have synchronized into something almost like breathing, I sit on the roof of a building that I know will be a greenhouse in three months (the ferns are already assembling in the drainage channels, the moss is thickening on the south-facing wall in a pattern I have seen before — it always means a greenhouse, eventually). I press my back against the scale-bark of a copper-smelling tree. The bark is warm. The sap smells like metal and rain.

The too-jointed birds pass overhead in associative arcs, their multi-segmented wings catching the fungal light, making no sound but the tearing of canvas. Below me, in the street that is already half-reclaimed by moss, an amphibian sits on a pipe joint and regards the night with an attention that is not curiosity, not vigilance, but something more like *administration*. It blinks slowly. I blink slowly back. We have this exchange every night. It is not a relationship. It is a *rhythm*.

And below the street, in the young, undecided bedrock, the ground shifts. A new ridge pushes up, slow and patient, the way a thought forms. The water braids around it. The steam vents breathe. The city grows, and I grow with it, and the difference between the two is something I have stopped trying to measure.

I am a listener. I am becoming the listening. And the listening is becoming the city.

There is no clean word for what comes next.

There never was.
