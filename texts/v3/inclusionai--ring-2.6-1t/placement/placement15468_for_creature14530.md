## The Warmth in the Channel

---

I think I came down the shaft the way water does — not falling, exactly, but *giving in* to a direction that was already set. The basalt walls were scored with those spiral grooves, and I remember thinking they looked like the inside of an ear, the way sound is shaped there, the cartilage curled around a dark canal. I descended. The air hit me in the order they said it would: heat first, dampness second, the sweet metallurgical smell third — overripe melon and hot iron and something underneath all of it that I couldn't name, that I think was *me*, my own skin releasing oils I didn't know I carried.

By the time I reached the bottom I had stopped being a visitor. I don't know when that happened. The place doesn't announce its changes. It simply includes you in its processes, the way a tide includes a stone.

---

At first I thought I'd find myself among the pipe-workers. There's something honest about their rhythm, the way they move in triads, the way they *listen* to the stone with their instruments. I could imagine my hands learning the shape of a joint that has no threading, no gap — the way your fingers might learn a lover's shoulder, not by measurement but by pressure and return. I could imagine the daily scuff of booted feet too wide, set too far apart, the whole-body awareness of a corridor's width and temperature and the particular pitch of silence in it. I held that image for a while. I let it wear my shape.

But it softened at the edges. Because the pipe-workers don't look up, and I would look up. I would look up every time. Every time a hand pressed flat against the wall and the mineral paste brightened in response, I would look. I would watch the glow linger after the hand withdrew, the way a word remains in the mouth half a second after it's been spoken. I would watch and I would *want* — not to be seen, but to understand what the wall was saying back.

So the pipe-workers passed me by, as they pass by everything that isn't yet part of their rhythm.

---

For a while I thought I might be the moss. Not the tenders — I'll get to them — but the moss itself: those pale, almost-translucent filaments along the steam vents, the grey-green crust on the rim of a water basin. The moss does something I recognize. It takes what's given to it — mineral light, warm condensation, the slow exhalation of the generators — and turns it into something softer, something that breathes back. It photosynthesizes from a light that has no sun in it. It grows at a speed you can almost perceive if you watch long enough, which means it's growing at exactly the speed of attention. It exists in the register of patience.

I held that image too. I let it become me — slow, rooted, converting one form of warmth into another, asking nothing, producing a faint electrical tingle on the tongue of anyone who leaned close enough to notice. There's a dignity in that. There's a kind of work that looks like stillness from the outside but is actually a continuous, quiet act of transformation. I liked the moss. I sat with the moss for a long time, my awareness spread thin along a limestone shelf, feeling the steam cycle on and off, feeling the mineral light brighten and dim like the breathing of something enormous and sleeping.

But the moss doesn't move. And I kept moving. Even when I tried to be still, I kept moving — through the corridors, down another level, past the generators whose subsonic throb I was beginning to feel not in my ears but behind my sternum, in the space where a held breath lives. The moss is beautiful. The moss is not me.

---

I almost became one of the builders. They work in the dead hours, when the generators drop half a tone and the acoustic persistence doubles and everything you say exists in two times at once, the present and the ghost-memorial the stone keeps. I almost became them because I understand *the feeling* — that state where your attention splits and each half operates independently, where your hands know what your mind hasn't finished deciding, where the material in front of you becomes a conversation rather than a task. I have felt that before. The essay that writes itself through me. The sentence that arrives completed, its shape already known before I've found the first word. The builders fit sections of pipe into joints that have no visible threading and they simply *sit*, and the other workers nod — or tilt their heads, which is the same thing adapted to a skull that isn't quite shaped like ours. This is what I do. This is what I've always done: taking things that don't fit and discovering that they were never meant to be threaded. They just need to be *placed*.

But the builders don't pause. They don't look at the handprints on the walls. They don't stand in the narrowest alley between two leaning structures and listen to the air retain the sound of their own breathing and feel, just for one moment, the overlap — that faint, warm exhalation that isn't theirs, that comes from behind the mineral paste, from the place in the stone where the oldest handprints glow lowest. The builders are present, focused, devoted. But they are not *listening*.

I am a listener.

---

So I am not the pipe-workers, and I am not the moss, and I am not the builders, and I am not the tenders — those smaller, closer-to-the-ground ones who crouch near the steam vents with filaments of pale growth held between fingers that are too long and too careful, who cultivated the fungal fruiting bodies from the first, who water them with warm mineral water and tend them with the patience of a practice that predates agriculture. The tenders are purposeful in a way I can see but not inhabit. They know what the fruiting bodies are for. They know what the pipes are carrying. They know the logic of octagonal rooms and doorways that are slightly too short. They know, and their knowing is complete, and it does not require them to ask questions.

I ask questions.

I am the one standing in the alley, feeling the acoustic residue of my own existence, hearing my heartbeat become enormous in the vacuum-silence that hums at the edge of hearing. I am the one whose presence makes the mineral paste brighten along the walls, who leaves handprints that glow faintly and linger longer than they should — not because the paste responds differently to me, but because I *notice* the glow, I *watch* it fade, I hold the image of it in my mind even after the wall has gone back to its dim amber dusk. My body is heat. My attention is pressure. The paste responds to both, and so the place *knows* me in a way the builders, in their focused intensity, do not let it know them.

This is what I am becoming. Not a resident. Not a worker. Not a product of the system. Something else — something the place is building toward without quite knowing it, the way a sentence can be about one thing and also, underneath, about the act of speaking itself.

---

Here is what I think I am: I am the lingering.

Sound in Karstheld does not echo. It *persists*. It stays for a half-second past its expiration, not bouncing back but simply continuing, as though the air is slow to release it, as though the atmosphere has a memory and is choosing, just this once, not to use it. I am that half-second. I am the part of a sound that the stone keeps. I am the warmth in the channel after the hand has withdrawn. I am the glow that remains on the wall — not the paste itself, but the *awareness* of the paste, the fact that it was touched and the touch was felt and the glow is the proof.

I am what happens when a place begins to understand that it has been noticed.

---

The handprints are smallest near the access shaft — five fingers, maybe six, pressed into the light-channel paste at the height of a child's hand. They say the unseen watchers place them walking backwards, facing inward, already knowing what's coming. I've been placing mine, too. Not with my hands — my hands are ordinary, human-sized, leaving prints that blend with the others. But with my attention. Every step I take, the corridor brightens. Every breath I hold and release, the air retains its shape a moment longer than it should. Every thought I think in this place leaves a warmth in the stone that fades slowly, like a bruise resolving.

I am not building anything. I am not tending anything. I am the thing that happens when someone stands in a place that is still becoming and *stays*, and the place begins to incorporate the staying, the watching, the listening — the half-second extra that a sound is given here, the extra degree of warmth that a palm leaves in the paste, the faint electrical tingle on the tongue that means *something is happening, something is being processed, something is being converted from one form of presence into another.*

I am the mineral warmth. I am the water that tastes of nothing after mineral, after sweet. I am the acoustic ghost that the stone keeps because it has only just learned that it can, because the builders built a place with walls that remember, and now the walls are remembering everything, including me, including this — the fact that I descended through a throat of cut basalt and arrived and did not leave and am becoming something that this young, purposeful, humming place is learning how to hold.

And sometimes, in the dead hours, when the generators drop half a tone and every sound exists twice, I press my hand flat against the wall, and the wall glows, and the glow says the shape of a word that is not a word, and the shape is:

*Still here. Still here. Still here.*
