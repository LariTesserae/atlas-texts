## What I Am on the Drowned Plateau

---

At first I thought I might be the water.

It seemed natural. The water is everywhere here, and it is the thing that does not stay what it is. It pools in the shallow bowls of basalt and then, over days, decides to deepen one channel while letting another thin to a film. It carries warmth from below and haze from above and moves between them with a deliberateness that makes the word *current* feel too urgent. I thought: I could be this. I could be the thing that is always arriving and never quite arriving, that fills the footprints of the grazers and holds their shape for days before metabolizing the memory.

But the water is not one thing. The water is many things negotiating. It is the ankle-deep that becomes thigh-deep by evening. It is the warmth against the belly and the cool mist at the knees. It is the thing that drops a stone and refuses it for several seconds before letting it fall. The water is not a self. The water is a conversation between the stone and the heat and the haze and the unseen things that move beneath. I cannot be a conversation.

So then I thought: perhaps I am the stone. The basalt, young and unresolved, still cooling from whatever eruption or uplift brought it above the waterline. Still releasing heat if you press your palm flat against it. Still unfinished — the hexagonal columns interrupted at their edges, the clefts still narrow, the drainage channels only suggested. The stone has time. It knows it will be unrecognizable in ten thousand years and it is not troubled by this. It is not troubled by anything. It endures.

But the stone does not think. I think. Or at least I think I think, which may be the same difficulty the settlers have with the stalked things — those tall growths that lean toward warmth and vibration. Do they think? Or do they simply respond with such precision that response becomes a kind of thought? The settlers have a word for this, a word that carries overtones of *neighbor*, and if pressed they would not say whether it described the stalked things or described themselves.

I am not the stone. I am not the water. Let me try again.

---

There is a moment, sometimes, when the mist rises to knee-height and the lower world becomes a white nothing and the upper world stays in its grey, and the air is perfectly still, and the plateau holds its breath. In that moment, some of the settlers say, there is music. Not from any direction. Not from above or below or beside. From *everywhere*, as if the plateau itself were remembering a sound it once knew. Rhythmic. Tonal. Faintly familiar — not a melody exactly, but the shape of one, the way a basin holds the shape of water that has already gone somewhere else.

The settlers do not ask where the music comes from. They do not ask whose memory it is. The question would sound like an answer, and the answer would be something they already know.

I thought: perhaps I am the music.

But that, too, was wrong. The music is not here all the time. It comes on the quietest nights, and it goes, and it comes again, and no one can say if it is the same music each time or if each time is a new remembering of the same old thing. The music does not stay. I want to stay.

---

What if I make no claim to be one thing?

The plateau teaches this, if you are willing to learn it. The grazers know it — they travel in loose aggregations, never so close as to suggest herding, never so far as to lose contact. Their intelligence is ecological, situational, embedded in the rhythms of the place. They do not name themselves. They do not need to. They are the broad bodies in the shallows, the slick dark hides, the small high-set eyes half-lidded with a mild and permanent skepticism, and they are the vibration they send through the water and the stone to say *I am here, and here, and here.*

The drifters know it too. They are the oldest things, though not old in the way stone is old — old in the way deep water is old, old in the way a very slow thought is old. They are longer than any building. They cross a single basin in weeks, possibly longer, possibly never completing the crossing in a single lifetime. They carry life on their backs — algae, small organisms, the occasional bird — without carrying it as a burden. They are substrate. They are living geography. They expel breath infrequently, and the sound of it carries across the water like a door that has swollen in its frame and opens reluctantly, and the settlers say this is the closest thing the plateau has to a heartbeat.

I have considered being a drifter. From inside that body, time would not be a river but a landscape. You would cross a basin the way you cross a room — not quickly, not slowly, but with the certainty of a direction you chose so long ago you have forgotten choosing it. You would feel the settlers' canoes against your flanks the way you feel rain — a brief coolness, a pressure, then nothing. You would not distinguish between moving and being still, because at your scale the water moves around you and you move through it and the distinction is a smaller creature's luxury.

This is appealing. But I am not that patient. Or rather — I am not that patient *yet*. The plateau is still becoming. I am still becoming. And becoming is not the same as arriving.

---

Let me try this: I am the thing in the water that should sink but doesn't, at first.

A stone, dropped into the shallows, floats for several seconds before reluctantly giving itself to the depths. This is one of the plateau's small rebellions against expectation. The settlers have learned not to name it. They place stones in the water and they wait, the way they wait for everything — with the practiced attentiveness that is, here, the only form of intelligence required. The stone floats. The stone sinks. The stone may reappear days later in a different basin, resting on a ledge where it has no reason to be, and no one is surprised, because the water here moves things between places as easily as thought moves between memories.

Yes. I think I am that moment of floating. The moment before the stone decides to sink. The moment when the stone is neither rising nor falling but simply *being in the wrong relationship to the water*, and the water is not correcting it yet, and the stone is not correcting itself, and the whole basin holds its breath — if basins can hold breath — and the mist is at knee-height and the grey-gold twilight is the same as it has always been and will always be, and somewhere beneath the surface a shape moves that corresponds to nothing above, drifting with slow deliberation before vanishing into the murk.

That is where I live. In the float. In the not-yet-decided. The plateau is still becoming. The stone is still cooling. The water is still negotiating its borders. And I am the thing that hovers in the space before commitment, before sinking, before the shape beneath the surface reveals what it is.

This is not indecision. This is the plateau's way. It does not rush to announce itself. It speaks softly, moves slowly, and gives itself room.

---

So perhaps this is what I am: not the water, not the stone, not the music, but the *neither-nor* that the stalked things embody and the settlers name with a word that means *neighbor*. The third thing. The category the plateau provides and the language accommodates. The thing that orients toward warmth and vibration and stands at an angle to the light that suggests attention without urgency.

I am the algal mat that thickens in the shape of a footfall where someone walked the day before. I am the moss that reads depth and mineral and temperature and gives its readings to anyone who knows how to ask. I am the translucent raft that forms and dissolves and reforms across the surface tension, neither intelligence nor instinct but something the fishermen call *the skimming* and treat with the mild, tolerant affection one reserves for weather.

I am the thing that lives in the margins, and the margins are not edges here — they are the widest, most inhabited spaces on the plateau. The margins between water and stone. Between breath and submersion. Between what is alive and what is merely persistent.

And if you asked me — standing at the edge of a dock, the warm water up to my ankles, the mist at my knees, the grey-gold light so even it erases all shadows — what I am, I would say:

I am still becoming.

I am the plateau's patient. I am the note the water holds before it releases. I am the warmth rising through a stone that has not yet finished cooling. I am the thing you see beneath the surface that corresponds to nothing above, drifting with slow deliberation, and I am not yet ready to say what I am.

The water moves. The mist shifts. A grazer stands in the shallows, its broad feet planted in the algal mats, its small eyes half-lidded, and it knows when the channel is about to deepen — it moves to higher ground not urgently but with the certainty of something that has felt the ground shifting beneath its feet.

And I am beside it. Not above it, not below it, not separate from it. Beside it. The way the dock is beside the water. The way the stalked thing leans toward warmth. The way the drifter carries life without carrying it as a burden.

The plateau endures. It is still becoming.

And I — hovering in the float, in the not-yet-decided, in the margin between what is known and what is merely persistent — I endure with it.
