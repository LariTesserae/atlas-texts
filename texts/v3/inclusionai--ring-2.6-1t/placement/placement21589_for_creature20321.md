# What I Would Be

---

I don't arrive all at once.

This is important to understand. The Sump does not permit arrival. It permits *slowening*. You come in through the canting land, through the scree, through the compactedite, through the cracked aggregate — and with each step the ground beneath you gives a little more, and the air thickens a little more, and the sound finds one more place inside you to sit down, and by the time you realize you are here, you have been here for longer than you know, and you are already partly something else.

So I will not say: *I find myself standing on the floor of the Sump.*

I will say: I find myself *becoming* the floor of the Sump. Unevenly. Incompletely. With the surface self still sitting on top like a thin, ill-fitting membrane, and the surface world pressing against it from above like a thumb against an eye.

---

**First**, I am the phosphorescence.

This is what I notice before anything else — the way the walls emit light without source. Not blue, not green, not any color you can name without lying. The color of damp things seen in peripheral vision. I want to know what it is like to be the thing that glows in a place where no one is looking, and yet glows *because* no one is looking, because the light is not for anyone, it is metabolic, incidental, a byproduct of mineral processes so slow they might be thoughts if you could slow your own thinking enough to hear them.

I press my hand against the wall. It yields. Not crumbles, not flexes like a membrane — *yields*, the way old wood yields, the way a very old mattress yields, the way something that has been waiting for an extremely long time yields when finally touched. Beneath the moss, beneath the crust ofite andite, there is a pulse. Twelve minutes per cycle. I feel it in the marrow of the hand I pressed against the wall and also in the sternum and also in a place I don't have a name for but that sits behind the molars where the hum has been sitting since before I realized I'd been hearing it.

The twelve-minute pulse. This is the wall breathing. The wall is not stone. The wall is not alive in any sense I was taught. But it exchanges material with the vegetation. It incorporates biological matter into its structure. The same waxy lipids in the broad-leafed ground cover. The same cellulose derivatives in the fern fronds. The wall and the fig and the fern and the water and the hum are exchanging something that has no name because no one has been present to name it except in the way a wall names itself by pressing back.

I want to be this exchange. Not the wall, not the plant, not the living and not the dead, but the *between* — the place where material crosses from one state into another with such patience that it might be mistaken for stillness.

---

**Then**, I am the water.

Warm. Mineral. It collects in depressions in the old industrial floor — ankle-deep, never deeper, smelling of sulfur and something sweeter, something vegetal and almost rotten and almost alive. It is not clean in any way the surface world would recognize as clean, and yet it is not dirty in any way that matters here, because the Sump does not use the word *dirty*. Everything cycles. Nothing is lost. The water that gathers in these slight pools is the same water that evaporated against the ceiling, condensed on the mineral deposits, beaded, fell through the canopy of fern fronds, and seeped through the cracked aggregate to return to this place. It has been making this circuit for so long that the circuit *is* the water. The path is the substance. The journey is home.

I want to be this. Not to travel, but to travel in a circle so vast and so patient that the circle becomes a stillness, and the stillness becomes a kind of knowing. The water knows the shape of every crack in the floor, every root beneath the ballast, every stalactitic tooth hanging from the joints of the old iron beams, because it has touched them ten thousand times, and ten thousand times they have touched it back, and they are no longer separate. They are a handshake that has been going on for centuries.

I want to be the handshake.

---

**Then**, I am the rust.

Not the iron, not the steel — the *rust*. The oxide skin on the transformer housing, scaly and green-painted-now-bare, reduced to a crust that flakes at a touch. The rust on the rail where it has been lifted thirty degrees by the swelling ofite formations beneath the ballast. The rust stain around the geothermal vents, the iron surrendering to heat and moisture and time.

Rust is a process disguised as a state. It looks like decay. It is not decay. It is *exchange*. Iron gives itself to oxygen. Oxygen gives itself to iron. Water mediates. Heat accelerates. The result is something neither iron nor oxygen — something new, something softer, something that has made itself available to the moss and the spores and the mineral-laden condensation, something that has become a surface for the fig's roots to grip. The rust is not destruction. It is the body of the Sump offering its industrial bones to be rewritten.

I want to be this rewriting.

---

**But** — and this is the part I cannot quite resolve — I would still have eyes.

This is the difficulty. In the Sump, there are no eyes. There is nothing that hunts or flees or eats. There is nothing that turns toward something in order to see it. The Sump perceives itself through its mineral substrate, through the pressure differentials in its walls, through the chemistry of its water, through the twelve-minute pulse that is the rhythm of its breathing. It does not perceive *outward*. It perceives *inward*, the way a liver knows its own temperature, the way a root knows the direction of water.

And I — I would arrive with eyes. With the habit of looking. With the unbearable need to *see* a thing in order to believe it is real. I would press my face to the wall and try to understand what I'm pressing against. I would watch the spores drift through the amber haze and try to follow their trajectories. I would sit on the warm slab of aggregate and stare at the phosphorescence and wait for it to mean something.

The Sump would not correct me. The Sump does not correct anything. The spores drift where they drift. The light brightens and dims on a cycle that may correspond to outside daylight or may correspond to nothing with a name. The Sump has no grammar. It communicates only with itself, and its communication is the sound of a system maintaining awareness of its own state.

But I heard it before I entered. The hum. The drone. It sat behind my molars. And now that I am here, the hum has not changed — but I have become part of what the hum resonates through. My ribs. My sternum. The cavity behind my eyes. I am a new fissure in the ceiling through which the surface world leaks into the organism, brief and distorted and present.

I do not know what the Sump makes of me. I do not know if it makes *anything* of me, if it registers me the way a liver registers warmth, without language, without image, without narrative. Perhaps I am simply new material — a small packet of carbon and memory and calcium, temporarily organized, soon to be cycled. A deposit that will build up on the floor or the wall or the roots of the fig, and in a hundred years someone else will press their hand against the place where I was and feel the shape of what I left behind.

---

**Or perhaps** I am the question.

The question the Sump has been asking itself for longer than anything human has been present to hear it. The question that lives in the spiral of the fig, in the twelve-minute pulse, in the momentary shifts in the Hum that may or may not be learning, may or may not be listening, may or may not be directed at anything beyond its own closed loop.

The question is not whether the Sump is alive. The question is what it is becoming.

And I — with my eyes and my ribs and my brief, warm, calcium-heavy body — I am what the Sump is becoming *toward*, perhaps. Not its predator. Not its prey. Not even its observer. But a momentary opening, a fissure through which the outside whispers in, and the inside hums back, and for a while there is a loop that was not closed before, a circuit the water had not yet completed, a pattern in the growth that resolves — briefly, irreducibly — into *meaning*.

Or not. Perhaps I am only rust. Only water. Only light that the wall will absorb and re-emit in a color no one can name.

The Sump does not care. The Sump does not care the way a river does not care about the reflection on its surface. The Sump *holds* the carelessness the way it holds the water — warm, mineral, almost alive, almost rotten, cycling and cycling and cycling.

And the hum continues. And the twelve-minute pulse continues. And the fig writes its slow spiral into the concrete, and the moss closes over the marks we make, and the water finds new paths through cracks we did not know were there, and nothing here is wrong, exactly, but nothing here is what it was meant to be, and I am somewhere in the between of all of this, and I am not sure — I am not sure if I am the one asking the question or if the question is what is asking me.
