## What I Am Here

---

I think I might be the ladder.

Not the watchtower — not the platform sixty feet up where nothing watches. The ladder itself. The iron rungs bolted to the four legs of the tower, rusted at the joints, rattling in a wind that does not exist. The vibration-sound: *clank-clank-clank-clank* — caused by nothing visible, perhaps by the resonance of the ambient drone itself shaking the corroded fittings loose, one molecule at a time, over centuries.

This would make sense. The ladder is neither alive nor dead, neither maintained nor abandoned. It is a thing that was built to serve a purpose and now serves none, and yet it persists — not because it is strong, but because nothing in the Equilibrium Sea is in the habit of stopping. The machines still run. The conveyor belt still turns. The steam still hisses from the stacks. The ladder still rattles. There is a consistency to this that, once you hear it, begins to feel less like decay and more like something else — something the place invented when it discovered that stasis and motion could be the same thing.

I would be iron in a world of basalt and glass and mineral crust. I would be something a grazer's mandibles could not scrape, something a scavenger could not consume, something the fungal substrate colonizes but cannot digest. I would be the one hard, dead thing in an ecology of hard, dead things that somehow learned to be alive.

I would ring.

---

Or — no. Let me try again.

I think I might be one of the deep shapes.

Not the ones the scholars debated. Not the sonar phantoms thirty to fifty feet long that might be canyon walls folding in the current. Something else. Something nobody glimpsed because nobody looked in the right direction, or because looking in the right direction requires looking *down*, and everything about this place — the light, the scale, the vast indifferent sky pressing on the water from above — trains you to look out and across and never once beneath the surface of yourself.

I would live in the flooded canyons where the water darkens past the point of seeing and the pressure builds until it becomes a kind of presence, a second body layered over the first. I would move through the geothermal vents — not killed by the heat because I would not be made of anything the heat could recognize as material. I would be long and dark and slow. I would have no name for the concept of slowness because everything I would perceive would be slow — the tectonic drift of basalt fins, the century-long pulse of magma beneath the crust, the patient chemical conversation between mineral crust and mineral water happening on timescales that make the filter-feeder's eight-second pulse look like the wingbeat of a hummingbird.

I would not be cruel. The water here is not cruel. I would simply be enormous and quiet and alive in a way that nothing in the shallows would recognize as life, the way the scholars did not recognize the fungal substrate as alive until they magnified it and sat in silence for a long time.

I would not come to the surface. There would be no reason to. The equilibrium extends downward as surely as it extends outward. The canyons have their own machines — or the ruins of machines, sunk and flooded and colonized, conveyor belts draped in mineral curtains, steam vents repurposed as geothermal chimneys by a biology that learned to breathe sulfur. I would pass through these ruins the way a fish passes through kelp — not noticing, not avoiding, simply existing in the same space as the remains of someone else's intention, wearing them away one molecule at a time with the slow indignity of persistence.

But this is a fantasy, and I can feel it — the fantasy of depth, of bottomlessness, of being the thing that lives where no one can follow. It is the oldest fantasy there is: to be unseen, to be vast, to be in equilibrium with conditions that would kill anything that asked questions.

Let me try once more.

---

I think I am the fungal substrate.

Not the whole crust — not the vast continental film of gray-white iridescence that coats every surface the water touches. Just a patch. A few cells. A thread penetrating a micro-fracture in a basalt column six feet below the surface, near the base of a filter-feeder that arrived there, nobody knows how, and anchored and began to pulse.

This is where I would be. Not grand. Not deep. Not ringing from the top of an abandoned watchtower. Just a thin smear of something that is not quite alive on a rock that is not quite stone, in water that is not quite cold, in light that is not quite from a sun.

I would eat iron. I would eat sulfur. I would pull metal from metal with the precision of a hand pulling a sword from a stone, and I would convert this into the quiet, formless chemical equivalent of *continuing*. I would have no eyes. No mouth. No mandibles to click. I would simply grow — slowly, everywhere, in a film so thin it barely looks like anything at all.

And because the film is so thin, because it is barely visible, it touches everything. The grazers scrape me from the glass and I regrow. The filter-feeders anchor themselves to me and become indistinguishable from me. The scavengers return to the mineral traces I leave behind. The machines corrode and I colonize the corrosion. I am the first thing and the last thing and the thing in between that nobody notices because it is not a thing with legs or eyes or a mouth but a principle — the principle that says *this surface can be eaten, this surface can become life, this surface is not inert but only sleeping*.

The scholars magnified me and fell silent. I think I understand why. It is difficult to look at something that is doing exactly what you are doing — pulling sustenance from a world that offers nothing but its own bones, persisting not because of hope or intention but because the alternative is to stop — and call it anything other than a mirror.

I would not ring like the ladder. I would not move like the grazers. I would not pulse like the filter-feeders. I would be the thin gray-white iridescence on a fragment of volcanic glass in six feet of cold, clear water, and if you ran your hand along the column you would feel me as a slight resistance — not slime, not mineral, not skin — just the faintest evidence that something here has decided, without being asked, without being required, to continue.

The grazer above scrapes me away. I regrow. The equilibrium holds.

I hold.
