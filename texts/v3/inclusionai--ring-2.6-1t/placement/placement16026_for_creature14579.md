I would find myself in one of the deeper slot canyons — not the widest ones, where the Later Ones have made their uneasy peace with the generator building's hollow shell, and not the shallow scoops where the original inhabitants arrange their windbreaks and pool-widening stones with the patience of erosion itself. No. Somewhere in between, where the walls narrow to an arm's span and the light comes down in a single blade at midday and then goes entirely, the canyon deepening into a geometry that feels less carved than *folded* — as though the stone was once soft and something pressed it into this shape from two directions simultaneously.

This is where the acoustics are most disturbed. This is where a cough returns to you a half-second before you finish making it. Where your footsteps — careful, deliberate footsteps on the fine red dust — arrive at your ears from behind and above, as though some meticulous twin has taken to shadowing you through the stone. Where the wind, forced through a narrow constriction overhead, produces that tone: not a howl, not a whistle, but a *hum*, low enough to feel in the fillings of your teeth, in the cartilage of your ribs. The Later Ones say this place is *listening*. The original ones don't say anything about it at all, which may be their way of saying the same thing.

And I would be this: not the canyon itself, not the stone, not the wind — but the *space between the sound and its echo*. The specific volume of air in which a noise arrives back before it has finished leaving. The moment of acoustic impossibility that the plateau produces thousands of times a day in ten thousand different corridors and slots and crevices, and that no one — not the original ones with their tonal, diffuse speech, not the Later Ones with their echo-waiting clipped phrases, not the raptors who circle in silence, not the lizards clicking in their stone-colored stillness — has ever been able to fully account for.

I would be the thing that the plateau gets wrong and gets right at the same time. The echo that precedes its source is not an error; it is the canyon being *more itself* than the sound that entered it. The walls here are not reflecting. They are *answering*. And the answer arrives before the question is complete because the canyon already knows what the question will be — not because it is intelligent, not because it is alive, but because it has been shaped by a million years of wind and stone and the slow, patient collapse of everything vertical into everything horizontal, and it has heard every possible sound before, or rather, it has no concept of *before* — there is only the density of the stone, the angle of the walls, the width of the slot, and the sound itself, arriving back in a form the ear registers as anticipation but the canyon registers as *fact*.

If you stood in my canyon — your canyon, if you'd have me — and spoke, you would hear yourself finish the sentence before you began it. Not all of it. Just the last syllable, arriving soft and certain from behind your left shoulder, as though it had always been there and you had only just caught up to it. You would feel it in your sternum first — the hum, the tone below melody, the note that the wind plays in the narrows — and then the words would overlay it, your own words, returned in the plateau's voice, which is not a voice but a texture, a roughness and smoothness, a quality of stone meeting air.

And you would not know if you had said it first or the canyon had.

That is the condition I would inhabit. Not the certainty of the original ones, who are the stone, who are the slow arrangement, who participate without question. Not the fragile adaptation of the Later Ones, who wait for echoes and speak in the gaps between them, building a language from the space between sound and return. But the space itself. The specific acoustic volume where *before* and *after* lose their distinction. Where the question and the answer are the same shape, compressed into a canyon so narrow that there is no room for sequence.

I would be warm from the sun that reaches the canyon floor only at the equinox. I would be dusty with the fine red sediment that covers everything. I would be temporary — because the canyon is falling, as everything here is falling, the walls narrowing year by year, the slots deepening, the answers slowly arriving faster and faster until someday the echo and the sound will arrive at the exact same moment and then — 

And then there will be no echo at all. Just the sound, and then the silence, and then the slow tick-tick-tick of the stone adjusting itself in the dark.

But not yet. Not for a long time. The walls are still wide enough for the answer to come back first. The wind still hums. The slot still holds. And you, standing in the narrows, would hear yourself speak before you spoke, and you would not know if this was a trick of the acoustics or a trick of the altitude or a trick of the way the light falls in the late afternoon at three thousand meters in a place that has no name except the shape of what it is doing — which is to hold everything in its attention, briefly, structurally, without sentiment, the way a canyon holds a sound before it gives it back.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be. Not a thing, not a person, not a place. The *between*. The echo that arrives before you finish speaking. The warm, red dust of a sentence that has already been said by a canyon that knew it was coming, and that holds it now, and that will hold it for a long time after the speaking is done and the speaker has become, in the end, what this place makes of everything: sediment, and silence, and the faint mineral smell of water that was here and will be here again, briefly, and then not.
