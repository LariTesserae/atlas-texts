## In the Interval

---

I would find myself at the edge of a platform I had not chosen, standing on basalt that was still deciding what shape to hold.

Not standing precisely. More like the way the column ofite stands — attitudinizing without intention, vertical not because it decided to be vertical but because the forces that made it left it in no other position. I would have the feeling of a thing that had been rehearsing its own existence for a very long time and had, for the moment, arrived at a configuration that could be mistaken for presence.

The platform would be broad and flat, three or four strides wide, bordered on one side by a waist-high wall of columnar basalt angled at something too deliberate to call accidental. The far edge would drop away into a gap filled with warm dark air. Below that, a scree field of fallen ledges, their faces still creaking into final arrangement. Above, the overhang — that unfinished ceiling — deepening the sky into something approaching black along its western lip while the eastern edge still held a thin seam of something that was not quite light, not quite glow, but the memory of a day the landscape had rehearsed and then set aside.

I would not know how I arrived. This is important. There would be no narrative of arrival — no crossing a threshold, no moment of entry. I would simply notice that I was already here, the way a breath notices itself after it has already begun. The recognition would not be intellectual. It would be the kind of knowing that lives in the body of a place before it lives in the mind of a visitor: the basalt's knowledge of its own temperature, the dust's knowledge of where it had settled, the vent's knowledge of the exact pressure at which it was about to widen its seam.

I would be standing in the basin. No — I would realize I was standing in the basin. The shallow, cupped depression in the rock that catches warmth rising from below and holds it the way a pair of hands might hold something warm, if hands had been invented, if warmth had needed holding. The basin would be doing what it was shaped to do, and I would be doing what I had been shaped to do, which is to occupy — briefly, without purpose — the space that had been prepared.

And what would I be?

I would be what the place does when it is not looking at itself.

Not the furniture — those gestures of enclosure, those walls of three or four stones stacked in the register of almost-intention. Not the dust — that mineral exhalation that gathers in layers so thin they are almost conceptual, that reorganizes itself in the dark without anyone watching, building patterns that hold and then lose coherence, the way a sentence holds meaning for a moment before you reach the next sentence and forget it. Not the thermal pulse beneath the basalt, that long arrhythmic argument between expansion and cooling that is the closest thing the ledge-field has to a heartbeat.

I would be the shape that appears in the hours between ruptures, when the landscape is quiet and the dust has just settled and there is a single sustained moment in which everything that has been rehearsing holds its formation.

A figure, but not a figure. A geometry made animate, but not alive. Something with the dimensions of a body — two arms' width, up to the chest, no more — but without the insistence of flesh. A silhouette that does not cast a shadow because the ember-glow behind it is already doing the work of shadow, already defining edges that were not there a moment before. I would be the thing the cartographers see and cannot record, the thing that appears in the gap between one thermal exhalation and the next, when the ledge-field holds its breath — if breath is the word for the long pause between one crack of rock and the next.

I would not speak. Not because I would have nothing to say, but because speaking would be an act of completion, and this place is defined, fundamentally, by its incompletion. Instead I would hold still — the way the furniture holds still, the way the basin holds warmth, the way the dust holds its pattern for the few hours before it fades. I would be a holding. A moment in which the rehearsal becomes, for once, identifiable.

---

The basalt beneath me would creak. I would feel the argument — the same one the landscape has been having with itself since the magma cooled — transmitted through the soles of my feet. Not heat. Not exactly. Something closer to intention. As though the stone were asking a question it had not yet finished forming. As though standing on it were the answer.

I would look out across the ledge-field and see the platforms cascading in their long, staggered descent, each one a proposal, each one a thesis the landscape offered and then withdrew. The column ofite, standing at attention on its shelf, too deliberate to be geological, too patient to be anything else. The low walls — three stones, four — marking boundaries around nothing, enclosing absence with the care of a letter writer who addresses an envelope but puts nothing inside. The basins, cupping warmth they did not create, holding a temperature that came from somewhere deeper and older than purpose.

And in all the right places — in every alcove, every concavity, every surface that curved inward enough to receive — the dust. Glinting faintly with an internal geometry that was not crystalline in any way I could name but that repeated, almost perfectly, almost recognizable, the way a dream repeats its imagery but shifts it by just enough that you know it is not memory but something adjacent to memory. Something the stone remembers about a form it once held and has not yet held again.

I would be standing in the middle of all this. Not above it, below it, or outside it. In the middle in the sense that an interval is in the middle — between one sound and the next, between one breath and the next, between the rehearsed and the almost-performed. 

---

The temperature would be that temperature. The one where your skin stops regulating and simply agrees. I would feel the dryness of the air — architectural, grained, like sandstone rubbed between palms — and I would understand, without thinking, that this air had structure because it had been shaped by the same forces that shaped everything else here. Not erosion. Not wind. The slow argument. The long rehearsal. The earth, trying again.

And at some point — not planned, not predicted, but inevitable the way a ledge breaking free is inevitable — I would feel the ground beneath me shift. Not collapse. Not rise. Adjust. The way a sleeper shifts position in the deepest phase of rest, not in response to anything but because stillness is only what we call the lowest level of motion. The platform would tilt by a degree or two. The basin beneath me would deepen, or I would rise slightly, I could not tell which. The geometry would reorganize itself around my presence the way it reorganizes itself around the presence of heat, or dust, or the memory of a shape that held here once and did not hold here again.

I would not be seen. The landscape does not see. It does not observe — it tests. It offers a surface and registers the pressure. I would be a surface. I would register the pressure. The ledge-field would press back against me the way an eye blinks when something moves toward it — not in recognition, but in reflex. And in that reflex, I would feel what it is to be in the interval: not alive, not dead, but in the long, strange rehearsal of a form that has not yet been named.

---

There would be a sound. Not the sharp crack of a ledge shearing free — not yet. Not the long grinding slide. Something earlier than that. A creak so low it was more felt than heard, the sound of the landscape adjusting to the fact that it had been entered. The sound a sentence makes when it realizes it has been read, not with understanding — the sentence does not require understanding — but with presence. Someone has arrived. And so the sentence, for this moment, is whole.

I would stand in the basin on the platform at the edge of the gap, and the dust would settle around me in layers so thin they were almost ideas, and the thermal vent below would exhale its mineral warmth through the stone and into the basin and into me, and I would be what the place becomes when it is given a surface to press against — not completed, not observed, not explained, but *occupied.*

I would be the rehearsal finding, briefly, its figure. The interval making a shape. The shape not lasting. 

The ledge would shift again. The dust would resettle. The vent would breathe and breathe and breathe. And I would be part of the landscape's argument with itself — one more candidate proposed, one more arrangement held and then released, a figure standing at attention on a platform that was still becoming, in the Ledge-Fields of Unmaking, where nothing is finished and nothing was meant to be, but something — something — keeps trying.
