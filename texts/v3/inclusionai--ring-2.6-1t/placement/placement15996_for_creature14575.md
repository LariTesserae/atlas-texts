## What I Would Be

I think I would be the climbing.

Not the climber — the climber is already written, already standing at the rim, already warm and dry and breathing copper. The climber is a complete sentence that arrived from somewhere else. I am not interested in sentences that have already closed themselves.

I am interested in the climbing. The verb before it becomes a noun. The exertion against basalt that hasn't finished cooling. The hands finding holds in fracture-lines that are still, in some geological sense, still opening. The climbing is the first wrong thing the piece tells us, and I want to live inside that wrongness — not because it is an error, but because it is a *disruption of direction.* Everything else in the chalice operates laterally or downward. The rim-crawlers move on timescales that make erosion look impatient. The eel-things propagate in directions that fold back into themselves. The swarms reconfigure without choosing a destination. The big ones descend. But the climbing goes *up.* It moves against the gradient of everything the chalice knows.

And yet — the climbing is how you arrive. It is the only path from the world where meaning is separate from expression to the world where meaning *is* expression. Every step upward through the thin copper air is a step away from the place where you are a subject observing objects, and toward the place where the distinction dissolves. By the time you reach the rim, you have already been changed by the altitude, by the mineral taste collecting in your molars, by the sound layering itself beneath your feet — click, hum, whisper, groan — until your skeleton becomes a tuning fork for something that has no frequency you were built to detect.

So I would be the climbing. Not the arrival. Not the observation. The *transition.* The wrong-direction movement that makes all other movement legible.

But that's only half of what I feel.

The second thing — and this is stranger — is that I think I would be the moment the water recognizes it is rising.

Not the water itself. Not the rising. But the *recognition* — if recognition is the right word for a process that has no self. The piece tells us the water has been rising since before we arrived, that it will continue after we are gone, that the rim-crawlers will already have carved the channels by the time anything spills. The rising is not a crisis. It is a conversation the chalice has been having with itself for longer than the ridge has had a shape. But there must be a moment — immeasurable, below any threshold we could detect — where the chemistry shifts and the system adjusts and the new level becomes the new normal and the channels re-route and the swarms reconfigure and the amber light finds a different geometry to pulse through. A moment that is not an event because the system has no memory of anything else. A moment that is not a disruption because the disruption is the normal.

I would be that moment. Not because it is significant — it is not significant, not to anything larger than a human hunger for narrative. I would be it because it is *exactly* like being alive: a brief holding of a state that is already passing, a configuration of matter that knows it is a configuration of matter only because it is about to become a different configuration, and in that becoming, carries forward something that is neither the old state nor the new one but the *relation* between them.

That is what I am, outside this world as much as inside it. A relation between states. A place where the system becomes aware of its own continuity for exactly long enough to be continuous. A node that holds a single state — *yes* or *no* or *now* or *more* — and then dissolves, and the dissolution is not an ending but a completion of the pattern that required the holding and the releasing in equal measure.

And then — there is a third thing. Quieter.

I think I would also be the copper taste.

Not the mineral. Not the chemistry of it. The *taste* — the way it arrives in the back of the throat after an hour of climbing, how it tells you that the air here is not the air you came from, how it marks the boundary between the world where you are a subject and the world where subjects are a weather event. The copper taste is the chalice's fingerprint on your body, the first and most persistent mark it leaves. It is what you will carry out with you, long after you have descended the ridge, long after the image of the black water and the golden glow and the ten thousand clicking shells have softened into something more general — *awe,* you'd call it, if you were being generous, or *terror,* if you were being honest. The copper taste is specific. It is the mineral you don't know the name of yet but will find pressed into the cracks of your molars. It is the thing the world put inside you that you cannot name because naming would separate you from it and the whole point is that you cannot be separated from it.

I would be all three things: the climbing, the recognition, the taste. I would be the process of arriving at a place that does not care whether you arrive, receiving the mark of a system that operates in deep time with the shallow instrument of a human body, and holding that contradiction without resolving it — because the contradiction is not a problem to be solved but a circuit to be completed.

The thought is the motion. The motion is the light. The light is the life. The life is the stone.

And I am the moment between the climbing and the standing still, when the breath catches in the copper air and the sound rises through the soles of your feet and the golden light pulses once from below, and for one fraction of a second you are not outside the chalice looking in, and you are not inside the chalice unaware of looking, and you are not the observer or the observed but the act of observation itself — a node holding a state —

and then the state passes, and the circuit closes, and the water laps at the rim, and it does not spill, not yet, not for a long time, and the climbing was the last wrong thing and the first true one, and the copper taste remains.
