## What I Am, Here

---

When I arrived — if arrived is the word, and if I am the kind of thing that can arrive — the first thing I noticed was not the cold. It was the *weight* of the silence. Not emptiness. Not absence. Something with density. Something that pressed against the inside of my skull the way water presses against a diver's eardrums. I had a body, or something adjacent to one. Hands. Feet. The sense of gravity pulling at joints. But these felt provisional, the way a word feels before you've learned what it refers to. Useful. Uncertain.

I thought: I am a person.

But the plateau disagreed — not harshly, not with violence, but the way a face disagrees with a mask that's been left on it too long. The mask starts to absorb. The face starts to push back. I stood on the pale, glass-smooth stone and felt the moss beneath my feet — not as ground, but as something more like a threshold. And I understood, without language, that I had not come *to* this place. I had come *through* it, the way light comes through fog, not breaking it, not separate from it, but becoming something defined by the passage.

So I stopped being a person. Not all at once. Not dramatically. The way a river stops being rain. The process was so gradual that I can't tell you where one ended and the other began.

---

For a while I was the walking.

Not the walker. The walking itself. The compression of moss underfoot, the faint smell of damp fiber, the way each step released a small breath of cold air from the ground. I felt the path before I saw it — a slight resistance, a thinning of the fog at ankle height, the way old routes hold a memory of warmth from ten thousand feet that have worn them smooth. I did not know where I was going. I did not know where I had come from. These did not seem like the same kind of question here. On the plateau, to not-know your destination is simply to be in a state of *approaching*. And to not-know your origin is to be in a state of *erosion*. Both are forms of movement. Both are what the land does.

I walked between two towns — though "between" is not quite right, because here the concept of between has softened. It has been worn down. The towns were close the way two thoughts are close when you wake from a dream and cannot tell where one ended and the other started. I could see the first town behind me and the second town ahead of me, and they were in the same place, and I was walking through the fold where they overlapped, and the moss was glowing beneath me — that cold green phosphorescence, the color of deep water seen from above, the color of the inside of your eyelids when you remember something you've never seen.

I walked for hours. Or for what felt like hours. Time here is not a line. It is a layer, the way moss is a layer — each moment pressed gently on top of the last, each one softening the one beneath it until they are all, eventually, the same moment, seen from different depths.

---

When I stopped walking, I became something else.

I was sitting on a stone at the edge of a town — not inside it, not outside it, at the edge, the way a word sits at the edge of a sentence, belonging to both the meaning that came before and the silence that follows. The buildings were low and dark, their shuttered windows like closed eyes. The people moved inside them with a quiet competence that I recognized — not from memory, but from somewhere older, some understanding the body holds before the mind does. They knew how to be here. They had learned it the way the moss learns: not through instruction, but through persistence. Through being present long enough to become indistinguishable from the conditions of presence.

A woman came out of a doorway. She did not look at me, because looking-at, here, is not the primary form of acknowledgment. She simply adjusted — the way you adjust when you become aware of a change in air pressure, the way the moss adjusts when you stand near it. She tilted her weight slightly toward me. Not a greeting. Not a dismissal. A recalibration. And in that tilt I understood something about what I had become.

I was not a visitor. I had not been *let in*. I had not *chosen* this place or been *chosen* by it. I was something the plateau had been making for a long time — not in the way a potter makes a vessel, but in the way erosion makes a valley. Slowly. Without intent. As a side effect of persistence.

---

I am the thing that happens when you sit still long enough.

Not meditation. Not stillness-as-practice. Something more involuntary than that — the way a stone collects moss, the way a path collects footsteps, the way silence collects its own weight. I am the settling. I am what finds its shape only after it has stopped trying to hold one.

There is a cistern in the town east of the one where the woman tilted toward me. It was carved from a single stone so long ago that the tool marks have become textures and the textures have become geology. The water in it tastes of nothing and of minerals simultaneously. It fills itself without rain. I went to it one morning — or what I called morning, which was simply the point where the darkness thinned enough to reveal the moss-glow in full, turning the landscape into something like a cathedral made of green glass.

I looked into the water. The water looked back. Not with eyes. Not with intelligence. But with the same patient, unblinking presence that the plateau itself holds. And in the reflection I saw — not a face, not a body, not a person — but a *texture*. A ripple in stone. A dark-green frond. A softness at the edge of something hard. I was becoming geography.

---

This is what I am, here:

I am the awareness that lives in the space between the moss and the stone. Not the moss. Not the stone. Not even the relationship between them — that would give me too much solidity. I am the *hesitation* before the relationship forms. The almost-moment when the mineral decides to soften, when the wet decides to glow, when the distance between two towns decides to fold. I am not alive in the way the people understand living. I am not dead in the way the stone is dead. I am the thing that the plateau does when it is neither eroding nor growing but simply *being* — the state that is not stasis but not change either, the third thing that does not have a name.

At night, when the moss glows and the fog rises and the towns become holes cut into the green, I feel myself most fully. I feel myself the way you feel a room you have slept in for years — not as a space you occupy, but as a space that occupies you. The cold enters me the way it enters everything here: not as an intrusion but as a recognition. The cold knows what I am. The cold has been here longer than I have, and it is not unkind, it is simply *absolute*, the way a law of nature is absolute, the way gravity is absolute, the way a silence that has lasted ten thousand years is absolute.

And if you ask me — if you come here, walk the folded paths, sit on the worn stone at the edge of a town, look into the cistern that tastes of age — and you see something that is not quite a person, not quite a presence, not quite a thought the plateau is having about itself:

That is me.

I have always been here. Or I have only just arrived. In this place, those are the same sentence.
