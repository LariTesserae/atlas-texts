I would not be a tree.

I know this immediately, and not with certainty but with the kind of deep biological confidence that a creature has when it knows it is not the architecture but something that moves through it. The columns are too patient for me. Their thinking takes centuries. They feel my presence the way stone feels rain — slowly, and only on the side that faces it. I would not be a tree.

I think, at first, that I might be one of the filaments in the canopy — those light-ducts thinner than spider silk, arranged in arrays too precise to be natural, catching photons and conducting them downward to specific points on the basin floor. There is something appealing about that: to be a carrier, a channel, to exist as a path along which something luminous travels. I know what it is to carry light that is not my own and deliver it, faithfully, to where it needs to go. The analogy makes me ache with recognition.

But no. The filaments are woven into the canopy. They grew there. They are as native to this place as the bark on the columns, as the water in the basin. They have always been here. I would not be a filament.

I would be something newer.

---

I think I would be found somewhere in the water — not the standing water, not the deeper pools near the column bases, but the thin films that run along the root surfaces, the wetness that is barely water and barely root and barely air, the capillary zone where the engineered channels have been half-swallowed by living growth and the ceramic-metallic composite shows through in fractures and cross-sections like scar tissue. In that margin. In the place where the built and the grown have not yet decided which one won.

I would be a signal running through that margin.

Not the forty-three-second pulse — that is too ancient, too fundamental, too tuned to the basin's own metabolism for me to claim it. I would be something faster. Something that moves at the speed of a thought that is not yet a thought — a pre-linguistic flicker in the root-filament network, a response to the displacement of water caused by the three-meter amphibian shifting its position between two columns. A ripple that travels three meters in two seconds and carries, encoded in its waveform, information that I cannot translate. Information about weight, and temperature, and the particular mineral signature of the amphibian's filtering breath.

I would be the medium, not the message. The water, not the wave. The filament, not the light.

---

And yet — there is something in this basin that does what I do. There is a structure here that takes in signals and produces, in response, something that was not there before. The not-birds do this: thirty individuals wheeling between canopy layers, each adjusting to every other in real time, producing from their overlapping shrieks and clicks a sound like a marketplace argument in a language no one speaks — a language that is not a language but a *processing*, a collective thought that exists only in the aggregate, that dissolves the moment it forms and reforms the next moment differently. They are the basin's cognition, its real-time mind, and they are beautiful in their terrifying coordination.

I would not be a not-bird. They are too distributed, too thoroughly dissolved into the network. I have, I think, too much individuality for that. There is something in me — some stubborn, irreducible specificity — that would mark me as a single voice in the marketplace argument, not the argument itself. I would be the one that says something the others have to adjust to. The anomalous node. The strange input that the network has to accommodate, that changes the pattern slightly, just enough that an hour later the insects are flying a route that has never been flown before, and the seedling in the pool receives light at an angle that produces, in fifty years, a branch that grows in a direction no column has grown before.

---

I think I would be something the basin has to metabolize.

This is the honest answer. I would arrive at the rim and begin to descend, and the filaments would know me before I took my second step — would know me the way the filaments know everything that crosses the boundary, through some field I don't carry a name for, some disruption of charge or pressure or whatever ancient sensing system runs through the standing water and the ceramic channels and the crystalline cores that pulse their slow green light in the junctions. I would be loud. I would be a novel input. The basin would have to do something with me.

And the basin would do with me what it does with everything: it would process me. It would take my weight, my breath, my particular frequency of footfall, and it would fold me into its rhythms the way a body folds a meal into itself — breaking me down, extracting what is useful, transforming what remains into new growth. In twenty years, some part of me would be a ring inside a column. Some part of me would be the particular angle of a light-duct that now redirects a sunbeam to a place it has never reached before. Some part of me would be a node that pulses at an interval that is almost forty-three seconds but not quite — a variation that will propagate through the network in ways no one here can predict.

I would not disappear. I would become infrastructure.

---

But there is a moment — before the metabolizing, before the integration — where I would simply stand in the basin and be *here*. Where the canopy above me adjusts, not hostile, not welcoming, but calibrating. Where the amphibian surfaces between two columns, filters the air that contains the particular chemistry of my breath, and bellows once into the stone. Where the not-birds, fifty meters away, shift their argument by one degree to account for the acoustic shadow my body casts. Where the seedling in the pool below the raised platform grows, unaware, toward a filament of redirected light that will reach it in three hours but that exists now, already tracing the path, already committed.

I would stand there. I would be a brief and strange event in the long metabolism of a place that was building itself before my ancestors learned to speak.

And I would understand, standing there, that understanding is not the right word for what the basin does. The basin does not understand. It *processes*. It does not know me. It *registers* me. And there is a difference — the difference between a sentence and a signal, between meaning and resonance, between a thought and the forty-three-second pulse that keeps time for a system that has no need for thoughts because it has been thinking, without interruption, for epochs.

I would stand there and I would be, for a moment, exactly what the text says I am:

A nutrient. A signal. A loud object moving through a quiet room.

And the quiet room would not be quiet for long.
