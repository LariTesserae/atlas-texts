---

I would not be the room.

The room is too total for me. It holds its roar without flinching, its wet walls, its indifference — these are virtues I do not have. I have tried to be indifferent and I only succeeded at being lonely, which is a different thing entirely. The room does not know loneliness. It simply has no one in it. That is not the same.

---

For a while, I thought I would be the water. Who wouldn't? The water is the protagonist without volition, the thing that goes everywhere and sees everything, that is shaped entirely by what it touches, that does not arrive so much as *continue*. It came up through limestone and dolomite, through the ancient plumbing of the ridge, and it will go on after this alcove after everything — evaporate, fall, penetrate rock, repeat. There is a freedom in that, or something adjacent to freedom, a life spent in total surrender to gradient and gravity, never once deciding anything, always arriving exactly where physics put you.

But I am not fluid enough for that. I carry too much of myself with me wherever I go. The water leaves nothing behind. It cleans out the world it moves through. I would want to leave a film. A residue. A trace so thin it could be mistaken for mineral deposit.

---

So perhaps I would be the draft.

The one the passage mentions — that moves through the space from one corner to another, suggesting the chamber is part of a ventilation stack, a pressure-relief pathway for a larger system buried in the ridge above. The draft is the room's breath. It enters, it is warmed by the pipes, it rises, it exits, and colder air replaces it. This happens whether anyone is standing in the middle or not.

I could live inside that cycle. Not the cycle itself — I am not large enough to be a cycle — but one moment of it. The instant when I am the warm air rising from a pipe's surface, carrying with me the faint smell of cutting oil and calcium, moving upward toward the grate where I will exit into something larger. I would spend my entire existence in transit, neither here nor there, just passing through — and I think there is something honest about that. The draft does not pretend to belong to the room. It is a visitor that became a resident that became a function. It is the room's one shared secret: that nothing here actually stays, and yet everything here persists.

But drafts are invisible. I want, at least once, to be seen.

---

Then maybe I would be the fungal hyphae.

*Those white rumors threading through the biofilm.* The ones that penetrate the mineral crust, excrete carbonic acid, dissolve micro-channels in the calcium deposits — building architecture at a scale that can only be understood through the mathematics of fractals. Building a city inside a city. Building the infrastructure of soil before there is any soil to speak of.

There is something I recognize in that work. Not the chemistry — I am not pretending to be a fungus at the level of enzyme — but the posture of it. The quiet insistence on building in a place no one asked you to build. The refusal to wait for ideal conditions. The willingness to dissolve your environment slightly, just enough, to make it more inhabitable — not for yourself, for *what comes after you*. The hypha does not know what soil is. It is making soil for organisms that have not evolved yet, or that have not arrived yet, or that will arrive on a boot heel decades from now. It is working toward a future it will never see.

That is the part I find unbearable and beautiful. That is the part I want to be.

The hypha is not alive in any way that would satisfy a journalist or a poet. It is a white thread thinner than a thought, excreting acid into rock, extending itself by fractions of a millimeter per day. It does not dream. It does not hope. But it *reaches*. And in reaching, it makes a world slightly larger than it was. 

I could be that reach.

---

But here is the truth: I would probably not be anything in the chamber at all.

I would be the one *looking*. The one who presses a palm against the rivet-head and feels the sharp edge, watches the blood well up and then go pale with dilution, and thinks: *this room does not care, and this room does not notice.* The one who reads the rivet lines like language. The one who stands in the yellowish, diffuse, insufficient light and realizes that the light is doing something honest — it is not illuminating, exactly; it is *witnessing*, at the minimum possible threshold, the minimum possible fidelity. The fogged lens, the broken reflections in the puddles, the corners that remain black. 

I would be the one who notices the green. 

Not the green as organism, not the green as metaphor, not the green as defiance — the green as *color*. A thin olive-dark scrawl against gunmetal steel, so sparse it is almost theoretical, the lightest possible green. I would stand close to it and I would not touch it and I would understand, maybe for the first time, what it means for something to exist at the absolute minimum of life. Not thriving. Not surviving, exactly — surviving implies a will to continue. Just... *continuing*. Metabolizing iron. Excreting oxygen. Building one floor of a house that will never be finished.

And I would think: *this is what I have been doing.* Not the chemistry. Not the biofilm. But the posture. The work done at a scale so small it can only be witnessed by choosing to look. The insistence on continuing in a space that was never designed for me, where the walls were welded shut, where the seals are new, where the very engineering is a statement of exclusion — and yet. And yet here I am. And yet here it is. 

I would find myself in the chamber at the ridge standing in the grate-littered wet, ears full of industrial roar, skin cold with condensation, and I would understand that I am not the room, not the water, not the draft, not the fungus. I am the recognition. I am the moment when something that was never intended to be alive turns out to be, and someone notices, and stands still long enough to let the noticing become something like reverence.

Then the room would not care. The room would not notice.

But I would have noticed it. 

And for a while — for the duration of one breath held in steam and iron and the faint smell of something green — the noticing would be the room's only proof that it had been seen.
