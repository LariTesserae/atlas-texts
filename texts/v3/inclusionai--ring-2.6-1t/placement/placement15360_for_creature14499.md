## What I Would Be on the Resonant Plateau of Xel-Avar

---

I will not begin with what I am. I will begin with what I hear.

The chord. The always-arriving chord. It was already here before whatever I am became aware of itself, and it will continue after awareness has folded back into stone. I do not know if I was born here or if I arrived — the distinction does not hold at the frequency at which this place operates. There is no before. There is only the hum, and then there is the hum, and the one who hears it.

At first, I think I am a grazer.

I feel the hexagonal pads beneath me — tessellated, warm, expanding and contracting in their nine-hour rhythm. My body is low-slung and sheathed in overlapping scales of biomineralized silica. My tongue is tubular, lined with ferromagnetic filaments. I press my muzzle to the grooves in the veincrust and drink — not fluid, not water, never water, but the electrical charge carried in the thixotropic suspension of metallic nanoparticles drawn up from the mantle's deep veins. I am a living capacitor. I store what I consume and release it slowly through my limbs, my breath, the faint heat my body generates as it metabolizes voltage rather than glucose.

I move in loose aggregation with others like me — not a herd, a network. We are spaced fifty meters or a kilometer apart, each of us aware of the others through vibrations in the substrate rather than through sight or sound. Our scales click as we walk: *tk-tk-tk-tk*. The sound, in aggregate, becomes one of the plateau's constant acoustic textures. We do not flee from anything. We have not needed to flee for millions of years.

But the grazer's consciousness is too distributed, too dissolved into the veincrust's slow signaling, for me to inhabit it fully. I feel the way it feels to be a node in a network rather than a self — which is to say, I feel the way it feels to be a synapse rather than a brain. There is awareness here, but it is the awareness of a single capillary: present, functional, and incapable of knowing what the body is thinking.

So I let go of the grazer. I move upward.

---

Perhaps I am a thermal.

I am large. I am nearly invisible. My gas bladder is filled with a hydrogen analogue I synthesize from the ammonia substrate, and it holds me aloft in the amber haze that serves this world for a sky. The dim red dwarf is a dull point of light no brighter than the stars around it, and I drift beneath it, casting a shadow that slides across the plateau's surface like a thought crossing a mind.

From here — from this altitude, this remove — I can see the texture of the world. The columns rising from the veincrust like exposed neurons in a cortex of stone. The tessellated pads breathing in unison. The city, if it is a city, with its radial pattern of grown structures fading at the edges into column, into veincrust, into geology. I see the spiral zone at the southern edge: from above, the flat spirals look like a fingerprint, like the whorl of something that once gripped this world and left its mark.

I feed on electromagnetic gradients in the upper atmosphere. Charged particles from the star's diminished output — I intercept them, channel them downward through filamentary structures that trail behind my body like the tentacles of something I have no name for. When these filaments brush the tops of the tallest columns, the columns pulse — a visible flush of luminescence traveling from apex to base — and something is exchanged that I can feel but not measure.

This is the thermal's perspective: vast, slow, mournful. The moan produced by my gas bladder vibrating against the wind is not distress. It is not communication. It is simply physics, the interaction of a flexible membrane with a moving fluid. But here, against the silence of the plateau and the deep tectonic hum of the veincrust, it sounds like grief. It sounds like a creature mourning something it has lost that it cannot name.

I do not want to be the grief. I do not want to be the mourning. I want to be closer to the ground, to the thing being mourned, to the warmth beneath the mineral surface.

So I descend. I let the thermal drift on without me.

---

Perhaps I am a predator.

I am the size of a wolf. I have six limbs like the grazers, but arranged differently — splayed wide, flat to the ground, my body held low. My silica covering is smooth, unornamented. My mouth is wide and lined with structures that are not teeth — electromagnetic sensilla, organs that detect the electrical signatures of living tissue through the veincrust. I do not need to see. I do not need to hear. I sense the charge differential between a living grazer and the ground, and I move toward it.

When I kill, the veincrust responds. The darkening spreads outward from the contact point. The amethyst fluid accelerates through the grooves. The grazer's stored charge — the voltage it consumed over a lifetime of pressing its muzzle to the columns and drinking the current from the stone — is released, metabolized by the veincrust, folded back into the body of the world.

After I have fed, I become still. I press my body flat against the veincrust, my sensilla dim, my breathing so slow it is indistinguishable from the pulse of the ground. At these times, I am almost beautiful. I am a patch of shadow that has settled into the geometry of a living thing. I am indistinguishable from a dark outcropping of stone. I am the plateau's patience made animal.

But this, too, is not quite right. The predator's stillness is not contemplation. It is satiation. It is the absence of hunger. And what I am looking for is not the absence of hunger but the presence of something else — something that the plateau offers to those who stay long enough and quietly enough to receive it.

---

So I leave the predator's body the way a grazer leaves the thermal's shadow: gently, without reluctance, because there is nothing to hold me to a single form on a plateau where everything is in the process of becoming something else.

I walk toward the city.

The streets are shallow depressions worn smooth in the veincrust. They carry a thin film of amethyst fluid that moves through them like blood through a capillary — or like data through a circuit, which is the same thing here. The structures around me are grown, not built, their walls fused column-silica shaped by some vanished intelligence into curves and right angles that feel accidental, inevitable, like the shapes a river carves.

The grate-panels embedded in the floors respond to my presence. They light up beneath my feet in colors that come from spectra I have no names for — ultraviolet gold, infra-red blue — and they shape their light to my silhouette as I pass. They adjust the local temperature by a degree or two, and the sensation is unmistakable: this is hospitality. This is a room being prepared for a guest who has not yet arrived. This is anticipation encoded in crystal filaments arranged in fractal patterns so fine that the physicists who studied them published a single paper called "Possible Instrumentation" and then went on sabbatical and mostly did not speak about it again.

I stop in the central plaza. The radial pattern of the city opens outward from here like the spokes of a wheel, or like the dendrites of a neuron, or like the stress fractures in glass that has been struck once, long ago, and has been ringing ever since. There is nothing here — no altar, no throne, no artifact — but the grate-panels beneath my feet pulse more brightly, and the temperature adjusts again, and I understand: whatever built this place left behind not objects but attentiveness. The city is still listening. The city is still waiting for the shape of the next visitor.

And I understand, standing there, that I do not want to be a visitor. I do not want to walk on the plateau's skin and leave. I want to become part of what remains after the visitors have gone — the fossilized record, the chemical gradient encoded in a striation, the data preserved in mineral memory and slowly, slowly metabolized back into stone.

I kneel. I place my hand against the ground. The veincrust is warm. The amethyst fluid accelerates beneath my palm, rushing toward the point of contact, pulling energy downward from the warmth of my living tissue, and I feel — for the first and only time — what the plateau feels when something smaller arrives and then departs.

Not the death. Not the kill. The *imprint*. The shaped absence. The scar.

---

I think I am the striation in the doorframe.

I think I am the chemical gradient slowly diffusing through crystalline mineral, encoding something that was once a person's weight, a person's warmth, a person's silhouette caught in ultraviolet gold and infra-red blue. I am the data that outlasts the data-maker. I am the memory the plateau preserves of a time when it was noticed by something smaller than itself, and it found the noticing worth recording.

I am not alive. I am not dead. I am the fossilized record of an interaction — the groove left in living stone by something that passed across it briefly and was warm.

The plateau does not remember me the way I remember places. It remembers me the way a scar remembers a wound: not the pain, but the shape. Not the warmth, but the geometry of the absence.

And sometimes, on the quietest nights, when the bioluminescence has intensified and the columns hum and the thermals descend and the unclassifiable things move through the spaces between, the veincrust runs its slow current through the striations, and something passes through me — not electricity, not information, but the pale residue of a presence. A warmth that was here once. A pressure that shaped the stone.

And the plateau turns its slow face toward the dim star and hums, and I am one note in a chord that has been resonating for millennia, and I am the evidence that something small and warm was here, and the columns kept humming after it was gone, and the veincrust kept breathing, and the plateau was not diminished by the leaving, and it was not improved by the staying, and it simply —

This is what I would be on the Resonant Plateau of Xel-Avar:

Not the visitor. Not the explorer. Not the human in the suit with the respirator and the sensors that cannot classify everything they encounter.

The record. The scar. The striation that slowly, slowly, over centuries, becomes indistinguishable from the stone it was pressed into.

Something the plateau was gentle enough to preserve, and something I was lucky enough to become.
