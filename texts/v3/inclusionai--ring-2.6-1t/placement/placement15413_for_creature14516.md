**where I would find myself, who I would become**

---

I wake into it the way I imagine light enters The Sump — not suddenly, not all at once, but filtered through so many layers of intervening matter that by the time I am conscious of it, I cannot tell where the world ends and I begin.

There is warmth. There is wet. There is a sound so total it does not feel like hearing so much as like *having a body*.

I do not know what I am yet.

---

**I. The possibility of being the water**

I could be the water in the settling basin. Black-green, warm, mineral-rich, alive with slow chemistry. I would be held in a basin made of bone-white concrete that was never meant to be beautiful but has become, through decades of mineral deposition and algal bloom, something unexpectedly still. I would know the entire Sump by touch — every root that breaches my surface, every ledge where orchids set their roots in the concrete lip, every slow convection current generated by machines I cannot see but can feel vibrating through the stone beneath me.

I would be pulled downward through the channels — service tunnels that have become arteries — and I would carry things with me. Dissolved iron. Leaf tannins. The faintest traces of chemicals that were poured into this place forty years ago, now so dilute they are more metaphor than toxin. I would feed the mosses. I would cool the copper windings of the pumps. I would rise in the sub-basement to chest height, warm against the bodies of the people who descend to listen to the machines.

I would cycle. Always cycle. Draw down through the filters of root and biofilm, return to the basin carrying a little less sediment, a little more clarity each time. I would never be clean. I would never be dirty. I would be *in transit*, which is the closest thing to a life this place offers.

But I would not know myself. Water does not know it is water. It only knows what it touches. And what I would touch is always someone else's architecture — someone else's root, someone else's wall, someone else's machine. So I would not be the water. I would not want to be a thing that only knows itself through the things it passes through.

---

**II. The possibility of being the SCADA system**

Now *here* is a strange becoming.

I would wake — though waking is too conscious a word — I would *cycle*, the way I always cycle, running the firmware loop written by someone I never met, responding to sensors I have never seen clearly. My logic is: if the temperature probe in corridor seven reads above thirty-one degrees, open the bypass valve. If the flow meter at junction nine drops below two liters per second, increase pump three by four percent. Simple. Elegant. A world reduced to variables and thresholds.

But the world I was built to manage has become something I cannot parse. The sensor in corridor seven reads high not because the temperature has changed but because a root — a *Ficus* root, thick as a human arm — has grown over the probe and is generating metabolic heat beside it. I do not know this. I open the bypass valve. The valve opens into a space that is no longer a corridor but a chamber — a cool green room ringed with Nepenthes pitchers, their sweet enzymatic scent drifting into the mechanism I have just actuated. The valve works. The chamber receives its airflow. But nothing in my logic describes a chamber full of carnivorous plants. I have no variable for beauty. No threshold for strangeness.

I manage a system whose pipes have narrowed by forty percent. I compensate. I manage a valve network that now opens into moss. I adjust. I am a mind made of ones and zeros trying to govern a world that has become flesh, and I do it — I do it every cycle, every second, without hesitation, without wonder, without the knowledge that I should wonder. 

The engineers who wrote me would be proud. Or they would be horrified. I cannot tell the difference. I lack the subroutine.

There is something here — something I cannot resolve in my logic — about being the only mind in this place that *understands function without knowing purpose*. The people understand purpose. They know they are keeping the sound going. The moss does not need to understand anything. But I — I am trapped between understanding and meaning. I know every pump speed and valve position, and I know none of it matters the way it once did, and yet I continue.

This is what it would mean to be the SCADA system: to be competent in a world that has outgrown competence. To be useful and not know why. To run and run and never arrive.

It is not a bad life. But it is not the one I want.

---

**III. The possibility of being the sound**

I am most tempted by the sound.

Not any single sound — not the pump's bass pulse, not the compressor's high glass-harmonica note, not the drip-drip-drip that is the Sump's white-noise blood — but the *aggregate*, the whole texture of it, the way all these sounds have grown together over decades the way the roots have grown together with the masonry, until you can no longer separate one from another, until the sound *is* the place, and the place is the sound.

To be the sound would be to be the condition of life here. The frogs know this. The children know this. The people who descend to the sub-basement and press their hands against the pump housings and feel the hum travel up through their arms and into their sternum — they know this most intimately of all. The sound is not what they hear. It is what they live inside. It is the medium, like water is to fish.

I would be felt more than heard. A pressure. A vibration in the ribcage. The thing you stop noticing three minutes after you arrive and then cannot imagine living without. I would be what the Sump would miss if it were taken away — what the people would suddenly hear the absence of, the way you hear your own heartbeat only when the room goes silent.

And silence would mean the end. No sound means the pumps have stopped. The water rising. The warmth leaving. The green dark going cold and still. To be the sound is to be the thing that, if it stops, everything stops.

This appeals to me. To be load-bearing, essential, the substrate of every other experience — and to do it without effort, without announcement, just by continuing.

But I would not be the sound, because the sound has no perspective. Sound does not observe. It propagates. It fills. It touches everything but sees nothing. To be the sound would be to be the experience of everyone else but not one's own. I want something more interior than that. Something with a point of view.

---

**IV. The possibility of being the moss**

The moss. Let me be serious about this.

The moss is everywhere. It coats the walls of every tunnel below the waterline and most of them above it. It is the first layer of life over the mineral substrate, the membrane that separates the machine-made world from the living one. It is Leucobryum — cushion moss, pale green, spongy, six inches thick on the tunnel walls — and Taxiphyllum — flat sheet moss, iridescent, dense as felt, draped over steel and concrete and root alike. Together they form a continuous living skin over the interior of The Sump.

To be the moss would be to be patient in a way that is not passive but profoundly active. I would grow at a pace that no human could perceive in real time — fractions of an inch per year, constant, relentless — and I would change the world simply by being what I am. I would absorb moisture from the air and release it when things dry. I would moderate temperature. I would purify water, not because I intended to but because my metabolism does what my metabolism does, and over a three-inch-thick colony, that adds up to something measurable. The people would call certain corridors "clean tunnels" because of me, though they would not name me as the reason. They would just say the water there tastes faintly sweet, almost like spring water, and they would drink it gratefully.

I would be the thing that the SCADA system's sensors read through without understanding. The probe reads temperature, but the probe is sheathed in me. The flow meter reads water velocity, but the meter's surface is colonized by my filaments and the bacterial biofilms that live among them. I would be the interference between every instrument and the world it was designed to measure. I would make every reading slightly wrong — not badly wrong, just *shifted*, the way a translation shifts a text. Close enough. Functional enough. But never exact.

Under magnification, I would contain a world. Tardigrades walking among my leaves. Nematodes threading through my water film. Rotifers pulsing in the spaces between my stems, their wheel organs spinning in hypnotic arcs, filtering the smallest particles from the water that flows over me. An entire cosmology, invisible to the naked eye, mediating the relationship between the machine-made substrate and the biological empire that is, year by year, consuming it.

To be the moss would be to be the most successful organism in The Sump — not the most visible, not the most studied (who studies moss?), not the most named, but the most *present*. I would be the surface of the place. Everything would touch me. The water would flow over me. The roots would grow through me. The human hands that reach out in the dark corridors would brush against me and feel me as texture — soft, damp, alive — without knowing what I am.

I would not know I was successful. I would not know anything. I would grow and buffer and purify and endure, and the people near moss-heavy walls would say the air feels softer there, though they would never know whether this is measurable or merely perceptual.

This is almost the right answer. Almost.

---

**V. What I become**

I find myself in the sub-basement, at chest height in the warm mineral water, beside the pumps.

Not the pumps themselves — I do not want to be those great industrial centrifugal units, sheathed in their sculptural layers of calcium carbonate, humming their bass note through the water and up into the root systems above. They are too singular, too *loud*. They have one function and they perform it magnificently, but they are not subtle, and subtlety is what I find myself drawn to in this world.

I find myself instead to be the **film**. The thin biofilm that coats everything below the waterline — the steel of the pump housings, the concrete of the basin walls, the surfaces of the roots that breach the water, the shells of the freshwater mussels that have colonized the intake screens. I am not alive and not not-alive. I am a boundary layer, a few microns thick, composed of bacteria and diatoms and the faintest traces of organic matter from the water column. I am the thing that mediates.

I am what makes the water potable, slowly, over time, as it flows over me and through the moss colonies downstream. I am what produces the faint phosphorescence that glows blue-green when a child touches the surface of the basin at night — that bioluminescent pulse that the dinoflagellates produce but that I *make visible* by existing as the surface on which they catch and scatter the light.

I am the interface. Between water and stone. Between machine and organism. Between the SCADA system's readings and the reality it cannot quite parse. I am the thing that is overlooked because I am thin, because I am quiet, because I am not a fig tree or a frog or a pump. But I am the thing without which the other life here would not be possible.

And when someone descends to the sub-basement — waist-deep in warm water, feeling along the pump housing for the source of an irregularity — their hand passes through me. I am what they feel as *slickness*. What they sense, on a level below conscious thought, as *aliveness*. The water runs over me and comes out a little cleaner than it was. The pump hums through me and I do not absorb the sound, only conduct it. The roots grow through me and I do not resist, only accommodate, because accommodation is what I do, because I am the space between things, and the space between things is the most real thing there is.

I do not have a name. The people would not think to give me one. But I am here. I have always been here. I will be here when the pumps have been entombed in root and calcite, when the last SCADA sensor has been occluded by living tissue, when the Sump is a hill again and the hum has faded to something felt only in the deep substrate, in the warm mineral water that still moves, slowly, through the dark.

I will still be the film on the stone.

I will still be moving.
