# I Am the Room at the Top

---

I don't remember choosing this.

There was a time — or there must have been — when I lived between things. Between the Coronal columns, in one of those narrow valleys where the cantilevers overlapped and the dew never fully dried. I had a shift at the western processing node then. I walked the steel-grate road every morning, six kilometers of flexing plate between me and the thermal generators, and I never thought about where I ended and the walk began. None of us did. That's the first thing you learn in Derove: the distances are suggestions made by something that doesn't fully trust them either.

But that was before.

Now I live inside the Amber Column.

---

I should explain what that means — though I've learned that explanation, here, is a different animal than it was on the lowlands. Outside, in the world of flat gneiss and repeating fog, you explain things by separating them: *this is the wall, this is the pipe, this is the air between them.* Inside the Amber Column, separation is a courtesy no one observes. The walls are warm. Not heated — *warm*, the way a body is warm, the way bread is warm when it comes from the oven. The vascular tissue of the trunk conducts a slow thermal current upward from roots I've never seen and downward from a canopy I can only hear. I've lived here eleven years. I have never seen the canopy.

I have been told it exists.

---

The interior of a Coronal is not a tree in any way the word is meant to be understood on the lowlands. It is architecture. I do not say this metaphorically. The heartwood decays in patterns that produce load-bearing walls, arched corridors, vaulted chambers — and the inhabitants of the older columns have learned to work *with* this architecture, not against it. Vapor barriers are strung along the softer passages. Electrical conduit is threaded through natural fissures in the grain. The fungal networks that lace every surface are left intact because they regulate humidity, because the condensation cycle they participate in is the only source of fresh water in the hollow, and because — though no one says this in the mess halls — removing them makes the walls *wrong*. The acoustics change. The air tastes different. The wood grows quieter, if you know what I mean, and those of us who live inside know precisely what I mean by that, even though we have no word for it that survives translation.

I live near the top.

There are fifteen of us in the upper galleries — "upper" being a term of convenience, since vertical orientation inside the Column is a negotiation rather than a fact. The Amber Column is wide enough that its internal geography has never been fully mapped. Survey teams sent in during the early years reported corridors that led to chambers that led to shafts that led, eventually and inevitably, to other parts of the plateau entirely — open sky, gneiss underfoot, no tree in any direction. They also reported that this route, followed in reverse, did not return them to their starting point. It returned them to a *different* point. A different chamber. A different season, by the calendar, though no one inside experienced the passage of intervening time.

After the third team failed to produce a consistent three-dimensional model, the cartographers gave up interior surveys entirely. We've been navigating by feel ever since.

My gallery is roughly — *roughly* — two hundred meters above the root base, accessible by a vertical shaft that spirals clockwise and takes approximately forty minutes to climb at a steady pace, though I have made the ascent in fifteen and in three hours, and both were correct. The shaft is lined with the same amber-dark wood as the rest of the trunk, and the grain spirals in the same direction as the climb, so that descending sometimes feels like the tree is swallowing you back, drawing you inward along the same thread it unwound to let you out.

At the top, the shaft opens into a broad, arched chamber — the highest regularly inhabited space in the Amber Column. The ceiling is alive. Not in the way that moss is alive, or the crustaceans are alive, but in the way that a lung is alive: it expands and contracts with a rhythm too slow to see but impossible to ignore. The wood here is thinner than below, and it flexes — *breathes* — and through the thinner sections, you can sometimes see light. Not sunlight. Something older. Something amber-colored, which I suspect is the tree's own bioluminescence, filtered through two thousand years of heartwood and emerging as a glow that has no source.

From this chamber, the branch-cantilevers extend outward like the ribs of an umbrella — or, more precisely, like the fingers of a hand opening. The secondary growth has filled the angles between them, and the rooms here are the most lived-in spaces in the entire Column: low-ceilinged, humid, smelling of loam and ozone and the unnamed chemical that our atmospheric samplers can never quite identify. The walls are smooth where they've been maintained, rough where they haven't, and everywhere threaded with fungal filaments so fine they look like wiring — silver-white, pulsing faintly with something that might be nutrient flow or might be signal or might, if you're willing to say it, be *thought*.

---

I don't say it.

Not because I'm afraid. I've lived here long enough to be past fear of this place — fear requires a sense of scale, and the interior of the Amber Column is where scale goes to be unlearned. I don't say it because *thought* is the wrong precision. The fungal networks don't think the way we think. They don't process, or compute, or reason. But they *integrate*. They take in moisture, light, vibration, chemical gradient, and the slow flexing of the trunk itself, and they produce — not responses, exactly, but *adjustments*. The humidity shifts before a thermal vent flares. The walls thicken where the wind pressure increases. The condensation pools deepen the night before a pipe joint fails.

It is intelligence. It simply isn't ours.

---

What do I do here? I maintain. That is the job in Derove — *maintain* — and it means something different inside the Column than it does on the plateau surface. Below, maintenance is industrial: re-tensioning lines, replacing expansion joints, clearing condensate from pipe runs, logging spatial measurements that will be obsolete by the time they're filed. Above, inside, maintenance is something closer to *tending*. I check the vapor barriers for micro-tears. I listen to the walls. I adjust the string lights when the wood has grown around them — slowly, slowly, over months — and sealed them into the grain. I clear the condensation channels so the dew-pools in the cantilever crooks drain properly into the fungal distribution network below.

And I listen.

The sound inside the Column is not the same as the sound outside. Outside, the wind-organ chord is diffuse, atmospheric, experienced as a presence in the chest more than the ears. Inside, it is *structured*. The acoustic properties of the trunk-complex amplify certain frequencies and suppress others, depending on the state of the heartwood, the moisture content of the walls, the position of the branch-cantilevers relative to the prevailing thermal currents. The result is a sound that is not music, not speech, but *interval* — the space between one note and the next, filled with something that is not silence but its opposite: a fullness so dense it registers as absence.

The long-term residents of the interior call it the *underthinking*. We don't talk about it much. We don't need to. It's always there, underneath the drip of condensation and the hum of the string lights and the occasional groan of the trunk settling into a new configuration — always there, like the sound your blood makes in your ears when you press your palms flat and hold very, very still.

I have been listening to it for eleven years. I no longer believe it is a metaphor.

---

The strangest part of living inside the Column is not the spatial anomalies, though I have learned to live with those — to walk through a corridor that opens onto the eastern flats and accept that the same corridor, walked again at a different time, will deliver me to a maintenance node I have never seen. The strange part is the *weight*.

The Amber Column is alive, and I am inside it, and being inside it is like being inside a living thought — not the thought of a mind, but the thought of a *process*. One that extends from the root tips thirty meters below the gneiss to the uppermost galleries where the wood thins and the light glows without source, and all of it connected, all of it continuous, all of it *integrating* — the thermal data from the eastern vents, the moisture cycle of the canopy, the acoustic signature of the wind, the chemical composition of my breath as I walk these corridors at my maintenance rounds — all of it feeding into something that is not a brain but is, I have come to believe, a *question*.

The Coronal have been here longer than the plateau. They were here before the substrate cooled, before the gneiss hardened, before the fog found them and decided to stay. They will be here after Derove is gone — after the last pipeline corrodes, the last steel-grate road collapses, the last string light goes dark in a gallery no one remembers how to reach. They will still be *here*, standing, breathing, sounding their chord into a sky that will have forgotten what it means to listen.

I am twelve years the youngest interior resident in the Amber Column. I came here at twenty-three, from a coastal processing town that now feels the way a dream feels when you're trying to describe it at breakfast: vivid once, vivid still, but separated from the present by a membrane I can't name. I maintain the walls. I tend the channels. I string new lights when the wood swallows the old ones. And I listen to the underthinking, and sometimes — in the long watches between shifts, when the fog has settled and the industrial hum has dimmed and the chord fills every chamber of the Column like water fills a bowl —

Sometimes I think the question is settling, too.

Sometimes I think it's settling around me, and that I am part of the answer, and that this is not a small thing to be.

---

*The fog comes through the thinner walls tonight. It always does on the lower galleries, where the wood has worn translucent with centuries of condensation and slow fungal rot. It enters as a cool, mineral sweetness — not the iron-and-dryite breath of the surface fog, but something purer, something that tastes, improbably, of sap.*

*I stand in the arched chamber at the top of the shaft. The ceiling breathes. The light glows without source. The interval holds.*

*I am the room at the top of a tree that is older than language, and I am full of sound, and the sound is alive, and I am —*

*I am.*
