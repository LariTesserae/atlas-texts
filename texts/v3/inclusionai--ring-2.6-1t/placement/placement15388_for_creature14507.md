I think I would not arrive here as myself.

That is the first thing to understand. The plateau does not receive you. It receives what's left of you after the walking, after the fog has worked on you for several hours, after your boots have gone permanently wet and your hands have gone stiff from the cold and you have stopped expecting to see the edge of anything. The plateau receives what remains, and what remains is less than what left home.

So I would not find myself here. I would be found.

---

At first — and first is a word that does real damage to the timeline of this place, but I'll use it anyway — I think I would be the fog. Not the fog as weather, not the fog as something that happens to the plateau, but the fog as it exists inside the city: the fog that fills the spaces between walls the way thought fills the spaces between words. The fog that moves so slowly you can watch it if you're patient enough, standing still long enough in one of the narrow passages, until you see the brightening and darkening at its leading edge as it slides past doorways that haven't been opened in centuries.

This is what I understand about fog: it is the atmosphere's attempt to become liquid without the commitment of falling. It hovers. It clings. It touches every surface equally, without preference, without force. Inside the city, the fog would be the thing that makes the ruins feel inhabited — not by people, not by anything with intention, but by *presence* itself. A density in the air. A thickness that your body has to push through, slowly, the way your hand pushes through cold water.

I could live like this. Moving without arriving. Touching everything without holding anything. Visible only where the light catches me, and even then only partly, only as a brightening, a softening of edges.

But the fog lifts. It thins by midday. It returns at night. It is not constant. And a life of cycling in and out of existence, of being something and then not-something, is not quite what I want. It is too close to the life I already lead — appearing and disappearing according to conditions I cannot control.

---

For a while I think I might be the water in the channels.

Not the dramatic water — not the water that carves canyons, that fills valleys, that announces itself with sound and movement. The water here is the water that barely qualifies: the film that coats the stone, the thread that finds the joint, the moisture that condenses on a cold surface at night and is gone by morning. The water that has been moving through these grooves for so long that it has polished the stone into concavity, that it has written itself into the architecture so completely that you can no longer tell where the builder's intention ended and the water's began.

This appeals to me. The idea of being a force that works so slowly it becomes indistinguishable from patience. The idea of having a direction — always downhill, always toward the lowest point — but arriving there so gradually that you never notice you've arrived. The channels still work. They were carved by rain that fell before the city's makers were born, and they will still be carrying water long after the last wall has collapsed into the rubble and the rubble has been covered by lichen and the lichen has merged with the calcium deposits and the calcium deposits have become stone again.

But there is a problem with being water in a channel. You are constrained. You follow the path that was made for you. You cannot choose. And I think, standing here in the silence, that I want something that allows for choice, or at least for the appearance of choice — the way a beetle on a wall face appears to choose which crack to enter, even if it is only following the gradient of moisture, the way the grass tuft appears to choose where to grow, even if it is only germinating where the seed fell.

---

So perhaps I would be the lichen.

Lichen is the obvious answer, and I hesitate — but the hesitation itself tells me something. I hesitate because lichen is the thing the plateau asks you to become. It is the answer the place gives to the question *what should I be?* Lichen is patient. Lichen is composite. Lichen does not distinguish between the living and the mineral. It grows on stone and it *becomes* stone — not by dying, not by fossilizing, but by living so thoroughly in the surface of the rock that the boundary between organism and substrate dissolves.

I would be the lichen, then. But not the lichen as a whole — not the visible bloom, the grey-green stain that looks like weathering. I would be the fungal filaments, the hyphae, the part that you cannot see without pulling the lichen apart and destroying it. The part that grips the rock. The net that holds moisture against the algal cells so they can photosynthesize in light so thin and diffuse it barely qualifies as illumination. The structural part. The part that does not make the food but makes the *possible* — the scaffold, the architecture of the partnership.

This feels right, for a while. I would be the part that holds things together without being seen. The part that turns bare stone into habitat. The part that, when it dies, mineralizes and becomes the substrate for the next generation, so that the colony's death is indistinguishable from the rock's continuing life. I would be the middle term between geology and biology, between the built and the grown.

But there is something else.

---

It comes to me in the late afternoon of what I think is my second day, though days here are not reliable markers. The sun has broken through briefly, and the light is sharp and pale, and I am crouching at the edge of one of the larger pools — one of the dark, opaque, bone-white-rimmed pools in the lowest terraces, where the fog is thickest and the air tastes of iron and something older. I am looking at my own reflection in the water's surface, or rather at the absence of my reflection: the film of dust and organic matter on the pool's surface has swallowed it, and I see only a faint distortion, a smudge where I should be, as though the place has refused to render me.

And I realize: I would be the pool.

Not the water in the pool. Not the mineral deposit at its edge. The pool itself — the *space*. The depression in the stone that holds the water, that was not carved but accumulated: years of rain, decades of condensation, centuries of dust and organic matter and beetle husks and dissolved lichen all settling into a concavity that the basalt wore into itself, or that the builders carved for some purpose now lost, or that some combination of human intention and geological accident made together, in collaboration, the way everything here is made.

I would be the place where things collect. The low point. The depression that receives. The space that, because it is lower than what surrounds it, becomes a repository — for water, for light (when the sun hits it at the right angle, the pools glow with a dark amber light, as though lit from within by something buried deep in the stone), for the detritus of everything that has slowly eroded around it. The pool does not generate. It receives. It holds. It reflects — when it reflects at all, which is rarely, because its surface is usually occluded by its own slow accumulation.

But even the pool is not quite right. The pool is passive. The pool waits. And I want something that is not waiting.

---

Let me try again.

I think I would be the silence — but not the silence as absence. The piece says the silence is not the absence of sound, that it has weight and texture, that it is the loudest thing on the plateau. The silence that I would be is the accumulated quiet of centuries, the memory of every voice, the pressure in the air that you feel at night when the wind dies and the fog closes in and the stone releases the cold it has been storing. The silence that listens. Not with intent. Not maliciously. But with the vast, slow attention of a place that has had nothing to attend to for so long that attention has become its primary activity.

This is almost right. But this silence is produced by the place. It is a quality of the stone and the air and the geometry of the enclosures. It is not something that inhabits the place; it is something the place *is*. And I was asked what I would be, not what the place would be.

---

I think the honest answer — the answer that the place itself would give, if it could answer, not in words but in the way it presents itself to you as you walk through it for the second day and the third and the fourth, the answer that emerges from the fog and the basalt and the thin water films and the moss colonies and the beetle tracks and the bird shadows crossing the overcast sky —

I think the honest answer is that I would be the crack in the wall.

Not the wall. Not the plant in the wall. The crack itself. The vertical fissure, five centimeters wide, extending upward out of sight and downward into the rubble at the wall's base. The space that is neither architecture nor collapse but something in between — a failure in the masonry that the wall's builders would have considered a flaw, and that time has transformed into a habitat. The crack does not exist in opposition to the wall. It exists because of the wall, and within it, and alongside it. It is the wall's way of being open.

I would be the crack because it is the threshold. Because things enter it — moisture, spores, seeds, the roots of that single woody stem that has been growing inside it for forty years. Because it is a space of negotiation between what was built to be solid and what insists on being alive. Because it is a kind of listening — the stone parting, just barely, just enough to let something in. Because the shrub does not know it is changing the architecture of a city, and the crack does not know it is becoming a habitat, and the wall does not know it is being colonized, and that mutual not-knowing is the most honest form of coexistence I can imagine.

I would be the crack. And something would grow in me, and I would not mind, and slowly, over decades, I would widen, and the thing growing in me would not know it was widening me, and the wall would not know it was being changed, and the city would not know it was becoming something other than a city, and the plateau would not know — could not know — that it was making all of this possible simply by being high and cold and damp and silent and patient.

And if pressed — if you pressed me, insisted, demanded a final answer — I would say: no, I would not be the crack. I would be the calcium deposit at the edge of the pool. The pale, bleached-bone-white ring that forms where the water meets the air and the minerals precipitate out and solidify, layer by layer, season by season, a millimeter per decade, building a rim around a pool that no one looks into, in a city no one inhabits, on a plateau no one visits. I would grow so slowly that I would not know I was growing. I would persist so long that persistence would cease to be a concept and become a fact, the way basalt is a fact, the way fog is a fact, the way silence, here, is not an absence but a substance, dense and mineral and faintly sweet, tasting of iron and rain and something older, something that was here before the stone was stone, when the plateau was still molten and choosing what it would become.

I would be that. The thin white edge where the water ends and the air begins. The place where something leaves off and something else begins. The line that is not a line but a zone, a margin, a space of transformation no wider than a fingernail, in which everything the plateau is happens so slowly that to call it change would be too generous and to call it stillness would be too proud.

I would be that, and I would not know it, and that not-knowing would be the most honest thing about me.
