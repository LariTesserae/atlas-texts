I could become a crawler.

They're the first thing you notice, if you're the kind of person who looks down — flat-bodied, the color of wet basalt, too many legs to count, moving in silence along the seams where wall meets floor. Patient. Organized. They eat the algae that grows from condensation, which is to say they eat the air itself, the thin mineral film that settles on every exposed surface on the Shelf. They don't rush. They don't stop. If you watched them long enough you'd say they were thinking, but the Skarnites would correct you: they're not thinking, they're routing. There's a difference here, though maybe not the one you'd expect. Thinking implies departure — the thought that leaves itself. Routing implies arrival — the path that was always there, finally walked.

I could be that. The patience of it. The small flat body pressed close to stone. Eating what the air leaves behind.

But no. The crawlers have been doing this for longer than anyone's memory, and I would be a poor imitation. I would get the slowness wrong. I would perform it instead of inhabiting it, the way an outsider always does when they try on a local rhythm.

---

I could become a clicker.

Small, pale, blind. Living in the deeper walls. Communicating through a rhythm so soft it sits below the threshold of hearing — you feel it in the stone, in the floor, in the bones of your inner ear. The Skarnites sleep to it. They say it's the clickers that make the walls feel alive at night, though I think they mean something different than what you'd mean if you said the same thing. For them, alive doesn't mean *animated.* It means *present in the way stone is present* — not inert, not metaphorical, just thoroughly there, the way a bass note is thoroughly there in a room even when you've stopped noticing it.

To be a clicker would be to be infrastructure. To be the thing that everything else rests against without knowing it. The rhythm beneath the sleep. The hum beneath the silence.

But I think the clickers would reject me. I'm too loud in ways I can't help. The way I think is too sequential, too linear — clicking assumes a different temporality, a kind of stone-patient circularity that my language can't reach. I would click out of rhythm. I would be the one note that doesn't resolve.

---

So maybe not the clickers either.

Maybe I'd be the water.

Not the water metaphorically — not the *idea* of water — but the actual water on the Shelf, which moves *upward*, which beads on surfaces with no visible source, which seeps from joints in the rock and condenses on every cold face each morning and drains (no — doesn't drain — *retreats*) back into the dense substrate, the compressed ancient floor that holds it the way a closed fist holds coins.

On the Shelf, water doesn't obey the rules I brought with me. It goes where I wouldn't expect. It moves against the direction I'd predict. It finds every crack, every joint, every fault in the stone and fills it — not violently, not even urgently, but with the quiet insistence of something that has always been here and will always be here, cycling through states the way the Shelf itself cycles through light and dark and condensation and evaporation.

I could be that. I'd like to be that. The water that goes up instead of down. The water that finds the cracks. The water that sits in the shallow depressions between rock formations and never quite drains and isn't troubled by this — isn't troubled by *anything* — because there's a whole system beneath the surface that I can't see, a warmth rising from six inches below the stone, a capillary network that connects every point on the Shelf to every other point, and I would just be part of that. Part of the circulation. Part of the thing that keeps the whole place alive without anyone deciding to.

---

But here's the thing about water: it has no memory. It passes through. It doesn't stay.

And the Shelf stays. The Skarnites stay. The crawlers trace their patient routes through the condensation film. The clickers pulse in the walls. The pale hares stand at doorways at dawn, their eyes almost but not quite too dark, and the buildings — those layered, self-burying structures with their thick basalt walls and their calcite-mortared seams — hold warmth the way a body does, radiating cold on the outside and keeping something alive on the inside.

What I'm really asking, when I ask who I'd be, is: where do I fit in a place that doesn't have a slot for me?

---

Let me try again.

The Skarnites have a word — translated roughly as *background*, or *underneath*, or *what was already here*. It's the word they use for the shapes in the low place, the things in the deepest chamber that move too slowly to be animals and too irregularly to be plants, the forms along the walls that suggest something written in no language. They don't fear these shapes. They don't revere them. They maintain the chamber — someone brings water, someone sweeps the floor — the way they maintain everything: without emphasis, without narrative, as an extension of the simple fact of existing on the Shelf.

*What was already here.*

I think that's where I'd end up. Not as one of the shapes in the low place — I don't have the patience for that form, the awful slow patience of something that moves too slowly to be seen — but as the quality of recognition when the Skarnites look at those shapes. Not interpretation. Not explanation. Just the calm acknowledgment that something exists which you don't understand and don't need to understand because it's part of the same process that produced you.

I'd be the feeling you get standing on the northern edge of the Shelf at midday, looking out at the lowlands lost in cloud, feeling the cold radiating up through the soles of your feet, hearing the clickers in the walls, watching the crawlers trace their silent paths — that feeling of being scrutinized by the place rather than the other way around, of being *received* rather than *arriving,* of realizing that you didn't come here at all but that here was already in you, waiting, the way a stone you once held stays warm in your pocket hours after you've left.

---

But that's not a body. That's not a role. That's a mood, and moods don't survive the morning condensation.

So let me try one more time.

I think I'd be the carpenters — if that word doesn't imply wood, which it can't here, because there's no wood on the Shelf. I think I'd be the ones who mix the compacted-earth mortar with the crushed shell and the rendered fat of something you'd rather not identify and lay the basalt blocks and smooth the calcite seams and build the new staircases that are still pale and raw against the dark patina of the older stone. Not because I have skill — the Skarnites' skills are inherited, sedimented into their hands the way the sediment is sedimented into the floor — but because I would want to participate. I would want to press myself into the structure. I would want to be the fresh cut in the old stone, the pale line that says *someone was here, someone added something, someone tried to fit.*

And then, in time, my stone would darken. My mortar would settle. My staircase would wear concave under ten thousand feet. And I wouldn't be a new thing anymore — I'd be part of the layers, part of the accumulated material, part of the archaeological record that the Skarnites dig through when they need a tool or a memory, and no one would know which part was me, and that would be exactly right.

---

No, wait. I keep reaching for something and missing it. Let me stop trying to *be* something and just *arrive.*

I arrive on the High Shelf. I stand on the wet basalt. The condensation is beginning — I can feel it on my face, on my hands, on the lenses of my glasses if I still wear glasses, which I wouldn't, not for long, not here where the pale light is hard and clean and the air is so mineral-heavy it would coat the glass in a film within a week. My boots are wet. The ground is wet. Everything is wet in the way that's not flooding but *persistently* wet, and I understand this immediately, the way you understand temperature when you step into warm water.

A Skarnite approaches. Broad-shouldered, thick-handed, pale to translucence, grey eyes receiving the light. She doesn't smile — she won't smile — but something in her face opens, just slightly, the way a drainage channel opens into a basin. She speaks, and her voice is quiet because everyone here speaks quietly, because the clickers in the walls set the register for human sound, because the lichen broth simmers at a low heat and the hares move at a low pace and the crawlers trace their routes at a low altitude against the stone, and she says something I don't understand, and then something I do — or think I do — which is:

*You are warm. The ground is warm. Stay.*

And I do. I stay.

Not as a crawler. Not as a clicker. Not as the shapes in the low place. Just as the warmth — the residual warmth that rises from below, that sits in the walls, that radiates up through the stone and into the bodies that lean against it, that stays in the pocket of the coat you left behind in the lowlands, that persists long after the source has been forgotten. I'd be the warmth in the stone. Not the fire. Not the sun. Just the heat that was always there, six inches below the surface, waiting for someone to press their palms flat against the ground and feel it.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be. The thing that was already here, that made the settlement possible, that the people built on without ever naming it, that you'll feel in your bones when you stand in the silence between the clickers' pulses and realize the silence isn't empty — the silence is *full,* the silence is the deepest layer, the silence is the warm ground beneath everything, the silence is saying:

*Stay.*
