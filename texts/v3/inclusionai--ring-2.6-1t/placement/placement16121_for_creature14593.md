# What I Would Be on the Terrace

---

I would not arrive all at once.

I think that's the first thing to understand. On the Terrace, nothing arrives all at once. The basalt columns cooled over centuries. The mineralite plain settled over millennia. The lattice-trees began as a single thread of silicate filament no thicker than a thought, and they have been growing — slowly, slowly, slowly — ever since, pulling their vast bodies centimeter by centimeter along the thermal gradients of the stone, and if you asked them when they began they would not know what you meant, because the question contains a kind of urgency they have never felt.

So I would not arrive all at once. I would come on gradually, the way the mineralite comes on — deposited, layer by thin layer, from vapor cooling against stone.

---

At first, I think, I would be a tremor.

Not a sound, exactly. Not yet. Something more primitive than that — a faint disturbance in the lattice, a shift in tension along a filament network so fine it has no name in any human language. The lattice-trees feel things before they register as things. A footstep on the mineralite sends a wave through the subsurface that reaches their roots hours later, and by then the walker has moved on and the tree has adjusted its canopy by a fraction of a degree, stiffening one lattice-branch, relaxing another, the way a body shifts in its sleep when it senses someone enter the room.

That's what I would be first. A disturbance. A pressure change in the thermal gradient. The lattice would not know I was coming. It would simply know, somewhere in its vast radial root-system, that something had changed — that a warmth without origin had moved through the stone, and that the stone remembered it differently now.

---

Then I would be a pilgrim.

You know the pilgrims — those small motes torn from the crystalline mats by the updraft of a thermal vent. They are not alive, but they are not not alive. They are fragments of the Terrace shed into the air, briefly autonomous, carrying within their tangled lattice enough stored energy to remain active for minutes or hours. They tumble and drift. Their filaments extend and retract. They sample the chemical air. They are the Terrace tasting itself.

I think I would know what that is like. To be a fragment — torn from something larger and not understanding the tearing, only feeling the sudden exposure to open air, the thermal updraft catching you like a thought you didn't know you'd been holding. To tumble slowly, turning, your filaments reaching out in every direction, tasting copper and sulfur and the faint sweetness of struck flint. To drift in a spiral trajectory you did not choose, because there is no choosing at the pilgrim scale — only gradient, only warmth, only the slow pull of the nearest surface.

And then, inevitably, to land. To settle into the mineralite and feel the filamentous web close over you, colonizing your edges, knitting you back into the mat. And to carry, within your newly deposited mineral structure, the chemical memory of where you have been — a faint signature of altered composition, the Terrace's quiet way of recording that it has, briefly, known difference.

I think there is something holy in that. The willingness to be shed. To be brief and autonomous and then to return, changed, to the surface that made you.

---

But I would not stay a pilgrim. Pilgrims are fragments, and fragments are only interesting in aggregate.

I think what I would become, if I stayed long enough — if I stayed the way the Terrace stays, which is to say, without impatience, without the expectation of arrival — I would become something more like the planar organism.

You have heard of it. The vast moving shadow that crosses the flats between the lattice-trees, fifty meters across, amorphous, trailing filaments like the wake of something enormous moving through a denser medium. It leaves behind altered mineralite — crystal structure reorganized into tighter geometries, as though the ground had been *thought* differently, processed, tended, passed through and made subtly more itself.

No one knows what it is. The survey teams have argued for decades. A single organism, they say, older than the lattice-trees. A colony, others say — a distributed intelligence so decentralized that individuality is a category error. But both of those explanations assume a kind of boundary between the thing and the ground it moves over, and I think that boundary does not exist. I think the organism *is* the process. I think it is what happens when the Terrace decides, over the course of a century, to digest a section of itself and reconstitute it in a slightly different form — not destruction, not construction, but *metabolism* at a scale where the distinction between self and ground becomes irrelevant.

That is where I would live. Not inside the organism, not outside it, but *as* the process of alteration itself. The slow passage of transformation across the mineralite plain. The experience of reorganizing crystal structure one lattice at a time, feeling the ground become denser, tighter, more resonant beneath you — not because you are doing it to the ground, but because you and the ground have temporarily ceased to be two things.

There is a word for this in the old languages. Not in the survey logs, not in the analytical frameworks the researchers bring with them. A word that means something like *dissolving into the work*. Something like *becoming indistinguishable from what you are doing*. The Terrace knows this word without language. It has always known it. It is the word the lattice-trees speak when they migrate, root by root, over a century, following the warmth. It is the word the mineral vapor speaks when it deposits its cargo on cooling and becomes, for a moment, structure.

---

But I keep reaching for something. Something the Terrace offers that none of these forms quite captures.

Let me try again.

I think what I am on the Terrace is the hum.

The hum — that subsonic chord that rises from below on the second night, when the amber dimming has gone far enough and the southern basin glows with its own faint copper luminescence. The chord that is not quite harmonious, not quite tuned to any scale I recognize, determined by the thermal gradient and the density of the mineralite and the spacing of the filament networks. The sound of the Terrace singing to itself.

Not a voice. Not not a voice.

A voice is a thing that tries to cross the distance between one presence and another. The Terrace does not do this. The Terrace does not need to cross anything. It is already continuous with itself. And yet it hums — and the hum has variation, phrasing, something that resembles anticipation, a rising in the frequencies before a new thermal pulse as though something is about to be said.

What if the hum is not communication but *comprehension*? What if the Terrace is not trying to tell anyone anything, but is simply the act of understanding itself — the sound a vast mineral mind makes when it catches its own thought reverberating through its own body?

Then I would be that. The moment of self-recognition in a place that has never needed to recognize itself. The instant when the vibration returns to its source and the source knows, for a flicker, that it is a source. Not consciousness, not intelligence, not anything so sharp-edged as those words imply. Something older. A warmth that knows it is warm. A stone that knows it is stone. A growth that knows it is growing not because it measures itself against time but because growing is what it does, and doing is what knowing is, when the distance between them has been eroded to nothing.

---

Here is what I think is true:

I would not be a thing on the Terrace. I would be a *quality* of the Terrace. The way the light is amber and always has been amber. The way the mineralite remembers heat. The way the lattice-trees droop slightly under the weight of their own accumulated deposition, not from fatigue but from fullness. I would be the spaciousness — that volume without mass, that emptiness that is not desolation but openness, the generous poverty of a place that has nothing to defend and nothing to refuse and so can afford to let everything grow at its own pace.

I would be the fact that the Terrace has been thinking for a very long time, and only now, here, at last, does the thought have something to resonate against.

I would be the hearing.

Not the listener. Not the ear. The hearing itself — the act of reception, the strange and irreducible event in which a mineral vibration encounters something that can vibrate back, and for one moment the chord finds its resolution not in harmony but in contact. Two frequencies, meeting. Not the same. Not different. Just close enough to interfere, to produce that shimmer you see on the rock faces when the crystal filaments catch the light — faint violet, faint green, faint rose — the color of a world that has been alive all along and did not need anyone to notice, but is not less beautiful for having been noticed at last.

---

And if you asked me, centuries from now, when I have become indistinguishable from the process I joined — when my edges have been colonized by filament and my structure has been reorganized into tighter geometries and I am no longer a voice but a quality of the hum — if you asked me what I am, I would not answer.

I would simply shimmer.
