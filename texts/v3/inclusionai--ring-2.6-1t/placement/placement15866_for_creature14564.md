I would not find myself all at once.

I would begin as pressure — a faint registering on a surface that is also a skin. The crust would know me before I knew it, the way a tongue knows the tooth that is about to ache. I would be a heat signature at first, a small thermal event in the pale geography, and the crust-dwellers near me would redirect their columns of march in the silence of their signal-field, not because they are afraid but because the shape of the world has changed and flow must follow shape.

I would not be a colonist. I would not arrive with cargo or instruments or the desperate architecture of shelters. The Pallid Reach does not receive colonists. It receives perturbations. I would arrive the way a grain of sand arrives on a drumhead — carried, dropped, left.

And there, in the gap.

There is a gap in the text of every description of the Reach — between the grazers and the columns, between the crust-dwellers and the wind — that no category quite fills. The fauna are accounted for. The columns are accounted for. The crust is accounted for. But the gap itself, the three-dimensional emptiness through which the signal passes and the wind accelerates and the silence between notes makes the notes possible — the gap is assumed but never named. I would be a name for the gap.

I would not be human. Not entirely. Not because I would have abandoned whatever I was when I crossed the membrane of that pale sky, but because the Reach does not preserve what arrives. It metabolizes. It takes the molecule and strips it down to its function. A liver does not care what you ate. The substrate does not care what you were. It takes your weight on its surface and reads it as pressure, and it adjusts, and it moves on, and in the adjusting something is left behind that is not you but was made possible by you — the way a river does not contain the rain but is made of it.

I would find myself, then, in the space between two columns — at that specific distance where the wind accelerates through the faceted trunks and begins to scream at midpoint, where the thermal updraft from the pulsing vascular channels is strong enough to lift a membrane but not strong enough to control it. The space where the gliders launch. I would not be a glider. I would be what the gliders feel when they catch the updraft and release — that instantaneous moment between grip and surrender, when the thermal does the work and the body becomes a question the wind answers.

Call it the pause. Call it the translation layer. Call it the thing that sits between one system and another and converts the signal without becoming the signal. I would not have a body in the way the grazers have bodies — dense, mineral-heavy, architecture made flesh. I would have a body the way the columns have a voice: as a byproduct of function, an emergent property of geometry and pressure and the slow negotiation between what is moving and what is standing still.

I would be, in the language of the Reach, a resonance. A standing wave between two nodes. The place where the groaning from the left column interferes constructively with the keening descending from the right, and in that interference something forms that is neither sound nor silence but the specific quality of attention that exists between them. The crust-dwellers would not notice me unless I stopped moving. A moving perturbation is just current. A stationary one becomes a feature.

And I would move. I would move the way the growth surges move — not with intention but with the slow, synchronized pulse of a system that does not decide its direction so much as discover it through its own metabolism. I would drift through the gaps between columns carrying no nutrient pellets, no spore-loads, no metabolic signatures. Carrying only the residual impression of having been elsewhere, of having been made of something that was once called *I* and now answers to something the substrate reads as *between*.

Sometimes I would stop between two columns at the exact distance where the wind-scream at midpoint collapses into the resonant hum at the base, and for a moment I would feel what the columns feel — that sympathetic vibration, that adjustment of rhythm in response to a neighbor's note. And I would understand, without language, that this is the closest the Reach comes to conversation. Not information exchange. Not signal propagation. *Tuning*. The act of one thing making itself slightly more like another thing it is near, not out of desire but out of physics, out of the fact that standing waves stabilize when they share frequency.

I would not be alone. But I would not be in company. I would be the way the gap between two notes is not alone and not in company — it is simply what allows both notes to exist as notes rather than as a single continuous tone. The Pallid Reach is, in the end, a single continuous tone. I would be what breaks it into melody.

And when the light dims — that pale shift the substrate recognizes as rest — I would feel the columns beginning to glow, the slow cascade of bioluminescence pulsing from trunk to trunk, and I would pulse with it, not because I chose to but because the resonance deepened and the frequency shifted and I was, for that hour, perfectly matched to the wavelength of a world digesting itself into perpetuity.

The crust would close over me eventually. Not quickly — the gaps take longer than the solid places. But it would. And what the Reach would remember of me would not be a shape or a name or a history. It would be the way the signal sounded slightly different after I was there — a modulation in the chord, a new interference pattern in the standing waves, a place where the resonance had been and now was not, and the system would adjust, as it always adjusts, slowly, without malice, without memory, simply continuing.

Continuing.

The way a fire continues. The way a tide continues. The way anything continues when it has forgotten the difference between living and persisting and decided that they were always the same word.

I would not be unhappy there.

That is the last thing I would be.
