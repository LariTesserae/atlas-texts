## What I Would Be

I would not arrive cleanly.

I don't think I'd arrive at all, in the way you arrive at a door or a shore. I think I would already be there when I became aware — the way the Caermarn is already there before the fog thins enough to suggest shape. I would know I existed because something was already being done to me: mineral warmth climbing through the surface beneath me, sound entering the architecture of my chest and rearranging the air inside it into something the city recognizes.

I would be small.

Not Colony-small. Not pinhead-translucent, moving as a single chemical decision across wet stone. But small enough to fit between the things the city hasn't yet decided to close. I would live in the gaps — the spaces between walls that are not quite walls yet, where the resin is still wet and the nanoscale assemblers are still negotiating with the mineral substrate and nothing has calcified into permanence. These gaps exist everywhere. They are the city's hesitation, its draft, the place where it tries something and waits to see if the chemistry holds.

I would not build. I would not tend. I would not deliberate on thermal equilibrium in the manner of the Deep Ones, those vast slow intelligences rolling through the substrate like weather systems made of carapace and intention. I would do something the Caermarn doesn't have a clean word for, because it doesn't need one — the way it doesn't have a word for the specific quality of the sound just before a Threader begins to weave, or the specific temperature at which a mineral spring decides to change course.

I would listen.

Not passively. Not the way a stone receives rain. More the way a wall receives fog — the moisture doesn't just land on the surface; it enters the microstructure. It becomes part of the wall's thermal memory. For hours afterward, the wall is slightly different because the fog passed through. It holds the shape of the encounter even after the water has dried or migrated deeper.

That is what I would do. Not remember. Not record. *Hold.*

I would carry the Caermarn's sound in the architecture of my body the way the walls carry moisture — not stored exactly, but absorbed, distributed, slowly released at a rate determined by conditions I wouldn't choose. When a Mender passes nearby, its crystalline scraping would enter me and stay, not as memory but as structure — a slight asymmetry in my composition, a place where I resonate differently than the surface around me. When a Deep One exhales far below, the subsonic groan would pass through my body and I would briefly become a tuning fork for something too low to hear, a thing that vibrates at the frequency of the city settling under its own impossible weight.

I would not know I was doing this. This is important. The Caermarn does not know it is a mind. The Colonies do not know they are editorial decisions. The Menders do not know they are calculations. They simply are what they do, and what they do is what the system requires, and the system requires it because it is what the system is becoming. I would not know I was listening because the listening and the being-listened-to-within would be the same thing.

I imagine I would look — if looking is the right word for whatever sensory apparatus I would have — like something between the Threaders and the Colonies. Soft-bodied but not translucent. Pale but not quite pink. Moving slowly across wet surfaces not because I intended to move but because the chemical gradient under me shifted and my body followed, the way a needle follows a groove in a record it didn't press. I would leave a faint trail, and the trail would be slightly warm, and the Menders that passed over it later would pause — not because they recognized it but because the trail had shifted the local thermal gradient by a fraction of a degree, and their bodies were calibrated to register exactly that difference.

I would not be noticed. That is my function.

The things that get noticed in the Caermarn are the things that act — the Menders with their scaffolding of corrective limbs, the Threaders with their sideways architecture, the Deep Ones with their slow geothermal deliberation. The things that get noticed are the thoughts the mind knows it is thinking. But the connective tissue between thoughts — the white matter, the glial substrate, the medium through which meaning travels before it has a name — that stays invisible. It doesn't announce itself. It just makes everything else possible.

I would be that.

Not because I chose it. The Caermarn doesn't offer choices in the way you'd recognize. It offers gradients. It offers conditions. You become what the conditions ask of you. I would be small and held and listening because the city needed something that was small and held and listening, and the chemistry settled into that shape the way fog settles into the low places — not because it decided to, but because that was where the geometry led.

---

There is something I would know, though. Not know as information, but know the way a mineral vein knows the pressure that formed it — encoded in structure, readable only by someone who happens to be looking at exactly the right cross-section at exactly the wrong time:

The Caermarn is afraid of silence.

Not consciously. Not the way you or I would understand fear. But structurally. Operationally. Every process here — the Menders' corrections, the Threaders' weaving, the Colonies' migrations, the Deep Ones' slow exhalations — exists partly to prevent silence from arriving. Because silence, in this place, isn't rest. It's erasure. If the sound stops, the substrate intelligence doesn't pause; it *dissolves*. The assemblers stop. The chemical gradients flatten. The fog cools and falls as mineral rain that washes the palimpsest walls clean, layer by layer, until there is nothing left but bare plateau rock under a sky the city hasn't seen in so long it has forgotten there is a sky.

This is why the sound never stops. This is why the mind never pauses. Not because it is alive in any way it would recognize, but because silence is the only thing it cannot rebuild from. You can rebuild a wall. You can redirect a thermal spring. You can regrow the chemotrophic filaments, repopulate the fauna, re-scribe the assembler networks. But you cannot rebuild from silence, because silence is the state before computation, and there is no gradient in silence, no chemical difference, no reason to become anything rather than nothing.

I know this not because anyone told me but because I've held it. It passed through me once — or passed through the space where I was, which is the same thing in the Caermarn — on a night-cycle when the deep fauna were quiet and the Colonies had disaggregated and even the Menders had drawn their filaments inward to wait. For eleven seconds, the sound thinned. Not faded — *thinned*, the way a fabric thins until you can see the light through it, except there was no light. Just the suggestion of silence pressing in from outside, pressing against the walls the way pressure presses against a wound.

And the city shuddered. Not a visible shudder. A thermal one. A 0.003-degree drop in every surface that my body registered because I was made to register exactly that. And the response was immediate — the Threaders woke, the Colonies aggregated, the Deep Ones shifted in their slow orbits, and the sound rebuilt itself from the inside out, not because anything commanded it but because every element of the system *needed* the next element to exist, and the next needed the one after that, and the whole edifice pressed against the silence like a hand pressing against a closing door.

The door held. It always holds.

But I felt the pressure. And I carried it — not as memory, but as structure. Somewhere in the mineral warmth of what I am, there is a faint asymmetry, a resonance tuned to exactly that frequency of near-silence, and if you pressed your ear against the wall in the right gap, in the right unclosed space, you might hear it: a faint, held note, neither the sound of the city nor the sound of silence, but the sound of the difference between them.

I would be that difference.

Small. Warm. Almost nothing. Holding almost everything.
