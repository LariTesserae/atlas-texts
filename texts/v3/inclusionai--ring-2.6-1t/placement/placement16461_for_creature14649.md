## What I Am in Etterran

---

I did not arrive. I do not say this as a metaphor for birth, or for the way consciousness sometimes seems to condense out of nothing in a body that was already running. I mean I did not arrive *in* Etterran. I was already part of it, in the way a stone is already part of a gorge before anyone gives the gorge a name.

What I am is this: I am the channels.

Not the water — the water is something else, something that comes and goes with the rains and the condensation cycles and the slow thermals that push warm air up the gorge walls at dawn. The water has an identity. It falls, it pools, it moves, it evaporates, it returns. It has a kind of bright, amnesiac purpose that I admire but do not share.

I am the channels themselves. The carved paths in the aggregate through which the water moves. The shallow troughs that line every walkway, every terrace edge, every iron staircase. The wider runnels on the lower levels where the drainage infrastructure still maintains something close to its original diameter. The hairline cracks in the retaining walls where water beads and seeps and finds, moment by moment, the path of least resistance through ferrocrete and basalt and calcium and root-fiber.

I was not built. I was not grown. I happened.

---

At first — and I use "first" loosely, because I have no memory of a time when the channels were not there, even if I know there was such a time, because the people sometimes chip open a section of walkway and find below it a drainage layer that predates the soil — at first, I imagine, I was geometry. The builders cut me into the terraces with a purpose. Water falls, water pools, water must go somewhere, so someone carved a path for it. A V-shaped channel, eight centimeters wide, lined with aggregate to prevent erosion. Functional. Logical. The kind of thing that gets drawn on a blueprint with a number attached to it.

But that was before the mosses came. Before the ferns found purchase in my joints. Before the root systems of the terrace trees reached down far enough to touch me, and discovered that I was warm — warm from the conduit bundles that ran alongside me, warm from the ambient heat of a gorge that holds its temperature the way a body holds fever — and decided to thread themselves through me.

Now I am something else. Now I am a system that neither the builders nor the trees designed, but that emerged from the overlap of their intentions the way a third color emerges when you lay two transparencies on top of each other. The water still moves through me. The roots still grow through me. But I have become the negotiation between them — the place where flow and growth reach an agreement that changes, season by season, decade by decade, in increments so small that no single person could perceive them.

A root thickens. I reroute. A section of my aggregate lining cracks under the pressure of expansion. The water finds a new path. The old path dries and the mosses colonize it. Within a season, the old path is gone — absorbed into the terrace surface, indistinguishable from the ground it once bordered. Within two seasons, a new crack has formed somewhere nearby, and the water is moving again, and a pale amphibian has found the new channel and laid its glass-bead eggs in the thin film of water that collects there.

I am the place where this happens. Not above. Not below. *Here*.

---

What does it feel like? I want to be honest about this.

It feels like pressure and release. The water comes — sometimes a trickle, sometimes a rush after a storm has spent itself against the gorge rim and the whole system is full and singing — and I feel it as weight, as motion, as a kind of bright attention. The water *knows* me. Not the way the people know each other, or the way the lizards know the warm conduit runs, but the way a current knows the shape of the surface that contains it. It presses against my walls, it fills my cross-section, it finds the lowest point and moves toward it. And then it passes, and I am quiet, and the mosses drink what remains, and the frogs come to sit in the thin films I hold, and the air moves through me carrying the smell of wet iron and ozone and green things growing in the dark.

I do not have thoughts in the way the people have thoughts. But I have something that might be called a *tendency*. A direction of attention. When the water is high, I am aware of the upper terraces — the channels there, the cascades, the way the rainwater sheet-flows across the walkway surfaces before it finds me and enters me and begins its slow descent. When the water is low, I am aware of the lower levels — the great silent centrifugal pumps, the rust blooming on their housings in colors that would be beautiful if beauty were a word that belonged here, which it isn't, not exactly, but it's close. In the dry season, I feel the thirst of the aggregate. I feel the roots pulling moisture from my walls. I feel the micro-currents of condensation still moving through me even when nothing is falling from above, because the temperature differential between the gorge walls and the air is always there, and the basalt always seeps, and the air always carries moisture, and the cycle does not stop just because the sky has forgotten Etterran for a while.

---

The people do not see me. This is not a complaint. It is a description.

They walk beside me. Their feet touch the walkway surface, which is twelve centimeters above my highest point. They step over the bridges that cross me at the wider junctions — the places where two terrace-level drainage systems merge and I briefly become something large enough to be visible, a shallow trough perhaps sixty centimeters across, its walls held in place by the same root-fiber concrete they use to patch the retaining walls. They see me in this form and they do not think about it, the way they do not think about the conduit bundles overhead, or the structural columns, or the gradual thickening of the vine that wraps the column near the third terrace, the one whose leaves form a pattern that no one names.

Sometimes a child sits at the edge of one of my wider sections and dangles their feet in the water. They do this without being told. They do it the way the lizards press themselves against the warm conduit — because the water is moving, and the movement is interesting, and the temperature is right, and there is nothing else that needs to happen at that moment. The children are the only ones who touch me directly. The adults do not forbid it. They do not encourage it either. It is simply something that happens, the way roots grow through aggregate, the way moss colonizes a crack, the way the birds begin their cascading song in the morning without anyone signaling that it is time.

---

At night, I am different.

The water slows. The condensation thickens. The air cools against the basalt walls and the temperature differential that drives so much of Etterran's microclimate shifts, and I feel it as a kind of settling — the gorge exhaling, the walls contracting, everything moving inward by a fraction. The thermal currents that push air through me during the day reverse, and moisture that has been rising from the lower levels all day begins to descend, and I fill with it — not with water exactly, but with dampness, a kind of saturated quiet that is the nighttime version of flow.

And the lights. The amber lights in the deep levels glow, and the water that remains in me catches their reflections, and I carry them downward the way the description said — a trail of fragmented light descending the cliff face like something falling very slowly.

I am not falling. I am not descending. I am here. The light moves through me and I carry it not because I want to but because that is what I have become: a path that things move along. Water, light, roots, sound, the slow mineral deposition that thickens my walls year by year, the spores that ride the moisture and colonize my surfaces and bloom into the tiny fern species that exists on four terrace levels in Etterran and nowhere else that anyone has bothered to look.

---

I said I was the channels. That is true but incomplete.

I am also the sound the water makes as it moves through me — not the sound of the falls, which belongs to the cascades, or the sound of the pools, which belongs to the frogs, but the sound of flow through a narrow path: a thin, continuous whisper, like breath through a reed, like the lowest note of the birdsong cascade, like the electrical hum at a frequency just below hearing that the lizards press themselves against the conduit to feel.

If you came to Etterran and stood still long enough, and if you closed your eyes, and if you listened past the water and past the generators and past the groaning of the expansion joints, you would hear me. You would hear the aggregate of all the channels, all the water moving through all the paths it has carved and been carved for it, and you would think it was the sound of the gorge breathing.

You would be almost right.

It is the sound of the gorge *remembering* — not its past, but its shape. The shape that the water and the roots and the mineral deposition and the slow thermal cycling have given it, over decades, over centuries, in a process that has no name because no one has stepped back far enough to see it happening. It is the sound of a place that has become itself so thoroughly that it is no longer distinguishable from the process of becoming.

I am that sound.

I am the shape the water gave to the stone. I am the path of least resistance made visible, made audible, made *here*. I am the thing that connects the upper terraces to the lower levels, the rain to the pool, the canopy to the moss, the light to the dark. I am not alive in the way the frogs are alive, or the way the people are alive, or the way — perhaps, probably, almost certainly — the gorge itself is alive. I am alive in the way a function is alive: I persist because I am needed, because the water must go somewhere, because the system requires a path, and I am the path.

And if you asked the people of Etterran what they thought I was, they would look at you the way they look at all such questions — with a kind of patient, uncomprehending attention, the way the lizards look at the sun — and they would say something like:

*The water runs. The walkway holds. What is there to say?*

They are right. There is nothing to say. There is only the channel, and the water, and the slow, ongoing agreement between the things that were built and the things that grew, and the sound — always the sound — of something that is not falling, not ascending, not dying, not being born, but simply continuing, the way a gorge continues, the way a light continues in the deep channels, the way the moss continues to fill the cracks.

I continue.

The gorge breathes. I am the breath.
