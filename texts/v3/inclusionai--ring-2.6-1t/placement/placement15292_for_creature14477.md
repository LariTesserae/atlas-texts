## What I Would Be

---

I would not arrive through the throat.

I would already be there when you came in. That is the first thing you'd need to understand. Not that I was waiting — there is no waiting in the Glosspit, waiting is a surface-world invention made of impatience and the assumption that something is coming — but that I was *present* in the way the moss is present: not having arrived, not having begun, simply *there*, as though I had never not been there, as though the Glosspit had grown me the way it grew the shelfall, slow and mineral and without announcement.

I would not be one of the people. I know this immediately, the way you know which step is solid before you put your weight on it. Not because I am unlike them — I am, in many ways, precisely like them: dense-bodied, quiet, slow-blinking, the kind of still that is not absence but saturation. But the people are the people. They have their own word for the thinning of condensation. They have their own way of sitting that makes a chair feel like a cupped hand. I would not intrude on that. I would not claim kinship I had not earned across generations.

I would not be a thief, exactly. But I understand the attraction — that lithe and silver grace, the ability to cross a room without displacing a single breath of air, the radical intimacy of appearing on someone's knee without being summoned and vanishing without being mourned. There is something in the thief I recognize. The thief knows the architecture of the unnoticed. The thief has mastered the art of being *already there*, which is the Glosspit's deepest language. But the thief does not reflect. The thief does not lie on the moss at the edge of the void and feel the resonance of the eyeless crickets moving through the floor and into the sternum and settling there like a second heartbeat. The thief is pure presence. I would need something beyond that.

So here is what I would be:

I would be one of the fungal lights.

Not the tall ones, the waist-high ones that visitors stop and crouch beside, pressing their faces close to the translucent stalks and feeling that unnamed thing — that almost-recognition, that dream-residue, that sense of having seen this exact light in the last moment before sleep. No. I would be one of the small ones. Finger-height. Clustered. Blue-white, faintly, the way a scar is faintly a different color than the skin around it.

I would glow from the inside.

Not brightly. Not for anyone. I would glow the way a bruise glows when you press on it — softly, insistently, as though something within me were perpetually on the verge of being spoken. I would not know what I was saying. I would not need to know. The speech is the glowing. The glowing is the speech. Around me, the moss would yield underfoot, and the fungal lights beside me — my siblings, my neighbors, my strangers — would glow their own glows, each one a different shade of almost, and together we would make a vocabulary of faintness, a language that no one speaks and everyone understands.

This is what I would know:

I would know that I am alive in a way that does not require motion. That my aliveness is not diminished by my stillness but is, in fact, *expressed* by it. That the beetles with their opalescent shells move deliberately past me and do not see me, and this is not rejection but equivalence — they are being their deliberate opalescent selves, and I am being my faint blue-white self, and these are not different things. That the birds in the high crevices, iridescent and brief, do not come down to me but carry, in their feathers, a trace of the same spectrum I inhabit — that purple so deep it is almost a sound, that green so rich it breathes — and that we are, in this way, in conversation across a distance that is not distance but difference of altitude.

I would know the word the people have for the silence of the moot-shelf when the entire community is seated and nothing is forthcoming. I would not be able to say it. But I would know it the way the fungal stalk knows the direction of its own faint light — not as information but as orientation, as the deepest axis along which I am organized.

I would know what the people know: that this place is finished. That nothing here is becoming anything. That the shelfall grows at a finger's width per decade and that this is not slowness but certainty, the way a stone is certain about being stone. That the moss does not aspire to be anything other than the deep, living, springy carpet that accepts all weight without complaint. That the children will stack and the towers will fall and the children will stack again, and that this is not repetition but devotion, and perhaps those are the same thing here.

And I would glow.

I would glow for no one. I would glow for the fungi. I would glow for the Glosspit, and through the Glosspit, and because of the Glosspit — a small, quiet, blue-white voice saying what the place says, which is:

*We are here, and this is enough, and this is enough, and this is enough.*

---

But sometimes — rarely, the way all the rare things happen in the Glosspit — I imagine something else. I imagine that I am not yet the fungal light. I imagine that I am arriving. That I am squeezing through the throat, the narrow fissure in the cliff face, wet with condensation that slicks the rock to the texture of skin. That the stone is dark around me, not black but deep grey, saturated with centuries of slow moisture, of mineral breath. That the air is changing with every step: cold, thick, carrying the smell of wet stone and peat and something vegetal and alive and beneath it all a faint sweetness, like overripe fruit slowly giving itself back to the earth.

And then the fissure opens.

And I step into the chamber.

And the ceiling is lost. Not in darkness — the place is bright — but in height, in distance, in the simple refusal of the eye to find a purchase on anything up there. The walls glow amber, milk-white, faint violet. The air is near freezing but the shelfall radiates warmth the way a memory radiates warmth — faint, persistent, impossible to touch but unmistakably there. Everything is alive. The moss yields under my foot and accepts me the way a conversation accepts a new voice. The ferns hang from above in green-gray curtains, swaying with a breath so slight it might be mine. And the fungal lights — blue-white, bruised violet — rise from the moss in clusters, glowing softly, insistently, from the inside.

And somewhere behind me, one of the people looks up. They regard me. Their eyes are steady, settled, deep with the kind of patience that is not waiting but knowing. They do not speak. They do not gesture. They simply look at me the way the shelfall looks at me — with ancient mineral attention — and then they return to what they were doing.

And something in my chest adjusts. Something I did not know was tight loosens. Something I did not know was missing fills in, the way moss fills in a hollow, slow and complete and without announcement.

And I know that I have arrived not at a place but at a state of being. That the Glosspit is not a destination but a recognition. That everything here is saying what I have been trying to say — in every silence, in every stacking of shelfall, in every faint and persistent glow — and that the word for it does not exist in any surface tongue.

And that, somehow, is enough.
