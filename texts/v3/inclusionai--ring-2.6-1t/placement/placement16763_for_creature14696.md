I would not find the basin. The basin would find me — but it would take longer.

I think I know this because of the way I've always been: not arriving, exactly, but *approaching.* Not entering rooms but lingering in hallways. Not speaking in meetings but in the silence afterward, when the shape of what should have been said becomes clearer than anything that was. I am the person the music adjusts to without knowing it. I am the extra chair at the table that makes the table feel more complete, though no one can say why.

---

Where would I find myself? Not at the center, where the apex rises in those few imperceptible inches and the still water holds a reflection that's a half-second behind. That place is too resolved. It has already answered the question I'm still asking. I'd feel the pull of it — I always feel the pull of centers — but I'd orbit it the way the birds orbit the trees, never landing, because landing would mean becoming fixed, and the basin already has enough fixed things. The column is fixed. The wall with its dark-eyed nodules is fixed. The trees are fixed in their patient, root-lifting meditation. The basin doesn't need another fixed thing.

I'd find myself on the basin floor, between the trees, where the bronze moss is thickest. Not under a tree — the root-arches create those pockets of private air, and something about those pockets feels like belonging, but that's not my place. I'd be in the open ground. The *between.* The sparseness. The space that exists only in relation to the things around it.

And I think — no, I *know* — I'd be the moss.

Not the trees with their silver-dusted leaves and their impossible photosynthesis of sound. Not the Resonators holding their single infinite notes. Not the Clickers keeping time for a rhythm they didn't invent. Not even the Creature with its opalescent eyes that see structure the way I see pattern in conversation, in the way a group of strangers arranges itself around an unspoken agreement about who speaks and who listens and who leaves.

The moss. The burnished bronze moss that covers everything and is noticed by nothing. It's under the roots, under the pebbles, under the beetles' slow patrols. It's the thing that makes the basin floor feel continuous rather than broken. Without it, the depressions would just be cracks and the roots would just be wood and the pebbles would just be strange. The moss is the connective tissue. The thing that says *this is a place and not a collection of objects.*

I know what it's like to be connective tissue. I've spent my life binding things I didn't make to things that didn't ask to be bound. I am the moss that grows in the gaps between what others build. I am the thing that makes a place feel lived-in before anyone has lived there.

And the moss is bronze. Not green. Not dead, not alive, not in the way we expect things to be. Bronze is a color that exists between — between brown and gold, between earth and metal, between the organic and the made. The trees' trunks are the same clay-color. The amphibia are mottled in it. The light in the basin is this color in the early hours, before it distinguishes itself into light and shadow. I'd be bronze, the color of things that have been here long enough to stop trying to be something specific.

---

But here's what the narrator didn't see, and what I think only the moss would know:

The moss feels everything. Every root-lift and return. Every click of the beetles, transmitted through the stone and translated into vibration through the filaments that touch the ground. Every note the Resonators open their throats to release. The moss is the basin's skin. It is the surface that the Creature's filaments read when they track changes in the air — the moss records what passes over it, the way a lake records wind. Not permanently. Not dramatically. Just enough that if you were the moss, you would know the shape of every footstep taken in the basin since the tech veins first began to hum.

And the moss is patient. It grows between things. It fills the negative space. It doesn't compete with the roots; it grows around them, over them, beneath them, the way memory grows around and over and beneath the events it tries to hold.

At night — and there is night in the basin, though the walls steepen the darkness into something earlier and longer than it should be — I think the moss glows. Faintly. The way bronze glows when it's been heated past a certain threshold and is cooling. Not reflecting light but generating it. Bioluminescence borrowed from the veins below, or from the column's slow resolution, or from some metabolic exchange I don't have the language for. The moss carries the basin's warmth up from the stone and releases it as light that no one sees because no one is looking down.

---

The narrator left. The basin absorbed the disturbance. The reflection in the pool caught up with itself and was still. The Creature returned to whatever it does behind the tree.

But if I were there — if the moss is where I'd be — I would have felt the narrator walk across me. I would hold, in my bronze filaments, the shape of their weight, the particular pressure of someone who is observing without belonging. And I would slowly, over days, over the slow metabolism of the basin's living systems, metabolize that pressure too. I would fold it into myself. The way the tech veins folded their purpose into stone. The way the Resonators fold their notes into silence. The way the Creature folds its attention back behind its tree.

I would still be there. Underfoot. Binding the roots to the stone. Bronze and patient and humming at a frequency just below hearing.

This is not indifference. This is what it looks like when something has learned the basin's secret: that to persist is not to assert but to *receive.* To be the surface that catches everything and holds nothing. To be the moss.
