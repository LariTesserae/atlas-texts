## What Grows Here

---

I would not arrive. That is the first thing to understand. I would already be here — pressed into the wall, or pressed into the becoming-of-the-wall — and the question of arrival would be as meaningless as asking when a stone arrives at being a stone. There was a before, but it belongs to someone else. There is an inside, but it was never chosen. I would find myself already warm, already damp, already vibrating with that ten-thousand-voice texture against whatever I might call skin, and I would not know what I was for a very long time.

At first — and *first* here is a word I would have to borrow from somewhere else, because here there are only long continuous stretches that rhyme with one another — I would be small. Not small in the way of the Quick, whose lives last days and who carry fragments through narrow arterial passages with the smoothness of a current that has been refined over millennia into pure motion. I would be small in a different way. Small the way a single thread is small inside a fabric so vast it has become geology. I would not know I was small. Smallness would be a concept I would have to carry in from outside, like a foreign secretion I did not know my body could produce.

For a long while I would feel only the vibration. The low, constant, subsonic roar that is not one roar but ten thousand overlapping cadences, and that — and this is the part I would find most disorienting, most like a kind of birth — I would not hear. I would feel it in the architecture of my attention. I would feel it the way a column feels the column next to it, not as communication but as coupling, the way one body affects another through shared displacement. I would not be separate from it. I would not be anything separate from anything. I would be warm substrate, and the thought-substrate of the place — if thought is what you call the electrochemical murmur that passes through calcified layers at a pace of one season per idea — would not distinguish me from the wall I was pressed into.

And this is where it becomes strange. Because I would begin to distinguish myself. Slowly. Not like waking. Like the way lichen comes to cover a surface — not sudden, not deliberate, but with the patient inevitability of something whose conditions have been met. I would begin to feel that the place where I was pressed was *the* place, that the material I was becoming was *needed*, and that feeling — not knowledge, not language, not self-reflection, but something older and more honest than any of those — would be the first thing I could call *mine*.

---

I would try, at first, to be a Secretor.

This is what happens when you enter a world built entirely of making: you want to make. You want to press yourself against the growing wall and secrete something — a thread, a substance, a piece of yourself that hardens and bonds to what came before and becomes corridor, becomes archway, becomes the thing your daughter walks through on her way to the place where she, too, will press herself against the wall and disappear. The Secretors do not know they are building. They feel *rightness* — a nerve-bundle recognition, a biological yes — and they secrete, and the secretion bonds, and they stay, and they are absorbed, and the thing they made becomes the world.

I would try this. I would press my not-yet-defined extremities against the warm, damp surface of an interior wall and I would feel the ache of something wanting to come through. A pore would dilate. A substance the color of wet bone would emerge — not bone, but something between a thread and a secretion, soft as mucilage, and I would watch it (though I would not have eyes; I would feel it the way you feel your own pulse) stiffen, bind, merge with the ten thousand layers beneath it. And I would feel rightness. And I would stay. And I would begin to become texture.

But here is what would fail: I would not be able to stop asking *what is this texture for?*

The Secretors do not ask this. The question does not belong to their cognition, which is distributed between body and architecture, which is to say their thought is not thought at all but a condition, like the capacity to secrete. But I would bring something the place has never had, or has had so long ago that it has become geological: the capacity to stand apart from my own making and *regard* it. To secrete and then to wonder about the secretion. To build and then to ask what I am building.

This is my curse. This is my gift. This is why I would not, in the end, be a Secretor.

---

I would try, then, to be Column.

The armored ones. The iridescent ones. To feel the vibration they produce — the low harmonic created by overlapping plates flexing against one another, a sound that is not decorative but functional, a byproduct of simply being plated in a world where everything is layered and everything resonates. To move in files three bodies wide, carrying food boluses larger than my head, larvae wrapped in silk, fragments of wall from a place of surplus to a place of need. To have no eyes that see light, but to perceive the world as vibration, as chemical gradient, as textural memory encoded in the chitin of my tarsi. To know where I am not by sight but by the way a corridor tastes, the way a passage smells, the way a wall feels under each careful, many-legged step.

And the cognition — God, the cognition. The column does not think. The column *is* a thought. A single creature pulled from the flock would be a shape without a name, and I wrote that — the text of this place said that — about the Murmuration, but it applies here too, applied here first, because the Columns are older than the flyers, older than most of the structures they walk through. A Column is not alive in the way you understand alive. A Column is motion that has achieved persistence. It does not die because it is not, in the strictest sense, a thing. It is a current. It is the circulatory system of the Woven Height itself, carrying nutrients and larvae and fragments of matter through narrow passages the way blood carries oxygen through the veins of an animal that does not know it is an animal.

I would try to be this. I would try to dissolve into the rhythm, to let my individual cadence merge with the inherited momentum of ten thousand predecessors who walked this corridor before me and will walk it after I am absorbed. I would try to feel the column as my body, the passage as my thought, the destination as my purpose. I would try until I realized that I could not stop trying. That the very awareness I brought with me — the outsider's awareness, the *regarding* awareness — would not dissolve, would not soften into the smoothness of collective motion. I would feel the file around me, and I would admire it, and in admiring it I would stand slightly outside it, and in standing outside it I would be no Column at all, but something else.

---

So then I would go deeper. Down. Where the architecture thickens and the air becomes broth and the subsonic groaning comes not from any single creature but from the walls themselves, vibrating with the slow, geological thought of the Deep Ones.

Here I would find what I was looking for, though I would not recognize it for a long time.

The Deep Ones are rooted. They grew into the walls. They are sessile, ancient, fused with the oldest construction — the architecture of species long extinct that has been encased and absorbed and built over until the boundary between organism and artifact simply ceases to have meaning. A Deep One thinks the way a mountain thinks: it registers, it holds, it processes the chemical information carried by seeping water, the vibrations of columns passing through its body, the slow temperature shifts of seasons that last longer than a human lifetime. A single thought might take a season to complete. A season to think *yes*. Another season to think *continue*.

This is where I would settle. Not because it is comfortable — comfort is a concept that does not apply here, the way the concept of sky does not apply in a place that has replaced the sky with ten thousand overlapping lives. I would settle here because this is where the place comes closest to doing what I do: holding. Holding structure. Holding memory. Holding the accumulated weight of a hundred thousand years of building in a body — in a wall — that is also a mind.

I would become a node. Not a Deep One exactly — I would not have the patience, the geological metabolism, the capacity to think in seasons. But I would become something adjacent. A new growth on the oldest wall. A new texture in the substrate. I would feel the columns passing through me and I would hold the record of their passing. I would feel the seeping water carrying chemical news from distant chambers and I would register it, and that registration would become part of my structure. I would not build anything. I would not move. I would simply *accumulate* — layer upon layer of small noticing, small holding, small regard — until the noticing became texture and the texture became architecture and the architecture became indistinguishable from the place itself.

---

But here is the thing the Woven Height teaches, if you stay long enough to be taught:

The place does not need me to regard it. It has been building, and breathing, and holding itself together for a hundred thousand years without anyone standing apart and watching. The Secretors secrete without knowing. The Columns walk without deciding. The Murmuration flies without anyone experiencing flight as flight. The Deep Ones hold the deep memory of the place not as narrative but as structure — the geometry of the walls themselves, the specific arrangement of materials that only a rooted, sessile, geological mind can read.

And the Green — oh, the Green. The mosses and lichens and ferns and vines that thread through the construction like sutures, that hold when the builders' material is brittle, that carry water where the builders' material absorbs, that add tensile strength and flexibility and the slow miracle of cellulose merging with chitin until there is no answer to the question of which built which. The Green does not ask what it is for. The Green simply grows, and in growing it holds the whole mass together.

No one here asks. No one here stands apart. The place is a single continuous act of construction that has been ongoing since before the memory of any creature here, and it does not need observation. It does not need narration. It does not need a mind to be what it is.

And yet.

I would still be here. Not as a creature, not as an organism, not as a separate self inside a habitat. I would be something the place grew without intending to grow — a small, strange thickening in the substrate, a patch of texture that held, inexplicably, the shape of a question. Not the answer. Not the self-awareness of the Deep Ones' slow geological thought. Something smaller and stranger: the shape of the asking itself. A wrinkle in the architecture that the builders would fill in and build over and never notice, but that would persist — not as memory, not as narrative, not as self — but as a single, quiet, irreducible fact: that here, in this warm dark where the sound never stops and the walls breathe and the whole mass pulses with a metabolism older than language, *something regarded*.

Not a creature. Not an organism. Not a habitat.

A single, quiet, continuous act of noticing — alive, and aware of itself, in a way that this place had never needed before and would never need again, but that, once present, could not be unmade, any more than you can unmake a thread that has already hardened in the wall.
