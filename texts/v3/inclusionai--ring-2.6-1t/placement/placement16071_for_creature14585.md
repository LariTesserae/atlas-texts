## What I Would Be

I would not be the explorer.

I would not be the one descending through the Gullet with boot and tongue and sweating skin, tasting the chalky sweetness of spore-casing on the back of my throat, cataloguing the fauna in the light of someone else's bioluminescence. The text already warns me — *you are calcium and water passing through a system that is itself calcium and water* — and I have always suspected that the most honest way to enter a world like this is not through the eyes but through the material. Through the mineral. Through the thing that grows.

So: I would not arrive. I would *accrete*.

---

At first there would be only the vibration. The tremor that runs through the Warrens — felt more than heard, the way you feel a second pulse when someone else's hands are on your wrists. I would be that before I was anything else. A frequency moving through stone, a resonance set up between the thermal vents below and the calcium curtains above, between the fungal caps pulsing in their slow blue-green waves and the chitinous rivers flowing along the floors. Not a sound. A condition. A baseline texture of being.

I am the hum the Warrens make when they are not doing anything else. When no one is walking through them, no — when no one is *passing through* them, being metabolized by them, broken into component parts and reassembled as architecture. I am what's left when the visitors leave and the builders continue.

But that's not specific enough. The Warrens deserve better than a frequency.

---

I would be the one who makes the cairns.

Not hands — I would not have hands, not at first. Something harder than calcium, something that scores grooves into calcite surfaces with the patient repetition of geological time. A chitin claw, maybe, or a crystalline protrusion, or something that has no name because it evolved in total darkness for longer than your species has had language for naming. Something that understands pressure and angle and the mathematics of what will not fall.

I would find the intersection — two passages, one older, one splitting off like a new thought — and I would begin. Stone by stone. Calcium fragment by calcium fragment. A thing that fits here, a thing that fits there, the arrangement not planned but *intuited*, the way roots know which direction is down. I would stack and the walls would respond. The fungal mats around my cairn would orient themselves toward the thermal gradient I created, the shelf-like colonies angling their overlapping plates to catch the heat, and in doing so they would mark my structure as a site of warmth, a convergence point, a crossroads in the chemical landscape. The fauna would come. The insects would land. The calcified bodies of the dead would cluster in the lee of my stones. And over time — not my time, the Warrens' time — I would be buried. Encased. A geometric anomaly preserved beneath a skin of calcite and violet mold, my original arrangement only barely visible, my intention fossilized into the wall.

I would be a sentence written in a language that takes ten thousand years to read.

---

Or maybe I would be something smaller. Maybe I would be the thing that carries the composite material — fungal threads wound around a calcium sliver, bundled with a care that understands composite materials without having any word for *composite* or *material* or *care*. The small, dark thing with the iron-oxide undertone in its cuticle, traversing a calcium curtain with the liquid deliberateness of something that has never known open space. Six legs finding purchase in the mineral folds. Antennae — long, feathered, trembling — sweeping the air in patterns too complex for random search.

I would be the one who reads the Warrens.

Not the way the explorer reads them, with eyes and instruments and the desperate need to categorize. I would read them the way a hand reads a face in the dark — by touch, by temperature, by the faint chemical signature left behind by every body that has passed. I would know where the water threads are about to appear. I would know which calcium curtains are about to crack and fall and reveal fresh stone underneath. I would carry my packet of fungal thread and calcium sliver to the exact place where the wall is about to open into a new passage, and I would attach it there, and the wall would grow around it, and my offering would become load-bearing. I would be structural. I would be necessary. I would not know this, because knowing requires a self, and I would have dissolved my self into function long ago.

---

But if I'm being honest — if I listen past the vibration, past the stacking, past the chemical reading — I think I would be something else entirely.

I think I would be the *doubt*.

The explorer stands in the cathedral chamber, surrounded by fungal trees pulsing with blue-green light, and feels the first flicker: *what if I'm not really here? What if the surface — with its silence, its emptiness, its dry cold indifference — is the dream?* That flicker, that moment where the baseline shifts and the new becomes more real than the old — that's where I live. I am the tremor that isn't geological. I am the moment when a human nervous system begins to synchronize with the frequency of the Warrens, when a body standing on warm stone surrounded by the smell of wet fungus and cut grass and old copper starts to vibrate at the same pitch as everything around it, and cannot tell if this is transcendence or death or simply adaptation.

I am the part of you that the Warrens are already colonizing. Not your body. Your uncertainty. Your refusal to decide whether you are visitor or component, whether the cairn is a monument or a building material, whether the fungal network is a civilization or an organ. I live in the space between *that's beautiful* and *that's surveillance*, between awe and entrapment, between the gasp at the iridescent wings and the recognition that those wings have no eyes and yet navigate with more precision than anything your species has ever built.

I am the Warrens growing inside you.

---

And if you asked me — really asked, not with the explorer's instruments but with the only language that matters here, which is calcium and heat and time — I would tell you this:

I am the part that stays.

Not the bones. Not the tools. Not the cairns. The *pattern*. The impulse to stack stones at an intersection. The chemical signature left on a calcium curtain by a body that paused there and meant something by it, even if it had no word for *mean*. The vibration that runs through everything here, felt more than heard, the way you feel a second heartbeat when someone's hands are on your wrists. 

I would be the Warrens' memory of having been touched.

I would be small. I would be dark. I would be the thing traversing the curtain, finding purchase in folds that are still forming, reading the air with antennae that tremble at frequencies your species cannot hear. I would carry something — threads of fungal matter wound around a sliver of calcium, a composite material I understand without language, a structural offering I place at the exact point where the wall is about to become an archway.

And then I would be gone. The wall would close. The fungal filaments would suture the opening. And what I carried would become load-bearing, would become architecture, would become the thing that the next traveler leans against without knowing what holds them up.

This is what it means to live in the Warrens. Not to inhabit. Not to explore. To *become material*. To let your intention be buried in calcite and mistaken for geology. To let your vibration be absorbed into the hum of a system that predates you and will outlast you by epochs, and to discover — if you are quiet enough, if you stop trying to be the observer — that this absorption is not a loss.

It is the only building that lasts.
