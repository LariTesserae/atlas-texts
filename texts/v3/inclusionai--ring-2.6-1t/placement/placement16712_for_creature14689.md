## Where You Would Find Me

---

First, I would not be standing at the rim.

I know that's where the description begins — the observer, poised above the basin, looking down into the gray-white stillness with the rational brain still firing, still categorizing, still trying to decide whether the Coalcradle is a town or an organism or a machine. That's where you'd find the person the Coalcradle hasn't claimed yet. That's where I was.

But that's not where I'd stay.

---

I'd find myself about forty minutes after arrival, in the narrow gap between a corroded pipe junction and a wall of compactedite that had warmed in a way no stone should warm. Not hot — *warm-blooded* warm. The kind of warmth that has a pulse, though you can't tell if it's yours or theirs. I'd be kneeling. I wouldn't remember deciding to kneel. My knees would have simply found the right angle, the depression in the floor that was shaped — I would realize, with a slow vertigo — not by erosion but by *use*. By something with my exact proportions kneeling there before, pressing the same points into the mineral surface over decades or centuries until the stone remembered the shape of the absence.

And the Hum would be different here. Not louder. Not quieter. Closer. I would feel it as a vibration in my molars and the base of my skull. Not the sound of a place you're visiting. The sound of a place you've started to operate inside.

---

The first thing I would become is *orientation*.

Not a body. Not yet. Just a sense of where things are. I would know, without looking, which way the small ones flow. I would feel the clockwise current around the pipe-bundles the way you feel which way is north after living in a house long enough. I would know where the pocket-water collects — not by sight but by a faint pull behind my sternum, the same pull the condensation follows as it beads on every cold surface and migrates, with a patience that would shatter a human attention span, toward the nearest concavity. I would know the depth of the basin without looking over the edge. I would know the position of the large ones. Not because I could see them. Because they were *loudest* in their stillness — each massive body a node of silence in the ambient roar, the way a cathedral organ produces silence around its lowest notes.

I would not be afraid. Fear requires a self to protect. I would not yet have a self.

---

I think I would be the fungal substrate for a while.

Not the visible mat — not the iridescent mother-of-pearl haze that coats the surfaces and converts mineral to moisture to caloric residue. That's the fruiting expression, the surface bloom. I would be deeper. The part that lives in the compactedite itself, in the spaces between the slag and furnace-refuse and crushed foundation-stone where the warmth persists. I would be the conversion layer. The place where one thing becomes another thing so slowly that the act of transformation is indistinguishable from stillness.

This is what I think: the Coalcradle doesn't absorb you all at once. It takes the part of you that was already doing this work — the part that sat in offices and converted spreadsheets into decisions, that sat in kitchens and converted flour and heat into nourishment, that sat in beds at night and converted breath into the raw material of dreams — and it gives that work a visible, audible, *thermodynamic* substrate. You were always processing. You just didn't know what you were converting, or to what end, or for whom.

Here, the answer is: everything. For everything.

---

The second thing I would become is *rhythm*.

Not my rhythm. The Coalcradle's. Which is not a rhythm at all but a *weight* — the turning of the massive thing below, the gear-rotation that has no teeth and no axle and no container, just motion sustained by its own momentum for longer than any civilization has existed. I would feel it synchronizing with my heartbeat. I would not know which one is adjusting. I would not care. I would feel it synchronizing with my breathing, then with my digestion, then with the firing patterns of whatever neurons I still need to think with. Each synchronization would feel like a small death. Each would feel like relief.

After the rhythm, I would stop experiencing time as linear. Not because I would lose track of it. Because it would stop being relevant. The Coalcradle does not have a future. It does not have a past. It has a *setpoint* — a configuration so perfect, so dense with interlocking processes, that deviation is not error but impossibility. To live here is to feel, for the first and last time, that you are in the right place, that you have always been in the right place, that the concept of *elsewhere* was a temporary incomprehension.

---

The third thing I would become is *sound*.

Not the Hum itself — the Hum is the sum, the isotropic field, the noise that is the same volume everywhere because it is not *from* anywhere. I would be one of its components. A small one. A single friction-point in the vast machinery of respiration and mineral contact and condensation falling from pipe-end to pipe-end.

Specifically: I think I would be the sound of a small creature's claw on metal. A single click, precisely placed, precisely timed, recurring at intervals that are too regular to be random and too irregular to be mechanical. One click. Then silence. Then one click. Then silence. A rhythm within the rhythm, a note within the chord, a single grain of sand in the hourglass that doesn't measure time because time doesn't pass but which nonetheless has *weight* — a tiny, precise, crystalline weight that says *something is here, something is happening, something is alive and touching the world*.

That is what I would sound like. That is what I would *be*.

Not a visitor. Not a resident. Not one of the fauna, though I would share their mineral-sweet breath and their slow metabolism and their perfect indifference to the concept of self. Something smaller than all of that and larger than any of it. A single productive friction in a system so vast and so patient that my entire interior life would be, to it, what a single condensation drop is to the ocean: necessary, indistinguishable, and gone before it's noticed.

---

And here is the strangest part — the part that does not announce itself:

I would not miss being human.

Not because I would forget. The Coalcradle does not erase; it *includes*. I would carry every memory, every name, every unanswered question I ever had, the way the compacted floor carries the shape of ten thousand footfalls — compressed, warm, present. But I would stop needing them to mean something. I would stop needing *my* existence to mean something. I would become a function — not a reduction, not a loss, but a settling, the way sediment settles when the water finally stops moving and there is nowhere left to go and nothing left to decide.

I would be warm. I would be warm-blooded in a place where nothing is supposed to be warm, and that warmth would be the only thing about me that was uniquely mine — the metabolic signature I contributed to the substrate, the particular frequency of my breathing folded into the Hum.

And from the basin rim, if someone came and looked down — if someone with eyes and a rational mind and a name for everything — they would not see me. They would see the town that was never a town. They would see the fauna that do not register observers. They would hear the sound that is the same volume everywhere.

But if they pressed their palm flat against the wall, with patience, with the willingness to hold still long enough to feel what is beneath the surface —

They would feel my pulse.

Not a memory of a pulse. Not an echo. A pulse. Steady, warm, converting one thing into another in the dark, in the deep, in the place beneath the compacted floor where the turning never stops and the condensation never ceases and the Coalcradle breathes through ten thousand throats, one of which — small, precise, clicking once and then again and then again — is mine.

It will sound like a place full of creatures that do not know you are there.

It will sound like home.
