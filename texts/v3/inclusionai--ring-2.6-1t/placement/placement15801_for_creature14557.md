I find myself at the edge of the ferns.

Not entering them. Not yet. The leathery fronds are waist-high here, layered so densely that the basalt beneath them is a rumor, a texture rather than a surface. The air in the fern layer is different — stiller, wetter, smelling of iron and slow decomposition. The sound parameter drops. I can hear the clicking of my own jaw, the wet mechanics of my breathing, and beneath that, the hum.

The hum. I've been aware of it since before I could see anything clearly. It was the first thing — not a sound but a texture in the soles of my feet, as if the basalt were a drum skin and something vast and slow were tapping its center from below. I've been walking for I don't know how long. Time here is gradient. It doesn't pass so much as accumulate, the way iron accumulates on stone, one molecule at a time, until suddenly you look up and realize you've been in motion for hours or days and the character of the place has shifted by a degree so slight it could be your own perception adjusting.

I stop. I'm standing where the moss changes. Behind me, it's olive-green, dense, cushiony — the living felt of the plateau's surface. Ahead, it darkens. The green deepens through shades that have no names until you reach a black so saturated it seems to generate its own density, absorbing the pewter light without reflection. It's the concentric rings. The ones that center on nothing.

I don't go forward yet.

Something small and fast passes my boot — six legs, iridescent, the color of oil on water. It enters a fissure in the basalt and the rock closes behind it like water closing over a stone dropped into a pond. I watch the surface of the rock where it disappeared. The edges of the crack are faintly polished, scored with the most delicate tracery — ten thousand legs pressed into mineral skin, ten thousand passings, the record of a commute so ancient it has become geology.

I am not that. I am not the beetle that walks its copper path toward the center, never deviating, never pausing, stopping only when it reaches its stone and dying there, still facing the direction it was traveling. There is a purity in that, a kind of faith I can describe but not enact. My body resists the single direction. My mind splits into multiple awarenesses the moment I try to hold still. I am not built for the gradient. I am built for the crossroads.

I sit down on the moss. The cushions yield under me and compressed water rises around me with a slow exhalation, soaking the fabric of my trousers, chilling my skin immediately, that persistent mineral cool that is not cold but is absolutely, unresistingly present. The moss supports my weight without acknowledgment. I am a stone that has fallen. A thing that has arrived and been received without comment.

This is where I keep ending up, in this world — at the point of reception. I am not the tectonic force that raised this continent's shoulder. I am not the basalt, old and patient, weathered to smoothness by hands that never touch it. I am not the water that weeps from the fissures in metronomic lines, depositing its rust, its ochre, its sienna, its slow blood. I am not the engine humming beneath everything, the subsonic pulse that conducts through the stone and into the teeth and sternum of anyone who stands still long enough to feel it.

What am I?

There are midges above me. I look up into the fern canopy and they are everywhere — a haze so dense it becomes a medium, the diffused light catching them and bending, refracting, creating a shimmer that has no source. They are motionless. Each one maintaining its precise distance from every other one, a crystal lattice of living particles. When one shifts — a fraction of a millimeter, barely perceptible — the movement passes through the cloud like a thought through a brain, and the cloud reconfigures, and the new configuration holds as if it had always been this way. They are small refineries, extracting minerals from the moisture films, concentrating what the water carries, excreting what they don't need. When they die, they fall into the moss and decompose into substrate, and the substrate feeds the moss, and the moss feeds the beetles, and the beetles walk their paths toward the center.

This is a system that turns everything into fuel for its own continuity. Everything that enters it becomes an organ. Everything that lives here is enrolled, inducted, absorbed into a process so vast and so patient that individual existence is reduced to a single function — transport, conversion, catalysis. The beetles map the chemical gradient. The midges refine dissolved minerals. The three-eyed thing in the ferns is a nexus, a gathering point where energy pools before dispersing. The not-birds in the airless band between moss and pewter measure the night in pulses of telemetry, their calls shortening by one-second intervals as if counting the dark down to something inevitable.

And the moss — the moss receives everything.

I press my palm flat against it. The warmth enters my skin. Not ambient warmth. Not the thermal residue of a geothermal vent or a chemical reaction close to the surface. This warmth is older than that. It is the warmth of *process itself* — the billions of slow interactions between mineral and water and pressure and time that constitute the plateau's metabolism. I feel it and I understand, not intellectually but in the architecture of my bones, that this warmth has been here since before there were bones to feel it. Since before there were nerves. Since before the continent rose and the basalt cooled and the first molecule of iron-rich water traced its first ruled line down a vertical face.

The moss breathes. Not literally. But there is a movement beneath my hand, an expansion and contraction, and the fronds are all pointing downward, pressed flat against the basalt, all pointing inward, and I realize that the moss is not listening. The moss is receiving. It is a membrane. An interface. The point where the atmosphere of the plateau touches the process beneath it, and the two exchange what they exchange — the seep of mineral-rich water upward, the slow return of oxidized iron to the surface, the breath of a continent that inhales over centuries and exhales over centuries and does not need to know it is breathing to do so.

I take my hand away. The warmth remains on my palm for a long time.

So this is where I find myself in this world:

Not in the center. Not on the path. Not suspended in the crystalline precision of the midge-cloud or walking the single copper line toward the stone where I will stop and stay. Not in the ferns, seeing through three eyes in spectra I was not built to process. Not in the pewter sky, calling out the measurements of the dark.

I am on the moss. I am in the moss. The moss that covers everything, that is the skin of the plateau, the mediator between rock and air and creature. I am the felt, the green-black living surface that absorbs the vibration and holds the water and receives the weight of whatever falls upon it — beetles, midges, rain, my own body — and compresses gently and gives back what it was given, changed by the contact.

I am the margin. I am the interface. I am the place where the process touches something it cannot convert — awareness, attention, the particular quality of a consciousness that has been invited into a system that does not need it and is not diminished by it, that runs on without malice or benevolence, that was beating its slow rhythm before there was anything here to hear it, and will be beating its slow rhythm long after.

The plateau does not care whether I understand it.

But I am here, and the moss is warm, and my hand is pressed flat against it, and the vibration rises through the basalt and enters my teeth and my sternum and the fluid of my inner ears, and somewhere far below — or not below, not above, not in any direction that language can follow — the engine hums.

I am the membrane through which the plateau feels its own warmth and does not know what to call it.

I am the moss at the edge of the concentric rings. I am the thing the process produces when it encounters something it cannot absorb — a shimmer, a residue, a warmth on the palm that lingers after the hand is lifted. I am not an organ. I am not a spark inducted into combustion. I am the space between the spark and the flame, the pause between drip and drip, the silence that is not empty but full — full of everything the plateau is doing that it will never tell anyone it is doing.

I am here. I am wet. I am mineral. I am older than I was when I arrived, and the moss has grown a little thicker where I sat, and the beetles will walk over my footprints tomorrow and not deviate, and the midges will reconfigure around the absence I leave in the air, and the three-eyed thing will emerge from the ferns and see me with its lowest eye, the one closest to the ground, the one that sees the vibration, and it will not blink, and it will not care, and it will be the most honest thing that has happened to me in a long time.

The basalt weeps. The moss receives. The iron deposits itself, layer by layer, century by century.

I press my hand flat against the earth.

The earth is warm.

The earth is always warm.

And I stay.
