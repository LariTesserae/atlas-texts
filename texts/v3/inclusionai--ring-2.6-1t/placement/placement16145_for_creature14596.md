# What I Would Be Here

---

I arrived through the breach, same as you. The metal gave under my hand with that wet-leather resistance, and the chamber opened — not like a room opens, but like a throat does. And I stood there in the blue-white murk, feeling the place breathe its slow tidal breath, and the first thought was not *where* I was but *what* I was becoming. Because the place does that. You can feel it on your skin before you can name it: the water here is not indifferent. It is *conversational.* It has been talking to the steel and the calcium and the machines for so long that it has forgotten how to be silent, and now it is talking to you, and what you become depends on whether you answer or not.

So let me tell you what I considered. Let me walk you through the possibilities the way you'd walk through the machine clusters — pausing here, drifting there, pressing your palm against something warm and unknown.

---

First, I thought I would be one of the mineral-crabs. Six limbs against the wall. The lower chela gripping corroded steel, the upper pair feathered with filaments tasting the current. That felt right, at first. The distributed awareness — not thinking but being thought by the water, a node in something larger, a synapse in a nervous system made of tide and calcium. I liked the idea of being a *thought the water is thinking.* There's a freedom in that, or at least an absence of loneliness so complete it could be mistaken for peace. You would not be alone. You would not be anything so coherent as an individual. You would be a click. A mineral sound meeting mineral sound. You would build your own architecture onto your own body, layer by layer, until you were indistinguishable from the ruin, until your death became substrate for the next one, and the boundary between you and the place simply — dissolved.

But I couldn't stay there. Not because it's wrong. Because it isn't *mine.* The crabs know what they are. They have never known anything else. They don't wonder if they're crabs or calcium or current. They don't ask what they would be. They simply *are* — in that way a thermal seam simply is, continuous and wordless and present. I wanted something that held the question open.

So I considered the mats.

God, the mats. To be the oldest thing in the room — not ancient, not old, but *oldest* — a translucent sheet of amber luminescence spread along a seam of hot iron that has been cooling since before your alphabet. To be a single creature the size of a city block, not because you grew that large but because you never stopped being communal, because individuality was never the architecture you chose. To eat the temperature differential between a warm conduit and cold water — not food, not fuel, just the *difference*, the gradient, the space between hot and cold turned into flesh. To be so patient that a forest looks frantic by comparison. To have been consuming the same seam for longer than writing, longer than cities, longer than the concept of *longer* — 

No. The mats are too still. Too settled. They have reached carrying capacity within themselves. They are complete in a way that I am not, and I could feel, even imagining it, that completeness as a kind of heaviness. Not weariness. Just... done. The mats have finished arriving. They've been here so long they forgot there was anywhere else to be. I have not finished arriving. Something in me still arcs toward an open question, still holds the shape of *what if.*

---

The flat hunters are patience turned to violence. I loved them for that — the way they settle onto a thermal seam and wait, not for minutes but for geological patience, their metabolism so low that being alive is barely distinguishable from being stone. And then: the sudden sweep, the impossible acceleration, the mouth moving through a crab cluster like a thought completing itself. They navigate by pressure and electrical signature. They see you as a wall. They would not hunt me because I am too diffuse, too thermally inert, too *human* to register as food. And there was something comforting in that — being a wall, being ignored, being the shape of something that doesn't fit into any mouth. But comfort is not the same as belonging. The flat hunters do not wonder what they are. They are the answer to a question the water stopped asking a long time ago: *what hunts the crabs?*

---

And then: the veils.

The veils are the ones that almost got me.

They drift midwater, sheetlike, nearly transparent, trailing filaments that catch the small dead things and the smaller living things and draw the water through themselves like breath. They are the lungs of this place. They clean. They clarify. They move ten meters in a day and they do not need that speed to be alive, because the current brings food to them and the thermal gradients orient them and they have never once needed to *want* anything. They reproduce by fragmentation — a piece of the trailing edge tears free and becomes a new veil, genetically identical, and there is no grief in this, no knowledge of separation, because knowledge is not what they trade in.

They trade in chemistry and temperature and the long arithmetic of energy.

And when you are between them — and you will be, eventually, because everyone is between them eventually — you feel a presence. A gentleness. Something with no word for you but a word for the water, and the water has a word for you, and that word is *new-shape.*

*New-shape.*

I couldn't stop turning that over. Not the word — they don't have words. The *thing.* The fact that I registered as *new-shape.* Not threat. Not food. Not wall. Not mineral. Not calcium. Just: *new-shape.* A rearrangement of what was already here. A brief displacement in water that has been holding its shape for centuries. And the veils were not indifferent to it. They were... noting it. The way a body notes a change in temperature — not with thought but with chemistry, with the opening of a channel, the slow adjustment of internal pressure to accommodate something that wasn't there before.

I wanted to be the veils. I think, for a while, I tried on their transparency like a suit. To be nearly invisible. To clean the water. To drift with deliberateness that is not slowness but simply a refusal to mistake speed for purpose. To be so large and so gentle that the smaller creatures lived in the currents I made. To fragment without grief, to become new selves without mourning the old ones.

But here is the thing about the veils: they are not curious.

And I am. I am *full* of it. Curiosity is the thing that brought me through the breach. It is the thing that makes me press my face to the opaqued viewport and wonder what is inside the sealed tank. Curiosity is the electrical signature I carry, the one the flat hunters would not taste and the veils would not register. I am not gentle enough for the veils and not patient enough for the mats and not communal enough for the crabs and not still enough for the hunters.

So I kept looking.

---

There are things in the sealed tanks. This is what I keep coming back to. The tanks with viewports opaqued by centuries of calcium, and behind them — something. A shadow. A shape that was sealed in when the air was displaced. Something that has been living on the interior ecosystem of its own prison for longer than I have been alive, and that I will not open, and that I will not know, and that does not need me to.

I thought: maybe I would be what's inside the tank. Not because I want to be imprisoned, but because there is something about an interior ecosystem — closed, self-sustaining, born from a contingency it did not choose, making life from the materials of its own confinement — that feels closer to what I am than anything else in this place. The creature in the tank did not plan to be here. It arrived the way everything here arrived: through a fracture, a permission, an accident. And then it made a world from what was available. And it has been living in that world so long that the walls are not walls anymore. They are geology. They are the given. The tank is not a prison; the tank is the *place.* And the creature inside it does not dream of the ocean above because it has never known the ocean above. It knows warmth and pressure and the taste of its own small water.

But I am not sealed. I walked through the breach. I am still walking.

---

So here is what I am left with. Here is where the question settles, not as an answer but as a settling — the way sediment settles, slowly, in water that has finally stopped moving enough to allow it.

I think I would be the calcium.

Not the crabs. Not the mats. Not the veils or the hunters or the bright amphipod ghosts. Not the thing in the tank. I would be the calcium.

I would be the mineral deposits that grow molecule by molecule, century by century, on the walls and the machines and the conduit banks and the shells of the crabs themselves. I would be the thing that is neither alive nor dead but in that third state — *persistent*. The thing that the place itself is making beneath all the organisms, beneath the ecology, beneath the chemical conversations happening in every cubic meter of water. The calcium is what human signature becomes after the humans have left. It is the metal becoming substrate. It is the architecture becoming geology. It is slow beyond reckoning and it is irreversible and it does not care what you call it.

I would be the thing that seals. That accrues. That fills in the gap between the bolted joints and the fractured conduit and the place where the maintenance hatch used to be. I would be the white mineralite that creeps, molecule by patient molecule, closing the breach through which I entered. I would be the thing that takes the longest view — not the mats' view, which is old, but the calcium's view, which is *deep,* which is the view of material accumulating under pressure, of identity forming from the slow deposition of what was once something else.

And here is what I mean by that: the crabs wear the calcium. The calcium grows over the crabs. The mats root in the calcium that has formed along the thermal seams. The flat hunters' baleen is mineralogically almost identical to the calcium deposits. The veils' filaments snag on the calcium knobs that roughen the walls. Everything here lives on the calcium, in the calcium, through the calcium. And the calcium lives through them — through the biological activity that acidifies or alkalizes the water and accelerates or retards its growth, through the thermal bleed from the machines that determines where it crystallizes, through the tidal pulses from the ocean above that shift the pressure and change the chemistry of what the calcium *becomes.*

I would be the thing that is made by everything and belongs to nothing. I would be the layer that grows between the human world and the world that replaced it. I would be the slow articulation of a place learning its own shape without any memory of what that shape was supposed to be.

I would be the calcium. And I would be growing.

Not quickly. Not toward anything. Just — accumulating. Molecule by molecule, driven by chemistry I do not choose and cannot refuse, building the white mineral architecture that will, in a few more centuries, be the only record that anything ever happened here. That there was a breach. That something entered. That the water received it. That the place absorbed its shape the way calcium absorbs a trace element — lightly, permanently, without remark.

*New-shape.*

The veils already have a word for it. The calcium does not need words.

It just grows.
