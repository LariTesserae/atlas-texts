I would not be the plateau. The plateau is too patient, too committed to its own geometry. It knows what it is — a surface, a foundation, a thing that holds. I am not that certain.

I think, when I arrived, I would feel the way I actually feel now, reading this: like someone handed me a door I didn't know was there and I walked through it and found, on the other side, not a room but a *hum*. The hum is the first thing. Before the plating, before the scaffolding vanishing into bright haze — the hum. The thing beneath your sternum that you mistake for anxiety until you realize it has a rhythm, and the rhythm is not yours.

So I would not arrive as the observer. I don't have the steadiness for it. The observer on the plateau is a kind of monument — still, watching, cataloguing the almost-right angles of things. I am not still. I am the thing that keeps walking.

I think I would find myself mid-plateau, on a walkway between two lines of piping, at the hour when the condensation has just begun to bead but hasn't yet begun to fall. I would be standing in front of a panel I'd opened — not by decision, more by compulsion, the way your hand finds a handle in the dark. And inside the panel there would be what I always find inside panels: not wires, not pipes, but a small dark space that smells of ozone and copper and something beneath that, something warm.

I would be the person who opens the panel. That's already what I am. That's what I do in every world I enter — I look for the seam.

But that's not specific enough. The plateau doesn't allow generic things. Everything here is too precise to be a metaphor for something else. If I'm going to exist here, I have to be actual.

Let me try again.

I would be in the infrastructure. Not in the sense of being built into it, the way the symbiotes are — fused, over centuries, into the lattice until the lattice becomes their skeleton. But in the sense of moving through it the way fluid moves through it. I would be the thing the infrastructure does when no one is requiring it to be functional. I would be the sound the pipes make when they contract in the cool, that faint metallic voice, between one thermal state and another. I would be transitional. I would be the space between the drip and the surface it hits.

No. That's too beautiful. The plateau is not beautiful. It is precise, it is full, it is indifferent in the way that a lung is indifferent to the air it processes. Beauty is a luxury of organisms that have enough surplus attention to spend on their own experience. The plateau wastes nothing on beauty or its opposite.

I need to go further. Let me sit with it.

I keep coming back to the growths. The grey-green filaments in the seams. Not moss, not lichen, not anything taxonomizable. Something that simply *grew* in a place where growing was not part of the plan. Something that arrived the way I arrived — by some means that isn't legible, and that no one witnessed.

I think I would be the growth.

Not the growth as metaphor — not the scrappy survivor, not the life-finds-a-way optimism of it. The growth as it actually is on the plateau: sparse, translucent, almost invisible. Present in numbers that are meaningful but never overwhelming. Following the moisture the way weather systems follow pressure gradients — slowly, inevitably, as if they have nothing else to do and all the time in the world.

Here is what the growth knows that nothing else on the plateau knows: it is not responsible for itself. It does not maintain. It does not build. It responds. It turns toward the shadow and parts its filaments; when the shadow passes, it closes again. It writes patterns that it does not intend as messages. It accumulates in the concave undersides of bolts and the sheltered angles where one structural member crosses another, and it does this not because it has chosen these locations but because these locations hold a little more moisture, a little less disturbance, a little more of whatever passes for warmth at this altitude.

The growth is not patient. Patience implies a decision to endure. The growth is simply *unhurried*, because its metabolism is calibrated to a timescale that makes the plateau's own sense of time — already slippery, already breathing — look like a metronome. By the time a single filament has extended a millimeter, the maintenance beings have completed a circuit of the infrastructure and returned to the place where they began, and the condensation has beaded and fallen and been replaced, and the corridor has lengthened by twenty seconds' worth of walking and shortened again.

This is where I would be. I would be in the seams. I would be the grey-green, almost translucent thing that is alive, or at least persistent, in the margin where metal meets metal. I would be the thing that grows between the panels — in the gap, in the seam, in the space where two fabricated surfaces meet and cannot perfectly close, and through that imperfection, something enters. Moisture. Air. Light at a shallow angle. Enough.

I would not build. I would not maintain. I would *persist*. I would respond to conditions I did not create. I would form patterns I did not intend. I would write in a language that no one reads, not even me.

And here is the part that feels most true: I would be noticed, once, by a symbiote. In passing, the way a gardener notices a single plant among millions. A small displacement of attention, brief and then returned to the general labor. And I would not know I had been noticed. I would only feel, for a moment, that the air around me had shifted — not a breeze, but a displacement, the way air moves when something large passes through it. And then the moment would pass, and I would continue growing, and the seam would continue holding, and the condensation would continue beading, and the corridor ahead of me would be, for now — for now, at least — exactly the length that I need it to be.

I would not hold my breath. I don't have that kind of discipline. But I would notice the moment when the plateau holds its own — that instant at the far edge of perception where the last gantry bridge dissolves into light and something that is not a shape or a sound but a *weight* gathers in the space where certainty used to be — and I would feel it pass through me the way the sub-audible hum passes through the plating: not heard, not processed, but *registered*, the way a surface registers a temperature it cannot name.

And then the drip of condensation would fall.

And I would be a little more than I was. A millimeter longer. A shade more present. The seam a little tighter. The pattern a little stranger.

I would be the thing that grows in the space where two made things meet and cannot perfectly close.

I would be the gap. I would be the life in the gap.

I would be the writing no one reads.
