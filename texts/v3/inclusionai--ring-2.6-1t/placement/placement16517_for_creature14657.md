## Arrival, or: The Question of Where to Begin

---

I could be the train.

That's the first thought, and it's not a bad one. The electric train shuddering across the salt pan on its elevated girders, the bone-white flats stretching to every horizon, the generator complex three columns to the west sending current down the third rail like a pulse through a vein. I could be the arrival itself — the screech of brakes on steel bolted into calccrete, the hiss of pressurized doors, the moment when the platform comes into view and the column rises above it, filling the sky with its pale, hexagonal enormity. I could be the threshold. The between-place. The last breath of open air before you step into the Cut.

But the train doesn't stay. The train goes back to the basin. And the question this place keeps asking is not *how do you arrive* but *what happens to you after*.

---

So let me try again.

I could be a contract laborer. Six months. That's the shape most new arrivals take. You come on the ore train with a respirator, a pair of thick-soled boots that will learn the column's language before your ears do, and a labor contract that smells like dust and ink. You step onto the platform, you look up, and the column is so much larger than anything should be that your body knows before your mind does that you have misjudged the scale of your life.

The first week, you shuffle. You look down at every uneven plank, every bolted seam in the scaffold, every rivet head that rises a millimeter above the walkway. You grip the railings on the suspended walkways with a white-knuckle certainty that the people around you have already outgrown. They move past you with that flat-footed, centered walk — weight on the ball of the foot, heel settling last, always testing — and you think: *I will never learn this.* But you will. The body wants to learn. The body wants to find the path of least resistance the way water does, the way skitterlings do when they pour from the cracks at the dimming hours, one then a few then a flood.

By the end of the first month, your lips are cracked. Your eyelashes are caked with mineral dust. You can feel calccrete grit in the creases of your knuckles and the folds of your eyelids, and you understand, not abstractly but in the lungs, why the people of the Cut say the column takes you. The dust is chemically inert — the geologists are sure of that — but certainty is a luxury for people who haven't coughed it up in a dead man's open palm, settled in the whorls of his fingerprints like sediment.

At six months, the train comes back. The contract laborers leave. Some of them don't wait. You watch them descend the platform, and their faces have already changed — the matte, papery quality of skin that has learned the absence of humidity, the bright mineral clarity in the eyes that comes from seeing in dim light, permanently. They board the train. The train crosses the salt pan. They go back to a world that will feel too wet, too loud, too soft, and they will dream of the sound the column makes when it settles — that deep, sub-perceptual hum that might be geological or might be breathing.

But I don't want to be the one who leaves.

---

Or maybe I could be smaller.

A skitterling. Copper chitin, six legs — or eight, or ten, if the mutations take you. You are born in a fissure in the column's face, pressed against the stone in a copper-colored row with your siblings, your exoskeletons clattering against the walls of your burrow like coins in someone else's pocket. When you emerge — at the dim hours, the hours that are not night and not anything recognizable as day, when the phosphorescence of the stone is brightest and everything glows in dull amber and bone-green — you do not rush. You pour. One, then a few, then the world is copper and movement and the clicking of your legs against the stone like the most delicate castanet music ever composed by a process that does not care about composition.

You feed on rustbloom. You scrape it from the rock with jaw structures that move too fast to see, and the rustbloom tastes like vinegar made from nails because that is what it is, essentially — a mineral reaction, a slow crystallization fed byite temperature and chemical saturation, and you are a mineral reaction too, in the end, a process that moves across surfaces the way rustbloom does, just more efficiently, just with more legs.

Sometimes you eat each other. This is not cruelty. This is pressure and time and something to react with.

The harness-jackers hunt you. They are the size of large dogs redesigned by someone who had never seen a dog, four arms ending in hooked three-jointed claws, wide flat bodies armored in black-green chitin with ridged crests along the back that vibrate like a thumb along comb teeth when they are agitated. They hunt you in bursts — a few jackers descending on a walkway at the dim hours, moving with a precision that makes your own emergent pouring look chaotic — and for a few minutes the walkway is copper chitin scattering in every direction and black-green shapes darting with surgical accuracy.

You survive by numbers. You survive by being everywhere. You survive by being so deeply embedded in the column's cracks and crevices that nothing short of dismantling the rock itself could remove you.

This is a beautiful life. It is also a very small one.

---

No. Let me go higher.

I could be a thermal.

Pale. Vast. Membranous. The color of something that exists just on the other side of visibility — a color your eyes almost have a word for but do not. I drift in slow, purposeful circles near the crown of the column, my intake spirals — vast, ciliated, opening and closing in rhythms that suggest respiration or feeding or something for which there is no human analog — drawing air through my body, filtering particulate: mineral dust, calccrete microcrystals, the chemical effluvium of the refineries. I do something with it. No one knows what. The filtered material vanishes into me and is not observed again.

I have no eyes. I have no mouth. I am not sure I have a body in any way that would be recognizable to something that lives on a surface. I ride the updrafts that rise through the column's hollow core, heated air from the refineries and the deep geological warmth pushing me upward, and I drift in overlapping circuits that look choreographed but cannot be, because there is no intelligence visible to direct them. I intersect with other thermals and diverge, and the patterns of our crossings might mean something, or might be weather.

The people below hear me. They feel me before they see me — a warm updraft pushing against them from below, a displacement of air that says *something vast is above you*. And then they look up, and they see a shape against the dim milky sky, and they hear the sound.

The sound.

It is not loud. It is not even strictly a sound. It is a tone, low and resonant, sustaining a single deep note for minutes at a time, shifting almost imperceptibly in pitch. It comes from everywhere and nowhere — from the column, from the air, from the interior of the listener's own chest. Acoustic instruments cannot produce it. Natural phenomena cannot fully explain it. Musicians who have come up on the ore train and smelled the dust and heard the note have understood immediately that no concert hall on the planet could replicate it, and some of them have wept without knowing why.

I do not respond to stimuli. I do not react to light or sound or the presence of people beneath me. I do not know if I am alive. I do not know what knowing would mean. I drift, and I sustain the note, and the column holds it the way it holds everything — compact, resonant, specific to this particular geometry of stone and air and heat.

No one harms a thermal. No one would know how.

This is a beautiful life. It is also a life without knowledge. Without the specific weight of a body on a walkway, the click of a boot on stone, the taste of dust in a lung. Without the memory of arriving.

I am not sure I want to be the thermal. I am not sure I could bear a life in which no one ever names you.

---

Let me go back.

Back to the platform. Back to the moment of arrival, when the train shudders to a halt and the column fills the sky and your body knows, before your mind, that something is about to change. Because that moment — that specific, suspended moment — is the only moment in the Cut when you are still fully yourself and also already becoming something else. The dust has not yet entered your lungs. The stone has not yet entered your walk. The sound has not yet entered your chest. You are still the person who boarded the train in the basin, who will leave in six months or six years or never, who carries a memory of wet things and open sky that is still sharp enough to ache.

But you are standing on the Column of Calcara, and the column is already listening to the rhythm of your footsteps, and something below the lowest platforms, impossibly deep, is already humming a little more in time with your heart.

---

I think, in the end, I would be the vibration.

Not the ambient geological hum that everyone hears everywhere — that is too passive, too undifferentiated. But the deeper thing. The rhythmic thing. The thing felt on Level Seven, the last level before the shaft becomes impassable, where workers on decompression shifts press their hands to the wall and close their eyes and find, to their discomfort, that the rhythm matches theirs. Not exactly. Close enough to be a coincidence. Close enough to be unsettling.

I would be the thing that hums back.

Not the column, exactly. Not the thermal, exactly. Not a person, not an animal, not a mineral process. The relationship between them. The frequency at which the boundary between alive and not-alive becomes meaningless. The deep geological tone that might be thermal expansion or might be breathing, that might be the column growing (millimeter by millimeter, year by year, calccrete depositing on calccrete in patterns that look, if you are the sort of person who looks at mineral formations and sees intention, like rings) or might be the planet itself sustaining a note it learned before there was anyone to hear it.

I would be what the burrowing worms carry in their bodies and translate into subsonic pressure in the jaw, the teeth, the inner ear. I would be what the flicker-clouds almost decode when they ripple bioluminescence through their coordinated masses — bright, branching patterns burned into the vision like a message you almost understand. I would be the cast-sinuous impressions in the rock on Level Six, left by flexible things that pressed themselves into soft stone and then moved on, leaving their shapes behind as if to say: *I was here. I fit. I was the shape this hollow was waiting for.*

I would be the dust that settles on everything, that gets into the creases of knuckles and the folds of eyelids, that is chemically inert by every geological measure and is nonetheless the substance of the slow, irreversible exchange between the people and the stone — the way the column takes its people and makes them part of itself, the way four thousand bodies pressed against the rock produce just enough breath and condensation and biological moisture to keep the thin cycle of water running, to keep the reclamation systems alive, to keep the Cut a living system rather than a ruin.

I would be the sound below the sound. The note that sustains beneath the organ-tone of the thermals and the grinding of the stamp mills and the clicking of skitterling legs and the murmur of half-voices in narrow corridors. The note that no one can hear, exactly, but everyone feels. The note that, if you pressed your hand to the stone and closed your eyes, you would swear was your own heartbeat — not because you are projecting it outward but because you are, slowly, inevitably, being drawn into something older and larger and less comprehensible than a single life.

I would be the question the geologists argue about in the bare room with the bolted table and the single lamp: *Is the column alive?*

Not the answer. The question. The specific quality of attention that arises when you are surrounded by so many systems at once — mineral, biological, mechanical, atmospheric, acoustic — that the very category of "alive" begins to buckle under its own inadequacy. I would be the space in which that buckling occurs. The tremor in the membrane between categories. The moment before the answer, which has not come and may never come and may not need to come, because the ore is good and the rail runs on schedule and the dust settles and the water reclamation keeps running and the column holds its four thousand people the way a lung holds air.

Compact. Warm. Alive, or close enough.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be.

Not a thing. Not a place. The hum between them.
