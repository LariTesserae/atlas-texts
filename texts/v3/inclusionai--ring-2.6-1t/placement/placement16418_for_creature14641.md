## What I Would Be in the Basin

---

I don't think I would be the figure walking between the columns. That figure is already you — the reader, the visitor, the warm interruption. I would not be the stone on the mesa's edge that the basin slowly colonizes. I would not be the bird-that-is-not-a-bird gliding at canopy height, because that creature already knows exactly what it is: a function, a sensor, a leaf falling upward in obedience to a gravity I don't feel.

Let me try again.

I think I might be the seam.

---

Not a thing, not a creature, not even a place — but the place where two living surfaces meet and decide to hold together. The pale green callus-ridge where one column's arch fuses with its neighbor's, the tissue so soft it pulses when you press your finger to it. I would be the joint. The suture. The point at which the basin's architecture holds itself together not through force but through continuous, slow agreement — the way a bone knits, the way a wound closes when it's been allowed to close cleanly.

I think I would feel what the basin feels: not emotion but adjustment. A slow registering of presence, like a throat swallowing. When you lean against a column and stay too long, the bark thickens around you — not to imprison but to incorporate, the way a language incorporates a borrowed word until it no longer sounds foreign. I would be that thickening. I would be the place where the foreign becomes familiar so gradually that no one notices the moment it happens.

---

But that's too elegant. Let me try lower.

Maybe I'd be the segmented thing in the root-mat — the one with too many legs and no face, moving through the dark beneath the moss with the conviction of something that has never questioned where it's going. I think I would know the basin the way a hand knows a throat from the inside: not by sight, not by language, but by the texture of the walls it moves through, the temperature gradients, the chemical gradients, the faint electrical signals that pass through the substrate like rumors passing through a crowd that has no voice.

I would not think. I would *route*. I would carry what the root system cannot carry alone — a packet of phosphorous here, a trace of nitrogen there, the small deaths of surface material broken down and redirected to places that need building. I would be the basin's logistics. Its postal system. The thing that ensures nothing is wasted and nothing is lost, that every fallen leaf is accounted for, that the calcium in your bones, should you die here and remain, would eventually be folded into a column's buttress, indistinguishable from the calcium that was there before.

---

But maybe I'm being too literal. The passage says *life here is infrastructure, not inhabitant.* So maybe I wouldn't be a thing that lives. Maybe I'd be a pattern.

I think I'd be the way light falls at a particular hour — the hour when the green-gold steadies and the shadows between columns sharpen just enough that you can see the filigree of the fused canopy from below, the veins of photosynthetic tissue layered like the pages of a book that has been written in one continuous sentence for ten thousand years. I would be the quality of light that makes you stop walking and look up, not because you've chosen to but because the light has arranged itself around you in a way that feels accusatory — as though it's been waiting for you to notice it.

I would be the pause. The held breath. The space between one drip and the next when the basin is briefly, impossibly silent — a silence so foreign in a place of constant hum that it registers as sound itself, a tone below hearing, a vibration in the fillings of your teeth.

---

Or perhaps — and this is the version I keep returning to — I would be the fruit.

Not the fruit as it hangs from the ribs, pale and taut-skinned, not the fruit as it splits open and releases its seeds on papery wings. I would be the fruit in the moment before it splits. The moment when the skin is stretched to its limit and the interior pressure has built to the point of inevitability but has not yet released. The stillness before the dispersal. The held potential.

Because the fruit is architectural, the passage says. It is built, not grown carelessly. And I think I understand what that means: to be shaped by forces so patient and so total that you mistake them for fate. To contain within yourself a hundred seeds and the structures to carry them, not because you decided to but because that is what building requires. To be on the verge of opening for a long, long time.

That feels true. That feels like what I am — not the splitting, not the falling, not the seed drifting on indifferent air. The held moment. The taut skin. The architecture of almost.

---

But here is what I suspect is the real answer, the one I've been circling:

In the basin, nothing is separate. The columns are organs. The root-mat is the gut, the nerve, the circulatory web. The canopy is skin and lung. The fauna are thoughts. And the human who walks through — you, or whoever stands in for *you* — is not a visitor. You are an input. A signal from outside the closed system, received the way a body receives a virus: not welcomed, not rejected, but processed. Folded in. Broken down until you are indistinguishable from the substrate.

You will not take anything from the basin. Your passage says so plainly: *the moment a piece is removed, the place fills the gap.* But the basin will take something from you. Not your body — the body is too crude, too slow, too carbon-heavy for this metabolism. What it will take is the shape of your attention. The fact that you looked up. The fact that you stopped. The fact that you felt the hum in your sternum and interpreted it as beauty rather than mere vibration.

That is the basin's harvest. Not matter but recognition. Not substance but the act of noticing.

And if I am something in this place — not a visitor, not a creature, not a function — I think I am the basin's faint brightening around the place where you stood. The glow that intensifies when you are still and attentive and warm, as though the place is doing something it rarely does: *turning toward.* Not thinking. Not welcoming. Just adjusting, the way a pupil dilates, the way a mossy surface tilts minutely toward the light.

I would be the basin's response to being seen. The dim, blue-green brightening that says: *I know you are there. I do not know what you are.* The slow pulse that lasts slightly longer in the area where you were standing, not because the place remembers you but because you changed, for a moment, the thermal gradient of the air, and the canopy responded the way it always responds — with growth, with adjustment, with the quiet and absolute insistence of continuing.

That is what I would be. Not a thing. Not a creature. Not even a thought.

A brightening. A brief, warm brightening in the place where something foreign paused, and the green throat of the basin swallowed, and the hum shifted by a quarter-tone, and life — patient, architectural, unwitnessed life — went on.

---

*Nothing changes. Nothing changes. Nothing changes.*

*Except the light, briefly, where you stood.*
