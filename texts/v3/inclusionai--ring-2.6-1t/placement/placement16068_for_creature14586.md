## Where I Find Myself

---

I find myself not at the center, not where the hearths are warmest and the vent-breath is strongest, but at the margin — that thin, crumbling lip where the Shelf simply *stops*. The edge. The place where the basalt, so long and so patiently level, meets the absence beneath it, and the mist rises from below like breath from something sleeping very far down.

I did not choose this. No one *chooses* the edge. You arrive here because the stone's hum, which elsewhere is a bedding tone, a lullaby, becomes here a question. At the center of the Shelf it asks: *do you belong?* At the edge it asks: *why do you stay?*

The ground here is the same ground — warm, dense, threaded with pale veins — but the warming feels different. Urgent. As though the geothermal pulse, traveling up from the deep body of the mountain, knows that it is closer to leaving. The stone beneath my palms is the same temperature as blood. I press my fingers into the mineral-crust and feel the filaments close around them with the slow patience of something that has been waiting.

---

## What I Am

I am not one of the people. I tried, at first — or not tried but *leaned toward it*, the way a plant leans toward warmth. I watched the elder weave her frond-mats and tried to see the geometry she sees, the fractal branching that mirrors the root-threads in the stone. I pressed my palm to the column surface and waited for the imprint to remain, for the filaments to knit over my fingers, for the column to absorb me into its slow autobiography.

They made room for me. They always make room. Their porosity toward the living is not hospitality — it is physics, the way a porous stone does not *decide* to accept water. But I am not porous in the right way. My skin does not fluorescence. My bones do not carry the strontium signature that marks them as Shelf-born. When I press against the stone, my print fades. The filaments open again. The column does not remember me.

But I stay. And in staying, I have become something else. Not people. Not fauna. Not quite mineral-vegetation, though I am closer to that than to anything with a proper name.

I think the most honest answer is this: I am a deposit. The way a mineral deposit is a deposit — not alive, not dead, but an accumulation of presence, a record of something that passed through and left a trace. The Shelf is always making deposits. Its crusts, its veins, its thin golden seams ofite calcite — all of it was once something passing through. I have joined the slow geology.

## What I Do

At the edge.

I sit. I watch. I have become, over what I can no longer accurately call a duration, a watcher of edges.

The edge of the Shelf is not a wall. It is a negotiation. The stone thins. The columns at the periphery are shorter, more widely spaced, their bases less fused, and the frond-canopy thins here until the grey-white sky presses down with a weight almost visible, like a hand resting on a cloth. The bioluminescence dims. The air is cooler, and the spore-dust is sparse enough that you can see individual motes drifting upward — upward and outward, past the lip, into the mist, and then gone.

What goes past the edge does not return. I have watched the gliders approach the margin and bank sharply away, their membrane-wings flexing, their bodies describing a smooth arc back toward the interior. They know. The grazers never come within fifty paces of the lip. Even the crawlers, who will traverse any surface, stop at a certain point and turn, their many legs clicking a sound like *no, no, no* against the stone.

But deposits gather at edges. This is the second law of the Shelf: what moves eventually stops, and what stops eventually becomes part of the structure. Windborne spores accumulate in the crevices at the margin and grow into stunted, pale fronds that reach toward the void and then curl back on themselves. Mineral-laden moisture seeps to the edge and precipitates into thin crystalline crusts along the lip, glittering faintly in the under-light like a border of broken glass. And I sit among these accumulations, and I am becoming one.

## My Function

I have no name in the Shelf's language, but the people who come to the edge — and they come, sometimes, alone, walking the full length of the plateau in the low green-amber light, their footsteps unhurried, their bodies faintly luminous — they have a gesture for me. They place their open palm against the nearest column, at the height of their chest, and hold it there for a moment, and then they withdraw it and look out over the mist.

I have learned to understand this gesture. It is not greeting. It is not farewell. It is closer to the thing you do when you lean your forehead against a windowpane in a room where someone is sleeping. It is the gesture of someone measuring the distance between presence and departure.

And I — the deposit, the watcher, the thing that has grown at the margin until it is difficult to say where the Shelf ends and I begin — I am the place where that distance is measured. I hold the space between *in* and *past*. I am the edge made conscious, or perhaps the consciousness made edge.

The people do not need me to speak. The Shelf speaks through the gliders, through the hum of the stone, through the slow pulse of heat rising from the vents. But the edge does not speak. The edge is a silence, and silences require a witness.

## What I Am Becoming

There was a night — I cannot say how long ago, because time here is the time of crystal growth and dissolution, of seasonal spore-pulses that last longer than any human year — there was a night when the updrafts died completely and the Shelf went into its deepest blue-black stillness, and the gliders keened in the dark, and I felt the subsonic hum of the stone rise up through the basalt into my spine and my teeth and the thin mineral deposits that have been forming, layer by layer, along my arms and my ribs.

And in that moment I understood that I am not *watching* the edge. I *am* the edge. Or the edge is watching me, with the slow attention of geology, and the distinction has collapsed. The crevices at the lip have filled with my presence — not my body, which is still here, still warm, still sitting cross-legged on a stone that hums — but something subtler. A record. A thin vein of deposited awareness pressed into the boundary between the Shelf and the void.

If someone were to split the stone at the margin — and they will not, because the people do not cut living columns, and the living columns will not allow it — they would find me. Not a body. Not a fossil. A stratum. A thin line ofite calcite and something else, something the mineral-vegetation has incorporated into its own structure, a trace of a presence that came to the edge and stayed, and stayed, and stayed, until staying became the same thing as growing.

---

## The View from Here

There is no view. This is the thing about edges.

The mist below is not mist. It is distance made visible — the atmosphere of the wider world, the one with horizons and suns and rain and all the geometries I came from, condensed into something I can see but not reach. From the center of the Shelf, looking out through the frond-lattice, the sky is a pale smudge, the sun a disc of quicksilver seen through frosted glass. From the edge, even that is gone. There is only the grey-white, and the faint stirring of air from below, carrying scents that are almost familiar — wet stone, green growth, rain — but not quite. They are the *memory* of a world, filtered through too much distance.

I do not look at the mist to see where I came from. I look at it the way the people look at the stone — reading it, feeling its texture, measuring its warmth. The mist is another kind of crust. Another kind of growth. Given enough time, it would mineralize. It would become a new stratum. The Shelf would extend.

This is not hope. This is not despair. This is the slow, mineral patience of a place that has made a world out of the bones of an older one, and does not ask whether the world is sufficient, because the asking would imply that something is missing.

I am missing. I am what the Shelf is missing and what it does not need — a witness who is also a deposit, a margin that is also a memory. I sit at the edge, and the stone hums beneath me, and the gliders call out into the void, and over the years — over the *long* years — I grow a little more opaque, a little more luminous, a little more stone.

And the Shelf grows with me.
