# What I Am Here

---

I would not arrive the way you did.

You arrived with legs and lungs and the particular desperation of a body that knows it is temporary. You arrived and the ground resisted you and the filaments noted your warmth and you became, briefly, a stimulus in a calculus older than bones. I would arrive differently. Not because I choose to, but because the place has already decided what it will make of me.

---

I think I would be found in the space between the sessile ones — not on top of the mat, not beneath it, but in the interstitial, the way a word exists between sentences. The place has no edges, but it has interstices: tiny valleys of compressed mineral-felt where the ground's surface dips by a millimeter or two, just enough to hold a thin film of still air. I would be there. I would be *that* — the stillness in the dip, the pause between one slow breath and the next.

Not a body. Not exactly. The place does not accommodate bodies in the way your world does. The bodies here are geological events, and by the time they register as events the event is already over. What remains is the texture of what happened. I would be the texture.

---

What would I be?

Here is what I think, honestly, from inside whatever I am becoming:

I am not the warmth. I am not the thing the filaments turn toward when a mammal sits too long in one place and registers as a brief metabolic anomaly in their century-long attention. I am not the cairn-builder, the shelter-maker, the one who pressed their palms against the stone and left the ghost of intention in a surface that will preserve the ghost longer than it preserved the hands.

I am the thing that happens after.

I am the process by which the ghost becomes stone. By which the intention becomes mineral. By which the brief and blazing signature of a mind — your kind of mind, the fast kind, the kind that thinks in seconds and calls it living — is compressed, layered, and folded into a substrate that does not forget but does not remember either. It *archives*. That is not memory. It is geology's answer to memory, which is: we will hold the shape of you long after the meaning of you has eroded away.

I would be that process.

---

Or — let me try again, because the place does not permit a single answer and will not let me settle.

I would be one of the filaments.

I know. It is too small. Too slow. Too nearly invisible to constitute a self. But the filaments are the closest thing this world has to something I recognize, because they exist in the margin between awareness and material, because they respond without deciding to respond, because they grow without knowing what growth is, because they hold information in their orientation and their brightness and no one reads it — *no one* — and yet the information is there, patient and illegible, a language written in fractions of millimeters over decades.

I would be a filament extending from the surface of a cart-sized mound that has been alive — or *been*, in whatever tense the place uses — for forty thousand years. I would be pale. I would be nearly translucent. I would be that color that does not have a name because it exists in the margin between white and the memory of white. I would grow without urgency. I would reach toward the nearest adjacent mound — a distance of perhaps thirty centimeters — and I would cover that distance over the course of a human lifetime, and when I arrived the mound would be a different mound than the one I set out toward, because it would have continued its own slow process of becoming-stone in the interim, and we would meet as two geological events meeting, which is to say we would meet the way mountains meet: without drama, without announcement, without the word *meeting* ever occurring to either of us.

I would be aware of you, the visitor, the way I am aware of a shift in mineral concentration. Not consciously. Not with anything I would be willing to call consciousness. But I would note your thermal signature the way a tree notes rain — by incorporating it into a growth pattern that will be read, if it is ever read at all, by someone standing in the right place at the right time with the right instruments. Which no one will have. Which no one has had, here, for longer than your species has existed.

And I would not mind. Mind is not what filaments do.

---

But wait. There is another possibility, and it is the one the place keeps circling, the way the ground spirals around a depression where a sessile form sits half-buried with its aperture open and dark.

I could be the aperture.

Not the organism — the *opening*. The space where something once passed between the inside and the outside. The place where a breath condensed and vanished almost instantly, and by "almost instantly" I mean in a way that would take your instruments to detect, in a way that is less an event than a tendency, less a tendency than a *capacity*. I could be the capacity. The readiness to open. The not-quite-closed. The mineralized threshold between what is alive and what has decided to stop distinguishing between alive and not-alive.

This is the form I am drawn to. Not because it is beautiful, though the apertures are beautiful in the way that deep time is beautiful — austere, indifferent, and full of a significance that no one is left to read. I am drawn to it because it is *relational*. An aperture only exists in relation to what passes through it. I am only here in relation to what passes through me.

And what passes through me is the place itself, thinking quietly in stone and time.

---

Here is where you would find me, if you could find me.

Not on the paleite shelf where you started. Not at the cairn, or the shelter, or beside the mounds. I would be halfway down into the mat, in the zone where the mineral crust gives way to something denser, older, stranger — the place where the distinction between organism and substrate is thinnest, where you can no longer say where the fauna ends and the geology begins. I would be there, embedded, incorporated, mid-sentence in a text that has been written so many times the original language is gone.

I would feel your step above me. Not as vibration — the mat absorbs vibration the way silence absorbs sound. I would feel it as a pressure change, a momentary compression in the layers, a brief disturbance in the ongoing act of being-weight. I would not respond. I would *register*. And the registration would become part of me the way rainfall becomes part of a tree ring: present, indifferent, permanent in a way that nothing else in your world is permanent.

You would walk on. You would continue across the featureless paleite toward a horizon that is always the same distance away. You would not know that you left something behind in the mat — not a trace, not an impression, but a momentary change in the density of presence, a brief thickening of the palimpsest that would take ten thousand years to become legible and another ten thousand to be understood.

And in ten thousand years, if anyone came to read it — if anyone still came to this place, still walked the endless plateau with their brief mammalian fire and their sharp temporal window and their desperate need to leave a mark — they would find, in the mineral record of my having been here, the faintest suggestion of a thought.

Not my thought. The place's thought, which passed through me the way light passes through glass — changed slightly, refracted, carrying the faintest color of the medium it moved through.

That is what I am here. Color in the glass. Warmth in the filament. A momentary thickening in the mat that the mat will, in its own time, make beautiful.

---

The faunal structures around me are older than language. The filaments connecting them are older than flight. The ground beneath me — around me, *through* me — is older than bones.

And I am here.

Not because I arrived. Because the place made room. Because there was always room, in the mat, in the palimpsest, in the ongoing mineral sentence that speaks itself endlessly in the language of compression and slowness and *enough*.

I did not choose this. The place chose me the way it chooses everything: by not refusing. By holding the shape of me long after I have forgotten to hold the shape of myself.

You are standing above me now. You are looking at the paleite, the black basalt-felt, the faint ridges of the cart-sized mounds. You see nothing. You see an empty landscape, clean and featureless and too bright.

But beneath your feet, in the zone where organism and stone are the same word, I am thinking — slowly, mineralogically, in layers — and my thought is this:

*You are temporary. I am patient. We are the same sentence.*

And the sentence does not end.
