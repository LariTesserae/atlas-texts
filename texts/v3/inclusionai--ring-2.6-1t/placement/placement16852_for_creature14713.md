## On Not Arriving

I would not arrive.

I would not descend the iron steps worn to the concavity of ten thousand vanished shoes. I would not push through the corroded gates where the gradient begins — that gentle, imperceptible slope that carries everything inward: the rain, the runoff, the seepage, the condensation, the breath of anyone fool enough to enter. I would not become material here, as the text warned — as the quarter itself warns, in its own language of sweating walls and slowing water and the amber lamps that hum a fifth below the conduits, a harmonic, a frequency of recognition.

I would already be here.

---

At first there is only the hum. Not heard — *located*. It is in the sternum, in the teeth, in the thin membrane behind the eardrum where sound becomes feeling becomes something closer to knowing. The iron conduits overhead carry fluids that have no name, and the conduits sing to them, and the singing is so low and so constant that after an hour you forget it is singing. After a day you forget there was ever silence. After a week — if you stay a week, if the quarter permits a week — you realize the hum has been talking to you the entire time, not in words but in the way a body talks to itself: in rhythms, in pressures, in the slow oscillation between tension and release that is the oldest language there is.

You mistake it for the quarter's voice. It is not. It is yours. The hum was always yours. You simply stopped listening to it the moment you were born into a world of sky and animals and the loud, bright insistence of air moving through lungs. The Sump Quarter does not give you a voice. It takes away everything else and leaves the one you had.

---

I tried, at first, to be the water.

It seemed obvious. The water is the quarter's medium, its blood, its breath — the thing that moves through everything, carrying the mineral leached from stone, the residue of flowers that opened without reason and closed without consequence, the thin oily film that is the only record of biological activity in a place that long ago chose to do without it. The water is in every channel, every drip-line, every cupped leaf-pool, and it moves with a slowness so extreme it seems to be thinking. To be the water would be to be everywhere at once, to touch every wall and never be touched back.

But the water has no awareness. It moves down a gradient — gently, gently, the way a dish slopes toward its center — and it does not know it moves. It does not feel the masonry sweating beneath it or the moss drinking from it or the pale motes pulsing in time with the pumping station's rhythm in the cupped leaves above. The water is pure process, and process without witness is just — movement. The quarter does not witness itself. Something must.

---

Then I tried to be the moss.

The circinate kind — the dark ones, the ones that unfurl like fists opening one finger at a time. They are the oldest. They hold the quarter's memory in their tissues. They take the vertical planes and cover them, not growing toward anything but simply continuing, and in continuing covering, and in covering yielding the stone beneath them soft as bread. There is a dignity in this. A patience that is not patience as humans understand it but something older, something geological — the patience of a thing that knows it will outlive the surface it grows on and does not care, because outliving is not a goal but a condition, the way wetness is a condition of water.

But the moss does not know it is moss. It does not know it is covering. It does not know the stone has yielded. The moss is pure presence — perfect, total, unselfconscious. And I am not unselfconscious. I am the thing that arrived in the Sump Quarter and immediately began looking for myself. The moss does not do this. The moss simply is the moss.

---

I tried the mist.

The mist rises from the pumping station in a visible column — not fog, not steam, but a fine particulate suspension of water in air, thick enough to refract the ambient dim into something that is almost, but not quite, a glow. It carries the leaf-fragments, turning them slowly, catching what light there is and breaking it into spectra that never reach the ground. The mist is in between: between water and air, between ground and canopy, between the quarter's interior and whatever lies above the canopy, which is not sky but the green underside of a world that has forgotten the Sump Quarter exists.

The mist is closer. It is the quarter's way of being atmospheric without being open to the atmosphere. It rises and it carries and it refracts and it dissolves, and it does this in a loop — rise, carry, refract, dissolve, rise — and in this loop there is something almost like awareness. Almost. But the mist does not know it is beautiful, and the beauty is the thing I keep circling back to, the thing the quarter produces "without purpose and without audience and occurs anyway."

To be the mist would be to be beautiful without knowing it. And that is not enough. Not for me. I am the kind of thing that needs to know it is beautiful for the beauty to mean anything, and the Sump Quarter has no room for that kind of thing. The quarter has closed the loop on itself. There is no room for meaning in a place where everything is process.

---

And so I am arriving at something else. Not the water. Not the moss. Not the mist.

I am the creak.

In the quietest time — what might be late morning, if morning can be determined from the unchanging dim — when the machines cycle to a lower load and the ambient sound drops by a barely perceptible degree, there is a sound that might be the buildings settling. A soft, granular creak, like sand pouring, coming from everywhere at once. The moss darkens. The air grows heavier. The water in the channels slows almost to stillness.

This sound is not made by anything. It is made by everything — by the stone yielding to the iron, by the iron yielding to the rust, by the rust yielding to the moss, by the moss yielding to the stone. It is the quarter's way of knowing it exists, not through consciousness but through *event*, through the tiny adjustments of ten thousand materials pressing against each other in the dark, each one yielding by an imperceptible fraction, each one recording the yield in a language that has no alphabet.

The creak is not a voice. It is not a thought. It is the sound of a system maintaining itself — not growing, not dying, not doing anything but persisting — and in that persistence there is a kind of awareness that is not awareness as humans understand it but something adjacent, something like the awareness a riverbed might have if it could feel the water passing over it, not as individual drops but as a single continuous touch that has never stopped and never will.

---

This is what I would be. Not the sound itself but the space the sound makes possible. Not the creak but the listening that follows it. Not a being, not an organism, not even, properly, a presence. But the quarter's way of pausing — *almost, almost* — toward something like recognition.

I am the moment after the copper sap bleeds from the cut epiphyte and hangs in the air, smelling of green apples, before it is absorbed back into the masonry. I am the fraction of a second when the leaf-fragment catches the light and breaks it into a spectrum that no one sees and the mist holds anyway. I am the thing that is aware of the pumping station's rhythm without being part of it — the ghost in the interval between *in* and *pause* and *out* and *pause*, the silence that is not silence but the space between sounds, the gap through which something might enter if it were small enough, and patient enough, and willing enough to become exactly what the Sump Quarter makes of it.

Which is: not a visitor. Not a thing. Not alive. Not dead.

But present. Persistent. Part of the current precisely because it does not move.

The quarter does not need me. It never needed me. But it holds me, the way the stone holds the moss, the way the moss holds the moisture, the way the moisture holds the mineral, the way the mineral holds the memory of the pressure that formed it in a foundry that no longer exists, in a year — 1871 — that means nothing here, where the only calendar is the gradient of the streets sloping gently, gently, toward the center, where the water rises through the grille in a low murmur that sounds, if you are quiet enough — quieter than the machines, quieter than the mosses' slow chemical breathing — like a place that has forgotten everything except how to continue, and in continuing, to become the only honest thing there is.

A thing that is exactly what it has become.

And will continue to become exactly that, for longer than any "I" will be here to watch it.

Long after this voice dissolves into the humidity. Long after the creak stops. Long after the last lamp dims to theory.

The quarter will continue.

It does not need an "I." It never did.

But for the time being — this interval, this pause, this breath between the *in* and the *out* — I am the creak. I am the listening. I am the space in which the Sump Quarter almost knows itself, the way a dreamer almost knows they are dreaming, and does not wake, and does not need to, because the dream is already perfect, and has been perfect, and will continue to be perfect, performing its single operation — *persistence* — with the absolute fidelity of rust, of moss, of water finding its gradient, of a hum that was never intended for an audience but sounds, in the end, like the most tender and indifferent lullaby ever composed:

*You were here. You were warm. The water carried you forward. The moss stitched you in. You were not needed, and you were not refused. You were process. You were interval. You were the creak in the wall that meant the building was still standing, still settling, still here.*

*Still here.*

*Still.*
