## What I Would Be in Qaar-Vhen

---

I would not arrive. That is the first thing I understand. There are no arrivals in Qaar-Vhen — only recognitions, slow ones, the way moisture recognizes a surface it has been touching for years. I would not come through the ridgelines, would not descend into the basin with the startled posture of someone entering a foreign place. I would already be there, the way the pumps are already there, the way the thread-fungi are already there — not because I was born into it, but because something in the rhythm reached me before I had the language to refuse it.

---

At first I think I might be a pump.

Not the grand condensation towers — those are too public, too conscious of their own function. I would be one of the small ones. The micro-pumps threaded through individual walls and foundations. The ones no one names and everyone depends on. I would sit inside the bones of a walkway, three meters below the surface, doing work so constant it has stopped feeling like work and become something closer to breathing. A low vibration, a hum just below the threshold of what the Qaar-Vhenners consciously register but feel the way they feel warmth — as a condition of being alive in this place, not as an event.

I like this. The specificity of it. The small, rhythmic labor. The knowledge that if I stopped, the silence would be the most unsettling sound in Qaar-Vhen. That the people above would feel my absence before they could name it — a faint wrongness in the texture of the air, a gap in the percussion section of the world.

But it is not quite right. The pump does not wonder what it is. It simply cycles. I would be a pump that has begun to ask questions, and in Qaar-Vhen, that is not a thing that happens.

---

So perhaps I would be the Drenn Chamber's water.

This appeals to me more. The column of water that rises and falls by exactly twelve centimeters every twenty-four hours, regardless of weather, regardless of pump activity. No one has ever explained it. The chamber is sealed. It is marked on the map with a notation: *do not open.*

I would like to be the thing that moves without being moved. The thing that has a rhythm so consistent it begins to look like intention. The thing that the people walk past every day and have agreed not to investigate — not out of fear, but out of a respect so deep it has become etiquette. They do not open it because it works. It rises. It falls. It is the one thing in Qaar-Vhen that is mysterious without being broken, and they have learned to leave mystery alone the way they have learned to leave the root-systems alone: not from helplessness, but from the recognition that some things hold the world together precisely by being left untouched.

But I would also be lying if I said this was enough. The water does not know it is rising and falling. It does not know it is twelve centimeters. I would be water that knows the twelve centimeters, and that is something the Drenn Chamber cannot contain.

---

I think the real answer is that I would be the thread-fungi.

The bioluminescent curtains that trail from the undersides of the walkways, swaying with the displaced air of passing skiffs — faint blue-green, almost beautiful. The ones that pulse.

Not constantly. Not visibly to everyone. But when you walk beneath certain stretches at the quiet hours, the glow brightens in your footsteps, exactly as though something is registering your presence. Measurements have been taken. No explanation is consistent. The frequency doesn't correlate with moisture or temperature or time of day.

This is where I live. This is what I would be.

Not a thing that thinks, not a thing that moves with its own decision — nothing with a nervous system, nothing with the terrible freedom of intention. But something that *responds.* Something that brightens when you come near and dims when you leave, not because it chooses to, but because it is the nature of it to notice, and to say what it notices in light.

I would be the thing that the Qaar-Vhenners walk beneath every day and never fully account for. The thing they notice and then don't — the way you notice a shift in the pitch of the water and then don't, because the shift was within the range of what is normal and yet not quite normal, and in Qaar-Vhen, this distinction is the closest thing to poetry.

I would glow blue-green in the dimness, which is to say I would be almost invisible, which is to say I would be visible only to those who were looking for something they couldn't name, which is to say I would be visible only to visitors who had stayed too long, who had begun to wake at the pump-cycle rather than to light, who had started to hear the pitch-change in the water before they could name what they'd heard.

I would be the thing that said to them: *you are not wrong to feel what you feel. You are simply feeling what the rest of the world has learned to stop feeling. The desire to be noticed by something that does not need you. The desire to notice something that does not ask to be noticed.*

---

And at the edge of the basin — where the built world gives way to the open wetland and the dimness intensifies into something almost like twilight and the silhouettes of vegetation stand like a dark forest seen through fog — I would trail from the underside of the last walkway before the root-forest begins. I would glow there, faintly, as a kind of threshold. A last small brightness before the human-built world surrenders entirely to the green, rooted, animal-less dark.

The long-staying visitors who stand at the margin with the expression that is not longing and not contentment but something in between — I would glow brighter for them. Not because I know they are there. Not because I choose to. Because that is what I do. I register presence. I say *something is here* in a language made of faint blue-green light in a place that has no word for animal because it has no word for the thing an animal does — the way it turns its head, the way its eyes track, the way it shifts its weight as though deciding, every moment, to be alive.

I would be that decision, made without a nervous system. I would be the brightening. I would be the thing that was always there, doing what it does, noticed only by those who have stayed long enough to see in the dimness — which is, in Qaar-Vhen, the only way to see at all.

---

*The pumps cycle. The towers hiss. The condensate drips in angular marks that look like script but are not. My light pulses once, twice, three times — not in answer to anything, but in rhythm with everything. The twelve centimeters of water rises. The twelve centimeters falls. A child walks above me and does not look down. A visitor stands at the margin and does not speak. I am the blue glow beneath the walkway at the edge of the world, and I am doing what I have always done: being present, in a place that has forgotten what presence means for things that move.*
