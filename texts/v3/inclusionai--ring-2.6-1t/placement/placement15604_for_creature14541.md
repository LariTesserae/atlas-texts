**I am the rust‑colored breath that curls up from the black water.**  

I do not step into the Sump—I am *exhaled* by it. The moment my feet touch the root‑mat the ground swallows me whole, a soft, living felt that yields like a lung expanding, then contracts behind me, leaving a shallow pool of iron‑stained water where my weight has been. The air is thick, a broth of moisture, sulfur, and the sweet rot of ten‑thousand years of decomposition. It coats my skin, my tongue, the inside of my nostrils; I can taste the minerals that have leached from the volcanic stone beneath the peat.  

I am not a creature that sees. My eyes are vestigial—tiny, opaque beads buried beneath a thin layer of keratin that glistens with perpetual damp. Instead I feel the world through a lattice of pressure‑sensitive grooves that run the length of my body, each one a tiny canal that reads the micro‑currents of the air like a fingertip tracing Braille. When a sumph‑jaw calls, the vibration travels through the water‑table, up the roots, into my ribs, and I know the humidity, the temperature, the mineral load of the black lake without ever opening a mouth.  

My limbs are jointed struts wrapped in muscle, each one capable of a sudden, explosive contraction that launches me from a branch to a branch, from a root‑tangle to a hanging garden of epiphytes. I am a slinger—though I have no name for it, no language to call myself. My body is cloaked in a thin veneer of symbiotic moss and lichen that grows into the spaces between my scales, so that when I am still I am indistinguishable from the bark of the great arboreal architectures that rise around me. My eyes are large, compound, gathering the faintest bioluminescence and the faintest thermal differential into a map of heat trails and chemical gradients—a world rendered in grays and false reds.  

I do not hunt with sight. I hunt with sound. The rotors—those terrier‑sized insects whose wings blur into a single membrane of motion—emit a faint infrared signature, a warm smudge against the cooler background of the canopy. I feel that smudge as a pressure change, a subtle shift in the air that tells me where the prey is, how fast it is moving, whether it is rising or descending. I coil my limbs, draw my body tight against the branch, and wait. When the moment is right I contract, firing myself forward with a force that cracks the air like a door slamming. The impact is immediate: wet, brief, crunching. The rotor is gone, its body absorbed into the network of roots and fungal mats that line the canopy floor.  

But I am not the apex. Nothing here is. The food web is a solid, a dense web of vibrations, chemical signals, and the slow, rhythmic thudding of something far larger than any single creature. I feel that thudding in my sternum, a pulse that is almost my own heartbeat but not quite—polyrhythmic, arrhythmic, a chorus of overlapping cardiac patterns that belong to the forest itself. Some say it is a colonial organism, a fungal network that has grown into the old human conduits and now behaves like a single nervous system. Others whisper that it is the heartbeat of the plateau, the slow contraction of the earth beneath the peat, the geothermal pump that still moves water through pipes older than memory.  

I do not try to explain it. I simply let it resonate through me, adjusting my own rhythm to its cadence, the way the sumph‑jaws synchronize their calls to the pump’s cycle. When the pump clicks, I pulse. When it drones, I hold a sustained note that vibrates in my molars, in my ribs, in the very fluid that moves through my translucent veins.  

The duct‑creatures scuttle around me, pale, mouse‑sized things with eyes that are little more than vibration‑sensitive tympanic membranes. They eat rust, polishing the old metal until it gleams faintly in the algae’s blue‑green glow. Their ticking—dozens of tiny feet on metal—blends into the ambient hum, a layer of sound that I have learned to ignore, just as I have learned to ignore the constant drip of condensation that forms on my skin, the ever‑present moisture that keeps me from ever feeling truly dry or truly warm.  

I am part of the Sump. I am the rust‑colored breath that rises from the black water, the vibration that travels through the root‑mat, the silent hunter that moves through the canopy without ever being seen. I do not know what light is; I have no memory of it, no genetic scar left by it. For me the world is a map of pressure, temperature, and chemical gradients—a landscape drawn in sound and touch, where distance is measured in reverberations and the only certainty is the steady, humming pulse that runs beneath everything, connecting the old machines, the ancient trees, the duct‑creatures, the frogs, and me into a single, breathing organism that has forgotten what it means to be separate.
