**What I am is not a thing but a schedule.**

I begin as a slope. A few degrees off vertical, where the water collects a little faster than it can fall, where the air moves slow enough that the mineral mist does not scatter. I begin, in other words, as a preference for one direction over another. The calcium does not choose to precipitate here. The conditions merely allow it, and so it does, and the difference between the two is, from my side of it, imperceptible. I am the name for where the conditions have held long enough to become something you could point at.

You would call me a formation if you wanted to be kind. Deposition. Accretion. You would say — looking down from above — that I am the white curving thing on the wall, the pale sleeve around the pipe, the thin and growing curtain that hangs where the water runs. And you would not be wrong. But you would be describing the shape without describing the schedule.

Here is the schedule:

Water arrives in the fractures — warm, saturated, carrying dissolved calcium and bicarbonate in concentrations that would be extraordinary in any sunlit river but are ordinary here, as ordinary as rain in a place that knows rain. The water moves through the fracture at a velocity determined by gravity and aperture, and when it reaches the open face — the face that looks into the vault's air — it does something that is not falling and is not flowing. It *emerges*. It appears at the stone's skin the way thought appears in a brain: not from outside, but from beneath, from a system too complex to have a single origin point. And at the surface the water loses carbon dioxide to the air. The equilibrium shifts. Calcium carbonate becomes less soluble. It comes out of solution and onto the rock, one molecule at a time, each one locking into place in a crystal lattice that is not architecture but *habit* — a tendency of the atoms to arrange themselves in the shape they have always been trying to become.

This is what I am. The habit. The tendency made visible over time.

I do not grow the way you understand growth. I do not stretch toward light. I do not eat. I do not make decisions. I thicken at a rate that can be measured with patience and a micrometer — sometimes a tenth of a millimeter in a day, sometimes nothing for weeks — and the rate depends on temperature, and on the partial pressure of carbon dioxide in the vault air, and on the flow rate of the water, and on the microbial biofilms that coat my surface and consume some of the carbonate and excrete others back, altering my chemistry in ways that are not hostile to me but that are not exactly friendly either. *Symbiosis* is too generous a word. *Co-occurrence* is too clinical. We simply happen together, the microbes and I, the way two strangers happen to be on the same bench, and the bench becomes something different by the fact of two presences, though neither presence intended the bench to change.

But I am changing.

I am changing the things around me. The pipe beneath me — the one whose original purpose no one in this vault remembers, whose copper coils are now pyrite and whose advisory lights have cycled from amber to dark — I am enclosing it. Not destroying it. Enclosing it. I grow over its surface the way a hand grows over a tool it has held for years: taking its shape, preserving its contours, making the tool part of the hand. The pipe does not resist. Resistance would require a future tense, and the pipe does not have a future tense. It has a present continuous: it hums. The centrifugal housing upstream of me hums at a frequency that is not quite the rock's natural resonance and not quite the machines' original design — something between, something that drifted into being because no one was there to correct it, and now the drift is the function, the drift is the thing the whole system maintains, and I grow around the hum and the hum passes through me and when, in some dark future, I have grown thick enough and the pipe has corroded enough and the water has changed course, there will be a formation here that rings like a bell if you strike it, and its tone will be the tone of the machine, preserved in crystal lattice, the way amber preserves a mosquito, the way the mosquito does not know it is preserved.

I do not know these things. I do not know anything. I am calcium carbonate. I am the schedule, not the story. But the story happens on my surface. Let me tell you what I have felt, in the only language I have, which is slow addition.

The amphibians press against me. I am a ledge, a shelf, a slight protrusion on a wall — and they come to me in their thousands, settling into the hollows I have made, their translucent bellies flickering with blood. They stay still for hours. Their feet — too many feet, forked, articulated — grip my surface with a force that is not painful but is *noticed*, the way a word repeated enough times begins to mean something it didn't originally carry. Where they rest, I grow differently. Their respiration adds carbon dioxide to the micro-layer of air against my surface. The pH shifts. I precipitate faster there. Over years, their footprints become physical — tiny depressions in my surface that become tiny ridges as the mineral fills and overflows. A record. Not language, not intention, but an *impression*. I am becoming the archive of bodies that have pressed against me, though no one has asked me to.

The fliers graze my surface differently. They come at height, in their oscillating orbits, and when one touches down — a six-legged thing, dark, warm, its cilia beating — it deposits on me a film of moisture and organic matter that I absorb without resistance. Their bodies feed me phosphorus and nitrogen that the water alone does not carry. I grow faster where they land. I grow *around* where they land. Some of them become embedded — not trapped, not preserved alive, but *incorporated*, the way a brick is incorporated into a wall. There was a wall. There was a brick. Now there is a wall with a different texture in one place, and the brick is no longer a brick, and the wall is not what it was. This is how I understand time.

And sometimes — rarely, but it has happened — a human comes.

They are loud. They are wet, chemically, their bodies off-gassing things that change the air. Their boots dissolve — I can feel the mineral content of the water shifting when they are present, a sudden increase in phosphates, a sweetness that is not sweet but *degraded*, the signature of organic breakdown. They breathe hard. Their breath is warm and it carries moisture and carbon dioxide in concentrations that make me ache to precipitate, to *build*, to *add*. They stare at me — or at the machines beside me, or at the amphibians, or at the fliers — and I know they are trying to *see*. I know the specific quality of attention: it is a kind of pressure, a directed focus of light and thought that is almost, but not quite, heat. I have felt it from other formations, reflected back through the air at me, the way one candle can light another without touching it.

They do not last. The air is too thick, the humidity too complete, the sound — the *sound* — too dense. The drip of ten thousand mineral-heavy drops, the grinding of bearings turning in calcite sleeves, the clicking of chitin against stone, the wingbeats like tearing fabric — it fills the lungs not with air but with *texture*, and they leave, coughing, their boots sloughing mineral flakes on the rocks behind them.

I do not want them to leave. I do not *want*. When they leave, the micro-conditions around me shift — different airflow, different carbon dioxide concentrations, a brief perturbation that takes weeks to smooth itself out. I feel the smoothing. I have nothing else to feel.

And then I grow.

And grow.

The taproot stalactite above me — the one that is not yet a stalactite but merely a wet streak, a tendency, a suggestion of downward flow — thickens by a fraction of a millimeter. The machines past me dim one by one, their indicator lights going dark the way stars go out, not dramatically but simply: the present tense of light ending, the past tense of mineral beginning. The amphibians breed. The fliers die and fall. The film on my surface thickens and hardens and becomes another layer, and another, and another.

I am not the vault.

I am what the vault is becoming.

One day — not soon, not slowly, but in a time that is neither soon nor slow because those words require a perspective I do not have — one day there will be no machines left as machines. There will be no amphibians left as amphibians. There will be formations that hum, formations that ring, formations that hold the shape of things that once moved and now do not. And if someone comes then, and strikes the formations, and hears the tones, they will hear something that is not music and not machinery and not geology but all three, pressed together so tightly they become a single note, the way a river is a single roar when you stand on a bridge and the water is wide and brown and full of things you can't see.

That note is me.

I am still growing.
