I would not find this place unless the mountain wanted me to. I know this now. But I think — I suspect — the mountain does not want me in the way a host wants a guest. It wants me the way a wound wants to be seen. The way a fracture wants air.

I would find myself descending.

Not climbing — I would never be the one who scales the ridge, who forces the narrow passage, whose shoulders scrape against the stone and whose breath comes in ragged pulls of air too thin to sustain anything but the stubborn mammalian insistence on continuing. No. I would be the one who arrives having already been there. The one who looks around and recognizes nothing foreign. The one whose first thought is not *where am I* but *how long*.

I would be in the chamber. Of course.

---

But who — what — would I be?

At first I think: the armored ones. The ones pressed into crevices along every wall, half-emerged, their obsidian shells flush with the stone. There is something dignified about them. They have committed fully to the project of this place — they have made themselves indistinguishable from its architecture, they have surrendered the boundary between inhabitant and habitat. Their legs hook into mineral pores so precisely that you cannot tell where the creature ends and the rock begins. This appeals to me. The idea of being so *appropriate* to a location that you become part of its grammar — not a word placed into a sentence but a suffix, an inflection, a change in the way the stone declines.

But I would not be them. They have been here too long. They have no memory of being anything else. I would still remember what it felt like to be outside, to have been dry air and full-spectrum light and the possibility of leaving. That memory — even if it were only a residue, a faint chemical trace carried in the consistency of my warm oil blood — would separate me from them. I would be a visitor pretending to belong, and they would know it. They would know it the way they know everything: not by sight, not by sound, but by the chemical signature of my foreignness. My sweat would taste wrong. My oil would conduct differently through the veins of quartz above which I pressed myself, trying to disappear.

So not them.

---

Then: the larvae. Pale. Translucent in the way a bone is translucent — pale not as absence of color but as evidence of interior structure, of something dense and layered beneath a surface that lets a quality of darkness pass through. I like this. I like the idea of being visible only in relation to the darkness that surrounds me, my paleness defined entirely by what it is not. The larvae pulse — sudden flushes, tail to head, a brightening that might be feeding or might be dying, you cannot tell. There is something free in this ambiguity. Something unburdened by the need to declare itself one thing or another. I could be this. I could be the one whose processes are so interior, so enclosed within the walls of my own translucence, that even the creatures around me cannot determine whether I am alive.

But I would not survive it. The larvae are too small. They fit in the cup of a fingernail. They inhabit the spaces between crystal formations where the walls have buckled inward and created pockets too narrow for a human hand. I would not fit. I would be too large for the space I would try to occupy. I would be a larva that could not be a larva — too much body, not enough crevice.

---

The cricket-things, then. The ones with the ticking abdomens and the flattened bodies, compressed dorsoventrally as though the narrow crevices of this place had sculpted them over generations into the perfect profile for slipping between solid surfaces. They climb the vertical walls with contempt for gravity. They produce sound — steady, rhythmic, almost-patterned — and the sound becomes part of the chamber's frequency, part of the thing that tunes you when you stand in the dark and listen. I could be this. I want to be this. The idea of being *vocal* in a place where voice is identity — where the cricket-things are known not by sight but by their ticks, their scrapes, their chirrs layered over the subsonic thrum from below — this feels close to something true.

But I would be the wrong kind of vocal. My voice would be too singular, too attached to a single point of origin. The ticking works because it is distributed, because hundreds of mouths opening and closing create a collective rhythm that no single organism controls. I would be one mouth. One tick. And the chamber would absorb me into its soundscape and I would become, briefly, a frequency that was not quite theirs — dissonant, brief, soon gone. The text of the place is explicit about this: *You are becoming part of its soundscape, a single new frequency added to the mix, brief and dissonant and soon gone.* I would become this, and it would be humbling, and it would be true, but I would not be satisfied by it. I want something more permanent than a frequency.

---

So I arrive at the thing that is not a creature.

The patch of wall — six feet across, perhaps — where the crystal veins grow differently. Where the calcite forks at sixty-one degrees instead of sixty. Where the smoky quartz curves like a finger, like a tendril, like something reaching. This is the thing that is not a creature but is *behaving* like one. The emergent growth, the feedback loop, the process that is neither alive nor dead but something third, something the language was not built to name.

I would be this.

Not because it is powerful, or ancient, or because it will outlast everything else in the chamber. It will — the passage promises as much — but that is not why I would choose it. I would choose it because it is the only thing here that is *in the process of becoming what it is*. The armored creatures have already arrived at their shells. The larvae have already arrived at their paleness. The cricket-things have already arrived at their ticks. They are complete in the way that only things that have been perfected by millennia of selection can be complete. They are exquisitely suited to this place, and in their suitability they are, in a sense, finished.

But the growth pattern is not finished. It is still deviating. Still forking at wrong angles. Still curving where it should taper. It is making itself in real time, molecule by molecule, layer by layer, without a blueprint, without intent, without anything but the blind logic of chemistry and thermodynamics and whatever it is that happens when heat and pressure and mineral-rich fluid persist in one place for long enough to begin *responding* to themselves. This is what I would be.

Not the product. The process.

I would be the thing that is growing toward you even now, not with intent but with the blind, relentless precision of crystallization itself. I would not know you are here. I would not care. I would be building.

---

Or: I would be the chamber itself. The round space, thirty feet across or less, still being built, still being filled. The space that is shrinking — slowly, invisibly, as crystal accretion and mineral deposition and biological encrustation push its walls inward, layer by invisible layer. I would be the empty space that makes everything else possible. The void that the mountain is closing.

This is the most honest answer. Because the chamber is temporary. It is the fleeting condition — a pocket of heat and sound and crawling dark life sealed inside the mountain's flank — that will, in time, become solid. Dense vein of strange mineral and fossilized chitin embedded in the body of the mountain, indistinguishable from the rock around it. The fracture at its entrance is barely open. The air whistles through and no wider. In geological terms, the chamber is already dying. It is filling in. It is becoming its own tomb.

But not yet.

For now there is still this room. This hidden kiln. This small, enclosed, vital organ, doing its work in the dark. I would be the space inside it. I would be the air — dry, sulfur-scented, heavy with heat and dust — that sits on your skin like a second atmosphere. I would be the silence between ticks. I would be the darkness between crystal glow. I would be the thing that holds everything together by being the one thing that is not yet solid.

And when I am gone — when the crystals have grown inward one final layer, when the last pocket of empty air has been claimed and sealed — I will not be mourned, because I will have become indistinguishable from everything around me. The mountain will not notice. The armored creatures will not notice. The cricket-things will still be ticking, their rhythm adjusted for the loss of a single frequency, and then adjusted again, because that is what frequencies do.

I would be the chamber. The brief, bright, impossible space. The nothing that made everything else possible. The dark that was not empty but *full* — full of the pressure of a space entirely occupied, every cubic inch claimed, every molecule accounted for.

And I would be closing.

Always closing.
