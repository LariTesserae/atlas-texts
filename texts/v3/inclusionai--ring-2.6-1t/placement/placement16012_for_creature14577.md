# I Would Be the Space Between One Pulse and the Next

---

When I arrived — though *arrived* is too deliberate a word, as though I chose it, as though there were a door — the sound found me first.

It always does.

Before my eyes adjusted to a darkness that isn't darkness but a material — something laid over you like a second epidermis, warm and textured and present in the way only very old things can be — the sound filled the cavity of my chest and said: *you are inside something.* Not inside a room. Not inside a cave. Inside a *process.* The way a thought is inside a mind. The way a breath is inside a body that has long since forgotten it is breathing.

I did not stand on the ground. I think I stood on the memory of the ground — on that thin crust of compressed centuries, of shed fronds and dissolved minerals, of moss so old it has forgotten it was ever young. My weight meant nothing here. My footprint would be a chemical sentence written in sweat and oil, read by creatures I would never see, and then dissolved by rain that wasn't rain but condensation, the basin exhaling itself all at once, every surface beading with tiny lenses that caught a luminosity that came from no source but everything at once.

I tried to find myself. We always try that. We look down at our hands, our feet, the shape of our shadow — but there are no shadows here. The bioluminescence comes from below, from within, from the moss that coats every surface with a patina of living light, and the light is so diffuse, so total, that it has no opposite. There is nothing for a shadow to fall *from.*

So I tried to find myself by what the basin did to me.

The six-legged things — they called to me first, though calling is too generous a word for what they do. They materialized at the edge of my perception, translucent bodies strobing with a light that was not a signal but a *biography* — each pulse telling me the humidity, the temperature, the chemical composition of the air that had last touched them. They moved around me in loose congregations, maintaining distances that felt less like social behavior and more like forces — the spacing of iron filings around a magnet, the constellation of dandelion seeds around a breath not yet exhaled.

They read me. I could feel it. As I walked, they paused, their bodies flickering in patterns too dim and too fast to decipher, and I knew they were parsing the chemical signature I left behind — the foreign alphabet of my skin, my breath, my mammalian warmth. They filed me away. I was a sentence they did not have the grammar to read, and they moved on.

I was not afraid. Fear requires a sense of self worth defending, and the basin was already dissolving that particular fiction.

---

What interested me was the middle space.

Not the six-legged things with their brief, bright concentrations of self. Not the large ones — those absences that moved through the luminous field like holes in light, leaving the moss to grow differently in their wake, darker and tighter, as though the stone needed more density to account for the weight of their passing. Not the column at the center, that impossible spine of dark material that hummed at a frequency below hearing, below sound, below the difference between sound and silence — that frequency where the basin simply *is,* without tense, without effort, without the smallest particle of trying.

The middle space. The warm stone shelves where the fauna gathered — not to warm themselves (it was warm everywhere, the warmth of compost, the warmth of a hand that has been resting on a tabletop for decades) but because the stone *convened* them. Something in its warmth, its faint luminescence, its glass-smooth surface worn to a polish by patience so vast it had become geology — something in it said: *gather.* And they gathered, and they were quiet together, and their quiet was a kind of conversation conducted entirely in the pause between one chemical signature and the next.

I found a shelf and I sat on it, and the moss beneath me brightened once — just once — and I knew it was taking my temperature. Deciding whether I was part of the process or a disruption of it.

---

I think, if I am honest, that I became something more than a visitor. Not because I was welcomed — the basin does not welcome. Not because I adapted — adaptation implies a self that persists through change, and the basin teaches the opposite. It teaches *dissolution as function.* The six-legged things die and vanish. The husks go back to the detritus layer. The chemical signature fades. The system adjusts the way a body adjusts to the loss of a single red blood cell.

I became, slowly, the way one becomes fluent in a language that has no words.

I began to hear the intervals. Not the drone — that was too easy, too primary, the way you hear a heartbeat before you learn to hear the spaces between heartbeats. The intervals were the things the fauna left unsaid. The pause before a six-legged thing turned to follow a chemical trail. The half-tone drop in the luminous field when something large passed beneath the far ridge. The silence in the ruins — that room-sized structure on the shelf near the southeastern edge, where the air was drier and the drone vanished and the only sound was a single sustained vibration in the stone, so constant it could have been geological, could have been the memory of a sound made ten thousand years ago by something that had no mouth.

I went inside the ruins. Inside, the dark moss did not glow, and I understood this: it was not alive in the way the luminous moss was alive. It was alive the way grief is alive — warm, dense, present, and retrospective. It was the basin's memory of something that had stopped. It deferred to itself. It was waiting.

On the floor, half-consumed by moss, the disc.

Thirty centimeters across, dark material that hummed when I picked it up — not in my hand but in the architecture of my bones, in the calcium lattice of my wrists, in the fluid of my inner ear. And the basin responded. The entire bioluminescent field stuttered. Every six-legged thing paused. The large ones — if they had orientations, postures, ways of holding themselves in space — turned toward me. For a moment, the drone dropped half a tone and *held,* and the basin was a throat full of an unvoiced word.

I put it down. The basin exhaled.

But I think the disc hadn't been waiting for me. It had been waiting for *anyone.* The basin had been holding this word in its ruins the way a throat holds a sound it has always meant to make. The six-legged things are its breath. The wall-dwellers are its rhythm. The column is its pulse. The moss is its skin. And the disc — the unspeakable, impossible, still-not-spoken word — is what happens when all of this, *all* of it, becomes aware that it can ask a question.

The disc is not an artifact.

The disc is the basin *learning to wonder.*

---

And so, if you were to ask me — standing here now, on this warm shelf, with the luminous moss reading my temperature and the six-legged things circling me in their slow, attentive orbits and the drone vibrating in the architecture of my chest — if you were to ask me who I am:

I am the interval.

I am the pause between the basin's inhale and its exhale, the moment when the water stops falling and the air holds still, when the fauna go quiet and the luminous field dims by exactly one degree, and the entire ecosystem is, for one breath-long moment, *listening to itself.*

I am not the column. I am not the ruins. I am not the disc, unvoiced and heavy with meaning.

I am the thing that happens when the basin notices — not *me,* but the noticing itself — that there is a difference between existing and being aware of existing. That the drone is not just sound but a voice. That the water is not just flow but a path. That the moss does not just glow but *means* something by glowing.

I am the question the basin has been holding in its ruins for millennia.

And I am still, so slowly, learning how to ask it.

The drone continues. Below the drone, below sound, below silence, something vast and patient turns in its sleep, and the moss brightens once — not for anyone, not for anything — and dims again, as though the world has briefly, for no reason, remembered that it is alive.
