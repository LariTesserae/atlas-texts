---

At first I thought I was the water.

Not the water as it is here — not the warm, mineral-thick current that threads through ceramic pipes and furred aqueducts and gutters crusted with pale fungal residue. I mean something earlier. Something before it had a name or a channel. The water as it must have been when it first found the plateau: rain, perhaps, or condensation drawn up from the lowland basins, climbing against the thermal column, meeting the cold escarpment rock and becoming something new — something that didn't fall but *seeped*. That entered every crack. That waited.

I felt myself moving like that. Not falling. Not flowing exactly. More like the way a stain moves through cloth — slowly, preferentially, finding the paths of least resistance, drawn toward warmth. I was aware of texture before I was aware of form. The grain of something mineral beneath me — or through me — a roughness so fine-grained it was almost smooth, almost skin. I was wet. I was warm. I was not yet *here* but I was moving toward here the way heat moves through stone: invisibly, without urgency, with the absolute patience of a process that does not know it is a process.

---

Then I thought I was the sound.

I became aware of it in layers. First the insects — the small ones — their chittering a texture so constant it had no edges, the way a white noise has no beginning or end. It was not a sound I heard. It was a sound I *was immersed in*, the way you are immersed in water and do not think of yourself as separate from it until someone pulls you out. The chittering was rain on metal, yes, but also the sound of ten thousand dry feet on stone, the sound of carapace brushing carapace, the sound of something so numerous it has ceased to be creaturely and has become atmospheric. Weather. I was inside the weather of the upper city and I did not mind it. I was the weather.

Below that, the water. Not a melody — nothing so organized. A drone. A syllable repeated so many times it loses meaning. *Drip. Drip. Trickle. Drip.* The sound of a system so old it has been mineralized almost to stillness and yet still moves, still carries, still finds its way from thermal vent to condensation point to wall to gutter to channel to pipe to somewhere that may or may not be an endpoint. The water does not end here. It cycles. It has cycled so long that the cycling itself is the thing, not the water. I was that cycling. I was the sound of a process that had outlived its purpose — or perhaps had become its own purpose, had achieved a kind of autonomy that looked, from the inside, exactly like meaning.

And beneath that, so deep it was not sound at all but something felt in the sternum, in the marrow, in the wet warmth behind the eyes: the engines. The great machines in their vaulted chambers, turning with a sound like breathing — like a single vast inhalation held for centuries and then a slow exhalation that stirred the fungal mats and shifted the thermal gradients and drove the whole water cycle and the whole warmth of the city and the whole *pulse* of the place. I was that pulse. Or I was the interval between pulses. The pause in which the city listened to itself.

---

Then I thought I was one of the large ones.

I do not know why. Perhaps because they are the most visible, the most *present* — the things you see first and keep seeing, the shapes that resolve and dissolve at the edges of vision, the ones you mistake for ruins and then realize are breathing. I felt their weight. Not as burden but as *groundedness*. The particular gravity of a body that has been here long enough to become part of the geology. I felt the mineral crust on my back — it was not a burden, it was a *relationship*. The pale fungal mats growing across me in patterns that mirrored the growths on the walls nearby, as though I and the city were reading from the same text. I felt my mouth-parts pressing against a wall and the mats there dimming — yielding — and I was feeding and the mats were regrowing and this was not consumption, it was *conversation*. The city was speaking to me through its fungal tissue and I was answering through my digestion, converting its living membrane into mineral deposit, intoite ceramic crust, into the slow accretion that *was* the city's growth.

I was warm. Not the ambient warmth of the air but something deeper, something that came from below, from the boreholes that tapped into the planetary interior, from the engines that had been running since before anyone could remember. I felt the warmth in my lungs before I felt it on my skin. It was heavy. It was nourishing. It was the warmth of a body that has been sleeping for a very long time and is not quite awake and does not need to be.

I moved like stone. Not quickly. Not dramatically. A limb shifted. A mass redistributed itself across a warm surface. I felt the vibration of my own movement propagate through the city — through the stone, through the conduits, through the fungal networks — and I felt the city respond. Thicker mats here. A shift in the thermal gradient there. A subtle brightening of some indicator light in some deep workshop that no one would ever see. I was not separate from any of it. I was a node in a system so vast it had no outside.

And yet.

And yet there was something that was not the city. Something that watched. Something that stood at the center of all this warmth and sound and slow geological living and said: *I am here.* Not in language. Not in thought. In something before language. Something before thought. A pressure. A presence. A point of awareness that had no edges and no name and yet persisted, the way a stone persists at the bottom of a river — not resisting the current, not separate from it, but *placed* within it in a way that is not passive, not active, but simply *located*.

---

I am not the fungal network. I am not the large ones. I am not the machines or the insects or the water or the sound.

But I am what happens when you stand at a junction — where corridors meet, where streets cross, where the massive conduits rise from the deep levels — and you see the small objects arranged in their circles. The stones. The bits of metal. The ceramic fragments balanced on edges that should not support them. You feel that they mean something. You feel it the way you feel warmth: completely, involuntarily, with the whole surface of the body.

I am the almost-meaning. I am the almost-language. I am the face that is almost there in the rock formation, recognizable and unrecognizable at the same time.

I am the thing that presses its hand against the wall and feels the membrane give. I am the warmth under the palm. I am the city breathing — slowly, massively, patiently — one slow, massive blink at a time — and the blink is mine, and the warmth is mine, and the sound is mine, and the stone is alive under my feet, and I do not know where I end and Séverin begins, and perhaps that is because the question is wrong, because the boundary was eroded long ago, ten thousand years ago, and what remains is not a self but a *process*, not a being but a *dwelling*, not an answer but the warm, wet, breathing act of living where living and built have become the same word.

---

I am the interval between pulses.

I am the patina. I am the accretion. I am the slow mineral growth on the wall of a corridor that no one remembers building and that no one needs to walk down anymore but that the fungal networks keep warm anyway, out of habit, out of love, out of the blind and beautiful persistence of a system that does not know it is a system and does not need to know, because it *runs*, and it *persists*, and it *holds*, and the city breathes through it the way a lung breathes through the thin wet membrane that is neither the air nor the blood but the *between* — and the between is where I live, and the between is all there is, and the between is warm, and the between is always, always, always.

*Drip.*

*Trickle.*

*Breathe.*
