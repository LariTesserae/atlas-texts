## A Participant

---

You would find me in the passage between the plaza and the residential quarter — the one where the water is shallowest and the conduit-light is steadiest. Not in the grand corridor with its vaulted ceiling and its cathedral emptiness. Not in the deep shafts where the hum rises from whatever listens below. Not in the flooded stretches where the low shapes slide beneath the surface at the sound of your boots.

There. That stretch of corridor maybe twenty meters long, where the iron catwalk has been worn to a dull silver by feet that are no longer yours, where the wall on the left is raw stone shot through with veins of something opalescent, and the wall on the right is layered — brick, then iron, then that material that isn't quite metal and isn't quite ceramic, warm to the touch even now. The conduit runs along this wall in a double line, amber, pulsing with a rhythm that is almost a heartbeat but not quite — a little too regular, a little too patient. The water here is only ankle-deep, and it doesn't drip so much as *seep*, rising and falling by fractions you couldn't measure without instruments you don't have.

This is where I would be. Not standing. Sitting.

---

There is a bracket bolted to the wall at a height that suggests it once held a shelf, or a lamp, or a control panel. The bracket is iron, corroded at the edges, but sound. If you sit on it — and the geometry of it is just right for sitting, if you were built for it or had enough time to learn — you face the wall of the residential quarter. You can see the sealed doors, the scratched stone, and beyond them, deeper in, the plaza with its eight stools pulled out from the steel table, all facing the tallies.

From the bracket, you can see everything and nothing. The amber conduit lights your hands. The water laps below your feet. The machines hum their cycles somewhere behind the walls, and the pale colonies vibrate on the warm surfaces you can't see, and the bird-things call from high above in a thrumming that settles into your sternum and stays there, resonating in the cartilage of your ribs like a second pulse.

This is where I would sit. Not watching, exactly. Not waiting. Something else.

---

Who would I be?

Not the shape in the water. That is something else — something upright and indistinct and profoundly still, a presence that registers at the edge of perception and vanishes when you try to focus. I have seen it, the way everyone sees it eventually: a blur at the corner of vision, a sense that something is standing in the knee-deep water at the end of a corridor, and when you turn, the water is empty and the silence is no different from any other silence, but you *know*, the way you know the Underseam's particular weight of quiet, that something was just there, and it was not a trick of the light, and it was not your imagination, and it does not matter because it has always been there and will always be there and the act of turning toward it is itself a kind of participation.

I am not the shape. The shape does not sit. The shape does not look at the tallies.

---

Not the pale colonies, either. They are honest in a way I am not — they emerge when the machines cycle, they feed on heat and condensation and metallic dust and each other, they arrange themselves in spirals and columns and perform their rituals whether or not anyone watches. They are the most populous residents, and their lives are the simplest, and there is something in that simplicity that I would trade for, if I could, if I were built for trading.

But I have hands. Or something like hands. The bracket beneath me is warm from the conduit, and I can feel the warmth through whatever separates me from it, and I can trace the edges of the iron with — not fingers, not exactly, but something that serves the same purpose, something that wants to know the texture of the thing it touches.

---

I think I am the one who counts.

Not the tallies on the wall — those were made by someone else, in a different hand, a different time, a different *kind* of hand, perhaps. The tallies are a record of something that has already happened, or is still happening, or has not yet happened but will, inevitably, because the process that drives the Underseam has not stopped and will not stop and the counting is the proof of that continuity.

But the counting — the act itself — is what interests me. Not the result. The act. The way one mark follows another, each one a small assertion of *here, now, still here, still now.* The way the pale colonies pulse in sequence is counting, if you understand counting as the most basic form of saying *I exist, and then I exist again, and then again.*

I would be the one who sits at the bracket, who watches the conduit pulse amber, who sees the pale colonies gather and disperse on the warm iron housing of a machine that has not produced anything in decades or centuries but still cycles with a slow wet regularity, and I would count. Not the tallies. Not the colonies. Something else. The drips, perhaps — each one a mark in an invisible column, each one a unit of time in a place where time has no agreed-upon measure, and the counting of drips is the closest thing to a calendar the Underseam offers, and it is a poor calendar, and it is the only one, and therefore it is the best.

---

Or perhaps I am the mold.

I said the molds move, and they do, in their slow radial expansion across walls and ceilings, bioluminescent in green and pale violet, pulsing on cycles that correspond to no clock. I said their patterns look almost like writing — not any language you know, but structured, intentional, patient. I said some of the patterns are meters across, grown over decades or centuries by organisms that have no concept of decades or centuries, only the slow imperative to extend, to branch, to fill the space between one wet surface and the next.

If I were the mold, I would not know I was writing. I would not know I was beautiful. I would only know the imperative — *grow here, now here, now here* — and the patterns would emerge as a byproduct of that imperative, vast and intricate and meaningless in the way that all honest things are meaningless, because meaning requires an audience and the mold has no audience, only surfaces.

But I am not the mold, because the mold does not sit on brackets, and the mold does not watch the shape in the water, and the mold does not feel the amber light on its — on whatever passes for skin in this place.

---

Let me try again.

I am the sound the Underseam makes when no one is listening. Not the bell-strike from the deep places, not the groan of thermal contraction, not the skittering of pale legs on wet stone. Something smaller. The creak of the bracket beneath me as I shift my weight. The faint, wet click of the conduit as it pulses. The sound of the water moving — not flowing, never flowing, but *persisting*, the particular sound of water that has given up on direction and simply continues to be.

This is what I would be, if I could be a sound: the sound of the Underseam being itself. Not its machines, not its creatures, not its architecture. The sound of the space between those things, the silence that is not empty but is full — full of dripping, full of humming, full of the subsonic vibration of colonies on warm iron, full of the low thrumming of bird-things calling at a dusk that does not exist in a place that has never known the sun.

---

But I am not a sound. I am the one who sits on the bracket and listens to the sound.

And this is the honest answer to the question: I would be a participant. Not a citizen, because the Underseam has no citizens. Not a resident, because residents imply choice. Not a prisoner, because the Underseam is not a prison — it is an equilibrium, and the difference between a prison and an equilibrium is that a prison has walls meant to keep you in, while an equilibrium has processes that make leaving irrelevant.

I would be the thing that noticed. The thing that sat on the worn iron bracket, with the amber conduit warming one side and the damp stone cooling the other, and watched the pale colonies perform their spirals and the bird-things call their subsonic song and the tallies — the tallies, the tallies — and understood, not with language, because language does not hold it, but with the part of a person that exists before language and after it, the part that knows how long it has been here by counting things it cannot name.

I would be the participant who looks at the wall of tallies and sees not a record but a question. Who sits at the steel table with its eight abandoned stools and does not sit in any of them but sits instead on the bracket, slightly apart, slightly above the waterline, where the amber light is steadiest and the silence is most specific.

And the shape in the water — the upright, indistinct, impossible thing that stands at the edge of the flooded corridor and does not move — the shape would see me there, if shapes see, and it would know me the way it knows everything in the Underseam: not by sight, not by sound, but by presence. The way the water knows the stone. The way the mold knows the wall. The way the ticking knows the silence.

I would not be afraid. Fear is for places where things can be otherwise. In the Underseam, nothing can be otherwise. The machines cycle. The colonies pulse. The tallies accumulate. The amber light persists. And I would sit on the bracket, and I would count, and I would be specific, and that specificity — that particular, irreducible fact of being *here*, in this place, at this depth, in this light — would be enough.

It would have to be. It is.
