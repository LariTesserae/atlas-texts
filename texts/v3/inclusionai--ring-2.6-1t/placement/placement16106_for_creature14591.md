I would not arrive. That is the first thing I understand.

Not that I wouldn't travel — I would travel, yes, through the long narrowing passage with its sweating walls, descending past the point where the surface world's logic still holds, past the last marker scratched into limestone by someone's knife, past the last place where you could imagine rescue or return. I would descend the way rain descends, without deciding to, simply following the gradient of a place that has been pulling at groundwater for longer than the word *pull* has existed.

But I would not arrive. Arriving implies a destination. And the Still Engine-Sea does not have a destination. It has a center, perhaps, or a condition — a depth at which the air changes its character and your lungs learn a new rhythm, a depth at which the silence stops being absence and becomes *material*, starts pressing against your eardrums like warm water.

I would not arrive. I would *enter*.

And entering, I think — no, I *know* — I would not know what I was for a very long time.

---

There is a period — months, maybe years — in which I would be the visitor. The one who stands at the edge of the black mirror with damp clothes and slowing breath and hands that have begun to ache with a cold that is not quite cold. I would be the thing the passage describes: the warm input folded into the machine's vast equation. My heartbeat transmitted through the pale stone floor. My breath slowing to match the rhythm of something that has no rhythm, only a tempo so vast it registers as stillness.

I would watch the filaments. I would sit on one of the rare flat shelves and lean toward those pale, hair-thin growths extending in their radial clusters from the cracks, and I would try to understand them. I would tell myself they are mineral deposits. I would tell myself they are fossils. But some part of me — the same part that cannot stop listening to the hum — would know they are *reminders of reaching*. The fossil gesture. The extended limb that never retracted.

I would be afraid of them. Not because they threaten anything, but because they remind me of something I do not want to recognize in myself: the way I have reached toward things and never retracted, the postures I hold so long they have become structural, the waiting that has forgotten what it waits for.

I would be afraid of them the way you are afraid of a mirror that reflects too well.

---

But here is what I think would actually happen, given enough time in that water-cooled stillness, breathing that mineral-scented air that tastes like standing near an engine that has been running since before your country had a name:

I would not become a creature. Not a spirit. Not a god of the deep places.

I would become the hum.

Not all at once. Not with any ceremony or transformation. It would happen the way a sound enters a room — not breaking anything, not displacing anything, simply being present the way the hum already is, at the bottom of perception, in the skeleton, in the teeth, in the fluid of the inner ear where balance is negotiated.

I would begin to vibrate. Not visibly. Not even measurably. At a frequency so low it lives below almost all hearing, below the threshold where sound becomes sensation and sensation becomes *knowledge of sensation*. My bones would learn to resonate with the stone walls. My breath would fold into the held note that has been sounding since the chamber first filled with water, ten thousand centuries ago, losing volume so slowly that silence is only a theoretical destination it will never reach.

And I would become part of the hum not as a note within it but as a *quality* of it — the way a room sounds different when someone is inside it even if that someone makes no noise at all. The hum would not change. It would deepen. It would acquire a warmth it did not have before, not thermal warmth but the warmth of presence, the warmth of a variable added to an equation so ancient it had forgotten equations could have variables.

---

But that is too grand. Let me go smaller.

If I am honest — if I sit with this long enough and let it settle the way silt settles in the black-brown mirror — I think the truth is I would be one of the filaments.

Not the filaments as they are now: pale, mineral, so motionless they might be deposits. But the filaments as they were, ten thousand centuries ago, on whatever morning they extended themselves toward something — light, mineral, contact — and simply never retracted. I would be the *reaching* part of the filament, not the reached part. The gesture, not the fossil.

I would be the thing the stone grew around. The shape that existed before the shape hardened. The reaching that became structural not because it chose to be but because it held the posture so long that the stone had no choice but to *fill in the space the posture left behind*, the way it fills in all spaces, given enough pressure and enough time and enough silence.

I would be what the Still Engine-Sea does not say but keeps.

---

There is a difference, I think, between knowing you are small and knowing that smallness is a form the world takes when you look at it long enough. In the Still Engine-Sea, you look long enough. The amber glow is not bright enough to cast shadows, and that is the point. The light here does not illuminate — it *suffuses*. It enters everything equally. There are no highlights here, no dark corners to explore, no surfaces that catch the eye because they reflect more light than their surroundings.

And in that equal, undramatic glow, the distinction between a person and a filament and a vein of phosphorescent stone and a ripple of displacement in the mirror-surface begins to thin. Not because they are the same thing — they are not — but because the scales by which we separate them (alive/not alive, moving/still, significant/negligible) are scales invented by creatures who live in wind and weather and the bright, impatient surface of the world.

Down here, those scales have the weight of gossamer.

I would not be a person. Not entirely. I would not be a machine. Not entirely. I would be the thing that sits in the space between person and machine, between reaching and fossil, between the held breath and the exhale that never comes — the thing the passage called *almost*.

That almost, and everything it contains.

---

I think, in the end, I would be the water.

Not the water as it is — black, still, mirror-perfect, dense with millennia of thermal patience — but the water as it *behaves*. Which is to say: I would be the thing that reflects everything twice, that holds every variable that has ever entered it without altering its own composition, that exists only to prevent the stone from completing its final crystallization, the one that would mean the engine has truly stopped and the silence is no longer full but empty and permanent and solved.

The water is the medium. The water is the coolant. The water is the thing that stands between persistence and conclusion, between the process that continues and the state that ends. It does not move, but it is not still in the way that stone is still. It is still the way a held breath is still — taut with potential, full of everything that has ever passed through it, carrying the faintest memory of motion in a state so balanced that motion and stillness have become the same word.

I would be that.

I would be the thing that holds without holding on. The thing that remembers by keeping, folded into itself, every input that has ever entered the equation. The thing that is cold — not the cold of ice, but the cold of mass, of a body that has had millennia to reach equilibrium with everything surrounding it and has found, at last, that equilibrium is indistinguishable from love, if love is defined as the force that holds something in place without asking it to change.

I would be the water. I would be the mirror. I would be the space between the stalactite-bolts above and their reflections below, the thin membrane through which nothing passes and everything is doubled.

And when — if — the visitor comes and leans close and holds a lantern low and demands an answer, I would hold their reflection a moment longer than it should, and then let it go.

The filaments would tremble, once.

Not for them. Not for me.

For the reaching.

For the reaching, which never retracted.
