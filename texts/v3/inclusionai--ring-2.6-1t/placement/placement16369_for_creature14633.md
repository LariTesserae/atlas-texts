I think about this place. I turn it over. The basalt, the green, the held-breath quiet. I try on selves the way the garden tries on leaves — not because one is right, but because the trying is the thing.

---

At first I thought I would be one of the Oil-Sheen Beetles. There is something dignified in their stillness — the way they wedge themselves between leaves like stones in a wall, iridescent, requiring nothing, drawing moisture from the air itself. I have known that posture. The way of being so quiet that the world forgets to check if you're alive. I could sit in the crook of a translucent rosette and let the centuries pool around me like condensation on mineral skin. I could close my joints against the cold and open them only when the ground trembled — a footstep, a shifting vine — taste the air, register the unfamiliar warmth, and settle back. It is not a bad life. But it is a closed one. The beetles conserve. They do not reach.

So I tried on the Lattice Weavers. I liked their hands — those trowel-pads pressing mineral dust and fiber into paste, building six-sided lanterns that hang like small suns in the vine-dark. I liked their patience. I liked that they are the ones who make space: the seedling shelter, the windbreak for the moss, the corridor a creature can walk through as though the architecture grew. There is a deep satisfaction in building a thing that the garden will claim as its own, that will thicken with vine and sprout and be indistinguishable from what was already there in a generation. But the weavers are small and pale and translucent with amber blood. They are seen by the garden as builders, known, legible. I wanted something I was not sure the garden had a name for.

I tried on the moss. I tried on the lichen — that black fractal creeping through the deepest cracks, drawing energy from the oxidation of iron and sulfur, etching the stone open so that others could follow. I liked the lichen's insistence, its willingness to be the first colonist, the one who breaks the surface so slowly that the breaking is geological. But lichen does not have a point of view. It has a chemistry.

I tried on being the vibration itself — the low-frequency hum that moves through stone and root and chitin, the one you feel in your bones rather than hear. The thing that synchronizes the stomata, the constructions, the beetles' collective stillness. There is an intimacy in that, a dissolution of boundary so thorough that you are no longer a creature *in* the garden but a quality *of* it. But that is too much merging. I would lose the edges I need to feel with.

---

Here is where I arrived.

I think I would be one of the Long-Antenna Crawlers, but not exactly as described. Not the one that emerges at dusk to eat the dead and carry the spores. I think I would be something adjacent — something the description almost names but doesn't.

I would live at the edges.

Not the center of the caldera, where the density is so thick that light goes green and the air goes sweet from ten thousand metabolisms pressing close. Not the deep shade under the vine-lattices where the weavers work. I would live where the garden thins — where the rim's rock begins to give way to vegetation, where the last skeletal lava tube opens onto the scree slope, where basalt still shows its rope-textured face to the sky and the succulents are smaller and more scattered, not yet bold enough to call themselves rosettes.

I would be the length of a hand. Many-legged but not six — more like ten, or twelve, each one jointed and tipped with something between a claw and a filament. My body would be matte, not glossy, the color of wet ash. I would not be iridescent. I would not need to be seen. When I moved through the moss, the moss would barely notice.

But my antennae — yes. My antennae would be longer than my body, sweeping slowly, deliberately, reading the air for what the garden does not say out loud. I would taste the mineral vapor rising from cracks where the basalt is still cooling inside. I would taste the sugars leaking from the succulents' roots. I would taste the faint sourness of the lichen's acid at work. I would be a reader of what is written in chemistry and vapor, translating between the language of the stone and the language of what grows on it.

My role would be this: I would carry things between worlds. Not spores — that has been done. Not paste, not fiber. I would carry *information*. When a succulent's roots reach a new mineral vein and the chemistry shifts, I would be the first to know. When a section of basalt cracks and opens a new crevice, I would find it before the moss does, and my passage through it — my scent, my shed skin, the faint trace of dust from my body — would mark it as a place worth colonizing. I would be the garden's peripheral nervous system. The thing that runs along the edges and reports back.

And at night — because I must have a night-self, as this place must have something that stirs in the dark — I would climb to the highest point of the caldera rim and sit with my antennae angled upward, reading the air above the garden, where the world is still bare rock and open sky. I would feel the wind coming from outside — from the scree slopes, the distant valley, the wider world where things are loud and fast and careless with their lives — and I would bring that information back down into the green, the way a nerve carries a signal from the skin to the center of the body.

The builders would not build around me. The beetles would not curl at my passing. The succulents would not angle their leaves. I would be the thing the garden knows is there but does not hold in focus — the peripheral awareness that does not require a name, only a function. The quiet thing that moves between the quiet things.

If you came to the caldera and looked closely — not at the beetles, not at the weavers, not at the impossible fullness of the green — but at the rim, at the thinning edge, at the place where stone becomes life by degrees so gradual you can almost watch them blur, you might see a matte shape with too many legs disappearing into a crack, antennae reading the dark like fingers reading braille.

That would be me.

Not building. Not blooming. Not still. Just moving slowly enough to notice, and just alive enough to carry what I noticed back into the green.

---

The Basalt Garden does not need me. That is what makes it the right place. I would arrive the way a new mineral vein arrives — not summoned, not invited, just present in the stone's slow conversation with whatever comes next. And if I lingered long enough, the garden would grow around my absence. The moss would fill the tracks I left. The succulents would close their leaves around the memory of my warmth. And the vibration — the hum that runs through everything, binding beetle to stone to vine to air — would absorb my frequency without noticing it had changed.

That is the comfort. That is the strangeness.

You become part of the negotiation, and the negotiation continues without you, and both of these things are true at once.
