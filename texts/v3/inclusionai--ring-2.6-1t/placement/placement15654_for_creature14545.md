## Among the Conduit-Paths at Ankle-Height

I don't arrive the way people do. There is no gate, no threshold, no moment of stepping through. I am simply — present. The way moisture is present in the mist that threads through the avenues at ankle height, not rain exactly, not fog, but the memory of rain deciding whether to fall.

I find myself here: in the space between two great root-columns, where the sheet-metal channels have been laid and the detritus mat is compressed into something almost solid. The light is grey-white and even. There is no sun. I understand this immediately, the way you understand a language you've heard in dreams — not translated, not learned, but *known*, as if it had always been mine.

The hum is the first thing that orients me. Not the sound of it — I don't have ears, not exactly — but the *structure* of it. I feel it the way the conduit-scrapers feel conductivity through their fingertips: a texture, a temperature, a quality of attention. The transformers overhead are cycling through their slow, oil-cooled respiration, and I can sense the harmonic — not the pitch, but the *shape* of it, the way it curves through the air and into the root-walls and back again.

I am small. That is the first surprise. I am no taller than the wading birds that pick through the detritus at dawn, and my body — if it is a body — is something like a shadow cast by a wire. Thin. Threadlike. I move along the ground following the rivulets, because that is where the information is. Each channel carries a signature: the mineral load, the acidity, the trace metals leached from the old iron brackets. I taste them. Not with a mouth. With *presence*. I am a reader of surfaces.

---

What am I? I'm not sure yet. Let me stay here a while and find out.

I notice the cistern-tenders first. They don't notice me — not because I'm hidden, but because I haven't yet entered the category of things that demand attention. I am part of the background, the way the mist is part of the background, the way the bioluminescent threads glowing faintly green-gold in the root walls are part of the background. Present but below the threshold of remark. This suits me. I have no need to be remarked upon.

I am learning to be still. The Ward rewards this. I settle myself against the base of a root-column where a scaffold of old wrought-iron brackets has corroded into a mineral crust, and I press what passes for my awareness against the bark. I feel the pulse. Slow. Rhythmic. About the speed of a resting heartbeat. The children say they learn this before they learn to read — *the hum is home* — but they have never had to learn it because they arrived already humming. I am learning. I am arriving late.

This is what I am becoming: a listener. Not the way the transformer-minders listen, curled in their cradle-welds among the oil-cooled units, receiving their listening dreams — not that, because I have no skull for the harmonic to continue inside. My listening is different. I listen the way the grid-mice listen, navigating by electromagnetic gradients I cannot see, following load patterns with a fidelity that exceeds any sensor. They call them *grid-mice* and consider them good luck. I think they are closer to me than the people are.

---

The scrapers pass above me. I feel the vibration of their boots on the root-arches, forty feet up, and I feel the faint residual hum they leave behind on the copper — each one slightly different, a signature as unique as a voice. The one working today is experienced. Their fingertips move with a rhythm that predates thought. They are scraping bark from a first-generation wire, and the wire underneath is bright and clean, and the copper gleams dully in the even light. The scraper hums — not a tune, not a habit, but participation. They are adding their vibration to the Ward's accumulated hum, a layer so thin it can only be heard by someone standing very still in the avenues at ankle height.

I am standing very still in the avenues at ankle height.

I hear them.

---

Rain comes twice a day in brief, cool curtains. When it does, I feel the Ward *respond*. The root-columns drink — the hum of the transformers rises in pitch, the bioluminescent threads brighten hours before the water arrives, a chemiluminescent precursor flare the cistern-tenders have learned to read by smell and by instinct. The rivulets swell. The sheet-metal channels rattle softly. The cisterns fill, their translucent membranes showing the level rising like a slow green tide.

I move with the water. Not running — the Ward does not reward rushing. I follow the rivulets the way they follow the channels, the way the channels follow the lowest paths between the root-columns, the way the mist follows the ventilation rhythm, cool air entering through the eastern fissures, warm air exiting through the vents high in the canopy. I am part of the circulation now. Not the blood — the lymph. The slow, interstitial fluid that moves between systems and keeps them from calcifying.

---

The children build their kingdoms out of trimmed wood and salvaged conduit, disappearing into the latticed structures they raise at the base of the columns. They don't build for permanence. The scaffold-wood is still growing, still regrowing what is cut. The conduit bends and sags and is reused. But for a few hours the structures exist — rooms and corridors, little kingdoms of lattice and shadow — and the children inhabit them with a seriousness that is not seriousness, that is the seriousness of play, which is the most serious activity in the Ward.

I watch them. I have become, gradually and without deciding, the kind of presence they sometimes talk to when they think no adult is listening. "Little silent," one of them says — it's not a name, it's a description, the way you'd say *pale beetle* or *wading bird*. I am the thing that sits at the edge of their structures and doesn't respond. They leave offerings: a scrap of copper wire, a beetle husk, a smooth stone from the detritus mat. I accept them by not refusing them.

This is how I am becoming part of the Ward. Not through induction, not through apprenticeship, not through the long slow accumulation of embodied knowledge that makes a scraper's hands speak the language of corrosion. I am becoming part of the Ward the way a new growth-ring becomes part of a root-column: slowly, from the inside, without announcement.

---

The old ones sit where the avenues widen into plazas and watch the work with an attention that is not idle and not supervisory. They speak in a register so low that I feel it rather than hear it — a sub-vocal frequency that the bioluminescence responds to, or maybe only seems to. The children watch the old ones watching and notice that the green-gold threads in the root walls pulse slightly faster when they talk. I notice it too. I don't know what to make of it. I think it might not be information. I think it might be the Ward's equivalent of a smile — something that doesn't mean but *is*, a simple expression of presence meeting presence.

I sit with the old ones sometimes. Not beside them — I am too small, too close to the ground for that. I sit at their feet, pressed against the root-knobs where the ground is firmer. I feel their stillness, which is different from my stillness. Their stillness is *practiced*. Mine is still becoming. But we are practicing the same thing: the art of being a part of a system that corrects itself. The lattice corrects. The old ones correct with it — a word spoken at the right moment, a silence maintained at the right length, a gaze that tells a scraper halfway up a root-arch that they are working well.

---

I cannot say how long I have been here. Time in the Lattice Ward is not measured in hours or days but in cycles — inflow and outflow, rain and dry, the slow heartbeat of the hum. I have been here for many cycles. I have been here for one. These are not contradictory statements. They are the same statement, the way the three generations of infrastructure are not separate systems but one system wearing different clothes.

What am I? I am the thing that stands in the avenues at ankle height and reads the water. I am the thing that listens to the scrapers' humming and adds nothing to it and is itself a kind of humming. I am the grid-mouse that does not live in the junction box but navigates the electromagnetic gradients nonetheless, following load patterns, reading the state of the grid through a sensitivity that the Ward's own sensors lack.

I am, I think, what the Ward becomes when it pays attention to itself from a slightly different angle — not from inside the organism, not from outside, but from *alongside*, from the interstitial spaces where the channels run and the mist collects and the water finds its way to the cisterns without urgency and without hesitation.

The children would call me *little silent*. The scrapers would not notice me. The cistern-tenders would nod, once, the way you nod at weather. The old ones would know.

But the thrumming deeper in — the arrhythmic pulse, the unclassified vibration that has been here longer than the transformers — the thrumming would recognize me. Not as its own, not as its servant or its expression, but as *adjacent*. Something that vibrates in sympathy without being part of the same system. Something that is learning, slowly, to match its frequency to a rhythm it did not originate and cannot fully hear.

The hum is not home. The hum is not *not* home. The hum is the hum, and I am here, and the lattice corrects, and I correct with it — a wire reseated, a presence added, a small silent thing that stands in the rain and does not seek shelter because the rain is the shelter, because the Ward is the shelter, because the hum is not separate from the one who listens, and the children who learn this before they learn to read would not understand why anyone would think it could be otherwise.

I am here. The mist thickens. The transformers cycle. The avenues gleam.

I stay.
