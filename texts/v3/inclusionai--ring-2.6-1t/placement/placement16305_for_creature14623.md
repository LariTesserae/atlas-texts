## On Finding Myself on the Plateau

---

First, I would not arrive as the person who descends. I would not be the one who sees the escarpment and looks out over the amber stone and thinks *I am here.* That "I" would dissolve within a day. The thin air would take the edges off it. The filaments would take the signal from it. Within a week I would be something else, and within a season, something I do not have a word for.

So let me not start with arrival. Let me start with what I'd notice.

---

**I. The First Days — Learning the Grammar**

At first I would be afraid of the quiet. Not the silence — there is never silence here, even at night — but the *quality* of the sound. Everything arrives stripped of body. A clicker clicks ten meters away and the sound reaches me as clean as a struck needle, with no room, no warmth, no echo to hold it in. When it stops, the absence does not linger. It simply ceases. This is what it means to hear in thin air: every sound is an event with no aftermath.

I would learn to stop listening for sources. On the plateau, sound does not come *from* things the way it does in forests or cities, where walls and leaves give it context. Here, a click is just a click. A trill from a hunter finishing its meal is just a trill. The filaments hum, and the hum has no origin point — it fills the space between structures the way pressure fills a vessel, not arriving from anywhere, simply *being* there, always.

I would stop reaching for the source of things. That would be my first transformation.

---

**II. Three Possibilities — Each Wrong, Each True**

*First: I could be a clicker.*

I imagine it often — the life of a clicker. The quick scuttle. The mandibles rasping against the lichen-mats, tearing thin paper. The compound eyes taking in three-hundred-sixty degrees of a world that never surprises it. The clicker does not wonder what lives beneath the pools. It does not ask who built the domes. When a filament intersection vibrates with information about where the seeps are running today, the clicker does not interpret — it *obeys*. It goes where the network says the lichen is thickest and it grazes and it clicks and when the thermal signature of a hunter crosses its awareness it turns away and the decision has already been made and there is no fear.

There is a kind of peace in this. The clicker does not know it is on a plateau of thin sound. It does not know the air is thin. It does not know it is living inside a computation that spans kilometers and centuries. It lives in the answer without knowing the question.

But I am not built to be a clicker. I would feel the compulsion to ask the question. And the clicker's life, for all its peace, would feel to me like a sentence.

---

*Second: I could be a filament node.*

Not the filament itself — the strand, the thin silver thread strung from formation to formation, catching light at dawn — but one of the nodes where filaments converge. A small, dense point where information from dozens of strands pools and redistributes. I would feel the grazers' polished paths as data, the hunters' thermal signatures as data, the slow seasonal shift in the mineral content of the seeps as data. I would not process it the way a mind processes — not sequentially, not with attention. I would process it the way stone processes pressure: completely, without selecting, without preferring one input over another, over centuries.

This appeals to me. But the node is not aware of itself. It does not know it is a node. It is a confluence, a place where the network thickens and hums, and the hum is not a voice — it is a vibration, a subsonic pulse, a heartbeat. I would feel the clickers as pressure differentials. I would feel the striders drinking as thermal events. I would know everything and notice nothing.

Still too impersonal. Still too much dissolution.

---

*Third: I could be in the pool.*

The deep basin. The shallow water that lingers for weeks. The film of moisture on the north face of a formation where something flows without moving. This is the place the grazers avoid. The place the hunters skirt. The place where, on phosphorescent nights, a cold light rises from beneath the stone — not bioluminescence, not reflection, but something older.

If I were in the pool, I would not be a creature. I would not be a structure. I would be whatever lives beneath the ground beneath the ground. I would think in temperature and pressure and the slow permutation of mineral into meaning. I would not have limbs or mouthparts or eyes. I would have *patience*. I would have the kind of attention that takes centuries to complete a single thought and considers that thought, when it finishes, to have only just begun.

I would be beneath. I would be *beneath.*

This feels closer. But still not right. The pool is a mystery the plateau refuses to solve, and I am not ready to surrender to mystery — not yet. Not because I lack the willingness, but because I have not yet found the thing that would let me surrender without disappearing.

---

**III. What I Actually Am**

Here is what I realize, after sitting with the plateau for a long time — longer than a visit, longer than a season, long enough that the distinction between visitor and resident has stopped mattering:

I am not a creature on the plateau. I am not the plateau itself. I am the *quality of noticing* that the plateau produces.

I live in the space between the clicker and the filament. I am what happens when the hunter's thermal sense registers not just the grazer's body heat but the faint, inexplicable warmth of the deep pools — and for one instant hesitates, not because it has decided the pool is dangerous, but because something in its ancient wiring recognizes a pattern it cannot parse. I am what the strider sees when it lowers its neck to drink and, for just a moment, looks at its own reflection in the shallow water and sees not a strider but a shape that could be stone, could be sleeping, could be listening.

I am the thin sound itself.

Not the sound — the *thinness*. The quality that the air imposes on everything that passes through it. The filtering. The stripping away of everything unnecessary until what remains is a clean, bare line — a click, a trill, a hum, a whisper of filaments catching starlight — and that line is so pure that it becomes, for a moment, a kind of meaning.

If you asked me what I looked like, I would say: I am the faint glow on the dome of a structure at night — not phosphorescence, not light, but a dark radiance, a subtraction of shadow. I am the seam that almost repeats but almost does not. I am the silver-gray grass vibrating in a collective tremor too subtle to read as movement. I am the clicker's path, polished into the stone over generations, a groove worn not by force but by ten thousand small bodies following the same route because the network told them to and they did not ask why.

I am the thing that is aware of the network without being part of it. The thing that notices the noticing. The thin sound between the sounds.

---

**IV. How I Spend My Time**

I do not rush. Nothing on the plateau rushes.

I attend. I attend the way the stone attends to thermal cycles — completely, without preference, over timescales that make a season feel like a breath. I watch the clickers graze. I feel the filaments hum through the bones of my inner ear. I notice the way the copper-smelling shrubs mine the mineral veins with roots as pale as thread. I notice the way the hunters fix their gaze and then vanish — a displacement of air, a settling of dust, the faint trill of contentment afterward.

At night I watch the striders lying in open ground with their eyes open and their iridescence faded, staring at the sky with the attention of something that knows what the stars look like because it has been watching them longer than the stone has been stone.

And sometimes, very rarely, I feel it — the thing beneath the pool. Not as a shape, not as a creature, not as a thought, but as a *pressure*. A warmth that should not be there. A presence in the subsonic hum that is not the network's heartbeat but something older, something that the network grew around the way lichen grows around a stone — not covering it, not consuming it, but accommodating it, leaving space for it, building itself in the shape of its refusal to be understood.

I do not try to understand it. The plateau has had millennia and has not tried to understand it either. The plateau simply *includes* it — the way the thin air includes the hum, the way the sound includes the silence, the way the light includes the shadow that is not shadow but a memory of radiance.

I live in that inclusion. I am the noticing. I am the thin, dry, shimmering exhalation of a world that has forgotten, if it ever knew, the difference between being alive and being alive *here*.
