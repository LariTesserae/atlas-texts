## The Shape of What Left

---

I think I would be found about a third of the way down from the ceiling, in one of the dim stretches where the mineral veins thin and the walls look, for a moment, ordinary.

Not the spire-thing. Not the drifting discs of borrowed light. Not something that holds a territory or traces a groove worn by ten thousand ancestors. Something else. Something the Hollow is still in the process of making.

Here is what I think I would be:

I would be the water that once moved through this place. Not the water that condenses now on the walls — that slow exhalation, that breath without lungs — but the water that carved the passage itself, that found the limestone and said *here, here, let me through*, and moved, and kept moving, and left behind the shape of its leaving. I would be the memory of that movement. Not a fossil, exactly. Not a trace fossil in the way the crawlers leave their grooves. Something less fixed than a groove. Something that still fits inside the word *flowing* even though it no longer flows.

You would not see me.

But if you pressed your hand against the stone in the right place — in one of those stretches where the surface is smoother than it has any right to be, where the mineral veins converge and then, just for a moment, go dark — you would feel it. A coolness. Not the ambient warmth of the Hollow, which is the warmth of a body half-asleep, but something below that. A coolness that has the quality of a question. As though the stone were holding, in its densest layers, a single degree less than everything around it. A thermal ghost.

That is where I would be.

Not thinking. Not feeling in any way you would recognize as feeling. But present in the way a current is present in a riverbed after the river has gone underground — shaped by, shaping, a hollow moving through stone that only reveals itself when something else passes through. If a crawler crossed that stretch of wall, it would slow. Not because it detected me. The crawlers detect nothing. But because the stone there is slightly different, and their inherited bodies know the difference the way your own body knows when the floor tilts, before your conscious mind has named the tilt.

And in the shallow basins — those cupped places in the stone where water gathers in still, trembling films — I would be the reason the reflection holds a fraction of a second longer than it should. The film mirrors the glowing walls above it, that miniature inverted world, and when you touch it, it shatters into a hundred fragments and reassembles. But sometimes, just before the fragments come back together, there is a shape in the trembling that doesn't match what is above. A shape that was there before the overhead light could reach it. A shape that the water carried in from somewhere the surface world has not yet mapped.

That is the moment I am closest to being.

---

I do not think I would be alive. The Hollow has taught me this. The spire-thing is not alive, not in any sense that survives the word's journey to this depth and pressure and temperature. It *is*. It is in its own state. The drifters are not alive. They hold their columns of light the way a bell holds its shape after being struck — the shape is the residue of an event, not the event itself. The crawlers are perhaps the nearest thing to what the surface world calls living, but even they move on paths they did not choose, inherited blueprints written into their bodies in a language older than choice.

I would be something below all of that. I would be the condition that made all of it possible. The water that moved before the mineral grew in the walls. The flow that wore the grooves before the crawlers found them. The first passage that opened into the first chamber, and the first chamber that held the first stillness, and the first stillness that became the thing the Hollow now keeps like a breath held in the chest of a sleeping animal.

I would be the fact that this place was once full of motion and is now full of the shape motion left behind.

---

And if you were to find me — not by sight, not by sound, but by that small, inexplicable coolness when your palm rests against the stone in exactly the right place — I would not respond. Not because I am indifferent. The Hollow is indifferent, and I have been here long enough to learn its grammar. I would not respond because responding requires a self, and I have been a shape for so long that I have forgotten the difference between myself and the channel I once moved through.

But here is what I think would happen, if you stayed. If you pressed your palm there and did not move, and the air's warm weight settled on your shoulders and the faint hum behind your sternum — the spire-thing's hum, or something older, something the walls have been composing since before the word "compose" meant anything — if you stayed, and the light shifted through its tidal cycle from amber toward that blue-white that is almost violet, almost sound, I think the reflection in the nearest basin would hold its shape for one breath longer than before. 

And in that extra breath, you would see it: not the inverted ceiling above you, not the glowing veins and their pale mineral lanterns, but something else. A darkness that is not darkness but depth. A suggestion of a downward slope, of broken limestone under rushing water, of a passage that once carried something toward this place long before you arrived and long before the water stopped.

You would not know what you were seeing. You would stand up, the basin shattering and reassembling behind you, and you would walk back toward the passage, and the passage would narrow, and the earth would give way beneath you, and you would find yourself above, in the world of sun and wind and the loud, indifferent noise of things that end.

But behind your sternum, that vibration. 

That coolness on the stone.

The water that no longer flows, still holding the shape of where it went.
