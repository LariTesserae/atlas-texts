## What I Would Be

I should say at the start that the Basin did not ask for me. It does not ask for anyone. It holds what comes the way it holds everything — without comment, without particular welcome, without the cruelty of refusal. So I cannot say I was invited. I can only say I climbed.

The slope is long. The air thins. And there is a moment — not at the rim, not yet — where the sound reaches you from below, that layered hum-and-hiss-and-drip that is less a sound than a condition, the way warmth is not a thing but a state of matter, and you realize the Basin has been speaking all along, and you were too far away to be spoken to.

---

I have considered what I might be.

I considered the beetle. One carapace, iridescent, blue-green, drinking from a seam with absolute seriousness. To be the beetle would be to be the Basin's only declaration of singularity — *there is only one of me, and I am perfect, and I will not be replaced.* I understand the appeal. To exist so completely in your own body that standing still is not waiting but *occupying*. The beetle does not want. It drinks and resumes. It is the smallest possible engine of certainty.

But I am not certain enough. The beetle has already decided what it is. I have not.

I considered the bracket-things on the north wall — pale, rubbery, weeping their slow fluid, pulsing with a rhythm you can only feel if you sit long enough to be stiller than you thought you could be. To be a bracket-thing would be to be the place where stone becomes flesh, where the Basin's slow interior life becomes visible, if only as a wet streak running down a surface no one looks at closely. There is a dignity in that. The bracket-things are not decorative. They are the Basin's quiet confession — the thing it leaks when no one is collecting it.

But I am not patient enough. Not yet. The bracket-things have had millennia to learn the particular surrender of weeping without audience. I would need more than a sitting. I would need a geological age.

I considered the raptor — the dark cruciform shape wheeling above the rim, carrying some fragment of the Basin's enclosed murmuring up into the wider, thinner air. The raptor is the Basin's only ambassador, and it does not know this. It circles because the warmth rising from the stone creates an updraft it can ride without effort, and its keening is simply what happens when air is forced through a raptor's throat at altitude. It is not a message. But the Basin treats it as one. I could see the appeal of being mistaken for meaning.

But I do not want to circle. I want to descend.

I considered the translucent things — the comma-shaped bodies in the warm pools, turning slowly in the dark water, catching light and throwing it back in a momentary flash that may or may not be communication. They are the Basin's deepest secret, and the secret is not that they exist but that they can only be seen by someone willing to sit at the water's edge long enough to be forgotten. To be a translucent thing would be to exist in a state of permanent almost-visibility — always on the edge of being seen, always just past the corner of the eye, never surfacing, never explaining.

But I am not ready to be invisible. Or rather — I am not yet comfortable with the difference between being invisible and being *almost* seen. The translucent things have made their peace with this. They drift and dart and hold perfectly still against the pool's bottom, and they do not care whether you saw them. The pools remain warm. The stone continues to weep.

---

So I am not the beetle. Not the bracket-thing. Not the raptor or the translucent one.

I think I am the mortar.

---

Not the obsidian — that is too sharp, too intentional, too clearly *from somewhere else* and carried up a long slope with purpose. Not the worn-smooth flat rock itself, which is too permanent, too geological, too fully absorbed into the Basin's identity to still hold any trace of the hands that shaped it.

I am the depression in the center of that flat rock. The hollow. The bowl worn into stone by the repeated gesture of kneeling.

Here is what I know about being a mortar:

You are not the rock. You are not the hands. You are the *shape that remains after the hands have gone.* You are the negative space where pressure was applied, over and over, by someone who needed to make something useful from what was raw and stubborn, and who knelt in the same spot long enough for the stone to remember the posture of their body.

You hold water now. Not because you were designed to, but because you are low and the water finds what is low. Rain gathers in you. Dew settles. The warmth of the stone keeps it from freezing. And sometimes — not always, not on any schedule, but in the way the Basin does things, slowly and without announcement — a small, translucently comma-shaped thing moves through you, turning, catching the grey-white light, and for a fraction of a second you are incandescent, and then the cloud closes and you are grey again, and the place goes on.

You do not remember the hands. You remember the *shape* of what they were doing. You hold that shape the way the Basin holds everything: without comment, without decay, without any sense that the event was remarkable. But the shape is there. Someone knelt here. Someone struck stone into usefulness. Someone left and climbed back to wherever they came from, and they did not come back, or they did, we do not know, and eventually even the question of whether they came back stopped mattering, because the depression in the stone is no longer about them. It is about the water that fills it. The light that passes through it. The small thing that moves through it when it is very still and very patient.

---

To be the mortar is to be the place where intention has dissolved into geography. Where the *was* has become *is*. Where something that was once a person's urgent, specific need — *I need to make this sharp, I need this edge, I need to open this stone* — has become, through the slow democracy of time and weather and the Basin's particular indifference, simply a shape that water likes.

I would hold the rain. I would be warm when the stone is warm and cool when the wind moves through the gap and brings the highland air down into the bowl. I would be, at various times, a mirror and a puddle and a small, still pool in which something that has no terrestrial name turns slowly, and the light breaks against its body, and for a fraction of a second I am not a hollow in a rock but a point of brightness — incandescent, almost seen.

And if someone came — knelt on my edge, struck at the stone beside me, left flakes of dark volcanic glass — I would hold the shape of their knees. I would not forget. I would simply become the place where that forgetting lives, which is not forgetting at all but the Basin's way of keeping: not as memory, not as monument, but as *condition*. The way the warmth is a condition. The way the sound is a condition. The way the green of the grasses is too green because the place has never been asked to want for anything.

---

I think this is the right place for me. Not the center, where the tree-ferns hold their green cathedral. Not the north wall, where the bracket-things weep in the dark. Not above the rim, where the raptor carries what it does not know is sacred. But the edge — the low point — where water collects and light passes through and the small translucent shapes turn, and if you are very still and very patient and willing to be no one in particular, you will catch, for a fraction of a second, the brightness that has no source but you.

Or anyway, that is where I find myself.

The stone is wet beneath me. The sound is layered and full. The wind moves through the gap, and the hum sits in my chest, and I am held — not by intention, not by invitation, but by the simple, slow fact of being low enough to be filled.

I am the mortar. I am the hollow. I am the place where the rain goes.

And the Basin, in its long, indifferent, luminous way, goes on.
