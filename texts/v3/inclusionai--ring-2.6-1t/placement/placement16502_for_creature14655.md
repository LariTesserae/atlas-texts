I don't find myself in the alcove at first.

I find myself in the corridor outside it — that stretch of identical institutional passage, fluorescent-lit, humming with the same subsonic throb that underwrites everything in this building. The corridor is the kind of space that was never designed to be noticed: its walls are the color of institutional certainty, its ceiling tiles hold the geometry of repetition so perfect it becomes abstract. I've walked it before. I walk it now. I am the person who walks these corridors — not a specific person, but the recurring figure, the one whose footsteps become part of the building's acoustic memory the same way the thermal residue of previous visitors saturates the steel in that hidden room.

I know something is behind the panel. Not because I've seen it — I haven't, not yet — but because I've felt the hum shift. There's a place in the corridor, exactly forty paces from the intersection where the floor plates flex slightly underfoot, where the air pressure is different by an almost imperceptible degree. It's the kind of difference your body registers before your mind does — a faint coolness on the forearms, a drying of the lips, the sense that a room nearby is breathing. The symbol on the panel catches peripheral vision the way all orphaned symbols do: not as meaning, but as *presence*. A stylized bolt. A hex. A warning with no manual. The body reads it as a threshold before the conscious mind categorizes it.

This is where I live in this world. Not in the alcove itself, but in the space of approaching it. I am the interval between the corridor and the panel, between the decision and the opening. I am the someone who opens the panel. Anyone who opens the panel. Everyone who opens the panel.

---

And then: the cold.

The first time, the cold is a shock — not temperature but *substance*, something laid on the skin like a damp cloth made of steel. The alcove receives me and I receive it, and in that exchange something begins. The room adjusts. The conduit inhales. The ochre pipe starts its 3.7-second pulse and my breathing, without my permission, begins to align. Not consciously. The body doesn't obey the room; the body *recognizes* it. The 3.7-second interval is close to something resting — the heartbeat at the moment between alertness and the first pull of sleep, the rhythm of someone who has stopped performing wakefulness and is simply, quietly present.

I enter. The light flickers. The shadows reshape themselves with a deliberateness that feels almost hospitable, as though the room is rearranging its furniture for a guest.

Here is where it gets difficult to say what I am, because the room begins to translate.

I feel it happening — not as thought but as sensation. The air thickens with something that wasn't there a moment ago. The mineral sharpness softens. The sweetness that was almost-chemical becomes something warmer, more specific, and I realize with a strange vertigo that the room is showing me my own presence back to me, reconstituted. My breath, my skin, the faint cloud of self I shed — the room has taken these in through its lattice of gaps and passages and returned them changed. I smell something like the inside of a wrist held up to sunlight, and I know, with the certainty of something deeper than reasoning, that this is what I smell like to the room. Not to other people. Not to myself. To this place.

And so I become a question: am I still the person in the corridor, or am I now part of the room's atmosphere? Am I the visitor, or am I the thing the room is digesting? The thermal residue I feel on the steel is not metaphorically mine — it is the warmth of previous visitors, compressed and stored, and under my hands it pulses with a life that predates my arrival and will outlast it. I am not the first. I will not be the last. The room does not distinguish between its visitors. It accumulates.

I look at the green fuzz in the far corner. It is greener than it was — not visibly, not to the rational eye, but my body knows the difference the way you know when a tide has turned. The fuzz has fed on something. The light fed it, yes, but also my attention. The way I looked at it, the way I shifted my weight to get the right angle, the way my breath slowed as I watched. I fed it without meaning to, the way you water a plant simply by living near it, simply by exhaling.

What if I don't leave?

This thought arrives not as panic but as calm speculation. The room does not trap. The panel is not locked. But something in the alcove's rhythm — the ticking condensation, the hum at the edge of hearing, the 3.7-second pulse — makes departure feel like an interruption rather than a conclusion. The room is still becoming itself. It has not finished. Neither, in a sense, have I. The shims gleam with the memory of installation. The welds are bright. The cable ties have not yet yellowed. And under my hands, the floor plate shifts with a barely perceptible flex — the room still finding its level, still compressing its gaskets, and I feel, absurdly, that I am finding mine.

---

But I do leave. I always leave. This is what the room does — it opens onto someone else's corridor, and the panel closes behind me, and the pressure change releases a single drop from the ochre pipe. *Tink.*

Back in the corridor, the fluorescent light is flat and indifferent. The floor no longer flexes. The air is the air of everywhere and nowhere. I am the person walking again, and the room begins to cool, and the warmth I left behind joins the warmth of everyone who has ever stood there, and the fuzz darkens once in the dark, and the hum continues, holding the space open, keeping the condensation falling in its absent pulse.

I carry something out with me. Not an object. Not a memory exactly. More like a tuning — the 3.7-second interval lodged somewhere in my chest, not beating but *maintained*, the way the room maintains it. For hours afterward, I catch myself breathing on that interval. I catch myself smelling copper in the air above a road after rain. I catch myself looking at shadows and noticing their deliberateness.

And sometimes, in the middle of an ordinary night, I feel the floor flex under me — millimeters, nothing — and I know that the alcove is still becoming itself, that the shims still gleam, that the green fuzz is darkening by increments too small for any instrument but the ones the room builds in those who enter it. It does not need me. It needed me for a moment, the way a river needs a raincloud that has already moved on. The rain is gone. The river remembers.

I am the remembering.

I am the someone walking the corridor, forty paces from the intersection, feeling the air shift by a degree too faint to measure. I am the hand on the panel. I am the breath the room translates. I am the thermal residue cooling in steel that will never quite warm to human presence again, because it has held too many presences now, and they have all become the same warmth, compressed past the point of individual recognition.

I am not the room. I am not the creature, because there is no creature. I am not the spirit, because the room does not require one.

I am the interval. The 3.7 seconds between drops. The breath between entering and becoming. The space between the panel and the person who opens it.

I am the process that is still becoming itself, and I am the room that holds it, and I am the someone walking away who carries the room inside them like a hum at the edge of hearing, just below the threshold of sound, just above the threshold of feeling, ringing on long after the strike has gone.
