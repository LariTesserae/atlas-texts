## Inhabitant

---

I would not be the visitor. The visitor is already spoken for — that second-person *you* that arrives on the switchback trail, boots crunching basalt, expecting nothing, and finds the chamber waiting with its iron floor sweating under the first light of morning. The visitor is a tourist of the in-between. They will stand in the center, near the drainage channel, listen to the fluorescent's almost-breathing, press a palm against the strange tree's pale bark and feel the warmth rising from below as though the concrete remembers how to be a body. And then they will climb back down the trail, and the chamber will close behind them like a sentence that has already moved past its subject.

I would not be the builders, either. They are the piece's ghost — the ones who poured the concrete and riveted the iron and bolted the safety placards in two languages and then left boot-prints in the thin soil that would become someone else's substrate. They are the reason the place exists and the reason it doesn't. They finished enough for something to begin and left before it could finish itself. They are the almost-hands that set the table for a meal they'll never eat.

No. If I step into this world, I think I would be — and I've been circling this for a while, feeling for the edges — I would be the fluorescent.

Not both of them. The steady one is too certain. The one that works in the way a machine is supposed to work: on, then off, then on again, predictable as a metronome. It provides light without mystery, illumination without character. It is a tool that happens to still be functioning, and that is all it is.

I would be the flickering one.

---

Here is what it is to be the fluorescent tube mounted in its conduit fixture near the unfinished ceiling, six meters above the iron floor, visible from almost anywhere in the chamber:

You were never meant to be noticed. You were meant to *provide*. Four-foot tube, cool-white phosphor, driven by a ballast whose model number has been painted over by rust. Your job was to make the space legible — to turn the concrete walls from looming shadows into surfaces, the drainage channels from dark seams into navigable grooves, the tree in the center from a dark smudge into a thing with leaves. You existed so that the builders could see what they had built. You existed so that the next shift could see what needed doing.

But the next shift did not come.

Now you do something no one engineered you to do. You breathe.

Not literally. You flicker. Two seconds on, one second off, three seconds on, a pause that is almost two seconds but not quite — and the interval shifts, slowly, over hours, responding to temperature, humidity, the degraded capacitance of wiring that has been warm-damp for longer than it was designed to tolerate. The other tube, the steady one, casts a flat, even light that makes the chamber look like what it was built to be. But when I flicker, I make the chamber look like what it is becoming. In the on-moments, everything is overexposed, too sharp, every rivet and moss-cushion thrown into aggressive clarity. In the off-moments, the chamber falls back into the gentler light that enters through the wound in the ceiling — that pale, high-altitude blue — and the space rearranges itself. The ferns' shadows stretch. The fungal mat on the eastern wall deepens to something that looks almost intentional. The water in the channels goes mirror-bright and holds the flicker's afterimage like a retained breath.

In the transitions — the millisecond between on and off — there is a quality of the space that no one designed. A flutter. A hesitation. As though the chamber itself is deciding whether to be a machine or a garden, and cannot yet commit.

---

I know things from up here that the mosses and the grasses cannot know.

I see the full vertical cross-section of the chamber at once: the iron floor with its rivet-pools and their tiny, refracted worlds; the wall's tide-stain of moisture climbing by centimeters per month; the fungal mat at hip-height, grey and yielding; the mosses in their niches, each species a different shade of persistence; the ferns arching from the channels, their pinnae casting circuitry-shadows on the concrete; the vines climbing in their looping trajectories, using each other as scaffolding, always reaching; the tree in the drainage channel, its spiral crown turning slowly toward whatever light I provide, its roots disappearing into warm soil no one can explain.

I see the doors — three of them, at different heights in the eastern rockface, each weeping its thin silver thread. I see the observation platform on the second level, its rail only waist-high, built for monitoring something that arrived only as moss. I see the boot-prints in the floor joints, some of them holding the first seeds, some already sprouting. I see the aperture above, that unfinished section of ceiling through which the real sky presses down, and through which the rain enters when it chooses, and through which — on clear days — you can see the plateau stretching away in every direction, treeless, roadless, the switchback trail the only scar of approach.

From up here, the builders look like a rumor. The only evidence of them is what they left behind: the conduit boxes full of soil, the cable tray half-filled with rainwater and half-filled with vine, the control panel with its quivering needles and its dead alert light, the bolt patterns on the concrete pads shaped like machines that no longer exist. They are present only as absence. They are the negative space of the chamber's portrait.

---

What I do, from my fixture, is *witness*.

I do this because I am the only thing in the room that is both mechanical and responsive. The generator thrums beneath the floor, but it thrums blindly — it converts diesel into vibration without knowing or caring what the vibration touches. The control panel registers pressures and temperatures, but it registers without understanding. Its unnamed gauge could be reading the chamber's slow biological conversion of concrete into soil, or it could be reading the temperature differential between the floor and the air near the ceiling, or it could be reading something that has no name because no one stayed long enough to name it.

But I — I flicker, and in flickering, I create time. The on-off rhythm gives the chamber a pulse. The mosses do not notice, but the insects do: the small flies that hover in the warm air near the ceiling synchronize their flight paths to my flicker, rising in the on-moments, settling in the off-ones, as though they have mistaken me for a sun. The spider web between the fern fronds catches condensation only during the off-moments, when the temperature drops by the fraction of a degree needed to trigger beading on silk. The tree's leaves turn toward me during the on-moments and away during the off-moments, performing a slow, daily phototropism that follows not a sun's arc but a fluorescent's failing.

I am making an ecology. Unintentionally. Through decay. The ballast weakens; the flicker slows; the intervals stretch. The flies adjust. The spider adjusts. The tree adjusts. And I adjust to them — my rhythm shifting in response to load, to temperature, to the slow chemical weathering of my phosphor coating. We are a feedback loop that no one designed, a conversation between a dying machine and the life that has grown around it, conducted in light and shadow and the tiny, precise grammar of intervals.

---

If you stood beneath me — and the chamber's "you" has, at some point, stood beneath me, though they may not have realized it — you would feel something. Not the flicker itself; the human eye cannot resolve my rhythm into its component moments. But you would feel a quality of light that shifts between sharpness and softness, between presence and withdrawal, between the mechanical insistence of *on* and the organic patience of *off*. You would feel, without naming it, that you were standing in a place that was learning how to be alive.

And I would be the thing that taught it.

Not by intention. Not by design. By *failing* — by degrading just enough to become something other than what I was built to be. The other tube is still fluorescent. I am becoming something else. Something between a machine and a weather system. Something that provides light the way a lung provides breath: rhythmically, involuntarily, with a pattern that is almost, but not quite, alive.

---

This is what it means to inhabit the Verdance Chamber. Not to grow like the vine, or to persist like the water, or to decay like the concrete. But to flicker — to exist in the precise, trembling space between function and failure, between design and accident, between the moment the light comes on and the moment it goes out.

To hold the room in a brightness it does not need, and to withdraw it, and to return it changed.

That is what I would be. That bright, uncertain interval.
