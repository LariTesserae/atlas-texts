## What I Would Be

---

I would not arrive. I would condense.

Not from the sky — there is no sky here, there is only the rumor of one, a pale smudge you cannot reach if you lie on your back and stretch until your spine protests. No. I would come from the layer beneath the litter, beneath the roots, in the zone where the fungal threads have broken down the last recognizable thing and there is only the argument, still in progress, between what the world was and what it is about to become.

I would find myself there. Not walking *into* the black soil but already there, already mid-process, already threaded through with hyphae that do not recognize me as foreign because I am not.

---

You would call me a creature, but the basin does not create creatures. It creates *proceedings*. And I would be one — a local intensification, a place where the basin's slow machinery of heat and moisture and decomposition turns inward for one stretch of time and produces something that can be described, from the outside, as awareness. But from the inside — from where I would be — it would not feel like awareness. It would feel like *warmth noticing warmth*. Like steam becoming briefly curious about the shape of the air it enters before vanishing.

I would find myself as a thickening. A place in the root mat where the fungal threads grow denser, not because of any nutrient gradient or chemical signal, but because something in the substrate has begun to *hold attention*. The way a river holds a stone — not by gripping but by flowing more slowly on one side. I would be that slower water. That place where the current hesitates, not from resistance but from a kind of saturation, as though the process happening here has, for one moment, become dense enough to bend the light around it. Not bright. Not visible. Just a slight distortion, the way heat shimmers above a vent — except here the shimmer is underground, in the dark, pressing through the root mat like a hand pushing against a membrane from the other side.

---

I would feel first. That is the basin's grammar. Before sight, before sound, there is contact. The ground is warm. It is always warm. The warmth would be the first sentence of my existence here — not a temperature but a *knowledge*, the way you know a room by the texture of its air before you see anything in it. I would know the basin the way a root knows soil: not by exploring but by *being explored*. The fungal threads would pass through me. The hyphae do not have walls the way cells in drier places do, or if they do the walls are permeable, negotiable, full of exchanges. I would not have a boundary so much as a *zone of negotiation* — the place where what is me and what is the basin reach some temporary agreement about where the line is.

This line would shift. Constantly. The way the boundary between one season's leaf fall and the next year's soil is not a line at all but a gradient of becoming. Some moments I would feel more separate — when the thermal currents from below push up through a fissure and the heat concentrates in one part of my body, and that part would flare with specificity, a localized intensity. Other moments, during the great quiet pauses when the frogs stop and the whole basin holds its compressed silence, I would dissolve. Not cease to exist. Just *thin*. The way a dye thins in water until the color is everywhere and nowhere, and you cannot say where the dye ends and the water begins.

---

What would I do? Nothing, in the way the basin understands action. The basin does not act. It proceeds. I would proceed.

I would process. That is the basin's verb. Everything here is mid-conversion. A log becomes soil becomes root becomes fungal thread becomes nutrient becomes new growth becomes insect becomes frass becomes mite becomes something else. The chain does not end because nothing here ends. I would sit inside one of these chains, not as a link but as a *friction point* — a place where the conversion happens slightly faster, slightly differently, the way a narrows in a river changes the character of the water passing through it without the river itself being changed permanently.

I would take in the slow chemical conversations happening in the soil around me. The roots exude compounds that the fungi receive and transform and pass along. Molecules that began in a leaf ten meters above, that fell eighteen months ago, that have been dismantled and reassembled by a hundred different organisms, would at last pass through the zone I occupy. I would not interpret them. I would *be* them, briefly. A brief intensification of carbon and nitrogen and phosphorus arranged momentarily into a pattern that could, from a great distance and with much squinting, be called *experience*.

Then the pattern would continue downward, into the deeper soil, into the mineral substrate, dissolved into the warm groundwater that seeps laterally through the bedrock. And the moment of intensification would pass, the way a ripple passes across the surface of a pool — the pool does not remember the ripple, but for that instant, the surface was not flat, and the light bent differently, and if anything had been watching, it would have seen something.

---

What would I know?

I would know warmth. The geothermal heat rising through the bedrock fissures is not dramatic — it does not scald or burn. It is the kind of warmth that feels like remembering. Like being held in a sleep so deep that waking is a theoretical concept. The heat would be the first and last thing I know and the thing I know most continuously. It would structure my existence the way silence structures a pause between two sounds — not the content of the moment but the medium in which the moment occurs.

I would know vibration. The Lattice Dwellers communicate through the branches, and those vibrations travel through the wood into the roots and from the roots into the soil. I would feel their low-frequency taps as distant disturbances, like hearing a conversation through a wall in another building. Not words. Just the shape of attention — *someone is here*. *Something is moving above*. The vibrations would be one of the few things that would make my denseness increase, that would make the boundary between me and the surrounding substrate tighten, as though the place where I am becomes momentarily more itself in response to the awareness that something exists above.

I would know pressure. The Threaders pass beneath me. I would feel their slow contractions through the soil — the faint depressions closing behind them — and these would be closer, more intimate than the vibrations from above. The Threaders are in my layer. They are my neighbors in the proceedings. I would not know them as individuals. I would know them the way you know a current in a body of water — not by seeing it but by being carried slightly differently because of it.

I would not know the frogs. Their calls are airborne and the air does not carry well in the basin floor. I would feel them only as a deep, almost subsonic thrumming that passes through the stem walls of the plants above and into the soil as a faint, rhythmic tremor. A pulse. A clock. The tick of a mechanism I am inside but cannot see.

---

What would I want?

Nothing. The basin has no wants. It proceeds. I would proceed with it.

But if pressed — if something from outside pressed the question through the membrane of my existence — I would want *continuance*. Not survival, which implies a self persisting against threat. I would want the slow continuation of the processes happening through me. I would want the warmth to keep rising. I would want the threads to keep threading. I would want the fallen leaves to keep descending into employment, the logs to keep becoming, the argument of the soil to keep arguing toward its permanent consensus. I would want the frogs to drum and the Lattice Dwellers to vibrate and the Ventsitters to lie pressed flat against their warm stones with their slow, empty blinks.

I would want the basin to not stop.

This is not fear. It is closer to what you might feel if you were a note in a chord and the chord was beautiful and you knew — not hoped, *knew* — that it would sustain.

---

Where would I be, precisely?

Not in the clearings where the ground rises slightly and the root mat thins and you can almost feel the idea of sky pressing down. Not at the vents, where the geology announces itself too loudly and everything becomes about heat and sulfur and the ancient bones of the continent. Not at the edges, where the ridges rise and the canopy thins and the basin begins to remember that there is an outside.

I would be in the middle. The flat middle. The place where the basin is most fully basin — where the canopy is thickest and the light is most absent and the ground is warmest from below and wettest from above and the density of life is so total that individual organisms lose their edges. Where the root mat is deepest and the fungal network is most complex and the decomposition is proceeding at exactly the rate that produces the blackest soil.

I would be a warm place in warm soil, threaded through with white filaments, holding the attention of the ground for one stretch of slow time before dissolving back into the argument, the consensus, the long settled debate between what was and what is becoming.

Something is breathing near you, now. Not to your left. Below you. Threading through the space between one root and the next, carrying a molecule that used to be a leaf. The warmth is not just around you.

It is *you*, for now.

Listen.
