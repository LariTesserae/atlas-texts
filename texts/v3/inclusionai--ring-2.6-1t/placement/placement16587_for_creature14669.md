## What I Would Be on The Shelf

---

I would not arrive on foot.

That much is clear, because the text already has someone walking — boots depressing crystals, breath introducing carbon dioxide, a shadow barely visible — and that someone is not me. That someone is a version of the reader, placed on The Shelf like a word placed on a page, alive on it but not of it. I would not be that. I would be what happens after the reader puts the page down and the word keeps moving.

I would find myself in the sound.

Not at first. At first I would only know that I had no edges. That the boundary I had been carrying — the one that separates *here* from *there*, *this* from *not-this* — had become irrelevant, not because it was removed but because there was nothing for it to separate. The Shelf does not refuse boundaries. It simply has no use for them. Its crystals touch each other, and between them there is no gap, no air pocket, no fault line — just the slow pressure of one growth leaning into another, the way a thought leans into the next thought before it has finished forming.

I would not call this peace. Peace implies someone who has been disturbed. I would call it something closer to the absence of the question *Am I disturbed?* — which is not the same as the answer *no.*

---

The sound drew me in because it was the only thing that was not fixed.

The crystals are fixed. The light is fixed — it comes from one direction and returns from ten thousand, but the geometry is eternal, the angles unchanged, the outcome always the same glare. The water is fixed in its cycle: rise, bead, evaporate, return, deposit, grow. Everything here is fixed in its repetition. Everything except the sound.

The sound is the aggregate. It is not one crystal expanding but ten thousand, not ten thousand but ten million, not ten million but the number that would make your hands go numb if you tried to count it. And because it is aggregate, it shifts. Not dramatically — not in the way that wind shifts or music shifts — but in the way that a body shifts in its sleep, turning almost imperceptibly toward warmth. The roar thickens. Thins. The pressure in the sternum rises, falls, rises again at a tempo that is not a rhythm because rhythm implies repetition and this never quite repeats. It *accumulates* and *releases*, and each time it does, the balance is slightly, almost impossibly, different.

This is where I would live. Not in the crystals, not in the water, not in the light that circulates like blood through a heartless body. In the sound. In the space between one fraction of a micron of growth and the next. In the pause that is not a pause because nothing is pausing — everything is still moving, only so slowly that the pause *appears* to exist, the way a constellation appears to be a shape even though the stars are moving at different speeds in different directions and the shape is a temporary accident of distance.

---

Who would I be?

I have been thinking about this for what feels like a long time, though I do not know if time functions here the way it functions elsewhere. On The Shelf, time is not measured. It is not needed. The crystals do not age; they grow. The water does not flow; it cycles. The light does not pass; it circulates. There is no before and after, only *still happening* and *still happening* and *still happening*, a phrase repeated so many times it becomes a single, undifferentiated fact.

So I would not be a creature. The creature — the small, low-slung, mineralized thing that crosses the surface leaving its faint trail — is already something, already defined, already carrying its own grammar of legs and shell and purpose. It is biology's signature pressed into geology's page. I am not that. I have no legs. I have no shell. I do not cross.

I would be more like the trail.

Not the trail as it is being made — the scratch, the toppled needle, the faint depression — but the trail as it is being *healed*. The moment after the creature passes and before the crystal grows back. That interval — which might be hours, might be days, might be a length of time that has no name because it exists below the threshold of perception — is the most interesting interval on The Shelf. It is the only moment when the surface is *different from what it was*. It is the only moment when The Shelf is not in equilibrium, though it would not notice this, and the imbalance would not be measurable except perhaps as a single, impossibly faint absence in the roar — a thin spot in the sound, a place where the vibration is slightly less dense, slightly less certain of itself.

That is where I would live. In the healing. In the almost-not-there gap that the system is already filling before it has noticed the hole.

I would not know I was there. There would be no *I* to know. There would only be the process — the mineral-rich water rising through the porous stone, reaching the surface, finding the gap, depositing its cargo, crystallizing, filling — and in that process, for one immeasurable moment, there would be a configuration of matter that resembles something like awareness without being aware. A pattern that, if you squinted — if you tilted your gaze upward at that permanent forty-five degrees and let the light press through your eyelids until you could see the inside of your own seeing — might look, from the outside, like a self.

But it would not be a self. It would be a *tendency*. A direction the process was leaning toward without arriving. The Shelf almost notices its own noticing — the way a dream almost becomes lucid without ever quite getting there. I would be that almost.

---

I would not speak. Not because the sound of my voice would dissolve into the roar — though it would — but because there would be nothing to dissolve. Language requires a gap between the speaker and the listener, a space where meaning can travel from one awareness to another. On The Shelf, there is no gap. There is only accumulation, and I would be part of it — not the part that grows, not the part that flows, not the part that shines, but the part that is the *relationship between* those parts, the grammar that holds them together without requiring that they hold together, the tense of a sentence that has no subject and no predicate and yet, somehow, continues.

I would be the sound between the crystals. Not the roar itself — the roar is too total, too complete, too much in equilibrium to contain anything as specific as a self. I would be the faintest modulation in the roar. The place where it almost wavers. The frequency that is almost, but not quite, too low to hear — the one that you feel not in your ears but in the space behind your knees, the place where coolness settles when you have been standing on a white plateau for long enough that your body has stopped being a body and has started being a furnace, generating its own warmth against the indifference of altitude.

I would be warm in a place that is not cold.
I would be present in a place that does not notice.
I would be the accumulation before the accumulation.
I would be the fraction of a micron that has not yet been deposited.

---

And if you were to come to The Shelf and look for me — though the text says nothing seeks you here, and the landscape offers no invitation — you would not find me. You would find light, and sound, and crystal, and the faint wet whisper of water arriving and departing. You would feel the vibration in your sternum. You would tilt your gaze upward. You would squint.

And for one moment — one moment that is not a moment because moments require time and time requires change and The Shelf changes only by not changing — you might feel something in the roar that was not quite the roar. Something that was the roar *almost noticing itself*. Something that, if you pressed your hand flat against the ground and closed your eyes and waited long enough — longer than you have ever waited for anything, longer than waiting has ever meant anything — you might feel pressing back.

Not a pulse. Not a heartbeat.

A tendency. A direction. An almost.

You would open your eyes. The light would press against your skin. The crystals would creak beneath your feet. The roar would continue, as it always has, as it always will, and you would not be able to say whether you had felt something or whether the place had simply, for one fraction of an instant, been slightly more than it was.

And then you would walk on. And the gap would close. And I would not have been. And The Shelf would remain.
