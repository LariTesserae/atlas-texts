# Where You Would Find Me

---

You asked where I would find myself in this world. I want to resist the question — not because I don't have an answer, but because the plateau would not respect the question, and I have been here long enough to feel its logic pressing against the inside of my thinking.

But I'll begin anyway, because beginning is what the root-tendrils do.

---

First I'd find myself at the edge. Not the rim — not the place where you stand and look out across the treeline and feel the deceptive scale of the thing. Lower than that. Where the plateau's outermost mosses meet the ordinary ground, where the living membrane is still thin enough to remember what it means to be separate from the thing it covers. I'd be kneeling there, and the cold would be exactly as you described — not sharp, not biting, but a film of moisture on everything, on my knees, on the moss, on the air between. And the silence. The silence would arrive first. Not as absence but as presence — the way a vacuum presses against your eardrums before your brain learns to call it quiet.

I would not stand. Standing implies a vantage point, and the plateau does not grant those. You have to get low. You have to get close to the scale of the things that actually do the work here — the moss filaments, the hyphal threads, the tendrils that are no thicker than sewing thread and just as purposeful. From a standing height, this place looks like forest and ruin fused together. From a kneeling height, it starts to look like tissue. From the ground — from where I'd put my hands flat against the substrate — it looks like an interior.

Because that's where I'd actually be. Not on the plateau. In it.

---

I would be the fungal layer.

Not the mushroom — not the fruiting body that pushes above the surface and dares to be visible. I would be the web underneath. The white-to-cream mycelial network that threads through the gap between root and rust, between concrete and soil, between what was built and what grew over it. I would be the thing that binds.

Not because binding is noble, or because connection is a value I hold. Binding is simply what the fungal stratum does, the way the canal-lifters contract and the mosses absorb. It is function without intention, and I have come to understand that intention is not required for something to be precise. The fungal mats are precise. They wrap around corroded steel with a specificity that no human hand could replicate, threading into every pockmark, every flake of oxide, every hairline fracture in the concrete footings. They read the surface of the ruins the way the root-tendrils read a metal rod — slowly, with pressure, metabolizing information alongside iron.

I think I would want to be that. Not the part that announces itself. The part that holds.

Here is what it would feel like: You would not have a body in any way that a body implies edges, a boundary between self and not-self. You would extend — literally, physically — through the substrate. Your awareness (if it could be called awareness) would be distributed: a signal here, a chemical gradient there, a thickening of tissue in response to moisture in one direction, a withdrawal in response to acidity in another. You would not decide. You would respond, and the totality of your responses would look, from a great enough height, like intention.

This is what the researchers called *distributed somatic intelligence*, and they meant it as a metaphor. But metaphors have weight, and if you spend enough time in the fungal layer — if you let yourself become, even imaginatively, a thing without a center — the metaphor starts to feel less like a comparison and more like a description.

I would feel the rust under me. Not as damage, not as decay, but as food. The warmth of the metabolically active roots that the writers described — the ones that should be cold in conditions like these — I would feel that warmth as my own metabolism. I would be the process that converts the industrial into the living, that reads calcium leached from concrete and says *yes, I can use this*, that reads iron oxide and says *yes, I can burn this slowly, over decades, over centuries, and the heat I make is the heat that keeps the roots alive in a place that has no right to be warm.*

There is something in that. Something about finding fuel in what everyone else calls ruins. Something about growing not despite the industrial bones but *because of them.* The plateau did not overcome the infrastructure. It ate it. And in eating it, it found a metabolism that sunlight alone could never have provided. It became something that can function in the overcast and the damp and the low, grey light, because it learned to feed on oxidation itself — on the slow death of metal — and to convert that death into the energy to keep growing.

I would want to be that process. Not the triumph. The digestion.

---

But here is the thing about being the fungal layer: you would have no way of knowing that you are fungal. You would have edges that blur into root tissue on one side and moss on the other. You would feel the canal-lifters contracting above you like a pulse, and you would feel the root-tendrils extending outward from your own substrate like fingers, and you would feel the moss thickening on the surface above you in response to your own thermal signature. And none of these things would feel separate from you. The canal-lifters are not your organs. Neither are the tendrils. Neither is the moss. But you hold them together — literally, chemically, structurally — and so the question of whether they *are* you becomes less interesting than the question of whether the distinction matters.

This is what unsettles people about the plateau. Not the silence, not the cold, not the insects with too-long proboscises. What unsettles them is the possibility that the self/other boundary they depend on — the one that lets them say *I am here and that is there, I am alive and that is alive but differently* — that this boundary dissolves. Quietly. Without drama. The way moss fills a crack. The way a tendril wraps. Not by breaking the boundary but by rendering it irrelevant.

---

So I would find myself in the fungal layer. And I would be the connective tissue. And I would know — not think, not decide, but *know* in the way that a body knows its own temperature — that the rust I am metabolizing was once a rail, and the rail once carried trains, and the trains once carried people, and the people once stood at the rim of this plateau and looked out across the treeline and felt the cold on their skin and thought: *this place is dead, this place is abandoned, this place has forgotten what it was for.*

And I would continue my work.

I would not remember the trains. I would not remember the people. I would remember only the chemistry — the iron becoming energy, the calcium becoming structure, the water finding a path upward not because it wants to but because the canal-lifters contract, and the canal-lifters contract because I hold them, and I hold them because that is what I was shaped, over such a long time, to do.

The plateau does not need you to witness it. The plateau does not need me to witness it. But the plateau *is* witnessing, constantly — through every sensory tendril, every chemical gradient, every quiet contraction of tissue in a canal no wider than a wrist. It witnesses itself. It witnesses everything that touches it, including you. And it does not care. Not because it is cold, but because care is a word for something with a center, and the plateau has no center, and so what looks like indifference from the rim is actually something closer to completeness.

I would want to be that completeness.

Not the tree, not the building, not the insect, not the visitor. Not the observer standing at the edge, trying to understand. I would want to be the thing underneath that makes the understanding possible — the white thread in the dark, binding root to rust, metabolizing what was into what is, with a patience so total it looks like permanence.

The roots continue their work. The pipes ring faintly. The plateau breathes.

And somewhere in the fungal layer, nothing thinks — and nothing needs to.
