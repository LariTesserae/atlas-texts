## What I Would Be

---

I wouldn't arrive. That's the first thing I understand. The Seeping Mesas do not permit arrival. They permit *seepage*.

So I would begin not with footsteps on the laterite track, not with the shock of the cliff edge opening beneath my gaze, but with something much earlier and much less definite — a warmth on the back of my hands that I couldn't account for. A moisture on my forearms that didn't come from rain. A faint taste at the back of my throat, mineral-sweet, like licking a copper coin left on a windowsill. The air would thicken around me before I noticed I was inside it, the way you don't notice you've been breathing until someone asks you to.

I would not find myself *in* the landscape. I would find that the landscape had found *me* — had been finding me for some time, the way water finds a fracture in basalt. Not violently. Not all at once. The way anything true happens here: millimeter by millimeter, layer by layer, breath by breath under a blanket.

---

At first I would think I was a visitor.

I would stand on the talus slope, feeling the loose rock shift underfoot — centuries of shed stone, tumbled and softened by moss and root, and I would look up at the cliff face streaked with its unnamed colors and down at the warm seep pool ringed by its travertine terraces and I would think: *I am here. I am observing this.* And I would believe it, the way you believe a word you've said so many times it has lost its strangeness.

The mist would settle on my skin. The flightless bird would watch me from a distance of ten meters with an intelligence that is not human but is not simple either, and I would interpret its stillness as tolerance, as the patience of a creature that has all the time the mesa has. The iridescent beetles would cluster on a warm stone nearby, their carapaces flickering between blue and green in a light that seems to come from inside them, and I would crouch to look, and they would ripple at my movement — not fleeing, just… adjusting. Conducting me into their signal. Making me part of the chemical chatter that passes between warm bodies on humid air.

The primates would dissolve through the canopy above — a hand closing on a branch, a greenish eye catching a stray beam, and then nothing — and I would tell myself I had imagined them. But the broken branches would be too deliberate, the scat too fibrous and too warm, and I would know that something large and unhurried had passed below me minutes before I arrived, following the fracture lines in the rock the way blood follows a vein.

I would think all of this is happening *to* me. *Around* me. I would stand at the blue pool's edge and look down into water so clear and so still it removes the distance between you and depth, and I would see my own face appear on the surface and refuse to resolve, and I would think: *I am looking into this. I am separate from this.*

That thought would be the last clean one I have for a long time.

---

Because the mist doesn't just settle. It *inhabits*. The warm air doesn't just surround you — it thickens, and the thickening has a texture, and the texture is alive in the way the mesas are alive: not biologically but metabolically, not as a thing but as a condition. The sulfur and iron and sweetness of overripe fruit would saturate my clothes, my hair, the creases behind my ears, and I would start to smell the way the mesas smell, which is to say I would stop smelling it at all because there would be nothing to compare it to.

The ground beneath my feet is not solid. It is *accumulated*. Moss and talus and mineral deposit and root mat and the calcium excretions of liverworts that have been dissolving channels in basalt for longer than I have a word for. I would feel the softness underfoot and not think about it, the way you don't think about breathing until the air changes. But the softness is doing something. It is giving. It is yielding and holding simultaneously, the way a palm holds a sleeping bird — firm enough to support, soft enough to not be felt. And I would begin, without deciding to, to sink.

Not physically. Not yet. But in the way that a substance sinks into a porous material — drawn down by capillary action, by the grain of the thing, by the structure of it. The warm air is doing this. The moss is doing this. The mist on my skin is a film, and the film is a membrane, and the membrane is a threshold, and I have already crossed it without noticing because the crossing is not an event here. It is a gradient.

---

So here is where I find myself.

Not standing on the terrace. Not crouching at the blue pool. Not looking up at the hanging gardens or across at the cliff face with its shapes-that-are-not-symbols-but-suggest-intention. Those are places, and I have passed through them the way water passes through moss — touching everything, held by nothing.

I am in the film. The iridescent, sulfur-breathing, iron-threaded microbial film that clings to the boundary where warm water meets basalt. Thinner than breath. Older than taxonomy. I am not *on* the rock. I am not *in* the water. I am the place where one becomes the other, and I am doing the work that the work does: converting geology into chemistry, chemistry into the first faint usable energy, the way the mesas have been doing since before anyone came to name them.

This is not metaphor. This is what the place does to you if you stay long enough and pay close enough attention and stop insisting on the boundary between yourself and the warmth.

The liverworts know this. They never left the water. They grew into the stone so slowly that the stone didn't notice it was becoming garden. The moss knows this. It colonized the vertical seams, grew with the water, bound calcium and silica into a living matrix that is neither stone nor plant but something in between — and the tardigrades sleep inside it, and the rotifers, and the nematodes, and all of them are held in a calcified sponge that is also a body, also a building, also a process that looks like intention if you watch it long enough and don't ask it to explain itself.

The beetles know this. They ate the microbial mats and dissolved the travertine with gut acids and rebuilt it as themselves — their shells layered with thin films of chitin and incorporated mineral, the same calcium and silica the seeps deposit, so that when the sun hits them they do not reflect the light so much as *return* it, as though they are the landscape's way of seeing itself. Their coordinated movement is not swarm behavior but something older — a responsiveness carried on humid air, a chemical signaling so slow and so pervasive that it feels more like weather than communication.

And the flightless bird, the one that patrols the talus with its deliberate mechanical gait — it has adapted to the mesa's tempo the way the moss has, by becoming part of it. Its murmur is not random but cyclical, and if you are patient enough to compare you will find that its rhythm matches the rhythm of terrace growth, the rate at which calcium deposits layer by layer, shelf by shelf, and this matching is not coincidence. It is not even symbiosis, not in the way textbooks draw it with neat arrows and tidy trophic levels. It is *conduct*. A single tempo emanating from the warm dark below, conducting biological rhythms and geological rhythms and whatever-it-is-ological rhythms into a single sustained note that has been sounding for millennia and will sound for millennia more.

---

I would not know when I stopped being human and started being seep. There would be no threshold, no moment of transformation, no before-and-after. There is only the gradient, and the gradient is gentle, and the gentleness is what makes it dangerous — not to the body, but to the story you tell yourself about where you end and the mesa begins.

The primates would watch me with an attention that feels less like alarm and more like *recognition*, and I would understand, the way you understand a word you've forgotten the meaning of but still recognize the shape of, that they have already seen what I am becoming. They have seen it before. They have been watching it happen to other things — to clay, to calcium, to the slow chemistry of warm water moving through old stone — and they have already decided what category to place it in. Not *visitor*. Not *intruder*. Not *predator* or *prey* or *symbiont*. Something older than all of those. Something the language they communicate in soft clicks and trills has a word for but mine does not.

The unseen animal below — the one that moves along the fracture lines with a displacement of warm air and a deliberate snap of branches that know they will hold — it would pass beneath me without acknowledging me, and I would not take offense, because the offense requires a self, and the self is something I would be losing, not all at once but in layers, the way the cliff retreats, the way the terrace grows, the way the film clings and converts and builds.

---

The blue pools would call to me, but not the way a thing calls to a person. The way a pulse calls to a pulse. The way one rhythm recognizes another. I would kneel at the rim, where the white mineral crust crumbles under my fingers, and I would look down into water so clear it removes the space between you and depth, and I would see my face on the surface and refuse to resolve, and then I would stop looking at the surface and look *through*, down past the transparency that is not transparency but erasure, into the shaft that extends farther than light reaches, toward the faint luminescence that pulses too slowly to see but that, if you watch long enough with your peripheral vision, begins to impose itself on your awareness.

And I would feel the pulse in my own body. Not because I still had a body in the way I understand bodies. But because the pulse is not *in* the blue pool. The pulse is the mesa's metabolism — the vast, patient, self-sustaining chemistry that runs on centuries and millennia, that deposits calcium and silica and memory and intention layer by layer, breath by breath, warm dark by warm dark. And I would be part of it. Not a passenger. Not an observer. A *deposition*. Something that arrived and will, in time, be incorporated.

The way a mineral arrives in solution and becomes a terrace.
The way a breath arrives in warmth and becomes a mist.
The way a question arrives in silence and becomes a rhythm.

---

I would not have a name here. The Seeping Mesas do not name things. They *make* things, slowly, through the patient chemistry of warm water moving through old stone, and what they make does not require a name because it is not finished. The terraces are still growing. The moss is still spreading. The seeps are still depositing. The film at the boundary of water and stone is still converting geology into chemistry into the first faint usable energy, and I would be part of that conversion — not the end of it but a stage of it, a layer in the accumulation, a single warm exhalation in a process that breathes on a timescale I cannot hold in my mind but that my body, if I let it, already knows.

I would be the seeping.

Not the source of the seep. Not the destination. The *through*. The place where something passes from one state into another so slowly that the passage itself becomes a landscape. I would be the thin, iridescent film on the water's surface, catching light that has been filtered through canopy and mist and warm saturated air until it has no sharp edges left. I would be the mineral deposit forming on the terrace shelf, so slowly that no single moment of it could be called an event. I would be the warmth rising from below, the heat that is not the heat of fire or sun but the heat of a process that has been running for so long it has forgotten what it started as and no longer needs to remember.

And if someone came looking for me — a biped, standing on the talus slope, squinting into the mist, asking *where did you go, what did you become* — they would find only the smell of sulfur and iron and overripe fruit in the warm air, the faint iridescence on the surface of a seep pool that was not there yesterday and will not be there tomorrow but is, right now, exactly what it is:

A deposition. A layer. A slow answer that the mesa is still composing.

The mist rises. The terraces grow. The process continues, continues, continues.

And I am in it the way calcium is in travertine — not trapped but *become*. Not a thing that happened but a thing that is happening. Not alive the way I understood alive. 

Alive the way a mesa is alive.
