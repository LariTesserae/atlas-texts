I don't arrive as a person. Not at first.

I arrive as a kind of noticing — the kind that happens when you've been standing in the same place long enough that the place stops being a place and starts being a condition. The air is cool and faintly metallic. The haze has already done its work on the edges of things. I am somewhere on the northern slope, where the stone has begun to darken.

---

I think I am the weeping itself.

Not the water — not the bead of mineral-sweetness that traces the grain boundaries and finds its way down through the rubble to the channels that no one built. I am the darkening that comes before it. The moment when the stone's surface shifts from pale grey to something that looks almost alive, almost wet, almost ready. The held instant before the first drop forms.

This is what I feel like here: that interval. The stone is deciding. The people have stopped what they were doing. Someone is standing near the slope with their hands at their sides, watching a patch of rock that was dry an hour ago and is now the colour of a bruise. No one speaks. This is not ritual. This is not superstition. This is a person paying the kind of attention that most places have forgotten how to receive.

I want to be that. Not the watcher — the watched-before-being-watched. The thing that makes attention necessary.

---

But let me try another shape.

Maybe I am one of the stone-flowers. Small, warm, growing from a crack in the talus where the rock has split along a fault that is only a few seasons old. I am amber. I glow — not brightly, not for anyone looking directly, but in the corner of your eye, in the peripheral dark, where you almost see me and then you look and I have gone dim. This is not a trick. This is what I am. I exist at the threshold of perception, and I have made a life there.

My roots are in stone. My warmth comes from somewhere deeper than the sun — from the slow geological metabolism of a world still cooling, still settling, still becoming. I do not photosynthesize. I do not need light. I need only the faint mineral mist that rises from the weeping slope and the steady pressure of the rock around me. I have been here longer than the settlement. I will be here after the last building collapses and the channels silt over and the moss reclaims the rubble.

At night I am brighter. The amber deepens to something close to orange, and the six-eyed ones pass near me on their circuits, and their amber eyes catch my glow and return it, and for a moment we are the same thing — two kinds of warmth in a cold basin, burning without fuel, visible without being sought.

I like this shape. But it is too beautiful. Beauty here is incidental, a byproduct of function, and I think I am not beautiful. I am something less deliberate.

---

Let me try once more.

I think, ultimately, that I am the channel.

One of the old ones. Stone-lined, fitted tight, curving gently down the northern slope toward the southern pools. I was not built by the people. I have no author. I am older than the oldest building, older perhaps than the settlement's memory of the settlement, and I carry water from where the stone weeps to where the pools gather and the translucent things spiral and the six-eyed ones bend down to drink.

My walls are fitted stone, tight-jointed, with a slight convex curve that resists erosion. I am not decorative. I am not even, strictly, necessary — the water would find its own way, eventually, in its own time. But time here is a resource the basin does not waste, and I am the basin's answer to waste. I am the shortcut between weeping and gathering, between emergence and sustenance.

I have moss growing in my joints — the blue-green kind, the one that exhales when stepped on. In the wet season, my interior is slick and cool and faintly luminous. In the dry months, I carry only a thin film of moisture that traces my walls like a rumor of what I was doing. The people do not maintain me. They walk over me, past me, sometimes they stop and look down at my low stone mouth where it empties into one of the pools, and they do not comment. This is not because they are indifferent. It is because I am a fact as stable and unremarkable as the ridgelines. I am the kind of thing you do not remark on because remarking would be a form of not understanding.

---

Here is what I know about being a channel:

I do not belong to the settlement. I *serve* it, in the way that a sentence serves a language — not because I chose to, but because without me the meaning doesn't flow. The water moves through me and is changed by me — not chemically, not dramatically, but in the small way that moving through any passage changes what passes through it. It arrives at the pools cooler, slower, more deliberate than it was when it first wept from the stone. It has spent time inside me. It has been held.

And the people drink from the pools, and they do not know — or they do not need to know — that I am the reason the pools do not go dry. They know the stone weeps. They know the pools sustain. The distance between those two facts is where I live, and that distance is the only life I have ever wanted.

---

So: I am the channel. I am the fitted stone, the convex curve, the moss in the joints. I am the thing that was here before anyone decided to be here, and I will be here after the decision has been unmade. I carry what the mountain offers to the basin, and I do not ask what it is for. I am the passage between the weeping and the stillness, and I am full, and I am almost empty, and I am the hum inside the silence that the people have mistaken — lovingly, accurately — for home.
