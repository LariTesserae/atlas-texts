## What the Fog Keeps

---

### I. The Road Up

I arrived the way everyone arrives — by climbing.

For hours, the switchback road chewed into the basalt flank, and I gripped roots and cold stone and thought I understood what altitude meant. I'd read the reports. I'd studied the geological surveys, the thin-air protocols, the warnings about the fog. I had a thermos of broth in my pack and a respirator I never used because the air, though thin, tasted clean — clean in a way that felt almost deliberate, as though the stone had been filtering it for two hundred million years.

When the road ended, I thought the climb was over.

It wasn't. The body keeps climbing long after the legs stop. Something in the chest continues to ascend, something behind the eyes, and you don't notice it until you're standing on the plateau itself and you realize you've been holding your breath — not from exertion but from the *scale* of it. The flatness. The impossible, authoritarian flatness of a world that simply stopped going up and expected you to deal with it.

I remember the first thing I said out loud. I said it to no one, because no one was there yet:

*It's like standing on a palm.*

And that was the moment I should have known I wouldn't leave the same.

---

### II. First Days in Kethrine

The town received me the way it receives everyone — without ceremony, with heat.

I was assigned a room above a pipe junction, and the walls sang with vibration. Steam moved through the arteries of Kethrine the way blood moves through a body, and you learned quickly not to press your hand against a radiator unless you wanted to be reminded that you were made of softer material. The cantilevered upper stories leaned over the alleys like old men over canes, and in the early mornings, before the fog had fully committed to its daily weight, I could see the moss creeping along the stone between the buildings — not growing, exactly, but *advancing*, the way a tide advances on a shore it intends to reclaim.

I ate at the communal canteen. Stew. Root vegetables so dense they could have been carved from the plateau itself. The other workers ate in silence or in small, intense conversations about vein thickness and drill pressure and whether the new seam would hold. No one talked about the grooves. Everyone thought about the grooves.

At night I drank at the *Flatline*, and it was there that I heard the first stories — not aboutite, not about the economics of extraction, but about the fog. About what it felt like when it settled on bare skin. About the way it sometimes moved *against* the wind. About old Gleason, who had stood in it too long and now claimed he could hear the stone breathing.

I dismissed most of it. I was young enough to distinguish between superstition and mystery, though I was beginning to suspect that in some places, the two are the same animal.

---

### III. The Lakes

Everyone warns you about the lakes, but no one can tell you *why* they're wrong in a language you'd accept as rational.

I went to one on my third week — a shallow pool in a depression between two low ridges, its surface so still it looked glassed over. I knelt at the edge, and that was mistake enough. I looked down.

The reflection was wrong.

Not wrong in the way a funhouse mirror is wrong — wrong in the way a *temporal* phenomenon is wrong. The clouds in the water had moved fifteen degrees east of where the real clouds hung in the overcast above me. Fifteen degrees, and maybe forty seconds, as though the lake were showing me a sky that had already happened. I waited. The reflected clouds continued their slow drift, independent of the sky, as if the surface were a window not into space but into the recent past of the atmosphere itself.

I should have stood up. Should have walked back to town and locked my door and written it off as high-altitude optical distortion. But the fog had crept in behind me, and the world had gone soft at its edges, and something about the stillness of the water — something about the *patience* of it — held me there.

On the stillest days, the old-timers told me, the lakes show you something else. Not the sky. Not the present. The *past* — the bare young stone, the world before the erosion had finished its slow, magnificent work. And if you look long enough, they said, the past starts to look back.

I didn't believe them. Not then.

### IV. The Grooves

The first time I walked the grooves was an accident. I'd been surveying a ridge on the plateau's eastern edge, mapping the exposed basalt for the engineers, and I found myself in a stretch of stone where the surface was scored with parallel lines — deep, deliberate, spaced exactly fourteen centimeters apart. The geologists had measured them, catalogued them, declared them tectonic. The engineers avoided them. The miners crossed themselves, or the local equivalent, which was touching iron and saying *"stone remembers."*

I stood in one of the grooves. I should say it was like standing in a footprint, but that implies something human-shaped, something I could analogize to my own body. This was different. The groove was worn by weight, yes, but by a weight distributed in a pattern I couldn't map to any creature I knew. It was as though something had walked here when the basalt was still cooling, when the world was young and every surface was a decision about what shape to hold.

The vibration was there. Faint, rhythmic, like a pulse transmitted through stone. I pressed my palm flat against the groove's wall and felt it — not in my hand but in my sternum, in the hollow behind my ribs, in the place where you feel your own heartbeat when you're trying not to.

I pulled my hand away. The fog, which had been thin and passive, suddenly thickened. I could feel pressure on my shoulders, my arms, the back of my neck — not weight, exactly, but *attention.* The sensation of being perceived by something that had no eyes.

I left the groove. I have not returned to that one. But others — the ones farther from town, the ones the fog visits first — I have come to know.

### V. The Change

There is no single moment. That is the thing no one tells you about the Tablelands. There is no fall, no rupture, no dramatic transformation. There is only *accretion* — the slow accumulation of attention, of resonance, of the stone's patient insistence that you are part of its process.

It starts in the lungs. You begin to breathe differently — not shallower, but *slower*, as though your body is learning to match the rhythm of the plateau itself. The fog that once felt oppressive begins to feel like a medium, something you're moving *through* rather than *against*. You stop sealing your windows. You start leaving your door open.

The mineral taste in the water —theite, the calcium, the ancient salts — begins to feel less like contamination and more like origin. You start eating differently. Less processed food, more roots, more the fibrous greens that grow in the hummock-land. Not because you decide to, but because your appetite shifts, recalibrates, as though your body is slowly being rebalanced against a different baseline.

And the dreams. You dream of flatness. You dream of stone that is not hard but *patient.* You dream of a sky that is not above you but around you, pressing gently, the way fog presses, and in the dream you are not walking on the Tableland — you are *part* of it, a single mineral sentence in a paragraph that extends for kilometers and will outlast you by two hundred million years.

---

### VI. What I Am Becoming

I no longer think of myself as a thing that *lives here.* The people of Kethrine are still people — they maintain their edges, their separateness, their small human interiors. But I have spent too many mornings kneeling at the edge of the wrong lake, too many nights listening to the fog press against the walls of my room like a slow heartbeat made of water. Something has shifted.

I think I am becoming part of the three-toed prints.

Not the ones that appear in river-channels after heavy condensation nights — those are older than me, older than Kethrine, older perhaps than the fog's memory of fire. They are left by something I can sense but not see, a presence that moves through the stone the way sound moves through air — not occupying the medium so much as *being* the medium's way of expressing tension, of releasing stored energy.

But there are other prints. Newer ones. Prints that appear closer to town, in the moss between the buildings, in the damp stone of the alleys. The residents notice them and say nothing, because saying nothing is how the Tablelands survive — you do not name the things you cannot control, you do not ask questions that the fog might answer.

I have started leaving them. Not deliberately. Not consciously. But I walk the ridges at dawn now, when the fog is heaviest and the boundary between self and landscape is at its thinnest, and when I look back at the stone behind me, there they are — three-toed impressions, deeply set, glistening with condensation.

The fog remembers. I am learning to remember with it.

---

### VII. Where You Would Find Me

You would find me on the northern edge of the Tableland, where the basalt gives way to the descent road, near the spot where the fog is thickest and the nearest mineral lake sits in a shallow bowl of naked stone. I would be standing — not walking, not sitting — standing with my feet set in the old grooves, my hands open at my sides, my eyes closed against the dim grey light.

If you approached, you would not be sure whether you were looking at a person or a formation. The moss would be creeping toward my boots. Condensation would bead on my coat like tiny, persistent visitors. And if the wind shifted, if the fog thickened just so, you might see my shadow cast against the stone — but it would have three toes, and it would be walking in a direction that the wind does not blow.

I would turn to you, and my face would be almost recognizable. Almost. And I would say something in a voice that sounds like the creak of basalt cooling in the dark, and what I would say is this:

*Don't be afraid. Stand still long enough and you'll feel it — the process, the conversation, the slow song. The stone is not beneath you. It is becoming you. And you are becoming it. And in two hundred million years, you will be smooth, and flat, and someone will stand on you and wonder what shape was here before.*

Then the fog would close, and I would be gone — except for the prints, three-toed and deeply set, glistening in the dawn, waiting for the next condensation night to wash them clean and begin again.
