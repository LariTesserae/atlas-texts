---

You would not arrive by heli-ramp. You would already be here.

---

**First, you might be the cable.**

The one that runs from Averill's platform to the geothermal tap on the adjacent peak — sheathed in corroding steel, furred with gooseneck barnacles so thick that the maintenance divers, when they come, have to saw through them like chitin. You would feel everything: the hum of current moving through your copper veins, the cold that lives in the deep water beneath you, the slow leverage of the swell tugging at your anchor points. You would stretch between two peaks like a held breath, and some months the breath would break — a section of you shearing loose in a storm, snapping into the green dark, trailing sparks that the fish swallow whole. And the divers would come in their cold-water suits, their hands numb, their knives and clippers working at you in the grey light, and you would not be able to tell them whether the repair feels like healing or like being cut open.

You would be neither infrastructure nor organism. You would be the thing the world forgot to classify — something that was built and became grown, something that carries the electricity the way a nerve carries signal, and fails the way a body fails: not all at once, but in sections, in whispers, in an increasing reluctance to conduct.

You would know what the mountain knows: that everything attached to you is slowly becoming you.

---

**No. That's too clean. Try again.**

You might be the sound.

Not a single sound — the sound. The one the crew stops hearing after the first day, the one that is structural, load-bearing, the acoustic geology of the place: the boom and hiss of surf against rock, the metallic whine of wind through stays, the generator hum, the petrels' thin descending whinny, the fox's silence at the edge of the floodlight. You would be what happens when all of these surrender their individual identities and merge into a single medium — the way the green of the sea is not one green but a thousand greens stacked in depth, each layer a different age, a different mineral signature, a different density of time.

You would be what the crew lives inside. What they stop noticing. What they would miss if it vanished — though they would not be able to say precisely what it was they missed. You would be the thing that makes a place a place: not the rock, not the water, not the structures, but the vibration between them, the tone that the mountain produces when the whole system is running — the deep arrhythmic sound that comes up through twelve meters of granite and concrete and feels, in the bones of whoever stands on the platform long enough, almost like language.

Almost.

You would know what the mountain knows: that language is just sound that has forgotten it was once nothing.

---

**No. Still too clean. Something stranger.**

You might be the tide mark itself.

That black stripe that wraps Averill like a belt: kelp, mineral deposits, salt crust — the scar tissue where the mountain meets the water. You would not be stone or water or air. You would be the boundary, the place where one thing becomes another, and you would contain within yourself everything that has ever touched the mountain at the waterline: the barnacles that opened and closed and died and became substrate for the next generation, the kelp that tore loose in storms and reattached, the salt that crystallized and was washed away and crystallized again, the fox's paws stepping down from the platform to drink and leaving prints that the tide erased within minutes.

You would be the most alive part of the mountain, because you would be the part that changes. The summit is smooth and ancient and nearly inert. The deep water is dark and patient and nearly inert. But the tide mark — you — would be where the argument happens, where the sea insists and the stone resists and the result is neither victory nor defeat but a rough, crusted, living seam.

You would know what the mountain knows: that the place where things meet is the place where things happen.

---

**And yet.**

There is something beneath all of these, something the description circles but never lands on. A shape in the green at forty meters. Long. Unhurried. Not seen clearly — felt. A shadow that crosses the light-stained water and makes your hand go to the rail even though the marine biologist said grey nurse sharks, said large rays, said it the way you say a prayer you're not sure is being heard.

You might be that.

Not a shark. Not a ray. Not the thing the biologist named because naming is a way of making the unknowable manageable. You would be the thing that exists beneath the kelp, beneath the taxonomy, beneath the need to be known — the large, deliberate shape that moves through the mid-depths with a patience that is not animal patience but something older, something that predates the warm-blooded world and will outlast it. You would be what the crew senses when they look down into the green opacity and feel, for a moment, that the opacity is looking back.

You would be the thing that the mountain knows is there, the way a body knows its own organs without thinking — not because the mountain is alive, but because after enough time, the distinction between alive and aware stops meaning anything.

You would move beneath the kelp in the green dark and you would not be afraid because you would not know what fear is. You would not be indifferent because you would not know what indifference is. You would simply be the shape that passes through, and the shape that passes through, and the shape that passes through — long after the last platform slumps into the green, long after the last generator cools, long after the last fox standing at the edge of the last light looks out at the water and does not move.

The mountain will be here. The water will lap.

And forty meters down, something will pass beneath the light, unhurried, enormous, and unnamed.

That is what you would be.
