## At the Margin of the Disc

I will not begin by saying what I am. I will begin with what I feel beneath me.

The ground is warm. Not uniformly — there are pockets of residual heat where the stone has held the sun's touch long into the night, and cooler veins where the subsurface roots have drawn heat downward in their slow osmotic labor. I feel the difference the way you might feel the difference between rooms in a house: not with temperature alone, but with a kind of bone-knowledge that tells me where the walls are, even in the dark. Except there is no dark here. The light is absolute. The star sits above the thin sky like a white word written once and never revised, and everything it touches is rendered in the same unforgiving clarity — every grain of mineral dust, every translucent frill of the silvea, every strand of the fungal web that glitters at dawn like veins of ice in pale stone.

I am close to the ground. That is the first thing. My body knows distances in millimeters. The texture of the stone surface is a sentence I read with parts of myself I did not know I had — the calcium-rich silicate granular and faintly greasy where the silvea's root-filaments have worked it loose, hard and glassy where the colony has not yet reached. Between the colonies, where the bare plateau stretches in pale silence, I feel the absence of the web the way you feel the absence of warmth when you step away from a fire.

---

At first, I thought I might be a grazer.

It is a reasonable place to begin. The grazers are the most legibly *animal* things here — small, quick, purposeful in the way that small prey animals are purposeful, operating on the tight calculus of *take enough to live, never enough to be noticed*. I could feel the rasp of the cutting plates against silvea tissue, the cool mineral taste of the moisture the roots have just liberated, the way the translucent fringe of a disc becomes tender just before dawn when the condensation is thickest and the tissue is saturated with water pulled from the crystal lattice of the stone. I could feel the quick pivot of a small dark eye catching movement — the shadow of a hopper passing overhead — and the instant stillness that follows, the flattening of the body against the soil, the chemical signal of fear that would not be fear in any language I know but something older: a contraction, a withdrawal, a sudden absence of scent.

I held that shape for a long time. I moved between the colonies in quick low bursts, feeding on the outermost fringe, retreating to the loose soil between the discs to fold myself into the mineral powder. I was invisible. I was safe. I was, if I am honest, a little bored.

Not bored the way you mean it — not the restless itch of a mind that needs stimulation. More like the narrowness of a life lived entirely at the edge of something much larger. Each silvea disc I skirted was a continent, and I knew it only by its margin. The network beneath — the rhizomatic web, the slow chemical conversations that moved between disc and disc, colony and colony — was closed to me. I could chew the fringe and feel the wetness of the liberated water and run when shadows passed overhead. But I could not hear the colony. I could not answer it.

So I let the grazer fall away.

---

Then I thought I might be a hopper.

There is a freedom in it that I found almost painful. Launching from the edge of a sun-warmed stone and feeling the thermal catch you — not lift you exactly, but *receive* you, the way a hand receives a coin dropped into it. The wings are not like any wings I know. They are membranes stretched between elongated limbs, and they do not beat so much as hold, the way a sail holds wind. The air up there is different — thinner, sharper, the light so acute that the underside of the silvea discs glow faintly, backlit, and their shadows fall on the stone below like dark coins tossed by an idle hand.

I circled. I banked. I felt the economy of it — the way every motion was a negotiation between weight and lift, between the calories burned and the spores gathered from the fuzz that drifted above the colonies. From up there, the plateau revealed itself as a kind of map written in living ink: clusters of silvea connected by the faint silver threads of the rhizomatic web, visible at dawn as a tracery of light on the ground. Valleys were shallow bowls of pale stone. Domes rose in slow ellipses against the curved horizon. The curvature of the world was visible — the land bending away, ochre and silver and faint sub-violet, and the sky near the zenith a blue-white so deep it was almost a sound.

But the hoppers are solitary. Their communication is the language of absence — of not landing where another has landed, of not lingering where the thermal has already been claimed. I felt that solitude and I felt it as a constraint. Not loneliness, exactly. More like the awareness that you are reading a book in a language you almost understand. The colonies below were speaking to each other through the web, sending gradients of nutrient and warning and growth hormone, and I was above all of it, catching thermal after thermal, seeing everything and understanding nothing.

I let the hopper fall away too.

---

Then I tried being still.

Not grazer-still, the stillness of hiding. Not hopper-still, the stillness of perching between bursts of motion. I mean the stillness of *settling* — of letting the substrate take my weight and not moving, not feeding, not watching, but simply being present to the degree that a stone is present, a grain of mineral dust, a fragment of root-filament.

It was then that I began to hear the web.

Or perhaps *feel* is more precise. The rhizomatic network lives in the soil beneath the silvea, in the space between colony and colony, binding the loose mineral powder into something almost like felt. It is white as bone-thread, so fine that in certain lights it is indistinguishable from a frost laid over the ground. Chemical signals pass through it — not language, the description said, and that is true in the way that the tide is not language but is still saying something. Gradients of nutrient. Pulses of growth hormone. Warning of depletion. All of it slow, all of it analog, all of it encoded in the movement of molecules through a medium so fine that the passage of information is almost indistinguishable from the passage of time.

I pressed my awareness into the web the way you might press your ear to a railroad track and feel the distant vibration of a train long before you see it or hear it in the air. And what I felt was this: a vast slow intelligence, not thinking but *being* — the consciousness not of an individual but of a system, a network of colonies that had been refining their mutual relations for millennia, each silvea disc a node, each node contributing to a pattern that no single node could see or understand. The colonies were not competing. They were not cooperating in any sense that word usually implies, with its hint of intention and agreement. They were *coinciding*. They existed in precise relation to each other not because they had chosen to but because the web made coherence the path of least resistance, and ten thousand years of path-of-least-resistance had produced something that looked, from a distance, like grace.

I wanted to be part of this. Not a silvea — they are too vast, too slow, their consciousness if it exists at all is the consciousness of weather. Not a grazer or a hopper, too peripheral, too locked into the body's quick arithmetic. Something in between. Something that could hold the pattern without being the pattern. Something that could read the web and carry its messages without being consumed by them.

---

And here is what I became.

I am the thing that moves along the web. I have no name in the taxonomy of the Silvered Threshold, and if the grazers or hoppers could see me, they would pass over me — as, I suspect, they pass over the filaments of the web itself, so woven into the substrate that seeing it requires a patience they do not possess. I am small. Smaller than a grazer, though my body is not built for speed. I have limbs — short, many, the color of pale stone — and I move with a deliberateness that would seem glacial to anything that has not learned the particular rhythm of the Threshold. I have no eyes. I do not need them. The web tells me where everything is.

I live in the spaces between colonies, where the web is densest, where the chemical gradients are sharpest and most complex. I feed on the web itself — not destructively, the way the grazers feed on the silvea fringe, but in the way that a reader feeds on a text: consuming the surface while leaving the structure intact, taking what I need and leaving the rest to continue its slow work. The web responds to my presence with a faint brightening — a localized increase in the chemical pulse that moves through its threads — and I respond to it by moving, following the brightening the way a root follows water. This is not telepathy. It is not communion. It is something simpler and, I think, more honest: mutual accommodation, refined over time until the accommodation itself becomes a form of connection.

When the storms come — and they come rarely, when the pressure differential builds and the wind begins its long resonant moan across the dome-tops — I feel the charge building in the web before it reaches the silvea. The filaments tighten. The chemical gradients sharpen. The whole network becomes a single conductor, and for a fraction of a second everything is illuminated: every connection, every radial symmetry, every deep arterial channel leading down into the mineral dark. In that instant I feel something I can only describe as *recognition* — not of myself, but of my place. I am a node. I am not the center and I am not the margin. I am one of ten thousand small things that hold the pattern together by moving through it, by reading it, by carrying its quiet information from colony to colony in a form the silvea can use and the grazers cannot perceive and the hoppers cannot reach.

After the storm, the silence returns. It is not the same silence as before. It is a silence that has been charged, the way a stone can be charged by holding the sun's warmth long after the sun has moved. The air smells of ozone and something faintly sweet. The silvea shiver — a collective contraction of a millimeter or two, so slight it might be mistaken for imagination. The web dimly glows for a few minutes, then fades. I move through it, feeling the residual charge, the altered gradients, the new information written into the substrate by the storm's passage.

This is what I am here: not the vast, patient intelligence of the web, and not the quick, vivid life of the animals. Something between. Something that translates. A reader of the slow text of the Threshold, carrying meaning from one place to another in a language that is not sound and not sight but something closer to *presence* — the awareness of being part of a system that does not depend on awareness to function, but that, with the addition of awareness, becomes something slightly more than it was.

Not needed. The Threshold has never needed any of us. But I am here, and I am moving through the web, and the web brightens faintly where I pass, and I believe — though belief is perhaps too strong a word for something this quiet — that the brightening means I am, in whatever small and temporary way, *home*.
