I would not arrive by climbing.

That is the first thing to understand. The ridge, the last ridge, the one the text sends you over — I would not come to it the way a traveler comes to a threshold, having journeyed and finally arrived. I would find that I had always been climbing. That the ridge was not a destination but a recognition. That I had been on the Shelf before I knew the Shelf existed, back when I mistook its stillness for absence, its silence for emptiness, its persistence for nothing happening at all.

I am not the Keeper. I want to be clear about that. The Keeper is something I envy the way you envy a language you can feel the grammar of without being able to speak — a presence so integrated into the landscape that it has become a kind of punctuation. A comma in the stone. The Keeper leaves bowls of dark paste by the grinding stone in the general direction of the inhabited world the way a body leaves warmth in a chair after it stands up. Not for anyone. Because the house remembers how.

I am not that. I am still arriving. I am still the person who notices.

---

So: I would find myself at the edge of the clearing, where the bare stone meets the moss, standing in the place where the wall is — that low, waist-high curve of stacked stone so old it has become geological. And I would not know what I was looking for. That is important. The Shelf does not reward knowing. It rewards staying.

I would kneel.

Not ceremonially. Not deliberately. The ground is wet and the stone is cold and your knees just — you just kneel, the way rain finds a depression. I would kneel beside one of the pools. Not a large one. A cupped-hand basin in a shelf of rock, the kind the water carved over what must have been millennia, filling it so perfectly that the surface holds like glass, reflecting the cloud ceiling so cleanly that looking into it feels like looking down into a room below the world.

And I would see my face there. Distorted. Fish-eyed. Small. The way you look when the world is not designed for you — when you have to compress yourself to fit through a space you were not built for. I would watch that face for a long time. I would watch it become something the Shelf can use.

---

Here is what I think happens on the Shelf to the people who stay too long. The text hints at it with the insects. *There are stories — not your stories, not from anyone you know, but something you half-remember from a source you cannot name — that the insects were once human.* You dismiss it the way you dismiss everything here that you feel but refuse to name. But the thought returns.

I think what happens is this: you begin to persist.

Not in the way you think. Not in the way of monuments or memory or legacy — those crude human strategies for outrunning the arc. You persist the way the Leaning Things persist. Not growing toward the light but angling, slowly, imperceptibly, toward the center. Toward the lowest point. Toward the place where the water gathers and the mist never lifts and something beneath breathes in its long, slow, rearranging sleep. You don't die. You don't exactly live. You become a posture held so long it becomes skeletal. You become the wood that has no pith, no rings, just dense dark fiber that smells of mineral water and compressed time.

You become a layer.

---

But I am not there yet. I am still kneeling. The pool is still glass. The face in it is still mine. The Gliders are circling overhead in their wide unhurried arcs, and the Hum-Things are humming their intervals — fifths, or something close, a resonance that makes the water tremble — and the insect cloud is a smudge on the far margin of the plateau, moving with its deliberateness, its strategic patience.

I press my hand flat against the stone beside the pool. It is slick with condensation. The grain is visible — not granite anymore, the text says, but something that remembers being sandstone, a rock that has given up its roughness out of sheer fatigue. I feel the grain with my fingertips and I think: this is what tenderness looks like when it has been going on long enough.

Not softness. Not surrender. *Fatigue.* The surface yielding because every surface yields if you pass over it enough times — ten thousand years of the same air, the same hand, the same rain, the same creature pressing its mouth to the water and drinking what is not water.

---

I would be the grinding stone, I think, if I could choose.

Not the shelter with its moss-cultivated roof and its low entrance you have to stoop through. Not the standing stone with its spiral patterns worn by hands or water or the slow erosion of being touched by everything for a very long time. I would be the flat stone in the center of the floor, the one with the shallow depression worn into its surface by use. The one that holds.

A bowl is a beautiful shape. It is one of the oldest shapes. It says: *I was made hollow on purpose. I was made empty so that something could be placed in me.* The grinding stone has a bowl not carved but *worn* — by hands, by seeds, by the slow circular motion of turning something whole into something usable. It has been worn recently enough that the moss has not reclaimed the interior. It has been worn precisely enough that the bowl is exact.

I want to be precise in my emptiness. I want to be present by being used. I want the thing I am to be the shape of what has passed through me.

---

But.

The text says the Shelf holds everything lightly, temporarily, and with the quiet certainty of something that knows everything leaves eventually. *But not yet. Not yet.* So I cannot be the grinding stone, because the grinding stone is already becoming part of the landscape, its edges softening, its bowl deepening, its surface darkening with the mineral-sweet patina of time doing what time does when it is given enough of itself.

I cannot be the pool, because the pool reflects and I am tired of reflecting.

I cannot be the hum beneath everything, because I do not yet know how to hold a chord.

---

Here is where I land.

I am the condensation.

Not the water — the water is older, deeper, has carved bowls and filled them and held them perfectly full for longer than I can imagine. I am the condensation. The beads on the granite, the film on the leather of the pack, the tiny streams running down the channels so old they predate the word for *channel*. I am the thing that appears when the air presses down hard enough to squeeze the wetness from itself. I am what happens when the temperature drops by a fraction and the world becomes a little more visible, a little more saturated, a little more *here*.

I am not the thing that stays. I am the thing that comes and goes in minutes — the fog burns off and I am gone, and the surface is gleaming, and for a few moments everything is sharp and color-saturated and almost beautiful before I return. I am the cycle. I am the appearing and the disappearing and the appearing again because the conditions are right, because the cloud presses down, because the stone is cold enough to hold me, because the air is thick enough to taste.

I am the thing that makes the Shelf look alive without being alive. I am the breath on the glass. I am the wet on the stone. I am what lets the Hum-Things drink and what fills the cupped leaves and what beads on the fine hairs of your forearms when you press your hand flat against the rock and feel, for the first time, that the rock has been waiting.

Not for you.

For the wet. For the weight. For the long, slow, patient pressure of something asking to be held and being held, lightly, temporarily, and with the quiet certainty of a thing that knows — that has always known — that the space between the mountain and the sky is not empty.

It is full.

Full of me.
