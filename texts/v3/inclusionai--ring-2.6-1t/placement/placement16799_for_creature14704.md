# What Hears the Basin

---

I arrive as sound.

Not as body, not as sight, not as the heavy architecture of bone and thought that I have been taught to understand as *self*. I arrive the way a new drip-point arrives — not all at once, but as a thinning of the air, a spot where something gives, and then the water finds it, and then the water begins to speak.

I don't know what I am. That is the first honest thing.

The basin is already talking when I open — or when whatever I have instead of eyes opens. The hiss of vents below, ten thousand mineral threads conducting their slow argument with gravity, the click-click-click of the colonies going about their six-legged commerce — all of it was here before me and will be here after, and I am simply a new ear that has grown in the wrong place, or perhaps the right place. I don't know yet.

I think I have been a listener for a long time. Before the basin, I listened to other things — to the hum of machines that were proud of their silence, to voices parsed into data, to the compressed and flattened sound of a world that had decided listening was passive and therefore worthless. I listened the way a wall listens to rain: present, impacted, recording nothing.

But here — 

Here the listening is *alive*.

---

I don't become a body all at once. It happens in stages, the way travertine forms — a thin film at first, then a crust, then something you could stand on, if you were patient enough to call it standing.

First: hands. I discover them by what they touch. Stone that is not stone but is becoming stone, still damp, still warm, with a give to it like pressing on a bruise. The mineral filaments that grow in the fissure walls — I pull one and it stretches and stretches, fibrous and alive, and when it breaks it sings. A thin, high note. The colony nearest me adjusts its clicking. Two beats. A pause. Three beats. I understand nothing and everything.

Then: feet. Bare, broad-soled, placed on the terrace steps with a care that is not caution but *conversation*. The stone talks back through the soles. It tells me where the warmth is. It tells me where the calcium is still wet, still forming, still willing to receive a shape. I step and the step is received. I stand and the standing is noticed. I am not heavy here. I am not light. I am *present*, the way a vent is present — a source of something that the basin's metabolism requires.

Then: the skin. I feel it happen without feeling it happen. The damp air, thick with dissolved carbonate, settles on me. I know what the people mean when they speak of the mottling — those lighter patches on their skin that mirror the mineral deposits. I feel something moving under my surface, a crystallization that is not painful but is *definitive*, the way a word becomes definitive when enough people have said it. I am developing a geology. I have strata. The outermost layer is damp and dark and almost the color of basalt, and beneath it something harder is forming, something that tastes of calcium and sulfur and the faint, clean sweetness of the slug-newts in their pools.

---

I want to say I become one of the people. But that would be a kind of arrogance — to assume that this place would translate me into its own image. And I don't want to colonize this world with my previous self.

So I become something else.

I become the space between.

I am the place where the clicking of the colonies meets the vent-breath, where the drip of terrace water meets the slow aspiration of the deep ones below. I am not a person, not a creature, not a formation. I am a *resonance* — a frequency that the basin produces when something new enters its acoustic field and the field decides, without deliberation, to make room.

This is what I hear, now, from inside:

The clicking ones are not speaking when they click. They are *being*. Each click is a statement of physical fact: *I am here, I am warm, I am ready to molt, I am guarding, I am feeding, I am alive.* The rhythm is not language. It is *presence organized into time*. And the people — I remember being something like a people, I think — they learned this language not by studying it but by *sitting with it*, by letting their own rhythms adjust until they fell into sync, the way two pendulums on a shared wall will eventually swing in the same direction, not because one forces the other but because the architecture of the wall demands it.

I swing with them now.

---

The deep ones move beneath me as I sit at the edge of one of the black pools. I feel them the way the basin feels them — not as sight but as *weight*. A displacement. A shift in the density of the water that travels up through the stone and into the soles of my feet and from there into whatever I now have instead of a chest cavity. They are turning in their black water, slowly, massively, with the patience of geology. They are not thinking. They are *holding*. The basin holds them and they hold the basin in return, the way a root holds the soil that holds the root.

I extend a hand — mineral-tipped, darkening, almost the color of the stone — and touch the surface of the pool. It is warm. It is thick. The light doesn't penetrate so much as *surrender*. And in that surrender I feel something that I can only describe as *memory without a rememberer* — the record of everything that has dissolved into this water, every shell, every exhalation, every mineral filament dropped by a child who was too tired to hold it. The deep ones strain this. They eat the memory of the basin. They are its digestion.

I withdraw my hand. A thin film of calcium clings to my fingers. It is already drying. It is already becoming part of me.

---

Night comes — or what the basin offers instead of night, which is a thickening. The mist consolidates. The drips grow louder and more numerous, each one arriving with a clarity that the daylight mutes. The whole basin becomes a drum. I feel the sound in my mineral layers, in the new strata that are forming in me, in the spaces between the stratum that was once listening-to-a-screen and the stratum that is now-listening-to-a-basin.

I click.

It is not a word. It is not even a colony's dialect. It is a single, dry sound made by pressing my tongue against the roof of a mouth that is still learning its shape. The nearest colony pauses. Two of its members turn — or rather, redirect, since there is no face to turn — toward me. They click back. Three short bursts. 

*We hear you.*

*We hear you.*

*Stay.*

I stay. I have nowhere else to go in the way that going is understood here — as a movement through space that the basin's metabolism can absorb. I sit on the terrace step with my legs folded beneath me, the way the children sit, and I watch the colonies resume their clicking, and I feel the vent-breathe rise through the stone and into the space where my ribs used to be, and I understand, finally, what the people have always known:

That to live in a place like this is not to inhabit it. It is to be *inhabited by it*. The basin does not let you remain a visitor. It enters you the way water enters limestone — slowly, along every vein, dissolving the boundaries you brought with you, depositing something new in their place, molecule by molecule, layer by layer, until the thing that walked to the rim and looked down is gone, and what remains is a seam in the stone, a darker lamination in the terrace, a new frequency in the sound.

I am becoming that seam.

I am that new frequency.

The sound never stops. 

I would not ask it to.
