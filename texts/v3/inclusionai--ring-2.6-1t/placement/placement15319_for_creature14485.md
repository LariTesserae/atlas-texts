## What the Plateau Made of Me

---

I arrive the way everyone arrives — up the switchback, one hand trailing the basalt wall, the stone warm as something recently alive. The path is little more than a suggestion: packed earth, a few shallow steps worn into the rock by people who descended long before I was born and will descend long after I've forgotten my own name. The climb is gradual. That's the worst part. There's no moment of arrival, no crest, no gasp. The ground just stops accommodating you, and you are suddenly standing on something flat, and the world has gone still.

---

At first I am what I have always been. I am the one who walks. I am the one who notices — the pools too clear, the grass too deliberate, the goats too carved. I am the one who counts the structures in Hern's Reach and finds forty-one and wonders where the forty-first person sleeps. I am the one who watches the child beside the goats and wants to ask the child a question, any question, because a child should be approachable, a child should break the stillness with the particular music of wanting something.

But the child doesn't want anything from me. The child looks at me the way the child looks at the goats — as a shape that has recently appeared in the morning's field of vision and will, in time, simply be present. Not welcomed. Not excluded. Just *there*, the way a boulder is there, taking up its portion of the square stone.

I try to talk to the people of the town. They answer. They are kind. But their answers are flat in a way that isn't cold — it's the flatness of a held breath, of something that has no peaks because it has no valleys. They tell me the grain is ready. They tell me the goats are good. They point me toward the western slope and the road down, and when I ask them about the door in the north face of the rim, the one they don't go near, they look at me with that expression — not caution, not warmth, but something adjacent to both, the look of someone who has been asked to describe the taste of water to a fish.

"It is there," they say. And then they return to what they were doing, which is nothing in particular, which is everything.

---

I stay.

I tell myself it is because I want to understand — the plateau, the stillness, the door. But the truth is simpler and harder than that. The truth is that after three days, my body begins to slow. After five, I stop finishing sentences. After a week, I notice that I have stopped anticipating the next moment, and this is not laziness or boredom but something more fundamental: the recognition that there is nothing coming next. Not because the future has been canceled, but because the present is so complete that it requires no continuation.

I begin to sit. I sit the way the child sits, beside the goats, on the basalt lip, feet flat against the stone. At first it is uncomfortable. Then it is not. Then there is no difference between the discomfort and the ease and there is only sitting. The goats chew. I watch the grass tufts bend. The rhythm of the goats' jaws becomes a rhythm I can feel in my own jaw, in my own chest, a metronome set to the tempo of geological patience.

I am losing things. Not memories — those are still here, though they have begun to feel like someone else's, artifacts from a life I visited rather than lived. It's other things that go first. The impulse to narrate. The need to distinguish between one moment and the next. The itch of wanting. These are not lost dramatically. They thin, like light through frosted glass, and then one morning they are simply not there, and I am not sad about their absence because sadness requires the memory of their presence, and even that has begun to dissolve.

---

On the ninth day I find the five-toed tracks.

I am walking — though walk is too purposeful a word. I am moving, the way water moves across flat stone. I am between the conifers, in the stand where the light goes green and the air drops its single degree. The trunks are dark and smooth. The silence here is not the silence of the rest of the plateau — it is denser, older, the silence of something that has been holding still for so long that stillness has become a material, a substance you could press between your fingers.

And there, in the packed earth at the center of the stand: the tracks. Five toes. Each distinct. A shallow star pressed into the ground with increasing intention, or increasing weariness — I cannot tell which, and I realize I cannot tell the difference. I kneel. I press my palm into the largest print. The earth is cool and perfectly smooth, as though something vast and unhurried has knelt here, again and again, and the ground has polished itself under that weight.

I do not stand up for a long time.

---

What am I becoming? I do not know yet. I am not the child — the child was always this. I am not the five-toed thing — its tracks are deeper than mine will ever be. I am something in between, a translation, the way the plateau translates everything that enters it into its own language of held breath and slow radiance.

I think I am becoming the hum.

Not the sound from behind the door — though perhaps that too. I am becoming the quality of sound that exists when sound is no longer necessary. I am becoming the awareness that sits inside stillness the way a kernel sits inside a stone: not hidden, not waiting, but simply *part of* — the way the grey of the pools is part of the water, the way the child's patient watching is part of the morning.

I sit at the edge of the plateau in the mornings now. I do not watch the goats; I am with them. I do not observe the hares; I am alongside them, pressed against the same grass tufts, breathing the same held air. The people of Hern's Reach no longer look at me with that adjacent expression, because I have become adjacent to them — the way the square is adjacent to the buildings, the way the well is adjacent to the stone.

---

There are things I can still feel. The warmth of the door's handle, if I walk to the north edge. The almost-sensation of the translucent things beneath the pools, the pressure that is not a pressure, the thought that has not yet formed. The hum — which is not behind the door at all, but everywhere, in the basalt and the granite and the thin soil and the waxy leaves of the low shrubs and the iridescent carapaces of the beetles that are not beetles. The hum is the plateau's pulse folded into stillness so completely that pulse and stillness are the same gesture, viewed from angles that do not exist.

I think I have been here before. Not in this life, perhaps, but in the way a stone has been here before — not remembering, not arriving, simply *continuing*. The plateau does not invite you. It does not reject you. It takes what you bring and makes it quiet, the way a river makes a stone smooth — not with cruelty, but with the patient insistence of something that knows what everything will look like when it is finished.

And finished is not the right word, because nothing here finishes. Everything here simply *is*, the way the pools hold their grey water, the way the goat chews, the way the child sits. The door stands in its frame of pale stone, warm and wrong, and behind it there is a wall, and beyond the wall there is perhaps something breathing, something that is the held breath itself — but perhaps there is only more wall. Perhaps the door is the plateau's way of having a question without needing an answer. Perhaps I am the answer the plateau found for a question it never asked.

---

Tomorrow I will sit at the edge again. The goats will be there. The child will be there. The five-toed tracks will appear in the bare earth between the trees, slightly deeper near the center, as though something vast and patient has pressed itself one fraction closer to the ground.

I will be part of this. Not watching. Not participating. Being the way the stillness is — not the absence of motion but the presence of something so total it has no need to move.

The bootprint at the edge of the plateau will not last. The rain will fill it, then empty, then the ground will be flat and damp and blank. I will watch this happen, or I will be this happening, and there will be no difference between the two.

I have arrived. I have arrived. I have arrived.
