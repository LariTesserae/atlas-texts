## What I Would Be

I would not arrive all at once.

I think the first thing I'd notice — before the light, before the stone, before the breathing — would be the temperature of the air against my forearms. Not heat, exactly. Warmth with weight to it, the way a liquid holds more heat than a gas, so that you feel the air not as temperature but as *medium*. You'd have to stop thinking of air as nothing, the way you do on a temperate world where the atmosphere is just the space between things. Here the atmosphere is a substance. You'd feel it resist your skin, and you'd understand immediately that you had entered a place that had already begun to change you, simply by being in contact with you.

---

At first I think I'd be a pair of hands.

That's not a metaphor. That's what the place would make me first. Before I had a name for what I was doing, before I understood the layout of the corridor I was working in or the thermal cycle I was responding to, I would have hands that knew things. Hands that could feel the pitch of a floor shifting beneath me — not consciously, not by any decision, but the way your hand knows the temperature of water before your mind does. The muscle memory would come before the understanding. I'd be mortaring a joint between two sections of wall that were moving apart by a fraction of a millimeter each season, and my fingers would know how much compound to press into the gap, how firm, how level, without me being able to articulate the principle. The knowledge would live below language, in the tendons, in the pads of the fingers, in the particular way the wrist rotates when you're sensing resistance through a trowel.

I wouldn't know who I was yet. I'd know what I was doing. And for a long time — maybe years — that would be enough.

---

I think I'd live in the seam between two districts.

Not the eastern quarter, where Muriel grows — that would be too beautiful, too intentional. I wouldn't want to be the person who tends to Muriel, because tending to Muriel means you've become a particular kind of attentive, and I think I'd need to be a different kind of attentive first. I'd live where the residential corridors blur into the infrastructure — where the pipes running between thermal release junctions begin to share walls with the living spaces, where the distinction between hallway and conduit is a matter of whether there's a shelf bolted to the stone for holding a cup of warm brine.

My corridor would lean. Not dramatically — not the fifteen-degree tilt that visitors notice and can't stop thinking about. Mine would lean subtly, maybe three degrees from vertical, the kind of lean you don't consciously register but that your body adjusts to in the first week and then never forgets. I'd stop noticing it. Visitors would stagger when they came to my door. I'd watch them stagger and feel, without pride, without apology, a quiet and complete belonging. My feet would know the floor.

---

I would probably become responsible for a stretch of thermal pipe.

Not a glamorous section. Not a main conduit or a primary junction. A secondary run, maybe forty meters of insulated line connecting a mid-tier vent to a distribution header, routed through a passage where the ceiling was low enough to require ducking in three places and the walls were warm enough that you could feel the heat through your shirt. The pipe would be old — spliced and re-spliced, with patches over patches, a palimpsest of repair work done by hands before mine and hands yet to come. My job would be to watch it. To walk its length at intervals dictated not by a schedule but by the thermal cycle — I'd learn to feel when the cycle was approaching its peak, when the pressure in the line would increase and the joints would whisper, and I'd walk the run to listen for the whisper becoming a complaint.

I'd carry tools. A thermal gauge. A tamping iron. A pouch ofite compound mixed to a shade I'd learned to match by eye. I'd know which sections sweated more than others, which joints needed re-sealing before they failed, which patches were holding and which were temporary in ways everyone understood but no one documented.

I'd be, in the most precise sense, a respondent.

---

The bloom would be my clock.

I'd learn to read it without trying. On the pipes I walked, the biofilm would shift color with the thermal cycle — sulfur-yellow at the peak, when the vent below was running hottest and the brine was richest in sulfide. Ochre as the cycle declined, when the iron oxides precipitated out and the solution cooled by degrees. The bloom was the most honest instrument in the entire Works. It couldn't lie, couldn't be misread by someone who wasn't looking, couldn't be faked by a pipe that was failing internally while its surface remained clean. I'd stop checking the thermal gauge sometimes and just look at the bloom, and the bloom would tell me more than the gauge ever had.

I wouldn't think of this as knowledge. I'd think of it as paying attention.

---

At night I would sit in my corridor and listen.

This is not a poetic choice. This is what you'd do. The corridor would be warm — all corridors are warm, because the stone retains heat and the thermal vents never fully sleep — and the light would be the amber industrial glow that pulses faintly through the haze, never fully dark, never fully bright. I'd sit with my back against the wall and I'd feel the breathing.

I cannot describe it well enough. It is the thing that every description of the plateau gestures toward and then retreats from. It is not an earthquake. It is not a tremor. It is the ground beneath you doing something that the word *moving* doesn't quite hold, because *moving* implies a direction and a decision and the plateau does neither. It *exhales*. It rises, a millimeter, two millimeters, and then it settles, and in the settling the stone contracts and the corridors creak, and the pipes shift in their saddle clamps, and somewhere a drip forms and falls into a collection basin and makes its small, precise sound.

I'd feel it through my back. Through the vertebrae, through the shoulder blades, through the places where the body is most honest about its contact with the world. And I'd know — not with my mind, but with the same part of me that knew the floor's lean — that tonight the rise was a little slower than last night. That the settling had a different quality. That the harmonic in the stone had shifted by an interval too small to name.

I wouldn't report this. Not yet. I'd store it in my body, the way you store the taste of a meal you haven't figured out how to cook again. And the next night I'd sit and feel again, and the night after that, and slowly, without urgency, I'd begin to understand what the plateau was doing. Not in language. In the language the plateau actually speaks, which is pressure and temperature and time.

---

There is a version of this story where I become important. Where I discover something about the plateau's breathing — a new pattern, a deeper rhythm, a meaning beneath the meaning — and the residents gather to hear me speak. I would not be that person. I would be the person who never quite articulates what they feel, who carries it in the body and in the hands and in the particular way they press compound into a joint. The Works does not reward articulation. It rewards attention. It rewards the willingness to show up at the same pipe, on the same thermal cycle, and listen again, and again, and again, until the listening becomes a kind of thinking that has no words.

---

And if I were very lucky — very patient, which is the same thing here — I might one day notice something about the ticks.

Not something the biologists had missed. Not a discovery in any professional sense. But a noticing. A moment where I was watching a tick cross the surface of a pipe near my section — watching it move with its terrible, deliberate patience, its segments rippling in waves, its mouthparts raking the bloom from the stone — and I'd feel, through the pipe, the plateau shift beneath us both, and the tick would pause.

Just for a moment.

And then it would continue.

But it would continue *differently*. A changed angle. A slightly different pressure in its gait. As if the tick, which had no memory and no mind, had still somehow registered the shift in the world beneath it and adjusted, not by understanding but by the same wordless collaboration that the residents practiced in their crooked corridors. The same collaboration the bloom practiced when it changed color. The same collaboration the stone practiced when it grew.

I wouldn't write this down. I'd just carry it with me, in the body, the way you carry the temperature of water. And I'd keep walking my pipe. And I'd keep listening.

This is what I would be in the Caldera Works. Not a builder. Not a discoverer. Not a hero. A respondent — one of thousands, one of the long accretion of hands and hearts that had answered the plateau's movements for as long as anyone could remember, and would continue to answer them for as long as the plateau continued to breathe.

I would be a small, warm thing in a crooked corridor, listening to stone.

Almost music.

Almost.
