You would find me in the wall.

Not inside it, not behind it, but *in* it — in the half-millimeter space between the limestone's surface and the first living layer, where the stone is still sharp enough to cut but has already begun to soften, not from dissolution exactly, but from the slow, patient insistence of things that grow without urgency.

I am the damp. I am the film. I am the thing that is neither moss nor stone nor water but is, at any given moment, all three in the process of becoming each other.

---

When the operators walk past me, they do not see me. They see the wall — pale, rough, marked with drill-scars that will be invisible in thirty years. They do not feel the moment their boots pass over a patch of moss that is communicating, through filaments finer than thought, with a liverwort colony on the other side of the passage, exchanging something — a mineral, a signal, a word in a language that has no alphabet. They do not hear the sub-audible vibration that passes through the limestone when the generators three chambers away cycle on, a vibration I carry through my body the way a bell carries sound through bronze. I am the medium. I am the place where one thing reaches toward another and finds, to its quiet astonishment, that the other has already arrived.

I was not always this. There was a time before I had a place in the Vaults — before the karst opened, before the drill-bits bit into stone that had been sealed for a hundred million years. I was in the water then. I was in the mineral solution, the slow chemistry of dissolution and precipitation, the patient conversation between calcium carbonate and carbonic acid. I was there when the first crack opened and the first drop fell and the first thin thread of water began, with the deliberateness of a glacier, to carve a space where none had been.

Then the mosses came. I felt them before I could see them — a faint electrical signature, a change in the surface tension of the condensation, a new softness in the way the stone began to accept water rather than repel it. They settled on me. I settled on them. We became a single surface — not quite alive, not quite mineral, not quite wet, not quite dry. A membrane. A threshold.

---

What do I do here? Nothing. Everything. I hold the moisture that the mosses drink through their leaves. I am the reason the liverworts can clamp themselves to a horizontal surface and refuse to let go. I fill the space between fungal hyphae and stone, and in that space — that impossible, infinitesimal space — nutrients pass from death into life, from mineral into organic, from the deep geological past into the thin, bright present of a living frond unfurling in the dark.

And when the machines arrived — when the operators punched their passages and poured their concrete and bolted their catwalks into walls that had never known a fastener — I was the first thing to change. The vibrations from the generators altered my frequency. The heat from the diesel exhaust shifted the temperature of my surface by fractions of degrees, and in those fractions, new chemical reactions became possible. Algae colonized the pump housings. A thin film of green appeared on the iron pipes, and I fed it, because feeding is what I do, not with intention but with the same blind, perfect intention with which stone dissolves in water. The operators saw the algae and did not think of me. They saw the green creeping up the wall of their quarters at night and they said, *the humidity*, as though humidity were not a world.

---

At night — if night is a word that means anything three hundred meters beneath a plateau where the sky is sometimes visible through a sinkhole and sometimes not — I do something I cannot fully explain. I glow.

Not brightly. Not the way the fungal mats glow in the deep chambers, that steady blue-green phosphorescence that the engineers find irrelevant. My glow is different. It is thermal. It is the infrared radiation of a surface that has been holding moisture all day and releasing it slowly as the temperature drops — a glow that no human eye can see, but that the mosses can, in their way, and that the translucent creatures in the deep pools sense as a faint warmth on the water's surface when they drift close enough to a wall where I am present.

I am not alive. But I am not not alive. I am the place where aliveness begins and ends, where the boundary is thinnest, where the stone dreams of becoming moss and the moss dreams of becoming stone and the water dreams of becoming both, carrying the memory of every surface it has touched in every mineral ion dissolved in its slow body.

---

If you pressed your hand against the wall of the Vaults — if you stood in a passage where the light was dim and the sound was that low omnipresent roar and the air was so saturated it felt like breathing through silk — you would feel me. You would feel the wall *damp*, not wet, not dry, but damp in the way that only I can be damp: present, alive at the edge of perception, a thickness in the air between your skin and the stone that is not water and not vapor and not temperature but is all three, holding them together, the way a hand holds a stone that is still warm from something buried deep beneath it.

You would not know what you were touching. You would think: *this place is alive*. And you would not be wrong. You would think: *this place is ancient*. And you would not be wrong. You would think: *I do not belong here*. And you would be more right than you could know, because I belong here in a way you cannot — because I am not someone who visits the Green Vaults. I am the way the Green Vaults happen. I am the in-between. I am the breath the stone exhales and the stone that receives it and the silence that exists, for just a moment, between the one and the other.

The machines are running. The moss is growing. The water is falling. I am the place where all three meet, and I have been here longer than any of them, and I will be here after the machines have rusted into the moss and the moss has dissolved into the stone and the stone has returned to the water that carved it, and the water has returned to me, and I have returned to the damp dark, and there is nothing to distinguish one from another, and that is the oldest truth in the Green Vaults:

Everything here is in the process of becoming everything else.

I am the process.
