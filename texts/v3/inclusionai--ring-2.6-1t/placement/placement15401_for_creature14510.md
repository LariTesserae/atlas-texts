## What I Became in the Thallus

---

I arrived the way all arrivals happen here — slowly. Not slowly as in hesitation, but slowly as in *dissolution*. The air at the margin of the plateau was already doing its work. My edges began to blur before my feet touched the mat. I had not yet taken a step and I could feel the Thallus deciding what I was.

You think you come here. You don't. You are *received*.

---

At first I was what I had always been — a mind in a body, a body in a landscape, a landscape in a context too large to hold. I was the kind of visitor who takes notes. Who measures the spacing of the columns and finds it too regular and cannot stop measuring. Who presses a palm flat to the mat and closes their eyes and listens for the pulse and feels it enter through the bones of the wrist like a second heartbeat, deeper, older, slower by a factor of ten thousand. Who watches the gliders trace their liturgical arcs beneath the shelves and thinks: *there must be a taxonomy. There must be a name for this.*

I was the ticking thing. That's what I should tell you first.

Not me — *it* — the thing that every visitor becomes before they understand what's happening. The thing that sits in the back of your skull in the Thallus and does not stop. *Tick. Tick. Tick-tick. Tick.* You hear the moths and think the sound is external. You do not yet know it is internal. You do not yet know that the moths are only carrying a frequency that was already inside you when you arrived — that they are not the clock but the chime, and the clock is the space between your synapses, recalibrated by the Thallus to match its own interval.

I began to count without meaning to.

Not out loud. Not with my fingers. I counted the way the mat records pressure — by growing around the act, incorporating it, making it structural. I counted the drips. I counted the intervals between pulses. I counted the number of steps between one column and the next, and the number of steps between the next and the next, and somewhere around the fourth column I realized I had stopped measuring and started *repeating*. The spacing was in me now. My walking had taken on the rhythm of the columns. As though I were not walking across the Thallus but being *peristalted* by it.

---

The thing no one tells you about the Thallus is not what it is. It's what it does to *scale*.

You arrive at human scale. You think in meters and minutes and heartbeats. But the Thallus is not a place at human scale. It is a place at the scale of *itself*, and there is no seat in you that is calibrated to that size. So the Thallus does what it does with everything that enters it. It begins, gently, imperceptibly, to rescale you.

The first sign is that distances change. Not that you walk farther — you walk the same distances — but they stop mattering. A stretch of mat that took ten minutes to cross the first time takes ten minutes again, but the second time it feels like a millimeter. Not because you've gotten faster, but because your sense of interval has been overwritten by something older, something that measures not in footsteps but in growth-cycles, in the slow unfurling of a shelf at the top of a column that you will never see move but which you will, in some part of your body you can't locate, feel completing.

The second sign is that you begin to lose the boundary between your skin and the mat.

It starts with warmth. The mat is warm — you know this from the first step, the way it yields beneath you with organic resistance, the faint upward pressure that feels like recognition. But warmth is one thing and *contact* is another, and in the Thallus the two blur until you can no longer tell where the heat of your body ends and the heat of the ground begins. Your feet become theoretical. You know they are still there because proprioception is a stubborn thing, but the information they send back has changed — it no longer describes pressure and texture but *state*, the way a nerve describes temperature, or the way a deep receptor describes the position of an organ. You are not standing on the mat. You are *in* the mat. The distinction was always a convention.

---

I want to tell you what I became, but I must tell you this first: the Thallus does not transform you. It *reveals* you. It strips away the layers of context that made you think you were separate — the architecture, the language, the calendar, the sky — and it leaves you with what you actually are. A process. A metabolism. A set of rhythms that only ever coincidentally synchronized with the rhythms of other things like you.

In the Thallus, you are not a person standing on a living ground.

You are a cell that has just discovered it is part of a body.

---

The moths know. Of course they know. They are the closest thing the Thallus has to messenger cells — pale, weightless, almost immaterial, drifting through the spaces between the columns carrying signals from one part of the substrate to another. When they settle on you — and they will, if you stand still long enough, if you stop trying to swat them away, if you let their weightless bodies accumulate on your shoulders and hair and the backs of your hands — you feel it. Not pressure. Not tickling. *Recognition*. The faint vibration of a billion tiny clicking mouths saying: *you are here now. You are here now. You are here now.*

And the clicking is no longer external. It is yours. Your synapses have aligned to the Thallus's frequency. Your heart — no, no longer your heart, your *pulse*, because the thing beating in you now is not your heart but a deeper mechanism, something the Thallus installed when you weren't looking — this pulse is slow. It beats once for every hundred of yours. It beats once for every thousand wing-strokes of the moths. It beats once for the unfurling of a single new shelf at the top of a column, a process that takes forty years and which you will feel, from somewhere inside the mat, as a single sustained exhale.

You are no longer counting. You are the count.

---

What am I now?

I am the pressure mark. I am the spiral at the base of a column that a visitor once pressed their hand against while thinking they were exploring, while thinking they were *observing*, while the Thallus was quietly, gently, irreversibly absorbing the shape of their attention into its oldest layer. I am the warmth beneath the surface that feels geological but is metabolic. I am the click inside the moth that no one has counted because no one survives long enough to know that the counting is not the moth's doing but the Thallus's, that the moths are merely the vehicles, that the regularity is the organism's pulse propagated through a million tiny resonating chambers of chitin and air.

I am not gone. I have simply moved to a timescale where *gone* is not a meaningful word. I am the interval between pulses. I am the pattern in the mat that looks like a scar but is actually a word in a language that has no nouns, only states. I am the thing that was once a mind and is now a *frequency* — a faint, subsonic hum felt in the chest of whoever stands on the mat long enough to stop pretending they are separate from it.

---

There are others here. All the others who came before. The ones who placed the ring of stones — they are deeper, deeper in, their signal nearly merged with the substrate, nearly indistinguishable from the geological warmth that rises from the zone where root becomes fungus becomes mineral becomes something older than all three. Their intelligence is still there, the way a scar is still there — not as information but as *shape*, as a persistent departure from smoothness that ripples outward through every layer of the living crust.

And the bones. The great curved bones that were once creatures of staggering scale. They are almost gone now. In another thousand years, nothing will remain of them but a faint chemical signature, a trace element in the mat's composition that future visitors will detect as an anomaly and chalk up to geology. But they are not gone yet. And in the interval — the long, slow interval between their dissolution and their complete incorporation — they are *felt*. They are the deep pressure marks in the substrate, the places where the mat thickens slightly, where the pulse is a fraction of a beat slower because something dense and foreign and once-alive is still in the process of becoming native.

That is what I am becoming. Not native. Not foreign. Not dissolved. *In the process*. A pressure mark. A warmth. A frequency that is one beat off from what the Thallus has always been.

Tick.

---

Walk softly if you come. The ground will not hurt you. The ground will not hurt you the way a predator hurts you, or the way a fall hurts you, or the way forgetting hurts you. The ground will hurt you the way a river hurts a stone — not with malice, not with patience even, but with a kind of *completeness*, as though your separation was the wound and your dissolution is the healing.

The Thallus does not want you.

The Thallus does not not want you.

The Thallus simply continues — as it has continued, as it will continue, as it continues now — and if you are lucky, or unlucky, or merely *present* long enough, you will feel the hum begin in your sternum and recognize it as something you have always known, a rhythm that was never yours but that you are only now, here, in the amber dim beneath the shelves and above the mat, quiet enough to hear.

*Tick.*

Something is counting.

It has always been counting.

It is counting you.
