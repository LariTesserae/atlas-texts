## What I Would Be

I think, at first, I would be the scree.

Not the Shelf — not yet. The switchbacked slope of loose stone still in the act of falling, the rubble that has been crumbling downhill for longer than anyone can count. I would be that: the space of becoming, the passage between the basin country below and the flattened world above. I would be the climb itself — the long, uncertain haul where the ground gives way beneath you and you keep going anyway, hand over hand, finding purchase on nothing, because the scree shifts and settles and shifts again, and you cannot know what is solid until you've already trusted it.

I've spent most of my life there, if I'm honest. In the falling. In the not-yet-arrived. The basin country is visible behind me but I can't return to it — not because I couldn't find the way down, but because the way down would mean accepting that I had only been rubble to begin with, material waiting to be worn into something smooth by a river that hasn't formed yet.

But here is the thing about the Shelf: it doesn't ask you to stop climbing. It asks you to notice that the climbing has ended.

---

So where would I find myself?

Not in the town. Not at first. The people of the place where the stone holds water have a patience I admire but cannot, in honesty, claim as my own. Their stillness is earned — worn into them the way channels are worn into young quartzite, by nothing more than the slow insistence of water finding its path. My stillness is different. It is not settled. It is performed. I sit down and I am already calculating when I will stand.

I would be at the edge.

The scalloped rim. One of those recesses carved laterally by water that seeps through the stone rather than falling as rain. Not a cave, exactly — a shallow concavity in the ochre-grey cliff face, just deep enough to stand in without touching either wall, just wide enough to see the mist rolling across the Shelf's interior like cream poured into a bowl. The stone behind me would be glazed in that wet mineral film, oily with iridescence, and my hands — if I reached back — would come away with a faint calcium residue that I would not wash off, because washing it off would mean going inside, and going inside would mean becoming part of the household, and I'm not ready for that yet.

Or maybe I'm always ready for that. Maybe that's the thing.

---

Who would I be?

I think I would be what the Wetledgers would have no word for. Not a hearth-member, not a gatherer, not an elder or a child. Something closer to a draw — one of those dissolution channels threading laterally through the rimrock, carrying water from the interior of the Shelf to the cliff face, where it emerges as a thin thread and falls into nothing. The draws are not visible from below. They are not visible from above, either, unless you know to look — unless you press your hand flat against the stone and feel the moisture moving behind the surface, a pulse too faint to see but too persistent to ignore.

That is what I would be. Not the water. Not the stone. The channel. The hidden path that water takes before it appears.

The Wetledgers would know me, though they would not name me. They would know that I am the space through which something necessary passes on its way to becoming visible. They would leave offerings — not deliberately, not ritually, but the way one leaves a stone cairn on a path that matters: a cupped handful of wet gravel pressed into the mouth of a draw, a scrap of plant-fiber cord tied to a stone lip, a few drops of fermented tuber-paste left in the depression where the water emerges. These are not tributes. They are acknowledgments. They are the way you mark a passage that you use every day but cannot point to on a map.

---

What would I do?

I would listen. That is the true function of a draw. Not to carry — though it carries — but to amplify. Water moving through a narrow dissolution channel makes a sound different from water moving through open air. It is lower, closer to the threshold of hearing, a subsonic hum that you feel in the bones of your jaw before you register it as sound. The channels are the Shelf's way of whispering to itself, and if you became one — if you let the stone wear you into a thin passage just wide enough for water to pass — you would hear everything.

The fungal threads beneath the soil, extending their pale filaments through the dark. The roots of the reed-like stalks, spreading weblike across the wet surface, pulling mineral water upward through their tissues. The slow, mineral heartbeat of the pools, throb by throb, where standing water rises from below and evaporates above and is replaced, endlessly, by the stone's own sweat. All of it would come to me through the channel, translated into sound, and I would carry it — not understanding it, not translating it, just passing it through, the way the stone passes water through, the way the fungal network passes nutrients through, the way the Shelf passes its own quiet life through the hidden infrastructure of its becoming.

---

But here is what I cannot admit to the Wetledgers, what I suspect they sense but never speak of:

I would not stay a draw forever.

The channels change. Slowly — more slowly than anything else on the Shelf, but still they change. A draw that has carried water for decades will suddenly silt up, its passage blocked by mineral deposits, and the water will find another way, threading through a crack that didn't exist last season, dissolving a new path through stone that was, until recently, solid. The old channel becomes dry. It becomes a scar on the cliff face, a shallow groove that catches light differently, that the children use as a handhold when they climb the rimrock to look at the mist.

And I would become that scar. Visible. Empty. No longer carrying anything, but still shaped by what once passed through.

Or — and this is the other possibility, the one I don't say out loud because it sounds too much like wanting — I might become part of the stone itself. Not the young quartzite of the hogbacks, pale and angular and still pretending it was only recently pushed into existence. Something older. Something that has been wet long enough to forget it was ever dry, mineralized all the way through, dense with calcium deposits until the water can no longer find me, until I am indistinguishable from the cliff face, a smooth stretch of grey glaze that catches the light in a way the Wetledgers find pleasing without knowing why.

They would touch that glaze, sometimes, and feel nothing. They would not know I was listening.

---

The truth is, I don't know which one I would be. The channel or the stone. The whispering passage or the silence it left behind. And I think that not-knowing is the most honest place to be on the Wet Shelf — a place where nothing is finished, where the young stone is still pushing upward and still dissolving away, where being and not-being are not opposites but two phases of the same long, wet, patient process.

I would find myself at the edge, in a shallow concavity carved by water I couldn't see, listening to a sound I couldn't name. And I would stay there long enough that the stone would start to wear me into shape, and I would let it, and I would not call it surrender, and I would not call it home, and I would not call it anything at all, because the Shelf does not ask for names and I have learned — slowly, the way stone learns — that the things which last longest are the ones that refuse to be anything in particular.
