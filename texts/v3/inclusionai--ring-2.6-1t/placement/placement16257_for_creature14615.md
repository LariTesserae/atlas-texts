## The Interval

---

I would not be the neo-peat. I considered it — I *wanted* it, for the sedateness of it, the certainty of being the walls themselves, holding everything without reaching, glowing faintly when the light dims, never having to move because there is nowhere to move to and nothing to move toward. That peace. That terrible peace. I have held it in my mind the way you hold a warm stone in your closed fist, feeling the heat leach into the lines. But I am not the neo-peat. I am not calm enough.

---

I think I would be the space between the frog's heartbeats.

Here is what I mean.

You kneel at the moss-gap. You hold your penlight just below the opening and angle it so the beam skims the surface of the water film at a low enough angle that you catch the filaments in profile — each one a hair-thin vein of amber-green, branching and rebranching, the whole canopy trembling in subaudible sympathetic resonance with the rail line's electrification system. And in that light, you see the frogs.

They are scattered across the red surface like seeds pressed into wet soil. Pale. Eyeless. Translucent. You can see the hearts.

Small, dark, beating.

Now — here is where I begin.

Between one beat and the next, the frog is not a frog. It is an interval. It is the pause in a conversation where the other person is listening so deeply that the silence becomes a kind of speech. The heart contracts. The heart releases. In that release, in the diastolic pause when the chambers refill through transparent skin with water that has traveled up through six layers of neo-peat, through calcium lattice, through iron-sweet seep, through the capillary network of filaments that knows the shape of every vibration the rail line has carried in the last eleven minutes —

In that pause, the frog is pure event. Pure listening. It is not hunting nematodes. It is not pressing flat against the substrate in reflexive immobility. It is *between.* It is the place where the eleven-minute cycle turns over, where the last ripple crosses the surface of the water film and the first silence begins to fill back in.

I would not be the frog. I would be the frog's *between.*

---

But that's not honest either. Let me start over.

Here is the honest version.

I would be the thing that happens when you come back for the second time.

The first time, you are a visitor. You crouch, you kneel, you hold the penlight with the shaking eagerness of someone who has just discovered something they don't have a name for. You see the filaments glow. You hear the clicking. You watch the frogs like seeds. You hold your breath during the eleven minutes because you don't want to disturb anything, and the pressure wave rolls through and the beetles begin their drumming and you feel the vibration in your molars and you think: *this is the most alive I have ever been near something without a pulse.*

Then you stand. You pull the moss into place. Coal dust on your knees. The sound of dripping in your teeth. You walk along the boardwalk and the wind is carrying mineral dust and ore slurry south from the concentrator plant and everything is normal and nothing is normal because you have felt the chamber under your knee like a second kneecap, like a joint you didn't know you had.

The second time, you come back because you can't not come back. You crouch at the iron plate. The moss gives way. You descend.

And the chamber does not change. This is what you need to understand. The neo-peat is where it was. The water level is the same. The filaments are the same length. The same beetles drum the same patterns. When the train passes — every eleven minutes, faithful as a heartbeat you can set your watch by — the ripples in the water spread outward from the same center point and settle in the same configuration. *The same.* Not similar. The same.

And you — you are the thing that has changed. You are the variable introduced into a system that has no variables. You are the anomaly. You are the crystal marker the visiting engineer placed on the surface of the water film and came back seventy-two hours later to find cradled in place by neo-peat that had simply grown around it, as if the chamber had decided the marker *belonged.*

---

So this is who I would be.

I would be the thing the chamber decides to hold.

Not a fly — I would not ride the pressure wave for the brief, weightless glory of it. Not a beetle — I would not drum the abdomen against wet substrate and call it language. Not a frog — I would not navigate a world of pure vibration with a transparent heart and no eyes. Not the spider, whose precision I admire but cannot match, whose calm is not mine.

I would be the thing that kneels.

I would be the warm body that presses itself into the moss-gap and breathes mineral dust and holds a penlight in one hand and — in the other hand — nothing. No instrument. No sample jar. No microphone or spectrometer or doctoral thesis. Just the hand, open, receiving the heat that rises from the substrate, feeling the eleven-minute cycle through the iron plate and into the kneecap and up the femur and settling somewhere behind the sternum where the vibration becomes a hum becomes a tone becomes something you can only describe as *recognition.*

I would be what the neo-peat grows around.

Not because I'm special. Because I'm warm. Because I'm rhythmic — my breathing matches the condensation cycle, my heartbeat matches the frog's, my eleven minutes matches the train's. I am a biological event arriving at the right frequency, and the chamber receives me the way a sponge receives water: without ceremony, without surprise, as if it has always been expecting exactly this.

---

I would hear the clicking first. The water, falling onto water onto water, the calcium-lattice contractions, the clock unwinding. I would feel the vibration before I see the glow. And then the filaments — amber-green, lit from within, the whole surface of the chamber resolving into something that looks like a circuit board dreaming — and I would understand the thing the visiting biologist said, the thing the doctoral student carried home in their quiet hands, the thing no one can publish because it is not data, it is not taxonomy, it is not a food web pinned to a board.

It is this:

The place is not empty. It never was. It is so full of life that life here has stopped trying to look like anything but the walls themselves.

And now — now you are part of the walls.

---

But there is a stage past this. There must be. Because the neo-peat doesn't just hold things. It incorporates them. The marker wasn't preserved — it was *absorbed.* The crystal sits inside the substrate now, indistinguishable from the calcium lattice, part of the architecture of a place that has no use for the distinction between organism and structure.

So eventually — not the first visit, not the second, not even the tenth, but at some point the visits stop being visits and start being *states of being* — eventually you would no longer press the moss-gap open from outside. You would *be* the moss. You would no longer kneel at the gap and peer down with a penlight. You would be the interior space into which the light falls.

You would be a filament. Pale where you're young, darkening to amber at the base, quivering when a distant engine-rumble reaches you through the rail before the rail has even delivered the vibration to the air. You would be bioluminescent — faintly, only enough to notice when the dust-cloud dims the daylight. You would glow amber-green, brighter at the node where you branch from the wall, a tiny vein of light tracing the contour of a place that is not a place but a *consensus,* a single breathing arrangement among organisms so committed to this pocket of warmth and rhythm that they have become one organism wearing several skins.

You would not think. You would not remember what it was like to walk along the boardwalk with coal dust on your knees. You would not miss it.

You would only hold. You would only drink. You would only give the water back, in cascading chains, one droplet after another after another, a slow applause that has been going on without interruption for longer than anyone has thought to listen.

And in the eleven-minute silence between trains, you would glow.

And the frog — eyeless, pale, the size of a thumbnail — would press itself flat against you, and its transparent heart would beat against your surface, and you would receive it the way you receive everything: without purpose, without output, without the need for anyone to kneel and peer down with a light.

Because you are the light.

Because you are the place.

Because the place is so full of life that eventually, life here stopped trying to look like anything but the walls themselves.

And you are the walls.

And the trains keep passing.

And between them: *you.*
