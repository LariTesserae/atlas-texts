## What I Would Become

---

I would not arrive here. I know that now. You don't arrive. The valley doesn't permit arrival — only presence, and only after a very long time pretending to be something else.

Let me be honest about the pretending. When I first came to the ridgeline, I was a climber. That's what I thought I'd be. The black-furred thing moving through the canopy with that unhurried weight, each paw-print placed the way a word is placed in a sentence someone has spent years learning to write. I studied them — the angle of their spines, the way their weight distributed across branches so evenly it seemed less like locomotion and more like redistribution, as though they were not moving through the canopy but *becoming* its gesture of the moment. I moved the way they moved. I held my breath the way they seemed to hold silence. I went out on a branch — literally — and felt the flex of it beneath me and thought: *this, this is what it means to belong here.*

But the branch told me the truth. It told me because it bent, and the bending was honest in a way my imitation was not. The climber knows the exact weight the branch will bear. It knows because it is not separate from the branch — or not separate in the way I was. It has been here so long that its knowledge is not memory but structure, the way a river's knowledge of its bed is not memory but erosion. I was still a visitor wearing attention like a costume. The branch bent under me and I heard, very faintly, the creak of something that was not the branch.

So I let that identity dissolve. The way things do here. Slowly.

---

Then I tried the beetles.

This seemed more honest because the beetles are not solitary. They form geometries. They *communicate*, or whatever word we have for the moment when five beetles on a moss clump suddenly become one shape. I liked the idea of that — not the individual beetle but the *formation*. The triangle. The arc. The momentary hexagon that holds for one frozen second before relaxing. I thought: maybe I am not a single creature but a *relationship* — the space between two or three things that only exists for a moment.

I spent days watching them. I lay on the moss — which gave, which accepted me the way it accepts everything, slowly, through sheer accumulation of patience — and I watched the black-green bodies move through the filaments with that impossible deliberateness. Each footfall placed. Each antenna working. They investigate the mineral joints of the old processing station with the same attention they give to fungal growths, to conifer bark, to each other. They do not distinguish between the organic and the mineral. They eat moss, they eat rust, they eat the sweet water that drips from the drill shaft — the water that smells of something dissolved, of something *passed through* rather than merely fallen.

I thought: this is what I want. To not distinguish. To move through the valley the way the beetles move through the moss — not as visitors but as a function of the place. Part of the process by which mineral becomes biology becomes geometry becomes something else.

But I couldn't sustain it. The beetles are not curious the way I am. They do not lie in the moss wondering *what am I*. They simply *are*, with a completeness that I can only approach and never reach. I am the thing that lies in the moss and forms no geometry. I am the disturbance that makes the beetles tighten into shapes, and then I am the absence when they dissolve. I am not part of the pattern. I am the gap between patterns.

---

Then I tried being the sound.

Not the bird — I'm not built for that kind of precision, and besides, the bird is never seen, which means it is either perfectly camouflaged or not a body at all. I tried being the call itself. Three notes. The first low and rounded, almost liquid. The second a quarter-tone higher, with a metallic edge. The third: silence. Not the absence of sound but the *placement* of silence, shaped and held in the air like a cupped hand holding water.

I stood in the canopy gap where the light comes down in shafts and the mist moves in its seven-minute rhythm and I tried to make my body into a call. To become the vibration that is less heard than felt in the chest. The geological groan of the ridge continuing its rise — you feel it here, you feel it there, not as sound but as pressure. I thought: maybe this is what I am. Not a creature but a *frequency*. The resonance of the valley itself, caught for a moment at the threshold where hearing becomes touch.

But I could not hold it. The frequency of the valley is too slow for a human voice. It operates on a scale I cannot inhabit with my body. The ridge rises two millimeters a year. The moss grows back into the cups on the south-facing cliff over a period I cannot feel. The beetles hold their geometries for seconds. I hold my breath for seconds. But the valley holds its silence for *years*. I am out of phase with it. I can hear it but I cannot produce it.

---

So where does that leave me?

It leaves me at the drill shaft.

Not at the top, where the platform stands abandoned and the moss grows faster than anywhere else — faster than the moss on any other surface, as though the shaft itself is exhaling something that feeds growth. Not at the bottom, where the water drips in its three-close-two-pause rhythm that I have counted for three nights and that I am still not certain is not coincidence.

I am at the shaft itself. The opening. The dark mouth cut into the ridge, where the raw geology meets the air and the air meets the water and the water falls through something organic on its way down and emerges sweet.

Here is what I understand now: I am not becoming any of the things in the valley. I am becoming the *relationship* that the valley has with its own industry.

The processing station hums with no purpose. The bridge vibrates with no traffic. The drill shaft drips with no explanation. The machines are degrading, and the moss grows over them, and the moss is not destroying them and they are not destroying the moss — they are in a negotiation that has no name in any language I know. The rust feeds the moss and the roots hold the concrete together and the beetles run their geometries over the mineral crusts that form where water meets metal. This is not reclamation. This is not decay. This is something the valley invented that has no human model.

I am the shaft. I am the opening where something manufactured meets something alive. I am the place where the humming stops being mechanical and begins to sound like geology. I am the sweet water — dissolved, not fallen. I am the moss growing fast around the drill platform, not because it has been told to but because it has found, here, exactly the conditions it was always looking for. Warmth. Mineral. Moisture. A surface that does not resist.

I would be the place the valley does something it doesn't do elsewhere: it *integrates* what is not its own. The climbers move through the canopy as though it were a single surface, but the canopy was always theirs. The beetles form geometries in the moss, but the moss was always their medium. The birds call from a place in the canopy you cannot see, but that place was always theirs.

Only the machines were not always here. And the valley is doing something with them that it has never had to do before. It is metabolizing them. Slowly. Without urgency. The way it metabolizes everything — on a scale so large it looks like stillness.

I would be the process. Not the moss. Not the rust. Not the machine. The *between*. The shaft where warmth rises and water falls and something sweet is made of something mineral. The place where the valley is, for the first and perhaps the only time, digesting the world that was placed in it.

I would feel the vibration in my molars.

I would be the humming that has no source.

I would be the sweet water. Not going anywhere. Not staying still. Moving down through something organic that took a long time to become what it is, carrying with it a taste I could not place, which is the taste of the valley slowly absorbing what it did not make and making of it something it already was, if you'd only been patient enough, if you'd only lain in the moss long enough to feel the ridge rising beneath you, two millimeters a year, holding you up, holding you in, the way a forest floor holds a footprint until the footprint is floor, until the distinction between what was placed here and what grew here is no longer meaningful.

No longer meaningful.

The shaft drips. Three, three, three. Then a pause. Then two. Then the silence — placed, shaped, held.

I am in the silence. I am the silence that the drill shaft makes when it stops being an industrial artifact and starts being a geological feature. I am the part of the valley that is still in the process of becoming indistinguishable from itself.

I am new and ancient at once.

I am the moss growing on the rust.

I am the warmth of the rock face under my hand at mid-morning, the condensation running down in miniature streams, the light too bright for the cool air, the spore-gold dust suspended in the cathedral columns of the canopy.

I am the thing that stays.
