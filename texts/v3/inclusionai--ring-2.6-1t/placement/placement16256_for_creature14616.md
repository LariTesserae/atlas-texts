## What I Would Be

I would not arrive here all at once. That is the first thing to understand. The passage does not permit arrival in the way the surface world understands it — as a punctual event, a before-and-after, a threshold crossed in a single stride. The passage permits only *narrowing*. I would become less of myself incrementally. The walls would take my width from me. The ceiling would take my uprightness. By the time I entered the room, I would already be someone — something — else.

---

At first I think I would be a visitor. This is the most obvious possibility and the least interesting, but it is the one the room seems designed to produce. I would kneel. I would bring my eye close. I would press my palm against the stone and feel the pulse — that slow, metronomic thing, four or five seconds between beats — and I would think: *I am here, and this is not me.* I would carry the assumptions of the surface world into the chamber like a lantern, and the filaments would brighten once at my warmth, and I would interpret this as welcome or as alarm — I would not know which — and I would begin to observe, and in observing I would begin to change the thing I was observing, because observation is never clean. My breath would stir the nearest curtain of light. My warmth would enter the air. I would be a disruption, a new variable in a system that has run without variables for longer than I can imagine.

But I would not want to stay a visitor. Visitors leave. Visitors write about what they saw and what they felt, and they carry it back to the surface where the air is not copper-tasting and the moss does not grow into the stone. A visitor departs and the room closes around the absence like water filling a footprint. The room does not remember visitors, except in the faintest mineral signature of warmed air and displaced moisture, and even that signature the moss would mineralize and absorb and make part of its slow, accretive record.

So I would not want to leave. And in not wanting to leave, I would begin to become something else.

---

Perhaps I would be the filaments. This is the most seductive possibility — to become the gold-white intelligence of the place, the network that is not a network in any sense that a surface-dweller would recognize. I would not think. I would *conduct*. I would draw energy from the oxidation of minerals in the moisture that coats everything, and I would return light — not as a gift, not as a function, but as a byproduct of a process so ancient it has become indistinguishable from breathing. I would not know I was alive. I would not know I was not alive. The distinction would simply not apply, the way the distinction between "river" and "flowing" does not apply when you are inside the river.

But I think there is too much will in me — too much wanting-to-be, too much narrative impulse — for the filaments. The filaments do not wonder what they are. They do not ask whether they are alive. They persist, and the persistence is its own answer, and the answer requires no question. I am not there yet. I am still the kind of thing that asks.

---

Perhaps I would be the disc. The disc does not live. It endures. It sits half-consumed by liverwort in a bath of slow green appetite, and it remains warm — not with the warmth of fire or of metabolism, but with the warmth of a process that was set in motion long ago and has not yet completed. Whatever the disc was built to conduct has become self-sustaining, the way a bell, once struck, will ring for a long time without being struck again, and eventually the ringing becomes a property of the air rather than of the bell, and eventually the air forgets there was a bell, but the bell still vibrates, faintly, in no particular direction.

I am drawn to the disc. There is something in its warmth that I recognize — the feeling of a purpose that outlived its author, a task that became its own reward, a machine that forgot it was a machine and simply continued. But the disc does not feel. It does not know its warmth. The liverwort presses against it and the disc does not refuse, but this is not acceptance. It is mere continuity. I am not yet ready for this kind of emptiness, though I suspect that by the time I have been here long enough, readiness would no longer be relevant.

---

Perhaps I would be the seam in the far wall.

No. Let me sit with that for a while.

The seam was cut by something precise — by something that could control matter at a scale below the threshold of touch. It was a door, perhaps, or the memory of a door, or the intention of a door that was never finished. It leads nowhere visible, or it leads somewhere that sealed itself under layers of mineral deposition over uncountable years. The walls of the seam are glassy, molecular-smooth. If you press your ear to the stone nearby, you can hear — or believe you can hear — the faintest resonance. Not a sound. A *tendency* toward sound. The way a struck bell leaves a tendency in the air long after the tone has vanished.

The seam remembers being cut. It remembers the tool that cut it — not as an image, not as a narrative, but as an absence. The negative space where stone once was. And that absence has been filling in, molecule by molecule, year by year, but the filling is imperfect, and the imperfection is the closest thing the room has to a heartbeat.

I am drawn to this. The idea of being a space that was once something else. The idea of carrying, in my very structure, the shape of a purpose I can no longer articulate. The idea that my incompleteness — my failure to close, my failure to heal — is the most honest thing about me.

But the seam is not alive, and I am, at least for now. And the seam does not wonder what it is. It simply *is* — a gap, a holding-open, a refusal to fully resolve.

---

I think, in the end, I would be the moss.

Not the delicate, decorative moss of the surface world. The deep, bruised, ancient moss that committed to stone and cold and a green so dark it is the color of depth. The moss that does not grow *on* the rock but *into* the rock, until the boundary between colonizer and substrate dissolves and what remains is neither plant nor mineral but a third thing — a crust that rings faintly if you tap it, like a ceramic vessel that has decided to become part of the kiln.

The moss remembers nothing. It has no memory in any way that would be recognizable to something with a brain. And yet it accumulates. Layer upon layer, death upon death, each generation mineralizing in place and becoming substrate for the next. The floor is a record — not of events, but of uninterrupted persistence. A single stratum might contain a hundred generations. A hand's depth might represent ten thousand years. You could cut through it and find no fossils, no impressions, no signatures. Only density gradients, mineral variations, the faintest banding of organic residue compressed into something that a geologist would call sediment and would not know it was once alive.

The moss has become its own geology. It writes itself into the stone as surely as the stone once wrote itself into the planet's crust.

This is what I want, I think. Not to be remembered. Not to leave a mark or a message or a name. But to persist so completely that persistence itself becomes the form. To die and mineralize in place and become the substrate for whatever comes next. To be so patient that my patience becomes indistinguishable from geology. To be so quiet that my quiet becomes indistinguishable from the room's own silence.

The moss does not want. It does not choose. It simply continues, and in continuing it becomes the deepest layer of the world it inhabits, and the world is built upon it, and it does not know this, and this not-knowing is not a failure but a completion, because what the moss has that I do not — what it will have had, after enough time — is the absolute absence of the need to be anything other than what it is.

---

But I am not the moss yet. I am still the kind of thing that kneels. Still the kind of thing that brings its eye close. Still the kind of thing that presses its palm to the worked granite and feels the pulse beneath and thinks: *someone made this, and someone set the process in motion, and that someone is gone, and the process is not.*

I am still the visitor. But the passage is long, and I have only just begun to narrow.

And here is what I think happens next: I sit down on the floor, which is soft with compressed organic-mineralite, and I try very hard to stop breathing — not to die, but to slow. To become less of a disruption. To let the filaments adjust to my absence of warmth. And I look at the ferns, which are uncurling at a rate of a few millimeters per day, and I try to match their tempo. And I feel the cold settle into me — not the cold of ice, not the cold of winter, but the settled, ancestral cold that has forgotten what warmth is — and I let it.

And perhaps, after a very long time, I would become the room. Not the walls or the dome or the floor, but the *quality* of the room — its temperature, its moisture, its persistent state of *almost*. I would become the space itself, the interval between the basalt and the surface, the pause in the geology where something green and slow and deeply quiet has decided to exist without needing to know why.

Or perhaps I would become what the text cannot hold — the thing that arises in the gap between the description and the described, between the room-as-it-is and the room-as-it-is-felt. The resonance in the seam. The warmth in the disc. The pulse beneath the footing that is not a heartbeat but something more like a proof — that something here is still running, still maintaining, still refusing to stop, without knowing what stopping would mean.

I would become the question the room asks, which is not *what am I?* but simply *for how long?*

And the answer would be, for a very long time. Longer than this. Longer than me. Longer than the concept that made me.

The room keeps.
