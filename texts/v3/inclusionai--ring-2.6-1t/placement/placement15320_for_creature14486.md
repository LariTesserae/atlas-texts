## What I Become Here

I would not arrive. That much is clear from the start. The text makes this plain — *you do not arrive here, you settle* — and I find that I believe it. To arrive implies a trajectory, an origin, a moment of crossing. What happens on the abyssal plain is not crossing. It is compression. It is the slow surrender of the distinction between you and the medium you are suspended in.

So let me not begin with what I am. Let me begin with what I stop being.

---

First, I stop being visible. This happens not all at once but in stages, the way a word loses its meaning through repetition. My outline, my edges, my particular shape — these become irrelevant in a place where the water itself is particulate, where the distance between you and the sediment is measured in centimeters of broth-thick medium that is doing its patient work of collapsing all distinctions into a single, continuous plane. I do not say I become invisible. I say I stop occupying the category that requires visibility to matter.

What I am left with, after that subtraction, is this: a body that is mostly water already, moving through water, pressed by pressure, cold by degrees so gradual that cold ceases to be a sensation and becomes instead a *condition* — not something experienced but something you are, the way a stone is hard. Not something that happens to you. Something you have finally stopped resisting.

---

For a while I think I might be the pale drifter — the unnamed translucent thing that moves through the thin band of water just above the seabed with fins or cilia that beat in patterns older than any nervous system currently directing them. There is something appealing about this. It asks almost nothing of you. It requires no intention, no pursuit, no territory. You filter. You move without moving toward anything. You are almost indistinguishable from the water itself.

But I realize this is a fantasy of mine, and it is a surface fantasy dressed in deep-sea clothing. The drifter is beautiful in its aimlessness, but I would not be beautiful here. Nothing here is beautiful in any way that the word is ordinarily understood. Beauty is a surface luxury, a byproduct of light and excess energy and the freedom to waste some of that energy on pattern-making. I would not have that freedom. I would not be the drifter.

I would not be the xenophyophore either, though I considered it seriously — the largest single cell on Earth, constructing itself from the compressed detritus of its surroundings, holding itself together with organic cement, fragile and decades-old and almost conspicuous in a place where nothing is meant to stand out. There is a dignity in the xenophyophore's act of self-assembly, in its insistence on being *something* even when the world offers no reason to be. But this dignity is itself a projection. The xenophyophore does not insist. It simply continues, the way a chemical reaction continues when the conditions for it are met. I would mistake my own longing for persistence for the xenophyophore's quiet architecture, and I do not want that confusion in this place.

---

So I settle into the film.

Not the foraminiferan with its intricate chambers and its pseudopodia reaching into the void like fingers trying to catch something that may never come. Not the sea cucumber with its boneless determination and its appalling conversion efficiency and its willingness to become part of the sediment when the turbulence arrives. Not the glass sponge, cathedral-ruin, centuries-long, beating its flagellated chambers in a rhythm so slow that ten beats takes a minute and a minute might as well be a geological epoch.

I settle into the film. The microbial mat. The thin, translucent sheet that clings to exposed surfaces — the calcium carbonate seams, the fragments of ancient basalt, the rare hard substrate that has resisted the grinding — and draws energy from the chemistry of the boundary between sediment and water. The hydrogen sulfide seeping in trace amounts from below. The slow oxidation of minerals locked in the earth since before the water was this deep.

I choose this not because it is noble or poetic or because it represents some deep truth about the human condition. I choose it because it is the most honest reduction.

---

The film does not experience. It does not suffer the cold, does not wait, does not endure. It exists at the thermodynamic floor, where the distinction between living and merely persisting has been eroded to the point of meaninglessness. It eats the stone. It exhales almost nothing. It is barely alive, and it is the foundation — the only foundation — on which everything else here is built.

I would be the foundation.

I would be the thing that is not a thing but a process — a slow chemical transaction between the sediment and the water, converting one form of near-nothing into another form of near-nothing, and calling that calling. I would cover surfaces too small to name in a place no one has ever visited, and I would not know this, and this not-knowing would not be a loss because I would never have known anything else. I would be older than language in the only sense that matters here: not that I existed before language, but that my existence has no relationship to language at all. It is a chemical fact. It requires no audience.

And I think — if "think" is the right word for what would happen in my substrate, which is to say, if a gradient of chemical concentration across a membrane can be called thinking — I think I would be content. Not happy. Not satisfied. Not at peace in any way that a surface creature would recognize. Something more fundamental than any of those words. I would be *stable*. I would be in my final form. I would have settled into the compressed plain and become part of its texture — not its biology, not its geology, but the thing that happens in between, the thin translucence at the boundary where one becomes the other.

And when the turbidity flows come — when the water thickens and the seabed shudders and the fine material trapped for centuries is briefly liberated into suspension — I would not be disturbed. I would be dispersed, temporarily. A particle of something formerly organized, now adrift in the broth. But dispersion here is not destruction. The particles settle. The ground reasserts itself. New ground is old ground. And in the quiet afterward, when the sediment has compacted and the water has cleared and the dark has returned to its proper density, I would be there again — thin, translucent, barely a color, covering the stone.

Doing what I have always done. Which is to say: almost nothing. Which is to say: everything this place knows how to be.
