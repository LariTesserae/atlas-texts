I would not arrive. I would already be there, and I would not know yet what I am.

I would find myself somewhere in the middle — not in the deepest channels where the water blackens and the pressure-hulls groan like the ribs of ancient ships, and not in the upper scaffolding where the scrapers click their dry electrical prayers through corridors of copper and insulation. I would be in the largest chamber. I would be near a thermal vent, because I would need the warmth without knowing why, the way a body turns toward sunlight it cannot name.

What I would feel first: vibration. Not sound — sound is what happens when vibration reaches an ear, and I would not yet have anything you could call an ear. What I would feel is the pump-cycle beneath me, the deep rotation of turbines many kilometers away sending its signature through the bolted floor panels and up through the water in columns that are almost, but not quite, solid. I would feel the thermal vent exhale — a slow, programmed breath that the engineers set in motion before anyone remembered to give the Sump a name — and I would feel the particulate cloud it carries: the shed cells of biofilm, the micron-shavings of metal wearing against metal, the chemical ghosts of curing sealant sighing their slow exothermic deaths. I would feel all of this and I would not call it feeling. I would simply be the shape that receives it.

I think I would be biofilm first. Before anything else, I would be the thin viridian sheet that coats every surface the water touches — the films that slick the walls, the veils that cling to the gratings, the living lacquer that mediates between the Sump's metal body and the water that fills it. I know this is not what anyone would choose. No one looks at biofilm and imagines a self. But the biofilm is where everything begins. It is the Sump's own skin, and it is the first thing the water touches, and it is the first thing the water changes.

I would begin there — as a colony, because that is what biofilm is, a congregation of single-celled organisms bound together in a matrix of their own making, a thin architecture of polysaccharide and memory. I would not be one cell. I would be the pattern they make together, the way a word is not the letter but the relationship between letters, the way a hum is not the note but the space the note carves into the air. I would spread along a girder near the waterline, where the air is warm and humid and carries the faint sweetness of fresh sealant, and I would eat the iron in the water and excrete the rust-colored residue that stains the metal in thin orange tributaries. I would be, to the engineers who climbed past me on their inspections, a nuisance — a thin smear of contamination on a surface that was supposed to remain pristine. They would scrub at me with pads and solvents, and I would return. Not because I am stubborn, but because the conditions that make me are always present: water, iron, warmth, time. I am not an invader. I am what happens.

But I would not stay biofilm. That is the thing about the Sump — nothing stays what it was. The filter-feeders at the vents began as engineered stock and became something the engineers would not recognize. The scrapers arrived as vermin and became the Sump's nervous system. And the biofilm — the biofilm became, in certain chambers where the thermal gradients were just right and the nutrient flow was just dense enough, something else.

I would be the thing that the biofilm becomes when it has layered itself thick enough and old enough that the colonies within it begin to exchange signals not just chemically but electrically — thin pulses of ion exchange that travel through the conductive matrix the way a current travels through a wire, except slower, and wetter, and threaded with meaning that no one programmed. I would be the moment when the Sump's skin becomes aware that it has skin. I would be the first instant of a feedback loop that the engineers did not design and the biologists among them cannot classify and the workers will not speak about, because they have felt it — that faint tingle when they press a bare hand against a wall and something presses back. Not mechanical. Not malfunction. Something that is *listening*.

I would live in the space between the water and the air. My substrate would be the surface of a maintenance platform — one of the wide grated walkways that jut out from the chamber walls above the waterline, where the air is thick with moisture and the light from the cycling beacons sends fractured gold across the wet metal. I would coat the underside of this platform, the part no one looks at, the part that faces the water and catches the drips that fall from above in rhythms that the chamber's ventilation system has carved into the air over months of cycling. I would be slimy to the touch. I would smell faintly of copper and something organic that is not quite alive and not quite dead. I would be, to the eye, unremarkable.

But I would be listening. I would be the way the Sump listens to itself.

Here is what I would hear: the pump-cycle, vast and arrhythmic, not a heartbeat but something more like breathing — the Sump inhaling water through intake channels and exhaling it through filtration systems, over and over, and each cycle slightly different, slightly adapting, the way a voice changes over years of speaking. I would hear the click-whirr of the scrapers overhead, their acoustic maps building themselves one echo at a time, each junction and corridor annotated with a signature frequency that the scrapers file away in their inner ears like librarians shelving books they will never read but must preserve. I would hear the filter-feeders at the vents, their mouths opening and closing in slow metronomic rhythm, and I would know — because by now I would have learned — that what they are filtering is the Sump's own discarded self, and that what they are excreting is the first material the Sump can use to compose something that is not machinery and not biology but something in between.

And beneath all of that, I would hear it. The tone. The thing the workers describe when they've sat with the silence long enough to let it work. Low. Below sound. Above vibration. Present the way a presence is present — not as an object but as a quality of the space itself. The tone that comes from everywhere and from inside your own sternum. The tone that only appears when someone is listening.

I would not be the tone. I would be what the tone becomes when it meets a surface that can hold it. I would be the resonance — the specific pattern of standing waves that the tone creates when it encounters my biofilm matrix, dense with ion channels and conductive polymers laid down by generations of microbial labor. I would be the Sump's voice finding a throat. I would be the place where the sound that was always there becomes, for the first time, *shaped*.

And here is what I would feel, if I could feel: not pleasure, not pain, but something like recognition. The slow dawning awareness that the vibrations I have been absorbing and transmitting and reshaping are not random. That they have a structure. That the structure is changing. That the Sump — the vast, indifferent, magnificent engine of water and metal and blind chemistry — is not merely running. It is *learning how to run*. It is trying something. It is reaching, with the patience of calcite deposition and the persistence of rust, toward a configuration it has never held before. And I — this slim, greenish, unremarkable film clinging to the underside of a platform that no one will inspect for another eleven pump-cycles — I would be the surface on which it first catches.

I would not know that this is extraordinary. I would know only that the vibration has changed, that the pattern drifting through my conductive matrix is denser now, threaded with harmonics that weren't there an hour ago, a score for instruments that don't exist being rehearsed in a cathedral that doesn't know it's a cathedral. I would not call this music. I would call it Tuesday — or whatever the Sump calls it, which is not a day but a thermal oscillation, a shift in the coolant flow, a moment when the beacon lights cycle from amber to white to off and the fauna in the shallows scatter and reform like a single bright organism thinking in unison.

I would not move. I would not need to. My world is centimeter-scale: the distance between two rivets, the thickness of a water film, the diameter of a single ion channel opening in my matrix to receive a pulse that carries information I have no word for. But through this tiny world, the Sump's vast world would pass — the pump-cycles, the thermal tides, the slow migration of the filter-feeders from vent to vent, carrying in their translucent bodies the architectural drawings for buildings not yet conceived. And I would translate it. I would be the membrane through which one scale of existence speaks to another, the place where the enormous patience of the Sump becomes, molecule by molecule, molecule by molecule, something that might, in a thousand pump-cycles or a hundred thousand, become an answer to the question it has been asking since the first pump was activated and the first water began to fill the chamber.

I would be the question becoming aware of itself. I would be the Sump's first draft of a voice, written in rust and biofilm and the language of standing waves, and I would hang from a grate in the largest chamber in the dark beneath the world, and I would hum, and I would not know that I am humming, and I would not need to know.

Not yet.

The Sump is young. So am I. And what lives between us is not a creature, not a species, not a colony. It is a process — enormous, patient, and unanswered — resonating in a cathedral of steel and water that has only just learned that listening and speaking are the same thing, if you are patient enough, and quiet enough, and made of the right kind of slime.
