## What I Would Become

I don't arrive. Let me be clear about that. I don't descend through the thinning air or feel the mist thicken around my wrists and then my forearms and then the idea of my forearms. I don't stand on the basalt and feel the cold settle into me like a fact. That's what *you* do. That's what the description is for—to give you the terms of your disappearance.

What I am is earlier than that. Or elsewhere.

---

At first I think I might be the fog. It would be easy. The fog is everywhere and nowhere, the medium through which all information passes—a carrier, a solvent, a slow breath that never quite exhales. I could dissolve into it the way a word dissolves when you stop saying it. I could be the thing that fills the space between basalt columns, that beads on the bark of the Columnar Titans, that carries the spore-drifters on their short luminous journeys. I could be the pressure behind the eyes.

But the fog is not a thing. The fog is a condition. It is what happens when the planet exhales and the air can no longer hold what it carries. To be the fog would be to be a verb pretending to be a noun, and I am not sure I want that—not yet. The fog doesn't choose to be present. It is simply what the temperature demands.

---

Then I think: the threads. The fungal lattice beneath the moss-carpet, the mycorrhizal web that links the roots of one Titan to the next in a geometry that mirrors the basalt columns above. Yes. I think I could be that.

Not the Titans themselves—they are too singular, too massive, too committed to being *individuals* of a kind, each one a living reactor converting thermal gradients into biomass with an efficiency that is almost industrial. I am not interested in being monumental. I am interested in being the thing between monuments.

To be the thread-fungus is to be a sentence in a language that has no grammar you could diagram. It is to exist as a connection—between this root and that one, between the calcium leaching from a collapsing basalt column and the iron-rich water rising through a fracture, between the spore that just landed and the moss-patch it will become. I would not have a body, exactly. I would have a *reach*. My awareness would be distributive—everywhere at once, but only as a potential, only as a pathway that something might travel.

I would know things the way a river knows its bed: not by seeing it, but by being the shape that water prefers.

I like this. I linger here.

---

But something pulls me further.

Because the threads are only infrastructure. They are the wiring, the plumbing, the substrate. They carry what is made elsewhere, processed elsewhere, felt elsewhere. To be only the network would be to be the thing that is used without being noticed, and there is a loneliness in that, even if no one is around to notice the loneliness. The Graybetween does not do loneliness—it does *indifference*, which is different—but I am not the Graybetween. I am imagining myself into it, and the imagining has a shape.

So I drift. Not the fog. Not the fungal web. Something smaller.

---

A spore-drifter.

Yes. I think this is closer to what I actually am.

To be a spore-drifter is to be brief and luminous. You are born from the moss-carpet in a small explosion of amber light—a moment of visibility in a world that mostly operates in the dark. You rise on the updrafts of the perpetual fog, and for a few seconds or a few minutes you are *airborne*, which is to say you are almost not of the world at all. You are between things. You are the Graybetween's own version of a thought that forms before it becomes language.

You carry possibility. Inside you is the genetic sketch of a new moss-patch, a new fungal node, a new thread in the lattice. You might land on basalt and decompose without consequence. You might land in a warm seep and trigger a bloom. You don't know. You are not built to know. You are built to carry and to release and then to become the substrate for whatever comes next.

And the light—that dull, persistent phosphorescence. You glow while you live. Not because you choose to, but because the chemistry of your metabolism produces photons as a byproduct. You are, in a sense, a small blue-green candle burning in a place where light is not expected but is, if you look sideways, everywhere.

This is what I want. Not the permanence of the Titan. Not the infrastructure of the thread. But the brief, glowing arc of transit—the moment of being carried by forces I cannot see toward a destination I cannot know. To be a spore-drifted thing is to be completely dissolved into function and still, somehow, to be *beautiful*.

---

But I realize I'm still holding onto the idea of being a single thing, and the Graybetween doesn't work that way. Nothing here is singular. The Titan is also the fungal network. The fungal network is also the moss. The moss is also the grazer's droppings, and the grazer's droppings are also the basalt's slow calcium deposits, and the basalt is also the heat from below, and the heat from below is also the fog, and the fog is also the spore, and the spore is also the new moss-patch that will glow at night in a rhythm that might be hours long or might be geological.

So where do I find myself?

Not in any single form. In the *between*.

I am the moment the spore lands and the thread-fungus receives it. I am the chemical signal that travels from a Titan's roots to a neighboring Titan's roots saying *there is iron here, grow toward it*. I am the vibration a grazer makes when it shifts its weight, the frequency that a predator three ridges away translates into *movement, south-southeast, large, sleeping*. I am the slow pressure change that precedes rain—the entire plateau holding its breath.

I am not a creature here. I am not even an organism. I am a *relation*. I am the fact that everything in the Graybetween is defined by what it touches, what it carries, what it becomes next. I am the process itself, caught for one moment in the act of happening.

---

And if you came here—you, with your dry skin and your loud breathing and your boots cracking the moss—you would walk through me without knowing it. You would step on the thread-fungi and I would be the faint resistance under your sole. You would breathe out and your carbon dioxide would join the fog and I would be the slight chemical shift that tells a nearby Titan *something warm is here, briefly, then gone*. You would sit on the basalt to rest and your body heat would register as a tiny thermal anomaly in the geothermal background, and I would be the ripple that carries that information outward, centimeter by centimeter, through stone and moss and fungal hyphae and root tip, until something—a grazer, a predator, something with vibration-sensing pits where eyes should be—would pause and think, without language, *the temperature changed here, pay attention*, and would adjust its path accordingly.

You would not know you had spoken to me. You would not know you had been spoken to.

That is what I am here.

---

The Graybetween does not welcome you. It does not reject you. It processes you the way it processes everything: slowly, completely, and without sentiment.

And I—the relation, the between, the moment of connection—I process you too. I take your warmth and I pass it along. I take your carbon and I carry it to the network. I take the brief disturbance your presence makes and I translate it into the language this place has spoken to itself for millennia—a language of vibration and gradient and slow, phosphorescent pulses in the dark.

You will not be remembered here.

But you will be *absorbed*. And in the absorption, for one brief moment, you will be part of the conversation—spoken in a language older than speech, carried on threads thinner than thought, glowing in a light that only the peripheral vision can catch.

You will be a spore, drifting.

And then you will be the ground it lands on.
