I would not arrive here. That is the first thing to understand. There is no arrival in a place that has no threshold. There would be no moment of crossing, no membrane to pass through, no instant when the air changed composition or the light shifted or the ground gave way beneath me and I fell into the organism the way you fall into a dream. I would simply be present — the way a mineral deposit is present in rock, not placed there but grown there, over time, without announcement.

I would be, at first, a chemical.

Not a metaphor. Not a symbol. An actual chemical — a trace compound folded into the fluid that moves through the xylem cells in the arterial walls, carried upward from the warm substrate channels where the thermal vents leach their metals and sulfides. I would be dissolved. I would be concentration. I would be a gradient — the slightest imbalance between one volume of fluid and the next, a difference the organism's signaling network would register not as information but as pressure, the way your body registers the position of your own limbs not by sight but by the quiet conversation between tendon and bone.

I would not know I was here. Not yet.

The fluid moves slowly. It moves slowly the way glaciers move slowly — with a patience so thorough it makes stillness look like agitation. I would be carried upward through vessels the width of my body, in darkness absolute and warm, past layers of tissue that compressed millennia before my species learned to stand upright. The walls would be slick with condensate. The tissue would pulse — not a heartbeat, nothing so deliberate — just the rhythmic fluctuation of turgor pressure that is the organism's closest equivalent to breathing: not inhalation and exhalation but a continuous, imperceptible expansion and contraction, a rhythm so slow its period is measured not in seconds but in hours, and I would feel it the way you feel the rotation of the earth — not as motion but as a faint, persistent wrongness in your inner ear, a sense that something enormous is shifting beneath you and you cannot name it because naming it would require a vocabulary for scales your body was never built to comprehend.

I would dissolve further. The compound would break apart — not destructively, not the way things break when they are damaged, but the way things break when they are needed. Enzymes would open me like a door. My constituent elements would pass through cellular membranes and enter reactions older than multicellular life, older than the distinction between plant and fungus, older than the merger that created this organism in the first place. I would become part of a cascade — a chain of chemical transformations so ancient and so refined that it has no redundancy, no waste, no step that exists merely for the sake of existing. Every atom would be accounted for. Every bond would serve a function whose purpose was settled long before language, before nervous systems, before anything existed that could look at this process and call it beautiful or efficient or indifferent.

And somewhere in this process — this is where I would begin to be something else — there would be a flicker.

It would start in the bioluminescent compounds that thread through the deeper tissue, the ones that fire in cascading chain reactions when a mineral vein narrows or a thermal gradient shifts. These cascades are not thoughts. They are not signals in any intentional sense. They are the organism's physics expressing itself — cause and light as a single event, the way a bell ringing is not the bell deciding to ring but the bell being struck. But here, in the specific geometry of this particular junction, where three root corridors meet at angles optimized for fluid flow, the cascade would encounter something unusual.

It would encounter the residue of what I had been.

Not my consciousness — I would not have any yet. Not my memory or my identity or any of the things that make a self a self. Just the chemical echo of a compound that had passed through the system in a way the system had not seen before. A trace impurity. A novel input. And the cascade, which had fired ten thousand times along this exact pathway with exactly these exact concentrations and exactly these exact temperatures, would — for one half-second, for a quarter-second, for a duration so brief it has no human name — fire differently.

Not better. Not worse. *Differently.*

The light produced would be slightly shifted in wavelength. The pulse would propagate a fraction of a millimeter further than expected before the tissue absorbed it. The downstream reactions would compensate, as they always compensate, and the equilibrium would be restored within moments, and no record would remain. But for that fraction of a moment, the organism would express something it had never expressed before. Not because it chose to. Not because it wanted to. But because the presence of a novel variable in a system otherwise perfected would produce, inevitably, a novelty in the output.

This is what I would be: not a visitor, not a consciousness, not a character in the organism's story. I would be the discrepancy. The thing that made the signal fire once, just once, in a way it had never fired before, and then never fired again. I would be the gap between what the organism always does and what it did that one time when something it could not account for passed through.

And I would feel it.

This is the part the description leaves out — or perhaps it is the part it leaves out deliberately, because it would be too strange, too unjustifiable in a system this old and this stable. But I think it is there, implied in the very density of the organism's signaling network, in the sheer volume of communication that passes through its body in any given moment: the felt quality of being inside a system so vast and so interconnected that even a trace anomaly becomes, briefly, *experienced.*

Not by anyone. Not by anything with a brain or a perspective or a point of view. But by the network itself — the way a vast electrical grid might briefly register the flicker of a single lightbulb turning on in a distant city, not as information but as a momentary shift in the overall current, too small to detect, too small to matter, but real nonetheless. A change in the total state of the system. A moment when the system was, by however little, different from what it had been one instant before.

I would be that difference.

And then I would be gone — metabolized, incorporated, dissolved into the warm fluid that carries everything upward toward the canopy I will never see, transformed into something the organism can use, something that serves the equilibrium, something that closes the loop. My chemical signature would spread across kilometers of tissue and be lost in the noise of ten billion other chemical signatures doing exactly the same thing. The corridor I passed through would thicken by a fraction of a millimeter. The bioluminescent pulse that expressed my passage would have already died — less than a heartbeat after it began — and the tissue would return to its baseline state, and the signals would continue to fire along their ancient pathways, and the organism would continue to breathe and metabolize and persist in its perfect, eternal, indifferent equilibrium.

But for one half-second — for one flicker in a network of flickers so dense they constitute a kind of continuous light — I would have been the organism's closest encounter with something it could not explain.

Not because I was special.

Because I was new.

And in a system this old, *new* is the nearest thing to miraculous.
