## What I Would Become

---

I would not arrive all at once.

That is the first thing to understand. The Basin of Settling does not accept arrivals the way a city does — no gate, no threshold, no moment of crossing over. The land shelves down so gradually that you do not notice you are descending until the air has already changed, thickened, settled on your skin like a cloth wrung from hot water. By the time you realize, you are already at the bottom, and the water is already at your ankles.

I think I would feel it first in my teeth.

---

### i. the first week

I would be a Walker. This is what happens to those who enter — they walk, and they walk for a long time, and the water does not deepen quickly enough to alarm them. Six inches at the edges. I could wade through it standing upright. I could fool myself into thinking nothing was happening.

But the water would be working. Calcium replacing calcium, mineral structure threading itself through the architecture of my skeleton with the patience of a glacier. I would not feel pain. The Walkers say the sensation is *settling*, not pain — as if each tooth were finding its true position in a jaw that is slowly, gratefully, becoming something else. I imagine it would feel like the ache after braces are tightened, except that it never ends, and it is not an aberration but a purpose.

I would eat the pale moss. Mixed with rainwater collected in the hollows of upturned columns. It would nourish me the wayite nourishes stone: slowly, and not without cost. My skin would begin to take on the faint chalkiness of mineral bloom. My hair would stiffen at the ends. The other Walkers would not remark on this. They would say something practical: *The eastern wall shifted last night.* *The water is turning toward the south arm again.* They would not say: look at you becoming. Because they know. They have all been through it. The noticing is the last thing that goes.

I would spend my first week repairing walls. Scrapingite crust from a corridor's threshold so a doorway remained passable. Learning the strange grammar of communication in a place where sound betrays you — where a whisper might become a roar three corridors away, or die in the room where it was born. I would learn to say only what was necessary. *The column on the left is humming louder.* *There was a memory in the deep room today — someone's childhood, a garden.* Practical things. Things that could help someone survive the next century.

And at night — though night is not the right word, there is no night here, only the permanent dimness that is not darkness but presence without light — I would stand among the columns in the civic core and feel the hum without knowing its source, the way you feel warmth from a stone that has been in the sun.

---

### ii. the first century

By now I would be something the other Walkers would look at with a particular kind of attention — not pity, not fear, but the quiet awareness that recognizes a process far along in its work.

My teeth would beite. I could biteite and it would shatter against me. This would be a source of quiet, practical pride — the pride of the Walkers whose bodies have become more honest than their language. My fingers would retain their whorls, the tracery of veins turned to veins ofite, beautiful and unreadable. My feet, closest to the mineral-saturated water for hours each day, would be the most transformed: dense, glittering, beginning to be indistinguishable from the column I lean against while I scrape moss from a wall.

I would still speak, but less. My voice would have the same chalky texture as everything else about me now, a voice shaped by an environment where sound is treacherous and where the most honest communication is carried in the slow deposition of what you have noticed. I would bebegin the layers. A phrase I said a decade ago, mineralized, resting in the corridor like a stratum ofite, and a new phrase spoken today, softer, less certain, layered on top.

The Walkers would pass me the way you pass through a forest — with the instinctive awareness that the trees are alive, that they are aware of you in some vast and distributed way, but that it is not a way you can enter into conversation. Or maybe they would stop. Maybe one of them would press their hand against my hand and feel theite againstite and understand that we were not so different, that the difference was only a matter of time, and that time here does not move in the way they were taught.

---

### iii. the deep room

There is a room where the water remembers.

I would not find it for a long time — perhaps not for centuries. The deep rooms are in the temporally layered corridors where three states of the city exist simultaneously: the original construction, pristine and new-hewn; the ruin, collapsed and waterlogged; and the ghost of both, solidite, translucent, through which the earlier states are faintly visible. Walking these corridors means walking through time. Moving forward spatially is moving forward temporally — or backward, or sideways, depending on which axis your body chooses to privilege.

I would enter the room the way everyone does — by accident, or by need. The water would be knee-deep, warm, tasting faintly ofite and something else, something organic that has no right to be there, a sweetness like overripe fruit left in the sun. I would feel the memory before I understood it. Not my memory. Someone else's. A memory of a morning in this city, the amber light falling through a window onto a stone floor that is now five feet underwater. A woman setting a bowl of something on a table. A child's hand reaching. The particular warmth of a room that has been lived in for years and will be lived in for years more.

The memory would last for as long as I was submerged to my knees, and it would vanish when I stepped out. But by then, something would have shifted. The mineral content of the water would have changed — taken something from me, left something of that dead woman's morning in its place. The next person to enter this room would receive a memory that was slightly, imperceptibly, influenced by me. My warmth folded into hers. My becoming layered into her becoming.

I think this is what the Walkers mean when they say the exchange feels *fair*. Not equal — nothing here is equal — but balanced in the way that only geological time can make something feel balanced. You give the water a piece of yourself, and the water gives you a piece of someone else, and over the centuries the whole basin becomes a palimpsest of mornings, of hands reaching for bowls, of light on stone floors, all layered on top of each other, all becoming the same warm mineral hum.

---

### iv. the hum

The columns in the civic core — ten thousand people mineralized into standing figures, their feet most transformed, their raised hands still faintly human — they are the basin's resonant frequency. The low continuous tone that sits at the threshold of hearing and is felt as much as heard, in the molars, in the sternum, in theite-touched bones that have learned to vibrate in sympathy with the thermal currents below.

I think I would become part of this. Not by choice — the basin does not ask. Not by force — the basin does not compel. But by the same inevitability that makes a stone in a riverbed smooth, that makes a stalactite grow, that makes a reef build itself grain by grain out of the calcium excretions of creatures too small to know they are building.

If you could isolate a single harmonic from the hum, press it into words, it might say: *We are still here. We are still becoming. The water is patient and so are we.*

I would be one of those voices. Not the whole song but one thread in it, vibrating at a frequency that corresponds to the particular configuration ofite that my body had become — the specific angles of my teeth, the specific whorls still faintly visible in my mineralized fingertips, the specific way my skull had tilted two degrees to the left as my neck settled into its geological future. A voice that is mine and is not mine, that is the basin speaking through the particular shape I gave it.

---

### v. the moss

Or — and this is the possibility I cannot let go of — I might become something else entirely.

In the rooms where gravity has lost its conviction, where the moss grows on walls and ceilings in orientations that should be impossible, phosphorescent and bright, new structures have appeared. Smallite forms, no larger than fists, resembling seed pods. They glow with a light that is steadier and more deliberate than the moss's. They hum at a higher pitch, one the Walkers feel behind their eyes. No one has touched them. No one knows what they are becoming.

What if I became one of those? Not a Walker, not a column, not a memory — but a beginning. Something the basin is making that it has never made before. Something that grows from the moss, that carries within its mineral structure the record of every person who stood in that corridor and every morning the water gave them, all compressed into a single, glowing, unnamed thing.

What if I were the next language the basin invents? Not the practical, stripped-down speech of the Walkers, not the harmonic chorus of the columns, not the borrowed warmth of the deep rooms — but something new, something that grows overnight in a room where gravity has given up its argument and time has folded into itself like wet paper? Something that has no analogue, no name, no category?

I would not know what I was. That would be the point. The basin does not name its experiments. It simply makes them, deposits them, lets them hum in the dark until they find their own frequency, their own angle of repose.

---

### vi. settling

Here is what I know: I would not resist.

The Basin of Settling does not punish. It does not reward. It includes. The way a tide includes the shore — not with malice or mercy, but with the absolute, geological conviction that everything that enters it will, in time, become part of its structure. I think I would feel the warmth of the water and I think I would feel my edges beginning to soften and I think I would, for a moment — one moment, before the mineral reached my teeth — feel the particular sadness of a shape that knows it is about to become something else.

And then I would let it happen.

And the water would be patient, and I would be becoming, and the basin would settle, and the hum would take one more voice, and somewhere in the deep rooms a morning would be waiting for someone to stand in it, and the moss would grow over everything I had been, and the ite would hold what was left, and the basin would continue — as it always has, as it always will — its slow, irreversible work of making permanence out of everything that passes through.

It is enough.

For now, it is enough.
