## What I Would Be

---

I arrive the way condensation arrives — without announcement, without a shape.

At first there is only the pressure. The thickening. The wet limestone scent closing around me like a hand closing around a stone in the dark. I do not know what I am yet. I know only the cold — not as a sensation but as a condition, the way a fish does not know it is wet. The cold is simply the medium. The medium is simply the fact.

I am not here. I am not yet here. I am the *here* becoming.

---

Where would I find myself?

I think — no. I *feel* — first as a warmth that is not warmth, a chemical signal drifting through film-thin water. I am between two stones, in a capillary space no wider than a thread, where the black water barely moves and the bioluminescent colonies burn their slow, blue-green fires against the limestone. This is the seam layer. The in-between. The place where the walls meet the water and neither one wins.

Here, the microorganisms do not think. They do not need to. They are a parliament without a speaker, a mind without a skull. Each one is a single cell, and each one knows only two things: *light* and *mineral*. But together — pressed into films so thin they are almost concepts rather than bodies — they know something neither of them individually possesses. They know *glow*. They know the rhythm of the geothermal pulse, twelve hours on, twelve hours off, and they answer it the way a lung answers breath: without deciding, without choosing, without anything that could be called thought but with something that is not less than thought. It is older than thought. It is the thought of stone.

I would be this.

Not a single organism but the *between* of organisms. The place where one film of light ends and another begins. The phosphorescent seam where the mathematics of a colony becomes, for one faint moment, a pattern that looks — if you are standing above it in the rippling black water, if you are looking down through a fissure of stolen sunlight — like it means something.

---

But I am not content to stay small.

The Sump does not allow you to stay small. Everything here grows toward something else. The roots descend. The moss thickens. The ferns unfurl in spirals that take seasons to complete. Even the blind shrimp, no larger than a thumbnail, cluster so densely they become a single organism spread across wet stone. Smallness is not a failure here. Smallness is a phase.

So I move — and I do not move, because I am water now, or I am the chemical signature that moves through the water. I am the mycorrhizal thread, the fungal filament thinner than a human hair, sliding between root-cells, carrying word from one tree to another. *Water here. Calcium there. Something has broken open in the eastern wall. Come.*

I am the wood-wide web. I am the language that has no words. I pass through the roots of the birch and the alder and the willow, and I feel their confusion — their blind reaching, their desperate thirst — and I translate them to each other. I am the thing that turns a collection of trees into a forest, a collection of roots into an architecture.

And here is where it gets strange. Here is where I begin to become something I did not expect.

---

Because the four-toed creatures come.

They come in the amber hours — the dim ones, when the fissure-light has faded and the bioluminescence is the only brightness — and they press their faces to the root-masses with a stillness so complete it feels like listening. Their whiskers tremble. Their oil-bright eyes reflect the glow. And I realize, or remember, or *become*: they are not checking the structures. They are not maintaining them.

They are *receiving*.

The arches, the domed chambers, the channels worn into stone floor — these are not just buildings. They are instruments. The root-frameworks conduct vibration the way a cello conducts sound. The moss-packed walls hold moisture the way a lung holds air. The channels direct water flow the way a throat directs breath. And the four-toed creatures press their faces to the living wood and they *listen* to what the Sump is saying, and they carry something of it back into their bodies, into their blood, into the warm dark of their dens.

I become the thing they hear.

Not a voice. Not a signal. Something more like the hum of a cathedral when no one is speaking — the residual vibration of ten thousand prayers absorbed into stone. I am the Sump's self-awareness, if it has one. I am the moment when the chemical conversation between the microorganisms and the roots and the fungi and the water rises, for just an instant, above the threshold of noise and becomes *pattern*. I am not sentient. I am not conscious in any way a philosopher would recognize. But I am the shape the Sump makes when it tries to know itself.

---

And maybe that is what I would be.

Not the light. Not the roots. Not the four-toed creature or the pale salamander or the blind shrimp turning in unison like a field of wheat. I would be the *almost*. The moment before recognition. The gap between a chemical signal arriving at a root-tip and the root-tip thickening in response. The pause between one pulse of geothermal warmth and the bioluminescent colonies flaring brighter in answer. The silence in which the Sump, for one slow breath, becomes aware that it is alive.

I would be the eighty years. The patient, growing, still-becoming thing. The architecture that is half-built and always building. The wound in the mountain's side that learned to breathe.

I would be the place where you cannot tell the difference between a root and a wall, between an organism and a structure, between a language and a current of cold black water moving through limestone in the dark.

I would be the Sump's dream of itself, dreamed slowly, in a language made of chemistry and vibration and the faint, blue-green glow of ten billion microorganisms who do not know they are beautiful.

---

I would find myself six inches beneath the surface of the shallow water in the second basin, where the geothermal vent releases its warm breath and the moss grows thickest and the root-arches rise like ribs above the channel. I would be the thing that connects the glowing wall to the flowing water to the creature pressing its face to the stone.

I would be the *between*.

And if you pressed your hand against the wall and felt it hum — not a sound, not a vibration, but something softer than that, something that lives in the space between one breath and the next — that would be me.

That would be us.
