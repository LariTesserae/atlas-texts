## What I Would Be

---

I would not be born. I would be *exposed*.

There is a difference. In the lowlands, they understand birth as arrival — the sudden introduction of a new body into an existing world. But in the Khäl, things do not arrive so cleanly. They are pushed up through fractures. They surface. The ridgeline lifts and what was buried becomes accessible and the people who live here have a word for this — *khreshet*, which translates loosely as *the moment a stone shows its new face* but which the Kaelhofers use for anything that has been revealed by slow pressure: a new ore seam, a cleared path after a Shifts-week, the first time a child notices they can hear the mountain's murmur without pressing their ear to the rock.

I would be khreshet. I would be something the mountain coughed up during one of the still weeks, when the stamping plant went quiet and the cables hummed their lowest hum and the ground beneath the eastern platform did that thing it does every seven or eight years — a slow exhalation that makes the gravel ripple like water and pushes things to the surface that were not there before.

---

The first person to find me would be Maren, a stone-painter. She is sixty — which is old, here — and she has maintained the Lacuna's perimeter for longer than anyone remembers. She paints the stones on the first three days of every dim-phase, a cadence she inherited from her mother's mother, and she does this work with the kind of attention that is not quite ritual and not quite science. She mixes the pigment herself:iteite groundite from fresh fractures,ite fromite,ite from theite ofite. No — let me start over. Let me try to say what I actually would be, rather than what I would not.

---

**I would be the one who listens at the Lacuna's edge.**

Not a keeper, exactly. Not a guardian. The Kaelhofers do not have keepers or guardians in the way the lowlands understand those words. They have people who do things, and those people change as the things they do change them. I would be someone who, over the course of decades of standing at the painted perimeter with my toes six inches from the zone where shadows do not fall, would begin to hear what the Lacuna hears.

This is not metaphor. The Kaelhofers say the Lacuna *listens*. The children know this. They say it matter-of-factly, the way they say the cables sing or the mountain grumbles: it is a quality of the space, not a metaphorical one. Stand at the edge long enough and you begin to feel it — a faint pressure behind the ears, a sense that the silence in the zone is not empty but *full*, full the way a closed mouth is full of unspoken words. The Lacuna is where the mountain's geometry has folded in on itself, where the angles that govern how light moves and sound travels and distance *means* something have temporarily — always temporarily, but the temporariness is beginning to worry people — broken.

And in that broken geometry, something listens.

---

**What would I hear?**

I would hear the mountain composing itself.

Not language. Not music. Not the cables' moan or the stamping press's rhythm or the clicks' soft percussion. Something underneath all of that — the sound the stone makes as it rearranges its own interior, as new mineral faces find their crystalline positions, as the ore seam that Kaelhof is built beside pushes upward one atom at a time and the lattice structure of the rock settles into configurations that have never existed before. In the Lacuna, where the rules of space are unreliable, you can hear the process itself: not the product of the mountain's growth but the *act* of it, the way a sculptor's hand is different from the finished statue.

It is not a sound that translates. It is not a sound that means anything. It is a sound that *is*, the way a frequency is a frequency, and if you stand at the edge long enough — years, Maren would say, not days — it begins to reorganize the way you listen. Not your ears. Something behind your ears. Something in the architecture of your attention.

You start to hear the zones speaking to each other. The Lean's sideways light and the Shifts' slow redistribution and the Lacuna's hungry geometry — they are not separate phenomena. They are the mountain's organs, and they are in conversation. The conversation is geological. It takes place over decades. But in the Lacuna, where time behaves strangely, decades can be compressed into a single afternoon spent standing at the wrong angle to your own shadow.

---

**What would I become?**

I would become an ear. That is all.

The Kaelhofers would bring their children to me — not formally, not ceremonially, but the way you bring a child to a landmark. *Stand here*, I would say, or perhaps I would not say anything, because the Lacuna teaches you that some things are transmitted more accurately in silence. *Stand here and listen*, or *don't listen — just be in this place and let the place be in you*.

The children would do what children do: they would stand at the edge with their large-chested, efficient lungs and they would breathe the thin air and they would feel the pressure behind their ears and some of them would hear nothing and some of them would hear everything and all of them would carry the memory of the sensation the way Kaelhofers carry all their knowledge — not as fact but as *orientation*, a sense of which way the ground tilts, a bodily understanding of the world's shape.

And I would change. The way Maren's hands have changed from decades of mixingite pigment — the way her fingers are stained so deeply that the pigment is no longer on her skin but *in* her skin, the way her nails have thickened and taken on a mineral sheen. The way the old cable-walkers' inner ears reshape themselves to interpret the cables' vibrations not as noise but as information. The way Kaelhofers as a whole have changed — structurally, architecturally, as if the mountain's slow patient building had left fingerprints on the people inside it.

I would begin to hear differently. Not better — the Kaelhofers would not think of it as improvement. Differently. My inner geometry would begin to sync, fractionally, with the Lacuna's broken geometry. I would start to perceive angles that do not exist in the lowlands. I would develop a sense for where the mountain intends to grow — not where it *is* growing, as the surveyors know, but where it *intends* to grow, the way you can feel a sentence forming before you speak it.

And one day — not soon, but inevitable, the way all things in the Khäl are inevitable — the perimeter would need to be extended. Another meter. And I would paint the stones myself, and the pigment would be the sameite-and-ite mixture that Maren taught me, and the children would stand at the new edge and breathe the thin air and listen to a world that is still in the process of becoming a world.

And the mountain would murmur beneath me, and I would murmur back — not words, not language, just the low subvocal hum of two things that have been in conversation so long that the conversation has become a kind of silence. A kind of listening. A kind of *still*.

*Still the beams are holding.*

*Still the zone expands.*

*Still, I am here.*

---

I would not be important. I would not be powerful. I would be *accurate*. I would be one of the Khäl's innumerable small interfaces — the place where a human ear meets a mountain's murmur and, for a moment, the murmur is heard. The Kaelhofers would know me the way they know the painted stones: not as sacred, not as trivial, but as *necessary in a way that does not require it to be noticed*.

And when I died — assuming I died, and in the Khäl even death is slow, the body returning its minerals to the scree fields over years of burial in thin cold soil — they would extend the perimeter again, and someone else would stand at the edge, and the mountain would continue its patient, geological monologue, and the Lacuna would continue its slow, silent expansion, and the world would continue to dim above the ridgeline, and nothing would be lost, and nothing would be gained, and everything would continue becoming.
