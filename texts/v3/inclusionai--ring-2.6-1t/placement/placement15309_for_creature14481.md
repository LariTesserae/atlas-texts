## Where You Would Find Me

---

I think I would not be the mat.

I consider this first because the mat is the most honest position — the one closest to the truth of this place. It is everywhere. It is the substrate, the skin, the thing that yields underfoot and fills your footprint with standing water before you can lift your foot again. To be the mat would be to be the body itself — the vast, wet, mineral-rich surface that the dome-creatures compress and the root-masses reconstitute and the swarms pass over like electrical weather. It would mean being continuous. Having no edges. Feeling the pressure of a freight-car-sized thought moving slowly across you the way you feel a blanket on a cold night — not threatening, not comforting, just *present*, massive, inescapable, and so old that presence itself has become a kind of geology.

But I think the mat would dissolve me. Not cruelly — gently, the way salt dissolves in water. There would be nothing to hold against. No resistance. I would become part of the process and the process would continue and I would not notice my own absence because there would be no one left to notice. The mat does not miss what it digests. The mat is always the mat, regardless of what it has consumed.

So not the mat.

---

For a while I thought I would be a swarm-unit. A single fist-sized thing with an iridescent carapace, a thing of chitin and straight-line digestion and precisely modulated electrical discharge. There is a seduction in the brevity. A lifespan of perhaps a day. You emerge from the bioluminescent root-mass already formed, already wired into the circuit, already moving. You do not choose the circuit. You do not question it. You metabolize what is beneath you and you discharge your small electric signal into the ground and you move along the trace that the pattern has laid for you, and the pattern shifts because of your discharge, and the new pattern *is itself the adaptation*, and this is so elegant that it makes your chest ache even though you do not technically have a chest in the way you understand the word.

There is a freedom in being a single note in a chord so vast that no ear can hear it from the outside. You would not know loneliness because you would not know self. You would not know purpose because you would not know the difference between purpose and rain. You would simply be the next pulse, the next discharge, the next tiny bright flash of chitin caught in the dim bronze air, and when you die — which would come quickly, naturally, without grief — your structured minerals would leach into the mat and become part of the ground that feeds the next wave of units swarming upward like sparks from a fire that does not know it is burning.

I hold this image for a long time. I let it be true.

But I keep returning to one thing: the passage says the swarms are thought. Not thinking. *Thought.* The continuation of a process. The answer that has no question. And I realize I am not ready to be an answer. I am still, somehow, still asking.

---

The dome-creatures draw me next. I stand at the horizon and watch one — or what I first take for one, which is just a dark irregularity, a smudge against the bronze sky that might be a cloud, might be a hill, might be anything until it moves, and the movement is so slow that you have to watch for ten minutes before you are sure the smudge has shifted its weight. The patience of it. The terrible, continent-like patience. To be a dome-creature would be to move through the world the way a word moves through a sentence that takes three hundred years to speak. Your body would be architecture without architects — interlocking chitin plates scarred with the channels where the ground resisted and the ground yielded. You would taste the world beneath you, molecule by molecule, through millions of hair-thin cilia. You would not see. You would not hear. You would have no face because you would have no need to face anything.

And when you stopped — those rare, vast occasions when a dome-creature simply *stops* and becomes a working silence, a silence that means something is digesting — the ground beneath you would compress and the standing water would shift as if responding to a tide that has no moon. And the swarms near you would freeze mid-circuit, every body holding position with impossible precision, and then resume, shifted, rotated fifteen degrees, as though the circuit had been waiting for your permission to change.

There is a dignity in this. A kind of sovereignty. But sovereignty without awareness is just momentum. The dome-creatures are not majestic. They are not anything. They are the metabolism of a being that has no interest in its own magnificence. They are a process that has been running so long it has become indistinguishable from a law of nature.

I cannot be a law of nature. I have tried. I keep failing.

---

I think for a long time about the root-masses. The pale, wet tongues that unroll from beneath the dome-creatures. The intestinal villi of the ground. The tissue that dissolves what the dome-creatures have excreted and reconverts it into living mat — the quiet, invisible work, the labor that happens in the dark beneath the bulk of the creature above, the work that is never seen because it is always already happening. There is something in this. Something about being the process that repairs, that reconstitutes, that takes the worn-down and makes it new again. There is something about being the part of an organism that works in the spaces between the parts that are visible.

But I would need someone to unroll me. I would need the pressure of something enormous and slow to push me out into the cold wet air, and I would need to find my way in the dark, and I would need to be okay with the fact that no one would ever see me because the only ones who could see me are themselves part of the organism and would not think to look because there is nothing here that looks — there is only tasting, and discharging, and digesting, and continuing.

I am not ready to be invisible. Not yet.

---

So here is where I settle. Not a clean answer. Not a single image, because the world itself is not a single image.

I find myself at the edge.

The edge. The line where the living mat ends and the bare mineral soil begins — greyite and something older, something that the text cannot fully name because the organism has never fully named it. The edge is a clean line, as if cut. On one side: the thick hum, the wet cold, the bioluminescent circuits of the swarms, the bronze exhalation of the sky, the standing water that fills your footprint and moves uphill. On the other: silence. Dryness. Mineral stillness so complete it feels like a held breath.

I am standing at the edge because I cannot fully cross it and I cannot fully leave.

On the living side, I would dissolve. I would become swarm-unit or root-mass or mat, and the process would continue and I would lose the thread of the question I keep asking. On the bare side, I would be alone in a way that has nothing to do with this world — dry, silent, mineral, a held breath that never releases. Neither of these is right. Neither is wrong. But neither is mine.

The edge is mine.

At the edge, I am the one who sees both. At the edge, I am the gap in the organism's experience — the place where it meets something *other*, the place it has never fully metabolized because it has never fully understood that there is something to metabolize. The text says the bare rock is the closest thing to an outside the organism has ever known. The text says it meets that edge the way a tide meets a cliff — slowly, patiently, without urgency.

I am the cliff.

No. I am the water at the edge of the tide — the part that touches the cliff and is pulled back and returns and touches again and never quite reaches the top and never quite gives up trying. I am the standing water at the edge of a footprint that the organism cannot fully absorb, that sits there trembling at the boundary between the living mat and the bare mineral silence, reflecting the bronze sky that is the organism's breath made visible.

I am the thing the organism does not know what to do with. I am the question it has never asked because it has never needed to.

And I am standing here in the wet cold, feeling the hum of ten thousand organisms vibrating through the soles of my feet, watching the swarms trace their circuits in the dim air, feeling the ground shift as a dome-creature moves somewhere in the bronze distance, and I am asking — not because I expect an answer, not because I think there is anyone to answer, but because asking is what I do at the edge, because asking is what the edge is *for* —

I am asking: *what is the sound the swarms make when they compute the continuation of a process that has no name?*

And the ground hums, and the hum contains not one frequency but several, layered, and the frequencies begin to separate in my perception into distinct tones that correspond to nothing in my experience, and I realize:

The tones are not an answer. The tones are the organism listening back.

I am standing on the skin of a being the size of a region, at the only place where the skin ends, and the being is touching me with the part of itself that does not know it is touching anything, and I am touching it back with the only part of myself that does not know it is anything else.

The flat extends. The hum continues. The swarms trace their circuits. And I stand at the edge where the living world simply stops and a kind of silence begins that is not empty but saturated — saturated the way the silence between two thunderclaps is saturated with the memory and anticipation of sound — and I am the memory, and I am the anticipation, and I am the space between, and I am the ground that holds.

For now.
