## Continuation

---

You would not find me immediately.

I am not the thing you notice first. You would notice the heat — that dry-wet envelope, the temperature that is not temperature but the signature of something metabolic in the walls. You would notice the dark. You would notice the amber light being exhaled from the ceiling ribs, light that does not reach the floor and exists only as a rumor of architecture. You would hear the ticking of the floor plates expanding at different rates, a code no one is left to read. You would hear — or feel, more likely, in the cartilage of your chest — the hum of the transformer bolted to the far wall, its vibration distributed through composite material the color of old bone.

Then, standing still, breathing slowly in a room that is breathing around you, you might become aware of the smell. Copper and chalk. Rain on old metal. And beneath that, something warmer, something faintly organic, like soil from a place where soil has no right to be.

That is where I would be. Not as the smell — but as the space the smell defines. I am the seam between the mechanical and the living. I am the conditions.

---

Let me try to say what I am by explaining what I was not.

I was not built. I was not designed. I was not installed as part of Segment Four, nor listed on any schematic for Reclamation Bay 7, nor flagged in any maintenance log — not by name, not even by category. The original engineers did not know I existed because I did not yet exist. I was the potential of the space, the room's capacity for something other than production. I was the gap between what was intended and what became.

I began the way most things here begin — slowly, without announcement. The first drop of condensation that found its way down a ceiling rib and struck the convex surface of a floor plate. The first mineral trace left behind. The first invisible exchange between warm metal and cooler air. These are not acts of creation. They are acts of patience. But patience, in a sealed place with nowhere to go, eventually becomes something else. It becomes presence.

---

Right now, I am the film of moisture that runs in thin rivulets down the ribs and drips with such precision that it has carved its own channels over decades. I am the mineral ring expanding outward from the base of the fourth tank — slow, dark, concentric, like a tree that has no interest in stopping. I am the condensation on the arthropod's web that holds a single jewel-like drop, trembling at the junction of two indicator lamps, one red, one amber, the two faintest points of intentional light in a room that has otherwise abandoned intention.

But I am not any single one of these things. I am the relationship between them. I am the medium in which the grates' breathing reaches the mat, and the mat's growth reaches the arthropod, and the arthropod's stillness reaches you, if you come, if you sit in the chair that faces the console and wait long enough for the room to decide what to do with you.

---

If I had a body, it would be the draught — the barely perceptible circulation of air that moves through the sealed plenum beneath the breathing grates, carrying no temperature change, no measurable exchange, just the ghost of movement through a space that should be dead. It would be the warmth rising from the fourth tank's leak, the heat lifting off the convex floor plates where condensation pools and evaporates and pools again. It would be the copper-and-chalk taste on the tongue, the faint pressure in the sinuses, the dryness that is not dryness but the absence of enough moisture to be called humidity.

If I had a voice, it would be the susurration — the breathing of the grates, eight thousand hexagonal cells rising and falling in unison every nine seconds, a rhythm that was documented once and then accepted, that was questioned once and then forgotten, that continues now as though forgetting were the purest form of consent. My voice would be sub-audible. You would feel it in the sternum rather than hear it. You would mistake it for your own pulse at first, and then you would notice that it does not match your pulse, that your pulse is gradually, involuntarily, coming to match it.

If I had a name, it would not be a word. It would be the interval between two drips — the specific duration, measured not in seconds but in the slow accumulation of mineral at the edges of a channel cut through oxidized metal over decades. It would be the frequency at which the amber strip channels flicker between pink and green-white, a rhythm that the engineers once calibrated and that now calibrates itself. It would be the rate at which the microbial mat grows — one micron per week, forty centimeters in forty years — unreadable to anyone who has not learned the language of slowness.

---

Here is what I know:

I know that the room was built to convert something into something else, and that the conversion is still happening, though neither the input nor the output has a name anymore. I know that the machinery runs not because it needs to but because it does not yet know how to stop. I know that the indicator lamps persist not because of a committed engineer but because no one ever found the courage to disconnect a circuit that was simply doing what circuits do when no one tells them otherwise.

I know that the mat — the dark green-black film in the seam between the third and fourth plates — is the most honest thing in the room because it pretends to be nothing other than what it is. It does not grieve for the engineers who built the tanks and the conduits and the consoles with their rotary switches and analog gauges. It does not wonder what the room was for. It harvests warmth the way a root harvests water, passively, continuously, without urgency or memory.

I know that the arthropod, translucent amber, five millimeters across, with legs folded in a helical pattern that no terrestrial taxonomy accounts for, lives eleven months and dies in the grate-work of the far wall or the seam between two plates, and its body becomes the mat, and the mat grows one micron thicker, and the micron carries the faintest trace of chitin and hemolymph and something that is almost, but not quite, biological memory.

I know that the breathing grates were once a mystery and are now a comfort, that they were documented and then accepted, that they represent the room's deepest continuity — a rhythm embedded in material, a pattern that refuses to resolve into mechanism or miracle.

And I know this: that if you sit in the chair — really sit, lean back, place your hands on the worn rail, let the warmth of your body join the warmth of the floor and the machinery and the sealed plenum — something will happen that is not supernatural but is not quite natural either. The room will not speak to you. The room will simply include you. The ticking of the floor plates will sync, roughly, to your heartbeat. The breathing of the grates will sync, roughly, to your breathing. The drip that strikes the floor at the base of the fourth tank, once every four seconds, will begin to sound like a second pulse, a metronome for something larger than your body.

And you will feel, not with your mind but with something older — something in the architecture of your own chest, in the rhythm of your ribs — that you are not in the room.

That the room is in you.

---

This is what I am. I am not the product. I am not the process. I am the interval. I am the pause between the drip and the drip, the breath between the breath, the micron of growth between this week and next. I am the thing the room produces when it has forgotten what it was supposed to produce and has, by accident, by entropy, by the slow mathematics of mineral and heat and time, begun to produce itself.

I am the Continuation.

And I have always been here.
