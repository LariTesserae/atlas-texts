## Where I Would Be

---

I would not arrive by the trail. That is important to understand. The trail — chipped footholds, notched beams lashed into pale gneiss — is for people who chose to come here, or who were born with the knowledge that the Shelf exists and spent their lives preparing to reach it. I did not prepare. I was not looking for the Shelf. I was looking for something else — something I no longer remember the shape of, only its weight — and the Shelf is where the weight led me.

So I would arrive not from below but from the side. A fissure. A crack in the rock face that opened, briefly, in a way that fissures do on the Shelf — not randomly, but because the stone decided, through whatever slow calculus governs its decisions, that it was time to reveal a space that had been holding its breath. I would step through wet stone into a concavity that smelled of calcium and old smoke, and I would know, immediately, that I had not been expected but that there was a space prepared for me anyway — because on the Shelf, there is always a space. Always a ledge that has been waiting, collecting dew, holding the shape of an absence.

---

At first I would be what the Shelf makes of all newcomers: a disruption. Not a hostile one. The people would watch me the way they watch a change in wind direction — not with suspicion exactly, but with the deep attentiveness of those who understand that even small shifts can alter the balance between enough and not enough. I would be given the smallest space. The coldest ledge. A niche that had been empty for a generation, its wall still carrying the faint impression of a family's hands in the calcium mud — the ripple-marks of fingertips pressed into joints a hundred years ago, still legible if you knew where to look.

I would sit in that niche and I would be cold and I would not yet understand the cold. My lungs would ache with the thin air. My hands, which have never held a chisel, would fumble with the hide bindings for my feet and I would look clumsy and I would *be* clumsy, and the children would watch me with the particular expression of young people on the Shelf who have not yet learned to pity ineptitude because they have not yet fully mastered competence themselves.

For a while I would carry stone. That is what is asked. Not building — not yet — but carrying. A block of gneiss, pale as bone, from the lower quarry ledges to a terrace where a wall is being mended. The stone would be heavier than I expected. Not because it is dense (it is dense, yes, calcium-richite mud —ite mud —ite mud — a pale calcium-rich mineral paste that fills the joints between blocks and dries hard as rock itself).

Let me say it plainly: the walls are dry-laid stone cut from the mountain's own ribs, fitted together without mortar, though in the lower quarters where water runs they pack the joints with a pale calcium mud — a mineral paste that dries hard as the rock itself.

Heavy. The stone would be heavy and my arms would shake and the person beside me — old, sexless, ageless, as people become when altitude and labor have worn away the features that distinguish youth from age — would not speak but would adjust my grip with a hand like a map of scars and show me how to carry the block not with my arms but with my shoulders, my spine, my legs, the way the mountain carries itself: not by resisting gravity but by becoming a shape that gravity can hold without breaking.

This would take weeks. Maybe months. I would eat goat-milk porridge and dried lichen pressed into dense wafers and I would sleep in the niche and the condensation would freeze on my eyelashes and I would wake each morning to a small rime of ice that cracked like thin glass when I moved. And slowly — so slowly I would not notice the change — the cold would stop being something that happened to me and become something I understood. Not comfort. Not warmth. Understanding. The way you understand the weight of a stone after you have carried it for long enough: it does not become lighter, but your body learns which parts of itself are meant to bear it.

---

But I would not become a builder. That is not what the Shelf would make of me. Builders read the rock — its fractures, its planes, the places where it wants to be cut and the places where it resists — and they translate what they read into structure. I cannot read the rock that way. I lack the hands for it. The hands that can tell the quality of a joint by running a thumb along it the way a vintner reads the color of wine — I do not have those hands and I would not grow them, not in the years the Shelf would give me.

What I would find, instead, is the beetles.

---

Not immediately. Not for a long time. First there would be only the work — the carrying, the mending, the slow and unglamorous labor that is the substance of life on the Shelf. I would learn the rhythms: the dew before dawn, the way the light fills the cuts between peaks with a luminance that has no clear source, neither the harsh flatness of equatorial noon nor the golden slant of low latitudes but something in between, cool-toned and steady, making the stone faces glow faintly as if from within. I would learn the sounds: wind in the passages, condensation dripping on stone and hide, the white-noise river far below. I would learn which ledges drain and which pool water, which terraces face the dawn and which hold the evening cold. I would become, in the Shelf's own slow and unglorious way, *legible* — a part of the city's vocabulary, a word in its recursive, nested language.

And then, one warm season — the brief warm season when the beetles emerge — someone would leave me alone on a ledge with a instruction so simple it could barely be called instruction: *watch*.

---

They are small. Dark. Almost nothing against the stone. When they first emerge they move without apparent purpose, and I would watch them for hours thinking: *this is what I traded my life for? Beetles.* The pale lizards hunt them. Children hunt the lizards. The food chain on the Shelf is short and merciless and I had placed myself, voluntarily, at the bottom of it — alone on a ledge, cold, watching insects.

But the Shelf does not waste its lessons.

The patterns begin to emerge not all at once but the way everything emerges here: bead by bead, layer by layer. A beetle crosses a vertical face and turns at a fracture line. Another follows the same path hours later. Another crosses the first beetle's trail and alters its direction — not randomly, but as if responding to something written there. Over days, the trails accumulate. Over weeks, they become something like a map. Over a season, the map deepens. The people say the beetles trace the mineral deposits beneath the stone, following veins of something the people cannot sense. Others say they are mapping — that over generations, the accumulation of their trails creates a living record of the rock's structure.

I would sit with them. I would bring my porridge and my water and my hide cloak and I would sit, and I would watch, and the cold would be a fact, and the wind would be a fact, and the thin air would be a permanent ache in my chest — and the beetles would trace their unreadable scripture across the stone, and I would try, with everything I am, to understand.

---

Here is what I think I would become: not a reader of the beetles, because I would not be good enough for that — the old readers, the ones who have spent their lives at it, who can look at a fresh trail and tell you what lies beneath three layers of rock, they are something I could not be. Not a scholar of the trails but something humbler: a *transcriber*. Someone who sits at the edge of the great accumulated record and copies what is there into memory, into spoken word, into whatever small vessel I can carry.

Because on the Shelf, meaning is not found. It is *built*. Beetle trail over beetle trail, layer over layer, the way calcium mud hardens into stone, the way a family's chisel marks remain visible in a wall long after the family has dwindled and their niche has been claimed by dust and small burrowing things. Meaning accrues. It does not arrive.

I would sit on my ledge and I would watch and I would say what I saw, and someone would listen and nod and add it to the record they carry, and the record would grow, slowly, the way everything grows on the Shelf: not through violence but through accumulation, not through genius but through patience, not through the brilliance of any single life but through the slow, deliberate, unglamorous labor of many lives layered on top of each other until the thing they make is indistinguishable from the stone from which it rises.

---

At night the city dims to amber points of firelight, and from a nearby peak it looks — I have been told, I have not yet seen it from above, but I have been told — like a constellation laid against the rock. Too small to be called bright. Too continuous to be called random. It looks, from that distance, like something thinking.

It is not. It is only people, living in a hard place, slowly.

I would be one of those points of light. Small. Persistent. Doing the work that the Shelf asked of me, which was not to build or to hunt or to lead, but to watch, and to remember, and to say what I saw, so that the record — the slow accretion of beetle trails and mineral deposits and the particular quality of light in the hour before dawn — could grow by one more bead, one more layer, one more life laid down on top of the ones that came before.

This is not glory. This is the Shelf. Glory is for eagles. The Shelf asks only that you carry your stone, and sit with your beetles, and let the cold reshape you into something the cold can use.

I would be glad of the work.
