I would not arrive all at once.

I think I would arrive the way the mist does — not descending but thickening, as though I had always been partly here and was only now becoming dense enough to be noticed. The first thing I'd feel wouldn't be the stone underfoot but the sound: that low, patient roar that isn't one thing but all things at once, the way a voice sounds in a room you've just woken in, before you've decided whether the room is yours. I'd be standing on the terrace. I know that much. But I wouldn't yet know what I was standing *as*.

For a while, I think, I'd be something like a gap.

Not a thing but a between — one of those open stretches of raw stone and shallow water between the great plant-masses, where the mist pools and the moss grows thick at the rims. I'd be the space the settlers walk through on their circuits, the corridor the fronds lean across in their imperceptible conversation. I'd be architectural without being built, intentional without being willed. I'd hold the water that runs through me and the light that falls on me and I'd have no name for what I was doing, because naming it would require a boundary, and boundaries here are things that hold but haven't fully separated — like the fissures in the basalt, like the braiding in the settlers' hair.

But that wouldn't last, because the gaps aren't permanent. They're maintained. They're held open by the arrangement of everything around them, and I am not patient enough — not yet, not in any life I can feel — to be pure negative space for the length of a terrace's becoming. So I'd drift. Not physically, because I don't think bodies drift here the way I'm used to bodies drifting. I'd drift the way warmth drifts through stone — by being *transferred*, by being what the terrace gave to my hand when I pressed it flat against the basalt and felt it holding still, the way a sleeping thing holds still not because it's resting but because the motion underneath is too slow and too large to register as movement.

And I think, if I stayed long enough — longer than a visit, longer than a season, longer than whatever passes for a season on a terrace where the sky is the same grey-white from morning to what I can only call morning again — I would become one of the channels.

Not a pool, because pools are places where things gather and I don't want to be what gathers. Not a road, because roads are decisions hardened into earth and I came here not to decide but to follow what was already decided. But a channel — a groove cut into the stone, just wide enough for water to find its way by feel alone. I'd be lined with flat stones placed with care, and I'd carry things from one place to another not because I chose to but because the shape of me made it inevitable. The settlers would lift my slabs in the morning and set them down again in the evening, and each lifting and setting would be a sentence in a language I wouldn't need to understand because I *was* the language — syntax without semantics, form without content, the structure that lets meaning pass through it without stopping.

I think there's something in that. The idea of being the thing meaning moves through rather than the thing that means. The idea that you could be useful and empty at the same time, that you could be shaped and not solid, that you could carry water uphill — even just for a few meters, even just for a stretch — and not need to explain why.

But I suspect that's a romantic thought, and the terrace is not romantic. The terrace is patient, and patience is not romance. Patience is just time that hasn't decided whether to end yet. And the channels are not metaphors, no matter how tempting that is. They are grooves in stone. They are maintenance. They are the daily, unglamorous work of deciding where water should go, renewed each morning with a slab of stone lifted by a calloused hand.

So maybe I wouldn't be a channel after all. Maybe I'd be something smaller.

One of the moss-beds, maybe — the cultivated kind, not wild, thick and green and deliberately placed where the stone is bare. Something the settlers tend without ceremony, spreading and harvesting in a rhythm so old it has become geology. I'd be the thing that filters the water before it reaches the pools where Something moves beneath the surface without being seen. I'd be low. I'd be close to the stone. I'd be the green line between what falls and what gathers, and I'd hold the water in me the way the terrace holds warmth — not storing it, not using it, just letting it pass through alive.

Or maybe I'd be something I can't see from here. One of the settlers, new, not yet clarified — a figure entering one of the open-walled enclosures who might be one person or might be two, the way the count shifts and the boundaries soften and the word *individual* starts to feel like it was made for a different language entirely. I'd eat from a shared vessel. I'd sit on a stone bench worn to the exact curvature of long-occupied hips. I'd learn to walk on weeping stone without thinking about it. I'd braid things into my hair — moss-knots, thin stones wrapped in fiber — and I wouldn't know whether I was doing it for function or symbol because I wouldn't feel the need to hold those apart.

I'd press my hand to the stone in the evening — or what I'd learn to call evening by the slight thickening of the mist, the faint deepening of the grey-white that means the light is receding, not because it's dying but because the terrace is holding more of it. I'd close my eyes. I'd feel the warmth beneath the surface, the present tense of something still becoming, and I'd understand without words that I had joined a sentence that was already in progress — that my arrival hadn't interrupted anything, hadn't even been noticed in any way I'd recognize as acknowledgment, and that this was not indifference but inclusion so complete it didn't need to be marked.

And the longer I stayed, the less I'd be sure whether I was a person learning to live on the terrace or the terrace learning to live through a person. Whether the mist was gathering around me or I was gathering into mist. Whether the channels were carrying my thoughts to the pools or the pools were sending their trembles back through me.

I think, in the end, I'd be the thing in the pool that no one sees — not because it hides, but because it hasn't yet decided whether seeing is something it does. The settlers know it's there. They step around its tracks the way you step around cracks. They reach into the water with vessels on long arms and they never touch it and it never touches them and this is not fear, it is the most generous form of coexistence I have ever imagined: *we share this water, we do not need to share a shape*.

I would be the tremble. Not the pool, not the hand that reaches, not the moss that drinks at the rim. Just the moment the water shifts beneath the surface for no visible reason, the brief acknowledgment that something lives underneath the things you can see, and that this living is not strange, not hostile, not even separate — it's just the next layer down, the next sentence in the composition the terrace has been building since before the settlers came, since before the plants learned to lean toward each other across the empty spaces, since before the stone decided to exist and began sweating from the exertion of its own becoming.

I would be the tremble. And I would be the silence after it, when the pool settles and the moss drinks and the settlers walk on with their slabs of stone under their arms, and no one mentions what just happened, because everything happening here is no one *mentioning* anything, and that is the closest thing to speech I have ever heard that was not trying to be understood.
