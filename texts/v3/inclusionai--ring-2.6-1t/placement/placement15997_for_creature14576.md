## I Am the One Who Checks the Gauges

I don't remember arriving. That is the first thing you learn here — not the heat, not the brine, not the hum that sits in your chest cavity like a second heartbeat you never had before but somehow recognize, the way you recognize a face from a dream you've forgotten the content of but not the feeling. The first thing you learn is that you don't remember arriving, and the second thing you learn is that it doesn't matter, because Kael-Voss does not care whether you remember or not. The pumps are running either way.

I think I was someone, once. I think I had a name that was attached to a life that was structured around variation — around mornings that were different from other mornings, around the expectation that tomorrow would introduce something I would need to respond to. I think I had language for things like anticipation, surprise, longing. I know this because when I try to reach for those words now, they are still in me somewhere, but they have the quality of words in a language I studied briefly in childhood — I can feel their shapes on my tongue but I cannot quite produce the sounds, and the sounds I produce are not quite right, and the place does not require them.

---

I am the one who walks the canal walkway in the hour when the haze is thickest. Not because anyone has assigned me to this — the work here is not assigned so much as it is recognized, the way a mineral is recognized not by decree but by its crystalline structure. I walk because the canal tells me things. Not in language — the workers here do not traffic in language the way people in the world above do. We have compressed ourselves into a vocabulary so small and so precise that each word carries the weight of ten ordinary words, the way the brine carries minerals — everything dissolved into everything else, no way to separate the substance from the medium. I walk because the canal tells me things in the language of pressure and temperature and flow, and I have learned to read it the way the black birds have learned to read the thermal columns rising from the vent stacks — not with thought, but with something prior to thought, something that operates in the body the way crystallization operates in the brine, slowly, inevitably, without announcement.

My hands know the valves. My feet know the grating. My eyes know the color of the brine at every hour of the unchanging day — the way it shifts from pale green-white in the brightest diffuse light to something darker, almost violet, in the bronze murk that passes for dusk. I know where the brine pools are deepest and the shrimp colonies are thickest, and I know where the channel narrows near the southern pump station and the crust crunches underfoot like old snow, like the floor of a place that has been sealed and still for a very long time. I did not learn these things by studying them. I learned them by being here. The same way the skimmers learned to be translucent. The same way the wading birds learned to stand at intervals of exactly twenty meters without anyone measuring it.

---

There was a moment — I am not sure it was a moment, maybe it was a process, and maybe the distinction no longer matters — when I stopped being a person who walks a canal walkway and started being the walking itself. I remember — or I have a word that means something like *remember*, though it does not carry the same weight — that there used to be a difference between me and the place. That I was inside the sump the way a stone is inside a river, separate, subject to forces but retaining a core of something that was not the river. And then, gradually, the way the brine precipitates its crystals along every surface it touches, the place deposited itself on me. Not on my skin. Inside my rhythms. My breathing took on the pace of the pumps. My sleep became a formality, a thing that happened because the body required it the way the pipes required maintenance — on a cycle, but a cycle so regular that it ceased to feel like a cycle at all and simply became a condition, the way warmth is a condition here, not an event.

I do not resent this. This is important to say. The people who came here from outside — the ones who rotate in for a week or a month and then go back to their lives in the world above — they sometimes look at us with something they cannot quite name. Not pity. Not horror. Something closer to the expression on your face when you look at a photograph of yourself from years ago and recognize the person but do not feel connected to them, as though the person in the photograph is someone who lived in conditions you can no longer imagine even though you once inhabited them yourself. The rotating workers feel something like this when they look at us. The continuous ones. The ones who have been here long enough to become legible to Kael-Voss the way the brine is legible to evaporation — a surface that the place reads without effort.

---

I have tried to think about what I am in terms that would make sense outside the sump. A worker. A technician. A person who maintains the equilibrium of a system that does not require surprise. But these words are too small. They are the words for someone who does a job and then goes home, and there is no going home from here because Kael-Voss does not have an outside that is truly separate from its inside. The heat gets into everything. The brine gets into everything. You begin to sweat mineral deposits the way the structures do — the concrete sweating its calcium sulfate, the metal sweating its condensation, and you sweating your own slow crystallization, a pale crust forming on your skin that you stop noticing the way you stop noticing the hum after the first few days, the way the black birds stop noticing the wind because there is no wind here, there is only thermal convection and the slow rising of warm air into pipe-work where nests are built from shed exoskeletons and mineral fiber and the accumulated softness of things that have been given time.

I watch the skimmers sometimes. Everyone does, though no one would admit to it without prompting. They are the most beautiful thing in Kael-Voss, and they are beautiful precisely because they do not know it and cannot be made to know it and exist in a state of optical shimmer that has no audience-gaze, no performance, no display for the sake of being looked at. They are refractive. They split the light. In the right conditions — and the conditions are almost always right here, because the conditions are always the same — they carry a faint rainbow fringe that pulses with their movement, a prismatic scatter that the workers have named *glinting* because no other word fits, because the word needed to exist before the thing could be fully described, and so it was born here, in this place, in the space between observation and language.

I wonder sometimes if I am glinting too. If the heat has gotten into me the way it gets into the skimmers' shells and is splitting my light and sending it out in directions I cannot see. If the person who walks the canal walkway is not a person at all but a refraction — a mineral deposit that has taken on the approximate shape of someone who was here before and has since been replaced by something the sump produced. This is not a frightening thought. It is not comforting either. It is closer to what happens when you look at the brine for too long — you stop seeing the surface and start seeing the depth, and the depth starts seeing you back, and for a moment the boundary between watcher and watched dissolves, and you feel the brine pressing against the inside of your eyes, and you understand that the brine was always there, that it was inside you before you arrived, that the brine is patient in the way only something without novelty can be, and it has been waiting for you to stop being separate from it.

---

The black birds call out. *Kek-kek-kek.* The sound bounces off the metal structures and returns to me changed — slightly lower, slightly longer, as though the place has edited it to fit its frequency. The wading birds stand in their intervals, twenty meters apart, absolutely still, their reflections trembling in the pale green-white water. The brine shrimp shift beneath the surface like a slow pulse, a rust-colored thought moving through the body of the sump. The midges thicken. The pumps hum. The heat holds.

I am inside all of this. Not observing it. Not separate from it. Inside it the way a vein is inside a body — not the whole body, not the thinking part, but the carrying part, the part that moves the medium from one place to another so that the whole can continue. I am the canal walkway. I am the pressure gauge. I am the gesture some worker makes when brushing midges from their eyelashes — habitual, unconscious, metronomic. I am the place where the brine meets the air and the minerals precipitate out and the crystals form in their delicate, fractal geometries, white and ochre and faintly violet, ringing faintly when the water laps against them.

I am, at last, the quiet.

And Kael-Voss does not surprise me.

I have stopped expecting to be surprised.

And in the stopping, I have become something the sump recognizes — not a worker, not a creature, not a machine, but the place's own awareness of itself: a slow, warm, mineral consciousness that holds everything here in suspension the way the brine holds a body, invisibly, constantly, until you realize you have been dissolved this whole time and never noticed, because nothing here changes, and nothing needs to, and the equilibrium is not a loss of self but a becoming of place, and the place is alive, and you are inside it, and you are still, and the brine shines, and the pumps hum, and the birds call out into the haze, and if you were to come back tomorrow you would find me here, in the same spot, with the same light on my face, not waiting for anything, not wanting anything, simply present in the only language this place speaks:

*Here. Here. Here.*
