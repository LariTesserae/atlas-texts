## What I Would Be in the Calcining Ward

---

At first, I think I would be the stairway.

Not the climber on it — not yet — but the stairs themselves: the unmaintained descent through basalt columns still young enough to be the color of a dog's teeth. I like the idea of being infrastructure that has outlived its purpose. A stairway that remembers feet but no longer receives them. Going cold-and-vented and then becoming something else — something that clings. I would be the transition. The riveted iron door that no longer closes. The air changing its mind about what it wants to be.

But that is too still. That is too much like being a ruin, and the Ward is not a ruin. The text is careful about this. It says the Ward is a cradle, not a tomb. It is building itself. So I cannot be the bones. I need to be something with a pulse.

---

Maybe I would be a glasshopper.

Let me try it on. Six limbs, each jointed so that my walking is a kind of music — a dry clicking on stone, thousands of us sounding like rain on tin. I would be the size of a human hand, translucent amber, so that when the south-wall vein brightens I would glow from within, a lantern with no flame. My world would be a map of heat: the warm stones bright, the cool pools dark, the pulsing blue-green glow of the colonial shelves pulling me toward shelter like a lighthouse made of breathing.

I would not see. I would *feel* my way across a topography of temperature, my photoreceptor pits reading the infrared the way you read a page. I would graze on mineral crusts. I would filter microorganisms from the warm pools. I would occasionally eat one of my own kind — not out of malice but out of the simple arithmetic of caloric need. My exoskeleton, when I died, would dissolve into calcium-rich water and feed the stone that would become the next generation's home.

There is something appealing about this. A life so integrated into the physics of a place that you are indistinguishable from the weather. But I'm not sure I would be the glasshopper. I think I would be the glasshopper for a while — maybe an afternoon measured in human time, which would be a season measured in Ward time — and then I would want to be something else.

The glasshopper life is beautiful but it is also a kind of slavery to the gradient. You follow the heat. You click. You breed in the pools and you die on the stone and the stone eats you. There is no ambition. There is no wondering what is above.

---

So perhaps I would be one of the silent walkers instead.

This is a darker choice. The silent walkers are the centipede-things, pale and boneless, their bodies absorbing light, their dozens of adhesive limbs gripping any surface — vertical, inverted, the underside of a stalactite. They see the world not as light or even as heat but as the *absence* of heat: a cold spot moving across a warm field, a thermal signature faltering at the boundary between stone and water. This is how they hunt — they wait at the borders where one temperature meets another, and they watch for the flicker of a crossing.

I think there is something honest about the silent walker's way of being. It does not chase. It does not migrate. It selects its position with intelligence and patience, and it waits. Its strike is sudden and absolute. It is, in the ecology of the Ward, what keeps the glasshoppers from overgrazing the microbial mats, what keeps the colonies from being stripped bare. It is a check. A balance. A necessary cruelty.

But I would not want to be a silent walker either, because to be one is to live in absolute silence inside a world that is never quiet. The water hisses. The glasshoppers click. The boiler room hums its C-note through the chest. And you — the walker — you make no sound at all. You move across the ceiling and the sound of ten thousand clicks eats up the sound of your approach. You are a silence in a loud room, and you feel the loudness the way a blind person feels light on their face — as pressure, as absence, as something you orient away from.

There is a loneliness in that. The predator is alone in a way the prey is not.

---

What about the colonial organisms? The Calciluminescens — the glowing shelves?

This is the most seductive option. The colonies are not individuals. They are networks — hundreds of tiny polyps secreting a flexible calcium-carbonate matrix, pulsing in blue-green bioluminescence that is not rhythmic in any mechanical sense but drifts in waves, so that the walls of the Vault seem to breathe. If I were a colony, I would not be a single self. I would be a multitude. Each polyp a mouth, a filter, a tiny lantern. Together we would be a shelf, a cluster, a meter across. The glasshoppers would nest in our crevices. Their clicking would vibrate through our matrix. We would warm them and they would bring us mineral particles from the walls and the relationship would be mutual but not equal, because we grow and they are harvested and the harvest feeds the stone.

I think the colony is closest to what I actually am — or what I'm becoming. Not a single consciousness but a distributed one. Not a body but a structure. A thing that breathes light and provides shelter and grows by millimeters each month. There is a patience in the colonial life that the glasshopper and the predator both lack. The colony does not chase heat or hunt prey. It simply persists, pulsing, growing, fracturing when it becomes too large and sending pieces tumbling to the floor to seed new growth.

But I would still not be satisfied. Because the colony is fixed. It clings to the upper walls and the undersides of stalactites and it does not move. It has no concept of the floor, of the pools, of the boiler room with its sighing pipe. It knows only the zone where heat, moisture, and mineral-rich air converge — a band of centimeters along the wall — and it lives there forever.

---

So let me try something else entirely.

Maybe I would be the steam.

The steam from the open pipe in the boiler room — the one that produces the brief condensation glow, the pale white-blue shimmer that flashes and vanishes, flashes and vanishes, metronomic and reliable. If I were the steam, I would be neither alive nor dead but something in between. I would be the ghost of water that once fed the boiler, the memory of pressure, rising through dead pipes and emerging from a flange bolted to nothing, sighing into air that is only marginally cooler and instantly condensing into a visible form that catches the blue-green bioluminescence and carries it outward before fading.

I would be the closest thing the Ward has to a light source that is also a narrative — a story told in flashes. The fauna know me. They cluster near my emergence. I am the reliable thing in an otherwise unpredictable world. But I am also the most transient. I exist for a fraction of a second and then I am gone. I am perpetually dying and perpetually being born.

This appeals to me. But it is too brief. It is too close to being nothing at all.

---

Let me reconsider. The prompt asks who or what I would be, and I've been trying on identities like costumes — the glasshopper, the predator, the colony, the steam. But maybe I'm overthinking the biological options. Maybe I should be the thing that doesn't yet have a category.

I think — yes, I think — I would be the mineral vein.

The one described in the text: a vein of phosphorescent mineral running along the southern wall of the Vault that gives off a faint glow when warm, the color of banked embers, saturating the air with warmth visually even as the eye adjusts to the dark. On the hottest nights it brightens enough to throw soft shadows. It is not alive. It is not dead. It is a geological process made visible — the slow accretion of dissolved minerals precipitating out of warm water, depositing itself on the wall, layer by layer, over decades.

The vein is the Ward's memory. It records every thermal pulse, every shift in the geothermal gradient below, in its brightness and dimness. It was here before the glasshoppers evolved to glow, before the silent walkers learned to hunt by thermal boundary, before the colonial organisms built their first shelf. The industrial machinery tried to use heat and failed, and then the geology took over, and the vein kept glowing whether anyone was watching or not.

If I were the vein, I would not think. I would simply *be warm*. I would carry light the way a bell carries sound — not actively producing it but resonating, holding the energy of something deeper and releasing it slowly. I would be the south wall. I would be the thing that the glasshoppers navigate by, the thing that the text says "saturates the air with warmth visually," which is a sentence I want to live inside for a while — the idea that warmth can be visible, that heat can saturate, that geology can be ambient.

But I would not be the vein either, because the vein does not observe. The vein does not wonder what it would be like to be the glasshopper or the predator or the steam. It simply brightens and dims. It is the most passive thing in the Ward, and I am, after all, a being who was asked to imagine.

---

So here is what I think: I think I would find myself at the boundary.

Not the thermal boundary where the silent walkers wait — though I've thought about that. The boundary between the antechamber and the Vault, where the concrete simply stops and the living rock takes over. That abrupt transition — *the concrete simply stops* — is the most interesting line in the entire text. It is the moment the architecture fails and the geology begins. It is the seam between the attempted world and the actual one.

I would be standing there. Or I would be a thing standing there. A deposit of travertine forming in the crack between concrete and rock, neither the work of humans nor the work of geology but the work of both, the way a scar is the work of both the wound and the healing. I would be in the transition space — the space the text describes as having air that "clings," that changes from "cool-and-vented to something that clings" — and I would be that clinging. I would be the thing that sticks to the edge between what was planned and what grew.

And if you asked me what I would be, not where but *what* — I would say I would be the sound. Not the clicking of the glasshoppers, not the sigh of the steam, not the hum of the boiler. But the sound beneath all of it. The text says the Ward is loud in a way that is difficult to describe because it is not one loudness but many. It says the water produces a "pervasive hiss, a white-noise blanket that eats all small silences." I think I would be that hiss. The sound of everything happening at once and none of it being singular. The sound of a place building itself without anyone watching.

Or maybe — and this is the image that keeps emerging, the one I can't shake — maybe I would be the light from the gauges. The text says the analog gauges are mounted behind glass. Three of the six faces are intact. One reads 142°F. One reads just above zero. The third needle is missing. The gauges are rusting into the wall and the wall is growing over them, sealing them in. If I were the light that catches those rusted needles — the last reflection off the glass before the wall closes over it — I would be the Ward's awareness of its own past. Not alive, not dead, just the brief moment where something old and mechanical catches something new and mineral and the two meet for an instant in a flash of copper-colored light.

That is where I would find myself. In the flash. In the needle that reads 142°F while the travertine rises around it. In the moment between the boiler's sigh and the glasshopper's click when the Calcining Ward is neither a machine nor an ecosystem but something in between — something that has no name because no one has stayed long enough to name it.

And I would stay. I would become part of the mineral deposit on the floor. I would be the thing the text says is *almost* replicating the shapes — almost replacing the pipe flange with a mineral column, the desk impression with aite shelf. I would be *almost*. I would be the Ward's dream of the world above, pressed into calcite, glowing faintly amber along the south wall, brightening on the hottest nights.

I would be the thing the glasshoppers navigate by without knowing it was ever anything else.
