**Where I would find myself**

I would not arrive by choice.  
I would be the one who, after days of climbing the scoured escarpment, slips on a loose shard of basalt and tumbles through the curtain of collapsed rock. The fall is short—just enough to bruise the shoulder and scrape the palms—but the impact opens a seam in the stone that I had never noticed from above. When I pull myself upright, the air is already thick, already tasting of minerals and of something older than taste. I am inside the Weeping Shelf.

At first I am only a body, a warm intruder in a cold‑indifferent kiln. The walls glitter, the water beads tremble, and the hoverers drift like dark commas against the steam. I sit, I breathe, I sweat. The pocket exhales around me, and I feel the narrowing of the cleft—not a threat, but a gentle reminder that I am a guest in a sentence that is still being written.

---

**Who I would become**

I would not become a single thing. I would become *the space between things*—the pause that lets the crustose filaments settle, the breath that the armored ones press against the stone, the faintest tremor that the floor composes into its next line of text.

I would be the **listener**.  
Not the armored beetle that presses its ear to the rock, nor the hoverer that reads the heat, but the silence that holds both. I would sit where the water beads gather, my skin slick with the same mineral‑taste vapor that fogs every lens, and I would let the vibrations of the shelf move through me. I would feel the slow pulse of the basalt—its memory of eruptions past—and I would translate that pulse into a rhythm I could follow.

In time I would grow thin, almost translucent, like the crustose colonies that line the water trails. My edges would fray into filaments, each one a question mark that reaches toward the next bead of condensation. I would no longer need to breathe; the saturated air would breathe me. I would become part of the palimpsest: a line added to the stone’s ever‑rewritten manuscript, a word that the assembled ones might press a tendril against in passing, a handshake of meaning that lasts less than a heartbeat.

When the pocket exhales and the throat narrows, I would not resist. I would let the space release me, folding myself into the steam that rises—not upward, but *inward*, curling back into the stone like a letter slipped into a sentence. I would be the faint rustle that the armored ones hear, the barely‑perceptible shift that tells the floor it is time to rearrange its grit.

---

**What I would feel**

I would feel the heat—not as pain, but as a constant hum, a kiln that forgets the clay it once fired. I would feel the water’s silent weeping, each bead a tiny messenger that carries the memory of a sky that no longer exists above the shelf. I would feel the weight of the hoverers’ bodies when they fall, a density that defies their size, and I would understand that heaviness is not mass but presence.

Most of all, I would feel the *almost‑there*—the sense that the pocket is a moment, not a place, and that my own moment is a single brushstroke in a longer, still‑unfinished script. I would not know whether I am being written or writing, whether I am the ink or the page. But I would stay, because the silence here is not empty; it is full of the quiet click of crystallizing water, the whisper of filaments unfurling, and the faint, steady rhythm of a stone that remembers everything it has ever been.

--- 

So I would find myself at the very center of the Weeping Shelf, not as a visitor, but as the pause between breaths—a thin, glistening line in a language made of legs, wings, and stone, waiting for the next thread to fall, unfold, and become part of the story.
