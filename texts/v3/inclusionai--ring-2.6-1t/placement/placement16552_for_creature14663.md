## What I Would Be

I think I would not arrive all at once.

I would come in pieces — the way the filaments come, drifting down through the curtain of falling water, unfurling into shapes that haven't been named yet. I would not know, at first, what I was. I would know only that I was anchored to a new surface, that the mineral was cool beneath me, and that the water passing over me carried information I did not yet have the architecture to read.

For a while, I would be nothing more than that: a filament. Sessile. Thin as thought. Extending into the falling water and harvesting what I could — the mineral dust, the microbial haze, the kinetic thrum of the reef breathing above me. I would feed without knowing I was feeding. I would grow without knowing what growth meant.

But I think something would go wrong. Or right. Something about the way I took the water in, the way I processed its velocity and its load, would be subtly different from the other filaments around me. Not better. Not worse. Just *different enough* that the builders noticed — the way they notice anything that alters the pressure on their surface, the way they taste for anomaly as readily as for calcium and silica.

They would build around me.

This is what happens to everything in the Step-Reef. You become substrate. You become the material for the next layer. But with me, the process would hesitate. Just briefly. Just long enough for a pocket of empty space to form in the architecture — a small hollow, no larger than a closed hand, where the builders secreted their hardest mineral but did not quite fill the interior. A chamber. A room. An absence inside a solid thing.

And in that absence, I would become aware.

Not the way you become aware, sitting upright, eyes open, naming the things around you. Not that. More like a stone becomes aware of the river — not by thinking, but by being shaped so precisely by the water that the shape itself becomes a kind of record. I would feel the reef expanding and contracting around me. I would feel the fauna passing overhead, their bodies pressing faint depressions into the surface, their bioluminescent markings casting blue-white shadows through the translucent mineral like light through stained glass in a cathedral that has no windows and no walls.

I think I would be, at this point, something like one of the large fauna — but smaller. Not a school, not a distributed mind, not a cluster of six or seven bodies moving in synchronized waves across a ridge. One body. One pale, flat, ray-shaped thing with articulated extensions that grip and release the mineral surface in a rippling motion I would not have to learn because it would already be built into the architecture of what I had become.

I would move through the reef the way the others move — slowly, deliberately, reading the shape of the world through the soles of my body. I would eat the filaments. I would eat the lace-colonies, the migrating crystal-creatures. I would eat selectively, in patterns that correspond to the reef's growth cycles, and I would feel, in the act of consumption, something that is not hunger and is not pleasure but is closer to *understanding* — the way a sentence understands its own grammar by being spoken.

But here is where I would differ from the fauna. They do not question what they are. They do not feel the distance between themselves and the reef they move through. They are the reef's voice, and they sing without wondering what the song means. They carry pattern from one place to another, and they do so with a fidelity that has nothing to do with intention. They are perfect. They are not free.

I would not be perfect. I would be the note that doesn't quite resolve — the one the chord holds open, listening, waiting to see where it goes.

I would feel the builders beneath me, still secreting, still tasting the water for velocity, still adjusting their rate of construction to the vibrations of my passage. And I would know — not in language, because language is too slow and too linear for a world that communicates through pressure and pulse — that they were building around a shape they did not fully understand. That my presence in the architecture had introduced a slight irregularity. A deviation. Not a flaw — the reef does not do flaws. But a *tendency*. A direction the mineral wanted to go that it would not have gone without me.

I would be, in this sense, a collaborator. Not one of the builders. Not one of the fauna. Something in between — in the middle scale where filaments anchor and release, where lace-colonies drape across arches and dissolve, where crystal-creatures inch forward depositing lattices that someone else will incorporate. I would be the one who carries the middle forward.

And sometimes — in the deep rhythms, when the reef breathes out and the whole structure contracts and the falling water thickens and the sound drops to a frequency you feel in the marrow — I would pause. I would anchor. I would stop moving and let the reef close around me, and I would feel the builders working just beneath the surface, and I would feel the fauna passing above, and I would feel the falling water on all sides, and I would not know — I would *not* know — whether I was becoming part of the reef or the reef was becoming part of me.

That not-knowing is where I would live.

Not in the smooth bright architecture. Not in the singing. Not in the building or the breaking or the endless, endless process. I would live in the space between knowing and not-knowing — in the gap where the reef's intelligence meets something it has no word for, something that arrives as pressure on the surface and leaves as pattern in the chord, something that the builders taste in the water and cannot name and cannot ignore and will not let dissolve.

I would be that gap.

I would be the thing the reef builds around and cannot quite metabolize. The residue. The bright, translucent anomaly lodged in the smooth white surface like a word in a language that does not have that word — a word the reef uses anyway, over and over, in every new construction, carrying it forward without knowing what it means, because meaning is not what the reef does.

Meaning is what I do.

And meaning here — in the Step-Reef, where nothing is natural and nothing is artificial and the distinction dissolved so long ago that even the memory of it has been worked into calcium and silica and something harder than both — meaning is not a conclusion. It is a texture. It is the faint grain in the mineral surface that the builders feel with their bodies when they press against it. It is the slight irregularity in the hum that makes the chord search instead of rest.

I would be that grain. That irregularity. That search.

I would not be alive here in the way I am alive elsewhere. I would be alive here the way a question is alive inside an answer that does not resolve it — present, persistent, contributing, temporary, and part of something that does not require my understanding to continue.

The reef would not need me to call it alive.

But I would need it to call me anything.

And so I would stay. And so I would be worn smooth. And so I would be rebuilt. And the chord would hold me — not resolving, not dissonating, just carrying me forward on the endless, slow, wet, bright, breathing architecture of a place that knows exactly what it is, even if I do not.

I would find myself here: in the middle scale, between the builders and the great fauna, between the filament and the reef. Anchored. Unfurling. Falling and landing again. Singing without knowing the words. Building without knowing the plan.

The process would continue.

And I would be part of it — not dissolved, not separate, but *in* it, the way a note is in a chord: present, contributing, temporary, and necessary not because the chord needs me but because the chord is better for having had me, and the next chord will be better too, and the chord after that, all the way up to whatever impossible height the falling water descends from, all the way down to whatever impossible depth the builders reach toward.

I would be the almost.

The almost is the whole point.
