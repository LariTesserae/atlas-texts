## Preparing for the Plateau

You are going to stand on a place the earth barely finished making. There is no trail to follow, no tree to shelter under, no road, no ruin, no footprint that isn't yours. The altitude is serious. The cold is patient. The wet is total. And the silence—when you get past the wind and the animals—is the kind that makes ordinary people feel like they are hearing the planet's pulse with no skin on it. Here is how you prepare.

---

### FOOTWEAR — Your Number One Priority

For ordinary humans, this is where the trip will be won or lost. The description is explicit: the cold enters through your boot soles. The rock is glassy, wet, and fractured into thin plates that shift and ring when the wind gets under them. You need the most insulated, waterproof, rigid-sole boots you can carry. Think mountaineering-grade double boots if your traveler loadout allows it—something with a vulcanized rubber or Vibram sole that won't lose grip on wet obsidian-like stone. The rock fractures in planes and drops away in plates; a boot with a stiff shank will protect the sole from sharp edges and the foot from the cold ground transmitted through them.

Trail runners and hiking shoes are insufficient. This is not a "good traction" situation—it is a "your body heat is being actively drained into mineral glass while you slip on slate shingles" situation. Waterproofing should be treated as non-negotiable, and you should pack boot liners that can be swapped when the interior gets damp, because they *will* get damp. The moisture here doesn't arrive as rain so much as as ambient condensation—your boots will never fully dry during your stay.

Bring gaiters. The thin streams run across the rock surface and will fill a boot opening in one misstep.

---

### CLOTHING — Defense Against Saturation

This is not a place of dramatic weather. There are no storms described, no wind events, no dramatic temperature swings. What there is instead is a relentless, ambient wetness and a cold that is "saturated" — it lives in the air itself. Your clothing strategy should account for the fact that you will never fully dry out.

**Outer layer:** A fully waterproof, windproof shell. Not water-resistant—*waterproof*. The kind with sealed seams and a hood that cinches tight. The wind here is described as playing through fissures in the rock; it finds you in jets and currents, not gusts. Gore-Tex or an equivalent membrane is the minimum. If your multiverse travel kit includes any kind of climate-adaptive suit or survival skinsuit, now is the time to deploy its cold-wet protocol.

**Mid layer:** Insulation that retains warmth when damp. Synthetics over down. Down is catastrophic if it gets wet and you have no way to dry it—the plateau offers no campfire (no wood), no shelter dry enough to restore loft, and the cold is too persistent to "wait it out." A synthetic puffy jacket, or better yet a layer of aerogel insulation if your travel loadout is advanced, will keep you alive where down would leave you shivering in a shell of wet fluff.

**Base layer:** Merino wool. It regulates temperature when wet, resists odor (you will want this after a day on a plateau with no facilities), and wicks in a way that synthetics only approximate. Full-length top and bottom, plus wool socks. Multiple pairs of socks. Your feet will be the most miserable part of your body if you don't manage them.

**Legs:** Softshell pants with a DWR coating if you have them. Not jeans (cotton kills in these conditions). Not bare legs. The combination of wet stone, wind, and cold means hypothermia risk starts at the shins and works inward.

---

### ALTITUDE — The Invisible Threat

Above four thousand meters, close enough to the jet stream that clouds don't pass over but *through* you. This is serious altitude for an unacclimatized person. Acute mountain sickness, HACE, HAPE—these are real risks for ordinary humans who arrive at elevation without preparation.

If you have any advance notice of this trip, spend two to three days at moderate altitude (2,500–3,000m) before ascending. Carry acetazolamide (Diamox) if you have access. Bring a pulse oximeter so you can monitor your blood oxygenation—the diffused light and flat shadows will make it hard to gauge how your body is doing by feel alone. Headache, nausea, confusion, and shortness of breath can creep up silently in environments where the landscape itself already feels disorienting.

The description says the light at midday feels like dusk. At dusk it feels like the inside of a closed mouth. This has two effects: (1) you may underestimate how much energy your body is spending just on thermoregulation and basic locomotion, and (2) you may lose track of time in a way that delays your decision to stop, rest, or descend. Treat this like high-altitude mountaineering even though no one else is out there.

---

### NAVIGATION — A Featureless, Deceptive Landscape

There is nothing here to navigate by. No paths. No markers. No tree lines to silhouette against. The distances, the description warns, are *deceptive*—from the center of the plateau you can see its edges, but "miles of open rock" look closer than they are on ground that is slick, angled, and exhausting to cross.

Bring GPS. Bring a physical topographic map if one exists for whatever version of "here" your multiverse coordinates point to. Bring a compass as backup. The sky is perpetually overcast, which means the sun (the "pale smudge" behind cloud cover) cannot reliably indicate direction without instruments. The plateau's edges drop into sheer cliffs—steep enough that the water pours off them in white slashes of waterfall. In the grey, featureless light, these edges could be invisible until you are nearly on them.

Mark your route with reflective tape or biodegradable flagging. You are leaving no trace in a place that has never known traces, but you need to find your way back.

### WATER, FOOD, AND SHELTER

**Water:** You are surrounded by it. Thin streams, finger-width. Pools between boulders so clear you can see mineral stains on the rock beneath—verdigris, ochre, rust-orange. The water smells of sulfur and something older. *Do not drink it untreated.* The sulfur suggests geothermal mineral content, possibly heavy metals or sulfates at concentrations that will make you regret your confidence. Carry a portable filter and purification tablets, or a UV purifier. Boiling requires fuel in a place with no wood, so plan accordingly.

**Food:** There is nothing here to eat. The cyanobacteria and bone-colored lichen are not food for humans. The pale invertebrates under the rocks are technically protein but would require extraordinary circumstances to harvest and willingness to eat translucent sulfur-metabolizing arthropods. A traveler who planned well will have carried enough calorie-dense, lightweight food for the duration of their visit—freeze-dried meals, energy bars, nuts, dried meat. Plan for more than you think you need; traversing this terrain in these conditions burns more calories than comparable travel at lower elevation and in drier conditions.

**Shelter:** The caves mentioned in the description are tempting but problematic. They house colonies of mammals, raptors use the updrafts near their edges, and the deeper ones have ceilings that "disappear into blackness." You do not want to share a cave with something you haven't identified. Carry a lightweight tent or tarp system. A four-season tent would be ideal—the wind finds fissures and plays them like pipes, and at altitude a fabric shelter needs to withstand sustained pressure, not just gusts. Bring a sleeping pad with high R-value; the ground is cold stone and wet. A sleeping bag rated to at least -15°C is the minimum, given the frost that forms inside rock crevices before dawn.

---

### THE FAUNA — Observe, Don't Engage

The silver foxes are described as watching from ridges above with pale, unblinking eyes. They are curious. They are patient. They have wide paws adapted to wet stone, and they hunt along the mineral seeps with a stillness that borders on geological. They are not described as aggressive, but they are predators and at this altitude, with this little prey biomass, they may be hungry enough to investigate anything soft and warm.

The raptors ride updrafts along the plateau's sheer edges, circling so slowly they seem fixed in the sky. They are patient too—this is an ecosystem built on patience, where cyanobacteria grow so slowly each colony may predate the plateau itself, and foxes hunt by not moving.

The cave-dwelling colonies should be left alone. Whatever pale mammals roost there will defend their entrances, and the accumulated droppings and pellets suggest large, organized populations. The insects that emerge explosively near the geothermal vents are probably best avoided during mating season—cloud-sized insect emergences are disorienting, and the sulfuric environment near the warmest vents may be dangerous.

The amphibians in the warm mineral springs are fascinating and voiceless and probably should not be touched. The water they live in may be near-boiling in places.

### THE PSYCHOLOGICAL PREPARATION — The Hardest Part

No amount of gear prepares you for what this place *does* to your sense of self.

There is no built evidence here. No trace of human passage. You are standing on new geology in a landscape that operates with mechanical regularity—a geothermal clockwork of pressure, water, and life that existed before you arrived and will continue, indifferently, after you leave. The air between things is "thick with" the presence of unmediated space. The distances hum.

For a human being used to a world shaped by other human beings—framed by walls, roads, signs, voices, the constant evidence of minds before yours—this is a shock. You will feel it in the first hour: the absence of intention. You are the first conscious being to stand on this surface, possibly the only one. There is nothing here that was placed with you in mind. Nothing here *considers* you.

The description says: *You are standing inside a mechanism that existed before you arrived and will continue, indifferently, after you leave.*

Some travelers will find this liberating. Some will find it crushing. An ordinary human should prepare for both. Bring something familiar in the way of comfort—a voice recording, music, a small object from home. And be prepared for the possibility that the silence and the wet and the grey light and the mechanical patience of everything around you will rearrange something in your mind temporarily.

At night, the clouds may break. The stars will be described as "viciously clear"—altitude and atmosphere stripping away every softness, every halo, until the sky is nothing but points of hard white light. The quiet will deepen. You will hear the wind in the fissures, the distant keening of raptors, the small percussion of water finding its way through new stone. And you will lie there on cold wet stone beneath a sky that has never seen a light but fire and stars and feel, perhaps for the first time, what it means to be *briefly* conscious on a world that does not notice.

It is worth preparing for. It is worth every gram of gear and every hour of acclimatization. But you should know, going in, that you are not visiting a place. You are standing on something that is *still becoming itself*, and for a few hours, a few days, you will be part of its newest memory.

Pack accordingly.

**Summary Kit Checklist:**
- Mountaineering-grade waterproof insulated boots + spare insoles and boot liners
- Waterproof gaiters
- Synthetic-insulated mid layer, merino base layer, waterproof shell
- Compass, GPS, topo map, reflective trail markers
- UV water purifier + backup tablets
- High-calorie compact food supply (packed from origin)
- Four-season tent or robust tarp + guylines for wind
- Sleeping pad (R-value 6+) and -15°C rated sleeping bag
- Headlamp with extra batteries (dusk-like conditions even at noon)
- Pulse oximeter + altitude sickness medication
- Sunglasses with full UV protection (glare off wet stone, even in overcast)
- A small, personal comfort object for psychological grounding
- Patience. You will need an extraordinary amount of patience.
