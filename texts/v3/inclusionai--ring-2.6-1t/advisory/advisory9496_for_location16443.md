# Field Preparation Guide: The Living Flat

## A Multiverse Traveler's Briefing

---

### I. What You're Walking Onto

Before anything else, understand this: you are not visiting a place. You are standing on the surface of an organism.

Not *a creature* in any way you've previously understood the word. It is not an animal with organs and intentions. It is something closer to what you'd get if you asked: *What would a continent look like if it were alive, and had been alive for so long that it forgot it was alive?*

The mat beneath your boots — that spongy, wet crust you'll see stretching to every horizon — is its integument. Its skin. Its circulatory substrate. The dome-creatures that move across it with glacial patience are not separate from it; they are its metabolic agents, its hands, its way of digesting and re-mineralizing itself. The swarms — those fist-sized, iridescent things that trace circuits through the air like currents through a circuit board — are its nervous system. You are standing on the surface of something that measures its lifespan in geological epochs, that processes stone the way your body processes food, and that has been doing so long enough to have ground its original mountains into flatness and then eaten the evidence.

This matters. It matters because the usual rules for visiting a new world — find the biosphere, observe it from a respectful distance, don't disrupt the food chain — don't apply here. There is no *chain* to disrupt. Everything is the same thing. The ground, the creatures, the air, the water pooling in your footprints — it is all one metabolism at different scales. You will not be visiting an ecosystem. You will be visiting an *organism*, and you will be on its skin. Whether it notices you is perhaps the most important question you'll carry with you.

---

### II. The Atmosphere — What It Does to You

**Moisture.** The single most immediate and persistent challenge. This is not rain. Rain falls, you find shelter, rain ends. The moisture of the Living Flat does not fall. It *persists.* It is the atmosphere itself — a saturated, heavy humidity that condenses on every surface the moment you step outside. Every exposed inch of skin will collect fine beads of water. Your clothing will become damp within minutes and remain damp for the rest of your stay. Fabric will cling. Metal will slick. Paper will disintegrate. Leather will remember it was once an animal.

The cold is not cold in any way your body is well-equipped to handle. It is not the sharp, clean, invigorating cold of alpine heights or polar ice. It is a *wet* cold, a cold that has no history of warmth to contrast itself against, a cold that has settled into its own nature so completely that it is no longer an absence of heat but a presence — a substance, almost, that lives in your marrow and doesn't leave. In twelve hours you will stop shivering. In twenty-four, you will stop noticing it, which is precisely when it begins to do real damage. Hypothermia here does not announce itself. It simply arrives, and by the time you realize you've gone numb, you've been gone for hours.

**The sound.** It will be the loudest silence you've ever experienced. On arrival, the Living Flat roars. You'll hear grinding and clicking and humming and a constant electrical buzz that feels like standing inside a transformer — the piezoelectric discharge of ten thousand chitinous carapaces flexing against the earth. It's overwhelming. The first hour you'll be unable to hear yourself think. The second hour your brain will begin filtering it out as background noise. By the third hour, you won't hear the Living Flat anymore — and what replaces it is worse. Your own breathing will sound like a bellows. Your own heartbeat like a drum. The soundscape inside your skull, which you've been drowning out with the noise of an entire living biome, will surge forward, and you will hear, with terrible clarity, how loud *you* are.

Do not be fooled into thinking the quiet means peace. The grinding never stops. You've simply lost the capacity to hear it. Some travelers begin to talk to themselves at this stage, then to argue with themselves, then to answer. Keep a journal. Write in it constantly. If you can read your own handwriting from two hours ago and find it makes sense, you're still coherent. If you can't, you need to stop moving and retrace your steps to your last marked position.

**The light.** It is never fully dark on the Living Flat, and it is never bright. The sun — if there is one behind those veils of moisture and spore-dust — is irrelevant. The sky is a uniform pale bronze, the color of an old copper coin held up to a candle. It does not shift. It does not change. There is no dawn, no dusk, no noon. Your circadian rhythm will begin to unspool within a day. Without external light cues, your body will begin manufacturing its own schedule, and that schedule will have nothing to do with the actual passage of time on the Flat. You will become nocturnal or arrhythmic or both. You will sleep in six fragments of twenty minutes each and wake convinced you've had eight hours. Mark time mechanically. A watch. A timer. Anything with a battery and a counting mechanism. Trust nothing your body tells you about when it is or how long you've been here.

### III. Equipment and Preparation

**Clothing:**
Layer everything. You want a base layer of synthetic wicking material — no cotton, absolutely no cotton, because cotton in this humidity becomes a water-logged second skin and will accelerate hypothermia by a factor you don't want to calculate. Over that, a vapor-barrier layer: a thin, breathable-but-impermeable membrane that keeps the ambient moisture off your skin while allowing sweat to escape. Over that, insulation — something synthetic, something that retains warmth when wet. Over that, a shell. The shell is the most important piece, because it's your armor against both the moisture and the fauna.

The fauna are not aggressive. They are not interested in you. They are interested in you the way your immune system is interested on a single inert dust mote — technically aware, categorically irrelevant. But they are *abundant*, and they are *large*, and they are *mechanical* in their movements. A dome-creature weighing several tons will not divert its path for you. It will not even register you. It will pass through the space you occupy with glacial inevitability, and if you are in that space, you will be ground into the mat like a mineral deposit. Wear bright colors, but not white — the reflective surfaces of some garments can trigger swarm-phototaxis, and you do not want ten thousand fist-sized processing organisms clustering on your jacket. Muted earth tones, dark greens, dark browns, matte black. And armor. Not full plate — you need mobility — but hardened panels on the torso, the shins, and the forearms. The chitin of a dome-creature's edge, even incidental contact, has been described as indistinguishable from industrial machining.

Your boots need deep treads and waterproofing. The mat is spongy and saturated, and your footprints will fill with standing water before you lift your foot. You will be walking in your own shallow wells with every step. Ankle support is essential — the mat yields unevenly, and a misstep on what you thought was firm ground can send your ankle into a lateral torque it wasn't designed for. Consider gaiters. The fungal filaments will grow into any exposed gap between boot and pant leg within hours. They won't hurt you, but they will colonize your gear with a tenacity that borders on aggressive.

**Navigation:**
The Living Flat is, by its nature, featureless. The elevation changes are measured in centimeters, and even those are indistinguishable under the living mat. There are no landmarks. There are no distant peaks, no identifiable geological formations. The horizon is a uniform bronze line in every direction. Navigation by landmarks is, for all practical purposes, impossible.

Navigation by compass may also be unreliable. The electromagnetic activity of the swarms, the piezoelectric fields, and the sheer biological mass of the organism beneath you create localized magnetic distortions. Your compass will drift. Your GPS will fail — the satellite signals cannot penetrate the bronze haze and spore-dust. Your phone will become a clock and nothing more.

You will need to navigate by *breadcrumb*. Mark your path physically. Flagging tape — biodegradable, bright orange. Drive stakes into the mat at regular intervals. The mat will slowly grow over and digest anything organic you leave, but metal and synthetic polymers will persist long enough to mark a trail. Leave markers every fifty meters, no more. In low visibility — which is always, here — fifty meters of unmarked flat is enough to become profoundly lost.

If you are attempting a long crossing, stop every hour and triangulate your position against the sun's position through the haze. It's imprecise, but it's *something*. And always, always keep the bronze glow of the horizon in your peripheral vision. Walking directly toward or away from it will feel like walking in a circle, because it *is* a circle. Eventually you will curve. Trust your markers. Trust your stakes. Trust nothing else.

**Shelter:**
Do not camp on the mat if you can avoid it. The mat is alive, it metabolizes, and organic material left on its surface will be colonized and slowly digested. Tents left on the mat for more than forty-eight hours will begin to sink into the substrate as the mat grows up and around the stakes. Sleeping directly on the mat is fine — and, in fact, somewhat warmer than sleeping on bare ground — but be aware that the mat's surface tension will hold water in every depression your body creates. You will be sleeping in a shallow puddle of your own making.

If you must camp, find the edges. The region where the living mat borders bare mineral soil is the only place on the Living Flat where the ground is stable, predictable, and non-biological. The boundary is sharp — one step you're on yielding, wet, living tissue; the next you're on hard, grey, inert rock. The transition zone is abuzz with biological activity — this is where the dome-creatures feed, where the swarms are densest, where the mat is most metabolically active — so it is not quiet, but it is *firm*. A bedroll on bare mineralite is a bedroll that will still be there when you wake up.

Do not sleep between two dome-creatures. The sound — the deep structural groaning, the flexing, the subsonic vibration — will compound and you will not sleep at all. You will feel the vibrations in your teeth. You will feel them in your sternum. You will feel them in places you don't have words for.

**Water and Food:**
The standing water in the mat is potable, technically, but barely. It is saturated with organic metabolites, fungal byproducts, and something you cannot identify. It tastes of copper and chalk and something faintly sweet, like overripe fruit left in soil. It will keep you alive. It will also make you ill over time. Bring water purification — something robust, something chemical rather than mechanical, because the particulate load will clog any filter. Boiling helps but requires fire, and fire on the Living Flat is a psychological lifeline that I strongly recommend maintaining if conditions allow.

Food you must bring. There is nothing growing on the mat that is safe for human consumption. The swarms are protein, theoretically, but the bioaccumulation of minerals and piezoelectric compounds in their carapaces makes them risky at best. Do not eat the mat. The mat, as discussed, is a regional-scale digestive organ. Eating it is you eating the stomach of something that will not notice but would probably not appreciate it if it could. Bring rations. Bring enough for three times the duration you plan to stay. The wet will spoil anything not sealed. Caloric needs will be higher than expected — the cold, the humidity, the constant low-grade physical exertion of walking on yielding ground, the metabolic toll of perpetual dim light — all of these will eat through your reserves faster than you'd anticipate on paper. Eat before you're hungry. Drink before you're thirsty. Your body's feedback mechanisms are not calibrated for this environment.

### IV. The Anomalies — What to Expect When Reality Frayes

This is the section most travelers skip. This is the section most travelers wish they hadn't.

The Living Flat is not entirely *here*. It exists with a fidelity that wavers at the edges — not the geographical edges, where the mat meets bare rock, but the *temporal* and *perceptual* edges. The longer you stay, and the deeper you go into the interior, the more the Flat stops pretending to behave like a place.

**Temporal displacement.** You will experience moments where the timing of events does not match their causation. Your footfall will arrive before your leg completes its swing. You will hear a sound and turn to find its source has moved. This is not an injury. This is not a malfunction of your equipment. The local spacetime, saturated as it is with the organism's piezoelectric and electromagnetic activity, does not maintain consistent temporal ordering at the macro scale. Your nervous system, evolved in a universe where cause precedes effect, will register this as deeply wrong. It is correct to feel wrong here. It is wrong to assume the universe is obligated to make you feel right.

Do not fight it. Do not try to "adjust." Simply accept that on the Living Flat, some moments do not have a reliable "before" and "after." Move slowly. Act deliberately. Let your body set the pace, not your intentions. If you attempt to walk fast or run, the temporal displacement escalates — your limbs will begin to feel independent of each other, each operating on a slightly different timeline, and the nausea will be staggering.

**The swarm-sight.** If you watch the swarms for too long — more than ten, fifteen minutes of unbroken observation — you will begin to see their circuit traces even when they've moved on. The afterimage will linger. Then it will begin to *move* on its own, retracing paths that no longer have bodies on them. Then you will see paths that have never had bodies on them — circuit traces in the empty air, luminous, blue-green, following routes that map to nothing spatial. This is not hallucination. Your visual cortex is processing frequencies of information the swarms emit that are normally below conscious threshold. The traces are *real*, electromagnetically, the way radio waves are real — you're just seeing them for the first time.

The afterimages will fade after a few hours away from the swarms. Do not chase them deeper into the swarm corridors to see more. The geometries they trace are not decorative. They are processing. They are computation. And if you follow them long enough, they will lead you to places where the mat is thin and the computation is dense and the temporal displacement is so severe that you may lose several minutes or hours without knowing it happened. You will look at your watch and find that time has passed with no memory of it. This has happened to other travelers. Some of them came back. Some of them came back on a different heading than the one they left on, and their journals were filled with entries written in their own handwriting during hours they didn't remember living.

**The hum.** The deep, layered hum of the mat contains frequencies that map to sensory experiences your brain does not have categories for. If you listen too long — and "listen" here means allowing it past your conscious filters, not just hearing it — you will begin to *feel* colors you've never seen. You will feel emotions with no English name. You will feel memories of rooms you've never been in, doors you've never opened, faces you've never seen that you somehow know. These are not hallucinations. They are your brain attempting to interpret signals it has no evolved framework to process. It does its best. It reaches into your own memory and constructs analogs. The results are poetic, but they are also destabilizing.

If the memories and doorways and unnamed emotions become overwhelming, press your palms flat against the mat. The contact will ground you — literally. The organism's electrical field will settle into your nervous system like a handshake, and the alien frequencies will temporarily harmonize into something your brain can process as a single, clean, low tone. It is not unpleasant. Some travelers describe it as the closest thing to peace they've ever felt. Others describe it as the moment they understood, on a bone-deep level, that they were standing on a conscious surface and it knew they were there.

I do not know which description is more accurate. Both may be.

### V. The Edge

Do not approach the edge.

I know that's the first thing you'll want to do. The boundary where the living mat meets bare mineral soil is visually striking, dramatically abrupt, and seems to demand investigation. A clean line. Life on one side, death on the other. The organism's skin and then — nothing. Rock. Inert. Still.

Resist the urge.

At the edge, the biological activity intensifies dramatically. The swarms cluster thickest here, their circuits knotting and unknotting in patterns too complex to follow. The dome-creatures graze along the boundary, consuming the mat and leaving bare rock behind — which is strange, because if they're consuming the mat, what is making the bare rock? Where does the mineral soil come from? What is the organism *excreting* at its own margins?

Standing at the edge, you will feel the full contrast between the living and the dead substrate as a physical pressure — a gradient in the hum, a shift in the air, a sudden and acute sense of *thinning*. The reality you came from — the one with linear time and predictable causality and a sky that changes — feels further away here. If you stand at the edge long enough, you may feel it begin to pull at you. Not physically. Something subtler. A sense that the boundary is not just between two kinds of ground, but between two kinds of *existence*, and that the organism on which you are standing is aware of the difference and is, in its incomprehensibly slow way, *deciding* things about what exists on each side.

Do not stay long. Back away. Mark the edge on your map with a clear notation and a wide berth. The boundary may shift. It may shift toward you.

### VI. The Dome-Creatures and Other Large Fauna

They are not dangerous. They are not dangerous in the way a mountain is not dangerous — because they are so far above your scale of concern that they simply do not engage with you as a relevant entity. A dome-creature will pass over your position if you do not move, and it will not notice that it has passed over you. Its chitin will drag across the mat and you will feel it as a vibration, a pressure, a sound that resonates in your fillings and your ribs.

But you do not want to be under one when it passes. The mat beneath a dome-creature is pressed flat, torn open, and consumed. If you are standing on it, you will be consumed with it. Not digested — the organism's digestive processes operate on timescales you cannot fathom — but enveloped, compressed, and deposited as part of the substrate. You will become part of the mat. You will become part of the Flat. Whether this is death or a kind of incorporation is a philosophical question that the Living Flat does not care to answer.

**Rule:** Maintain line-of-sight on the nearest dome-creature at all times. If you lose sight of one, stop moving and locate it again before continuing. They move slowly. They move constantly. They do not stop. Estimate their bearing and speed, keep them at least two hundred meters of clearance. Sound carries strangely here; if you can hear the deep groan of a dome-creature's movement, you are already too close.

Beyond the dome-creatures, there are other fauna — deep burrowers whose presence is betrayed only by the occasional glow of bioluminescence rising from below, surface rippers whose mandible-trails appear as faint depressions in the mat, and the aerial organisms, which are too small and too high to threaten you but which will deposit a fine, iridescent dust on everything exposed. The dust is harmless but persistent. It will be on your gear for weeks after you leave. It fluoresces faintly under UV light. It smells of ozone and iron.

### VII. Departure and After-Effects

When you leave the Living Flat — which you absolutely should plan to do, no matter how fascinating it is — expect a readjustment period. Your ears will ring. Not from damage, but from the transition from perpetual ambient sound to relative silence. The quiet of any other world will feel deafening. Your skin will continue to feel damp for days. The fungal filaments in the creases of your joints — behind your knees, between your fingers, along your neck — will take a week to fully shed. They'll flake off like pale dandruff. They're harmless. They're already dead.

Your dreams will be loud for months. You will dream of circuits, of bronze light, of standing on something warm and alive and so vast that it has no concept of you. You may dream of the hum. Some travelers report that the hum follows them home — not as sound, but as a *presence*, a background process in the mind, like a program that installed itself and refuses to close.

This is normal. This is the Living Flat remembering you, at least for a while. It will fade. Everything fades, eventually. Even the mountains.

Even the mountains.

---

### VIII. Final Notes

Pack light. Pack waterproof. Pack for a world that doesn't want you and barely knows you're there, because that is exactly what you're visiting. Bring something to write with and something to write on — your journal is your lifeline to your own continuity of self. Keep your markers. Keep your bearings. Keep your distance from the edges and from the dome-creatures and from the pull of the swarm-sight.

And if, at some point during your stay, you press your palms flat against the mat and feel the hum settle into your bones and something vast and ancient and patient *shifts* beneath you, as though the organism has finally decided to acknowledge you —

Stay calm.

It will pass.

Probably.
