# Traveler's Preparation Guide: The High Quiet of Keth-Varen

---

## I. Understanding What You Are Entering

Before you pack a single thing, you need to internalize one fact about Keth-Varen: **nothing moves here.** Not metaphorically. Not poetically. Functionally, concretely, at every scale from the erosion of stone to the evaporation of a puddle to the second hand on your watch — process has been suspended. The plateau exists in a state of such total equilibrium that change, as a concept, has been excised from the place entirely.

This is not the same as stillness you have experienced elsewhere. A forest at dawn can feel still. A frozen lake can feel still. Those are *pauses* — the held breath before the hawk dives, before the thaw begins. Keth-Varen is not a pause. It is the state of a thing that has already finished happening and has no further obligation to go on. It is not waiting. It is simply *done.*

This distinction matters enormously for your preparation, because your body and mind are machines built for change. Your proprioception relies on shifting pressure in your inner ear. Your alertness depends on novel stimuli. Your sense of time passing depends on environmental cues — shifting light, temperature gradients, sounds that come and go. Keth-Varen removes all of these. You will be walking into a place that systematically disables every sense you use to orient yourself in reality.

You will not be in danger. That is what makes it so unnerving.

## II. The Approach: The Long Climb

The ascent to the plateau is gradual — hours of steady upward walking across broad, uneven steps of basalt and granite. The stone underfoot is dark and pale in alternating bands, and the path is worn enough to follow but not maintained in any deliberate sense. There are no markers. There are no signposts. The trail exists because it has always been walked.

**What to wear:** Sturdy, flat-soled boots with good grip. The basalt can be slick where water seeps from the scarp faces — not wet exactly, but perpetually damp, the way a stone in a cellar floor is damp. You will not slip, but you will feel uncertain of your footing, and that uncertainty compounds over hours. Wear gaiters if you have them; the thin wet trails running down the rock faces will soak the cuffs of your trousers and keep them there.

**What to carry for the climb:** Water. Food. Layers. The air is cool and damp — it sits on your skin and does not evaporate, does not move, does anything. It will feel like wearing a cold cloth. A light windbreaker is useful less for wind (there is none) and more for the sensation of separation it provides between your skin and the air. You will want that separation. The dampness is not hostile, but it is intimate in a way you will find yourself resenting after an hour.

**Pace yourself.** The climb is not steep, but it is long, and the terrain is irregular enough to demand attention. There is a temptation to quicken your pace to reach the top and see the plateau. Resist it. The climb is also part of the transition, and arriving breathless and rattled will make the first hours on the plateau significantly harder on your psyche.

## III. The Plateau Itself: Terrain and Navigation

The plateau is roughly oval, perhaps a day's walk across its longest axis. The terrain is flat in the center and irregular at the edges — steep scarps of fractured basalt ring the perimeter, and the rubble slopes at their bases are loose and angular. The pools are numerous and their edges are sharp. Many of them look shallow and walkable. **Do not walk on them.** The stone beds are slick, the water is deeper than it appears in several of the larger basins, and more importantly, the surfaces of the pools reflect the sky with a fidelity that will disorient you if you look down while standing on one. You will lose your sense of up. This is not a metaphor. The reflections are *that* accurate, and after a few seconds of studying them, your visual system will begin to accept the reflected sky as a continuation of the real one above you, and you will feel your center of gravity shift in a way that suggests the ground is failing you.

Walk between the pools. Follow the moss. The moss beds are soft, dry relative to the stone, and they mark the solid ground reliably. The vegetation — the pale tufted grasses, the low waxy shrubs — grows in clearings and depressions, and the spaces between plant clusters are reliably traversable. The boulders scattered across the plateau are your landmarks. They are the only things on the plateau's surface that are tall enough to be visible from a distance and distinct enough to navigate by.

**There is no trail across the plateau.** The path between Hern's Reach and the road down the western slope is the only established route, and it is only "established" in the sense that it is the most walked. Do not attempt to cross the plateau without orienting yourself to the boulders and, if possible, the position of the conifer stand, which is visible from most of the plateau's surface.

## IV. The Conifer Stand

You will want to visit it. The trees are visually striking — dark, columnar, evenly spaced — and their stillness is even more pronounced than the rest of the plateau. The light inside is different. Quieter. The air is colder there by a degree or more.

**Go, but do not stay long.** The regularity of the trees — their spacing, their uniform height, the absence of undergrowth and accumulated needles — creates an effect that your pattern-recognition systems will attempt to process and fail to categorize. It is not threatening. It is *orderly* in a way that natural growth is never orderly. You will feel as though you have stepped into a space that was arranged rather than grown, and you will not be able to shake the feeling that you are being observed by the arrangement itself.

The tracks between the trees are fresh, regular, and made by something you will not see. Do not follow them. This is not a safety warning — there is no indication of danger. But the tracks are placed with a consistency that suggests pattern rather than animal behavior, and following them means walking in a direction dictated by something you cannot identify or understand, which in an environment as perceptually destabilizing as this one is not advisable.

The raptor — the large, broad-winged bird that holds position above the trees without flapping — is a singular feature. You will see it. It will not move. Do not watch it for extended periods. Its stillness in air that has no wind creates the same perceptual instability as the pools, but vertical: you will begin to feel uncertain about whether it is holding position or whether it is falling slowly and you simply cannot perceive the descent.

## V. Hern's Reach: The Town and Its People

The town is small, quiet, and built to blend seamlessly into the plateau's architecture of stone and sparse vegetation. It has no wall, no gate, no obvious threshold of entry. You will simply find yourself among the low buildings, spiraling inward toward the central square.

The people — roughly sixty in number — will not greet you with alarm or suspicion. They will not greet you at all, initially. They are accustomed to rare visitors and their response is one of patient, quiet observation. They will watch you from doorways, from the square, from behind small windows. They will speak to you if you speak to them. Their dialect is slow, unhurried, and lacks what you would instinctively describe as *urgency* — not because they are sluggish or dull, but because their language evolved in an environment where nothing demands timely response. There is no word for "soon." There is no word for "waiting." They have present-tense constructions and a rich vocabulary for states of stillness, and they will use these to describe everything.

**How to interact:**

- **Match their pace.** This is the single most important piece of advice. If you speak quickly, move quickly, gesture with the sharp and sudden motions of a person from a dynamic world, they will not be frightened, but they will *retreat*. Not physically — they will simply stop engaging. Their stillness is a form of comfort, and your motion will disrupt it in a way they cannot ask you to correct because they lack the vocabulary for the disruption. Move slowly. Speak slowly. Let pauses between sentences last as long as they need to last.

- **Do not ask about time.** They have no concept of days, weeks, or hours in the way you understand them. They eat when they are hungry. They sleep when they are tired. They work when the light is sufficient. Asking "how long have you lived here?" or "when did you last see someone from the lowlands?" will produce answers that seem evasive, but are actually sincere — they will answer in terms of states rather than sequences. *"The well was new"* means something happened an unknowable number of years ago. They are not being poetic. They are being precise within the framework they have.

- **Accept their offerings.** They will offer food if you are there near a mealtime. The grain is dense and slightly bitter; the root vegetables are roasted in stone-hearth embers and taste faintly sweet; the goat meat, when available, is dried and chewy and seasoned with a mineral salt that likely comes from the plateau's own deposits. Eat what they offer, slowly, and express appreciation in whatever way feels natural to you. Refusing food is read — correctly or not — as a signal that you are distressed, and your distress will concern them in a quiet, helpless way that will make the rest of your visit more difficult for both parties.

- **Do not attempt to help with tasks.** They farm, tend goats, work metal, and maintain their buildings with the slow, deliberate rhythm of people who have never known rush or emergency. If you offer to assist, they may accept — but you will find their pace agonizing and your attempts to work quickly will be gently, silently redirected. You are not being mocked. They simply do not understand why you would want to finish something before it is finished.

## VI. Practical Concerns

### Temporal Disorientation

Your watch, if mechanical, will likely stop. If digital, it may continue running but will feel meaningless. The sun — when visible through the cloud cover — appears to hover at a fixed altitude. The light shifts between "bright" and "dim" without the gradual transitions of dawn and dusk. There are no shadows to track. You will lose track of how long you have been there.

**Bring a notched wooden tally or a string with knots.** Something physical and manual. Mark each time you eat or sleep. This is the only reliable way to track your own passage through time on the plateau, and it is imperfect at best.

### Food and Water

The water from the pools is safe to drink. It is cold and tasteless — the "near-transparent grey" description is accurate, and the water has no mineral taste beyond a faint, clean flatness. It will not spoil in a container while you are on the plateau. Neither will any food. The stasis affects organic material: bread left on a stone for days will remain exactly as fresh as when you set it down. This is convenient and deeply strange.

**Bring your own food regardless.** The people of Hern's Reach share what they have, but their surplus is small, and you should not arrive expecting to be sustained by them. Dried goods, hardtack, preserved meats — anything that would normally keep for weeks will keep indefinitely here, which means your rations will last the duration of your stay no matter how long that turns out to be.

### Fire

Fire behaves normally. This is unexpected and worth noting. The people of Hern's Reach use stone-hearth fires, and if you build a fire in one of their hearths or on flat stone away from the pools, it will burn, consume fuel, and produce heat and light normally. This is one of the few things on the plateau that visibly changes, and the residents seem to find it faintly remarkable — not because they have never seen fire, but because it is one of the only phenomena they encounter that operates by a process they understand as *consuming.*

### Cold

The plateau is cold. Not severely, but persistently. The air temperature sits at a level that would be comfortable with moderate exertion but will chill you to the bone if you are sitting still. Bring layers. A wool cloak or heavy wrap is more useful than a coat, because you will want to adjust gradually. The conifer stand is noticeably colder — cold enough that breath will mist — so if you visit, be prepared.

### Sleep

Sleep on the plateau is possible but peculiar. You will not dream, or rather, you will not remember dreaming. The silence is so total that the absence of auditory input becomes a kind of pressure, and many travelers report waking with the uncanny feeling that they have not slept at all, despite being rested. This effect fades with repeated exposure but never fully disappears.

If you are sleeping in the town, you will be offered a sleeping platform in one of the houses. Accept. The stone floors are hard and cold, and the houses are arranged so that interior windows face the central square rather than the open plateau — a design that, once you understand it, feels less like architecture and more like a preference for enclosure.

If you are sleeping on the open plateau, sleep among the boulders. The tufted grasses provide some insulation. Do not sleep near the pools. The stillness of the water surface at night, when the dim-light sky is reflected with perfect fidelity, will make it impossible to tell whether you are lying on solid ground or floating in open dark.

## VII. The Door

You will think about the door. This is inevitable. It is the only anomaly on the entire plateau — the one element that does not fit the pattern of equilibrium, the one thing that suggests something is happening beneath the stillness.

**Do not open it.**

This is not a prohibition based on danger. As far as anyone knows — and the residents of Hern's Reach have surely thought about it — nothing terrible happens if the door is opened. The wall behind it is solid pale stone, tight and unbroken. You may open it and find nothing but wall, and close it, and the hum will stop or continue or have never been there at all, and the silence will return to its exact previous state, which is indistinguishable from its subsequent state, because nothing here changes, and this is the maddening, beautiful, suffocating truth of the place.

But you will think about the door. You will stand at the northern rim and look at it and feel the handle's warmth through your glove — always warm, always, regardless of the air temperature — and you will wonder what is on the other side. You will construct theories. You will decide it is sealed, or abandoned, or a threshold to somewhere else. You will wonder if it was meant for you, or for someone who came before you, or for no one at all.

**Do not open it.** Not because something terrible will happen. Because you will add nothing to your understanding, and you will lose something — the mystery, the hum at the edge of perception, the one thing on this entire plateau that suggests it might not be as finished as it appears. As long as the door is closed, it is a question. And a place like this — a place that has resolved everything, that has settled into permanent equilibrium — needs its questions.

It is the only thing that makes it bearable to stay.

---

## VIII. Departure and Return

The road down the western slope is the same packed-earth switchback you climbed. The descent is faster than the ascent — steeper, less cautious — and when you reach the lower country, when you feel wind on your face for the first time in what may be hours or days, you will feel something shift in your body. Your inner ear will recalibrate. Shadows will begin moving. The sky will change.

And you will realize that you have no reliable way to know how much time has passed — in the world below, in the world you came from — since you arrived on the plateau.

This is normal. It is disorienting and, for some travelers, deeply distressing. But your body will readjust. The first wind you feel after leaving Keth-Varen will feel like the most alive thing you have ever experienced.

Whether or not you return is, of course, entirely up to you. Some travelers do — once, twice, never again. Some find the stillness comforting in a way they cannot explain to anyone who has not been there. Some find it unbearable and cannot imagine going back.

All I can tell you is this: the door will still be there. The handle will still be warm. And the hum, if you press your hand against the stone and stand very, very still, will begin again — just as it always does — just before the silence settles back into place, exactly where it was, as though nothing at all has happened.

As though nothing at all ever does.
