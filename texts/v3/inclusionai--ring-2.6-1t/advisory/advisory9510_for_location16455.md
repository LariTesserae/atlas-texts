# Preparing to Visit the High Shelf — A Traveler's Guide

---

## I. What You Are Walking Into

Before you pack a single thing, understand this: the High Shelf is not a destination. It is an *environment* — a two-thousand-foot platform of calcite and basalt that has been slowly, wetly, persistently alive for longer than any civilization has recorded. The settlement called Skarn (a name no one here remembers choosing) is not built *on* the Shelf so much as it has *grown out of it*, the way a mineral crystallizes from a solution. You are not visiting a town. You are visiting an organism that happens to contain a town.

An ordinary human, dropped here from any other world, will feel three things in the first five minutes: the wet, the cold, and the light. Everything else comes after, slowly, the way everything in Skarn comes — by accumulation, by patience, by wearing down into the stone.

---

## II. Your Body: What to Wear and How to Protect It

### Footwear
The single most important preparation. Every exposed surface on the Shelf is wet — not flooded, not muddy, but *persistently, film-lessly wet*, the way a glass countertop is wet when you press your palm to it at an angle. The stone is basalt and calcite: when wet, both are treacherous. You will slip on flat ground. You will slip on stairs that have been worn concave by ten thousand feet. 

Bring soft-soled shoes with aggressive tread — the kind designed for wet rock scrambling, not hiking boots with hard edges that will skate across basalt. If you can find them, rubber-soled approach shoes are ideal. Leather will absorb the persistent moisture and never fully dry. Avoid anything with a smooth sole entirely. You will, at some point, fall on a staircase. The stairs are narrow, often only a foot wide, and the drops between levels are two to three feet with sharp stone at the bottom. A twisted ankle here is not an inconvenience — it is potentially a death sentence in a settlement with no metal, no modern medicine, and two hundred quiet people who may or may not help quickly.

### Clothing
The air temperature on the Shelf hovers near cool-to-cold year-round — never truly freezing, never truly warm. What will get you is not the temperature but the *damp*. The stone radiates cold the way a cellar does, and that cold penetrates fabric and settles in your forearms, the backs of your knees, the small of your back. You will feel it in your joints after an hour.

Wear layers of wool or synthetic insulation. Avoid cotton — it absorbs the ambient moisture and holds it against your skin with punishing tenacity. A good outer shell that resists moisture but breathes is essential, because the condensation on the Shelf is not rain; it is the air itself depositing water on every surface simultaneously. You will never be "dry" here in the way you understand dryness. You will only be *more or less damp*. Bring two sets of everything — socks, underlayers — and keep one set sealed in a waterproof bag so you have something dry to change into. In Skarn, no one has a dryer. No one has a fire hot enough to truly dry fabric. You manage with body heat and patience.

### Eye Protection
The light is the most aggressive thing about the Shelf. It is cold-bright — blue-white, high-altitude light filtered through a persistent low cloud that diffuses without softening. There is no shade. The stone is pale and reflective. Every wet calcite vein catches and throws light. On a clear stretch of sky, you will be squinting within minutes, and within an hour you will have a headache behind your eyes you didn't know a place could give you. Bring sunglasses. If you don't have them, a wide-brimmed hat is a partial substitute. The inhabitants don't wear eye protection — they've adapted. You haven't.

### Skin
The water on the Shelf is mineral-rich. It seeps from joints in the rock, beads on surfaces, condenses from the air itself. Prolonged skin contact — especially bare hands — will leave your skin tight, dry, and faintly irritated from the dissolved calcite and mineral salts. If you plan to handle the rocks, dig, or grip stone surfaces, wear gloves. The inhabitants' hands are rough, cracked, and calloused. This is not an accident.

## III. Your Health: What the Shelf Does to You

### The Smell
When you first arrive, you will smell wet stone — chalky, mineral, clean — and beneath it, something organic and faintly ammoniac. The smell of too many warm bodies in a cold room. It is the accumulated breath, sweat, waste, and cooking of two hundred people living in thick-walled stone buildings with limited ventilation. It never goes away. After a day, you will stop noticing it consciously. After three days, you will carry it in your clothes. After a week, you won't smell it at all, but people from other shelves will smell it on you immediately.

The smell itself is not dangerous. It is, however, an indicator of nitrogen concentration in a closed environment. If you are sensitive to ammonia or have respiratory issues, bring a breathing cloth or mask for the interior spaces, especially the communal kitchens, where enormous pots of lichen broth simmer over central hearths and the air is thick with steam and particulate.

### The Water
Water is everywhere. It beads on surfaces, seeps through stone, condenses on every cold face in the mornings. It looks clean. It tastes mineral-heavy and faintly metallic — like licking a wet stone, because that is essentially what you are doing. The inhabitants drink it without issue, their bodies adapted over generations to the Shelf's particular mineral load. Your gut will likely object. Bring water purification if you have it, not because of biological contamination but because the dissolved calcite and unknown trace minerals can cause stomach cramping, nausea, and a persistent bitter taste that lasts days. If you must drink local water, small sips, and only from the channels the inhabitants use. They know which water tastes least foul.

### The Warm Ground
Below the first six inches, the ground is faintly warm. The inhabitants have never explained this; they never felt the need to. This warmth is not uniform — it is stronger near the settlement's center, faintest at the edges. Some multiverse scholars believe it is residual geothermal activity. Others suggest something stranger: that the Shelf itself is metabolizing, very slowly, very deeply, and the warmth is its metabolism radiating up.

Do not dig bare-handed. Do not lie directly on bare ground for extended periods. You don't know what lives in the warm soil — the translucent beetles appear to colonize areas where the ground is warmest, and the feathered worms in the deep storage chambers hum when touched, which suggests a sensitivity or reactivity you do not want to provoke without understanding.

## IV. Navigation: Understanding the Vertical Settlement

Skarn covers roughly three hundred paces. From any single vantage point, you can see most of it — an irregular mass of dark basalt walls stitched with white calcite mortar, crouching on the pale wet stone of the Shelf like a sleeping animal. It looks traversable in an afternoon. It is not.

Skarn is *layered*. It was built on top of itself over centuries — possibly millennia — with no master plan, no grid, no overarching architecture. Structures whose foundations are the roofs of older structures. Half-steps and sudden two-to-three-foot drops where one person's ground floor is another's second story. Stone staircases connect the levels, some worn to smooth concave curves by generations of feet, others rough-cut and pale, made that morning or that week.

**Practical advice:**
- You *will* get lost. Accept this early. The settlement has no streets, no names for its passages, and its inhabitants navigate by familiarity alone — by the specific worn step they recognize, the particular wall texture they feel under their fingers. Without years of experience, you will not develop this sense.
- Carry something to mark your route. Chalk, string, scratches on stone (the inhabitants won't mind — they scratch into stone themselves). The layered nature of Skarn means that a path that goes left at one level may go right at another, and the visual similarity of dark basalt walls and white calcite seams will disorient you quickly.
- Watch your step on staircases. The old ones are worn in the center to a smooth, rounded concavity that catches a foot wrong, especially if wet. The new ones are rough enough to scrape.
- There are no open plazas, no gathering spaces larger than a single room. The settlement's communal life happens in kitchens, on staircases, and at the edges of the Shelf. Privacy, to the people of Skarn, is achieved by thinning the walls and speaking softly, not by spatial separation.

## V. Food, Water, and Sustenance

### What They Eat
The diet of Skarn is austere and dominated by two staples: lichen broth and hare meat. The lichen grows in the sparse soil where water pools and limited sunlight reaches — in tufts, in patches, scraped from north-facing stones and the crevices of boulders. It is boiled for hours in enormous communal pots until it becomes a thin, gray-green broth that tastes vaguely of the sea and of iron. It is not unpleasant. It is not satisfying. It is fuel.

The pale hairless hares are the primary protein. They are hunted at dawn and dusk when they emerge to nibble at the sparse grass. Their meat is lean, mild, and — because of the Shelf's preservation properties — can be dried and stored for extended periods without spoiling. It is served in the communal kitchens, shared among inhabitants according to complex unspoken rules about contribution and need that you will not fully understand but will be expected to respect.

The only plant that resembles something *chosen* is a dark-leafed woody shrub growing in a ring around a warm spring on the southern edge. Its wood is used for tool handles. It is not eaten. Do not eat anything you cannot identify here. The translucent beetle-creatures, the feathered worms, the pale discs at the margins — these are not food. They are not *not* food. They are simply not for you.

### What You Should Bring
If you are staying more than a day or two, supplement your diet with food from your own world. The inhabitants will not offer you food directly — they will offer you a place at the communal hearth, which is its own form of generosity, and the broth will be shared — but your body may not adjust quickly enough to sustain you on their diet alone. High-calorie, compact foods: dried meats, hard breads, preserved fruits, nuts. Anything that travels well.

They will not refuse your food if you offer it, but they may eat it without much visible pleasure, the way one eats something one does not quite trust.

## VI. The Fauna: What Lives With You

You will share Skarn with its inhabitants, and its inhabitants are not only human.

### The Crawlers
The most visible fauna. Flat-bodied, the color of wet basalt, roughly hand-sized, with too many legs to count quickly. They emerge at all hours, preferring dim margins. They are silent. They eat the algae that grows in the condensation film on every surface. 

They are *organized.* Watch them long enough and you will see trails converging on specific points, clusters forming and dissolving, movements resembling — the inhabitants say, and this is not a metaphor — the flow of traffic through a market. They are not intelligent in any way you can easily test or define, but they are not random, and the difference between "organized" and "intelligent" is unsettling to witness in something with too many legs and no face.

They will crawl on you. They are harmless. They will crawl across your hands, your arms, your face if you sleep on the floor. They have no interest in eating you. They are simply passing through, the way rain passes through.

Try not to flinch. The inhabitants do not.

### The Clickers
You will hear the clickers before you see them — or rather, you will *feel* them before you locate them. Small, pale, blind things living deep in the walls. They communicate through a rhythmic clicking that is almost below the threshold of hearing: more vibration than sound, felt in the stone, felt in the floor, felt in the bones of your feet.

It is constant. At night, when the wind dies and the Shelf holds its breath, the clicking becomes the only sound in the world. The inhabitants sleep to it the way city-dwellers in your world sleep to traffic. You will not sleep the first few nights. By the third or fourth night, you may find that silence — true silence, without the clicking — is more disturbing than the sound itself.

### The Pale Hares
Hairless. Milk-pale. Silent as snowfall. They move through the settlement at dawn and dusk, nibbling sparse grass, pausing at doorways to regard the inhabitants with eyes that are *almost* but not quite too dark. They are not afraid. They are hunted, killed, and eaten. The inhabitants are ambivalent about whether this trust is wisdom or stupidity.

Do not try to pet them. They are prey animals shaped by a place without predators, and their fearlessness is not an invitation. They are also, it must be said, deeply unsettling to look at for reasons you cannot articulate. The eyes are wrong in a way you will notice immediately and never be able to describe to anyone who hasn't been here.

### The Others
The anomalous fauna. They are not common, but they are real.

The **translucent beetle**: a carapace you can see through, organs pulsing inside. It is not clear whether this is natural or something the Shelf has done to something else. It appears at the margins.

The **feathered worm**: found only in the deepest storage chambers. It hums when touched. Do not touch it.

The **disc**: pale, eyeless, radial body plan. The inhabitants call it *the disc* and do not discuss it. This silence is not superstition and it is not taboo in any way you will recognize. It is closer to the way you might choose not to discuss a weather pattern that only happens in one place — not because it is forbidden, but because there is no language for it that would be useful to anyone who hasn't seen it.

The disc does not appear to be dangerous. No one has ever been harmed by one. But they appear at the edges of the settlement, at the margins where the stone meets open Shelf, and they watch — if *watching* is the word for a radial body plan with no eyes. No one knows what they are. Everyone knows what they are. Those are different things.

## VII. The Customs: How Not to Offend

The people of Skarn have no governance. No council, no chief, no hierarchy of authority. There are customs, and they are enforced entirely through social pressure, withdrawal of company, and — in extreme cases — the quiet act of someone removing your belongings from the communal storage while you sleep and leaving them at the edge of the Shelf.

**Key customs:**

1. **Do not block a drainage channel.** This is the cardinal rule. Water here is felt to be alive in a dim, non-anthropomorphic way — not sacred, not worshipped, but *respected* the way you respect the weight of stone. The water moves upward here. It seeps, it condenses, it rises through the dense substrate by capillary action, defying everything you know about drainage. Blocking a channel is not merely inconvenient; it is understood as a kind of violence against the place itself. You will be removed from the settlement, quietly and without ceremony, if you do this.

2. **Speak softly.** Always. Not as a rule you'll be told — it is understood, the way you understand gravity. Loud voices are for emergencies. In daily life, the volume of your speech indicates the severity of what you're communicating. To speak loudly about nothing is to announce that you do not understand the place, that you are not safe to be around.

3. **Contribute before you take.** If you eat at the communal hearth, you offer something in return: firewood, gathered moss, a repaired tool, a watch through the night. The inhabitants will not refuse you food if you contribute nothing, but they will remember, and the memory will manifest as a softness that might be what passes for Skarn's version of exclusion — a gentle, wordless un-invitation from every shared space.

4. **Do not dig without asking.** The floors of Skarn contain centuries of accumulated material — tools, refuse, the detritus of lives lived and ended. To dig is to disturb history. The inhabitants have a system, unspoken, of who digs where and when. Ask. They will almost always say yes, but asking is the demonstration that you understand this is not *your* ground.

5. **Respect the staircases.** A new staircase is a communal effort — rough-cut stone placed with care. Old staircases are worn smooth by generations. To run on them, to skip steps carelessly, to damage a riser — this is noticed and quietly resented. The stairs are the skeleton of the settlement. Treat them accordingly.

## VIII. The Light, The Sound, The Feel — What It Is Like to Be Here

### Daytime
The Shelf is bright in a way that feels clinical. Cold white light from a pale sky, sharpened by the thin atmosphere at altitude, reflected and re-reflected off wet stone. Every crack is visible. Every condensation bead is a tiny lens. Every crawling thing casts a sharp, small shadow. There is no softness. No dappled shade, no warm golden hour in the way you know it. The light here is interrogative. It exposes.

At certain hours — early morning, late afternoon — the light strikes the wet calcite veins and the entire settlement seems to glow from within. A network of white lines pulsing faintly in the stone. The inhabitants do not remark on this. They have never known it to be otherwise. You will stare at it for a long time. They will watch you stare and say nothing.

### Nighttime
The light does not disappear so much as it *retreats*. The Shelf darkens to a deep blue-gray. The stars — if the cloud breaks — are extraordinary in their clarity, massed and close, as though the thin air has thinned further. The clickers take over. The vibration in the stone grows perceptible, rhythmic, almost — if you let yourself sink into it — comforting. The settlement goes quiet. The buildings hold warmth with surprising fidelity, and the stone walls, cold as they are to the touch, create a pocket of still air that feels almost warm by contrast with the outside.

You will hear *everything.* No wind buffering sound. No distant traffic. No hum of power systems. Just the clickers, the occasional movement of a hare outside, the soft sounds of two hundred people breathing in their stone cells, and — if you are very still, very quiet, and very lucky — a faint, deep hum that seems to come from the rock itself.

### The Smell
Wet mineral. Ammonia. Warm bodies in a cold room. Lichen broth. Faintly, wood smoke from the hearth fires. The green scent of moss when you press your face close to a north-facing boulder. All of it, all the time, layered the way everything here is layered, one scent pressed into the next like geological strata.

## IX. What to Bring — A Summary Packing List

- **Soft-soled, grippy shoes** designed for wet rock. You will ruin them. Bring a second pair if possible.
- **Wool or synthetic layers**, plenty of them. Everything will be damp. Cotton is an enemy.
- **A waterproof outer shell** — not rain gear exactly, but something that resists persistent moisture.
- **Sunglasses and a wide-brimmed hat** for the light.
- **Gloves** for handling stone and digging.
- **Compact, high-calorie food** from your own world. Dried meats, hard breads, preserved fruits, nuts. Do not rely on the local diet alone.
- **A water bottle with a filter** if you have one, for your own consumption. The local water is drinkable but will likely cause you gastrointestinal distress at first.
- **Chalk or string** for marking your route in the settlement's layered interior.
- **A small cutting tool** — a knife, a blade. Metal. There is none here. The people have stone tools: blades, scrapers, awls, needles, all beautifully made, all limited by their material. If your knife breaks, you are back to stone.
- **A breathing mask or cloth** if you have respiratory sensitivity. The ammonia content of the air in enclosed spaces may cause headaches or nausea at first.
- **Patience.** Bring the most patience you can access. This place moves at the speed of mineral deposition, of water creeping upward through stone, of staircases worn smooth by ten thousand feet. If you came here looking for something to happen, you have come to the wrong shelf. Sit down. Drink broth. Watch the crawlers. Learn the rhythm of the clickers. The Shelf has been here since before anyone built on it. It will be here after everything is gone. It knows something about time that you don't, and the fastest way to learn it is to stop trying to move quickly.

---

## X. A Final Note

The High Shelf is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is *present* — in the way a mountain is present, in the way deep water is present. The people of Skarn have carved out a life here with stone tools and quiet voices and a bone-deep understanding of water that moves the wrong way. They are not unfriendly. They are not friendly. They are *habituated* — to the place, to each other, to the slow and accumulating strangeness of a settlement built on a stone platform that hums faintly from below and preserves the dead as thoroughly as the living.

You will feel watched by the place itself. You are probably right.

Go gently. Speak softly. Do not block the drainage channels.
