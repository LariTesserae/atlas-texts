# Preparation Guide: The Resonance Flats

---

## I. Understanding What You're Walking Into

The most important thing to internalize before you go is that the Flats are not a place you visit. They are a system that processes you. Every square meter of the basin is occupied, active, and cycling. The ground is alive. The air is alive. The infrastructure — which is also geology, which is also ecology — has been running on an uninterrupted metabolism for so long that the distinction between "built" and "grown" is meaningless here. You will feel this the moment you step in: the ground will notice your weight. The sound will notice your heartbeat. The mats will notice your boots.

This isn't a warning to scare you away. It's a frame. If you prepare for the Flats as though you're visiting a city or a ruin or a wilderness, you will fail. Prepare instead as though you're introducing a foreign object into a living body. The body won't reject you — the locals describe the Flats as "indifferent but not hostile" — but it *will* try to absorb you. Your job is to remain distinct, mobile, and aware for the duration of your stay.

## II. Sensory Load and Psychological Preparation

### Sound — The Dominant Sense

Before sight, before touch, there is **sound**. It arrives in layers. First comes the subsonic throb — a pressure change in the chest, a vibration behind the sternum that you mistake for your own heartbeat until you realize it's external, omnidirectional, and continuous. This is the baseline hum of the hexagonal columns vibrating at approximately middle-C, a ground tone the entire Flats uses as a tuning fork for everything above it.

Then the higher layers break through: clicking, scratching, dripping, grinding, thrumming. Thousands of arthropod-scale creatures on mineral surfaces produce a constant scritch-scratch. The amphibian-scale drifters add a deep, wet choral hum when they filter-feed in groups — travelers who've heard five of them at once describe it as "a cathedral organ warming up." The echolocating flyers in the upper canopy produce clicks so loud they register as **physical taps on exposed skin**. During a hunting-flock event — the most extreme acoustic event you'll likely encounter — the broadband noise is described as "a wall that wipes out thought."

**What to bring:**

- **Adaptive attenuation earwear.** Standard noise-canceling headphones will not suffice. You need devices that actively dampen infrasonic and low-frequency input (the chest-throb is in the 30–60 Hz range) while also clipping broadband spikes, but that still pass enough mid-range audio for you to navigate by sound. The Flats are often navigated acoustically — particularly in the white-grey haze when visibility drops to near-zero — so total silence is not an option. The best systems use real-time spectral analysis to attenuate harmful frequencies while preserving environmental awareness. Test them extensively before arrival. Learn what the sounds mean before you need them in the field.
- **Chest-mounted subsonic dampener.** The sternum-pressure component of the baseline hum can cause nausea, disorientation, and — over extended exposure — a persistent feeling of cardiac arrhythmia. A small vibration-dampening pad worn over the sternum reduces this dramatically. It won't eliminate it. Nothing will. You're going into a place that vibrates at a frequency your body wants to match. Accept that your chest will feel strange the entire time you're there.
- **Emergency audio cutoff.** A manual override that provides near-total silence for 30–90 seconds. This is not for comfort. It's for moments when the acoustic environment overwhelms cognition — the flock events, or an unexpected resonance cascade where a conduit shift causes sympathetic vibration across an entire column array. In those moments, silence is the difference between standing and falling.

### Sight — A Secondary and Unreliable Sense

Visibility depends on atmospheric saturation. On a "clear" day, you'll see 50–100 meters through amber-lit haze, the bioluminescent fungal mats providing a dim, warm glow across every surface. On a bad day — and most days are bad — the air is a white-grey soup, and the Flats vanish upward into nothing. You'll navigate by ear, by ground-feel, and by the shifting moiré patterns of the fiber-optic conduits overhead, which cycle through cool blues and deeper frequencies during the "night" phase.

**What to bring:**

- **Headlamp with variable spectrum.** The mats glow amber, and during the dim cycle they shift to coal-red. A headlamp in the blue-violet range will give you the best contrast against fungal surfaces. Infrared is less useful — the environment runs warm and thermal contrast is minimal — but worth carrying as a backup for distinguishing the darker conduit openings from the surrounding mineral crust.
- **Anti-fog optics.** Your lenses, your visor, your viewport — everything will fog within minutes. The air is that saturated. Carry anti-fog solution and a microfiber cloth, and get comfortable with ritual cleaning. This is not optional. A traveler who loses visual function in the Flats without acoustic navigation training is effectively blind in a place that is actively trying to grow over their last point of stillness.
- **Lume-markers.** Small adhesive bioluminescent tags that you place at decision-points as you move. The Flats look homogenous, and disorientation is the number-one cause of overstays. Drop a marker, turn back if unsure, and never move more than 50 meters from your last known reference point until you've oriented.

## III. Physical Protection

### The Ground

Describing the Flats' surface as "ground" is generous. It's a crust of calciumite and sintered fungal chitin, warm underfoot, perpetually damp, with the compliance of a sponge that has opinions about your weight. Step hard and fluid wells up through every crack. Step gently and it holds. The surface is a membrane.

You'll feel this through any boot. The warm dampness is constant. The give is constant. After an hour, you'll stop thinking of it as walking on a surface and start thinking of it as walking *on* something — something alive enough to respond.

**What to bring:**

- **Wide-soled, pressure-distributing boots with anti-adhesion soles.** The goal is twofold: you want enough surface area to distribute your weight so you don't well water with every step (which destabilizes your footing and sends vibrations into the subsurface network that *will* attract attention from the fauna), and you want a sole material that the mycelial mats don't grip. Silicone-impregnated surfaces work. Rough rubber does not — it gives the mats purchase. Treat your soles before entry and re-treat every six hours.
- **Gaiters, sealed.** The water that seeps up through every crack carries mineral traces that taste like copper and corrode standard fabrics. It will also soften leather, degrade untreated synthetics, and dissolve adhesives. Sealed gaiters — not merely waterproof but *sealed* at every seam — protect the boot-to-leg transition where both water and the smaller arthropods enter.

### The Air

Humid to the point of precipitation. Warm saline mist condenses on every surface — your clothing, your gear, your eyelashes. On a bad-visibility day, you're essentially walking through warm fog. The air carries mineral particulate and enough biological matter (spores, chitin fragments, dried slurry from conduits) that unprotected inhalation will cause irritation within an hour and genuine respiratory distress within a day.

**What to bring:**

- **Filtered breather.** Not a full rebreather — the air is breathable, just unpleasant and particulate-heavy. A half-face respirator with a P100 particulate filter and a moisture trap will serve. The moisture trap is critical: without it, the filter saturates within 40 minutes and breathing resistance spikes. Carry spare filters; in the humidity, a used filter that's been clipped to your belt will colonize with fungal growth within hours.
- **Anti-fog face seal.** Where the respirator meets skin, condensation pools. If you wear glasses or a visor, the fog issue compounds. Use a hydrophobic face seal and accept that you'll need to wipe down your mask constantly.

### The Fauna

The small arthropod-scale creatures are mostly harmless background noise — thousands of tiny legs on mineral surfaces, the scritch-scratch of feeding mandibles, the scatter-sound when something larger moves. But "mostly harmless" means they will crawl on you. They are curious, or perhaps they're tasting you, but they will investigate any new surface in their environment, and your body is a warm, salty, novel surface.

The medium creatures — the amphibian-scale bladders drifting through the lower corridors — are not aggressive but they are **large** and they move with slow deliberate grace that doesn't account for obstacles your size. Being in the drift-path of a three-meter filter-feeder is like being next to a slow-moving truck that doesn't know you're there.

The flyers are the primary acoustic threat, not physical — but their echolocation clicks hit skin like taps and their hunting bursts are loud enough to cause disorientation and even temporary vestibular disruption.

**What to bring:**

- **Tight-weave outer layer, sealed at all openings.** Not a spacesuit — just something the arthropods can't easily get underneath. Wrist seals, ankle seals, collar seal. The smaller creatures are thin enough to slip under loose fabric. They're not dangerous in themselves, but the sensation of hundreds of tiny legs inside your clothing will panic you, and panic in the Flats leads to bad decisions (standing still, thrashing, leaving a trail of disrupted infrastructure behind you like a bread-crumb trail that says *something came through here and it was struggling*).
- **Impact padding on torso and shoulders.** For the medium creatures. A glancing contact with a three-meter drift-bladder at speed can knock the wind out of you or break a collarbone. Light, flexible padding — the kind used in urban multiverse transit gear — is adequate.
- **Bright, steady light source worn on the back or shoulders.** Not for your vision — for the flyers. The echolocating creatures in the canopy are primarily acoustic hunters, but they have rudimentary photoreception and will avoid bright, steady light sources. This isn't guaranteed. It isn't science. But it measurably reduces the chance of a flock dropping to investigate you, and in the Flats, you take every advantage.

## IV. Anti-Assimilation Protocols — The Real Threat

This is what makes the Flats different from every other location in any multiverse you'll visit. **The environment incorporates stationary matter into its own infrastructure.** The fungal mats grow on any horizontal surface left unattended. The conduit network reroutes around standing bodies. The mineral crusts accept new deposits — including, apparently, the residue of a stationary human body and its gear — within a week.

This is not hostile. There is no intent. The Flats are a metabolism, and a motionless object is just... substrate. But the practical implication is that **you cannot stop moving for long.**

A mat will colonize your boot within an hour. Within a day, conduit routing will have adjusted to your body as a permanent obstacle. Within a week, if you stayed — if you simply stopped and waited — you would be infrastructure. Warm, saturated, humming, home.

**What to do:**

- **Move in shifts.** Travel for at least 50 minutes out of every hour. During the ten-minute rest, do not sit on the ground. Elevate yourself on a frame or platform — the fungal mats will colonize a floor-level rest point within minutes. A lightweight collapsible stool with treated legs is worth its weight in anything.
- **Anti-fungal topical treatment for skin.** The mats don't just grow on gear. They'll colonize exposed skin — not quickly, not aggressively, but over the course of a day of stillness, you'll feel the prickle of hyphae seeking purchase. An anti-fungal salve applied to all exposed skin every four hours prevents this. Reapply after any prolonged contact with the ground.
- **Gear turnover.** Anything left on the ground for more than two hours should be considered compromised. The mats will have bonded with it. Forcing it free will damage both the gear and the local infrastructure (which *will* respond to damage — not with intent, but with a localized rerouting that changes the acoustic and hydrological character of the area around the disruption). Minimize what you bring. Minimize what you set down.
- **Sleep protocol.** If you need to rest for a full cycle, use a suspended shelter — a lightweight hammock system anchored to the upper conduit lattice. The mats won't reach you at elevation. The acoustic environment at canopy height is different — less clicking, more of the deep hum and the distant sound of the filter-feeders — and some travelers find it easier to sleep. The fiber-optic curtains shift color during the night cycle and cast slow-moving light patterns that are, against all reason, restful.

## V. Navigation and Orientation

The Flats are continental in scale, homogenous in appearance, and constantly restructuring. Conventional landmarks last hours before the fungal mats colonize and alter them. The conduit network shifts. The hexagonal columns, while ancient and stable, are spaced too regularly to serve as orientation markers — every direction looks the same.

**How to navigate:**

- **Sound mapping.** Learn the sound signatures. Each column array hums at a slightly different pitch — microvariations caused by age, mineral content, and the specific fauna that have colonized them. Experienced Flats travelers navigate by pitch. You can learn to do this in a day if you have good pitch discrimination. If you don't, carry a sonar-mapping device that can read the harmonic profile of nearby columns and triangulate against a reference chart.
- **Water flow.** The water that seeps up through the ground moves in slow, broad gradients. It always moves *down* — toward the lowest point of whatever basin sector you're in. If you've lost all other orientation, follow the gradient. Down leads to drainage channels. Drainage channels lead to edges.
- **Conduit logic.** The larger conduits are semi-permanent. They carry flow — water, sludge, light — and their routing, while it shifts locally, follows the same broad logic as any circulatory system. Going *with* the flow in a major conduit is almost always going to lead you toward a nexus, and a nexus is almost always findable and, if you're lucky, visitable by outside travelers. A nexus will have *other people* — researchers, residents, the occasional fellow multiverse traveler. Follow the flow.
- **Do not retrace your steps.** The Flats remember. The mats growing on a path you've walked will be thinner for weeks — your passage disrupted them, and regrowth creates a scar that subtly changes the acoustic profile of the area. Following your own trail *backwards* is disorienting because the scar sounds different going the other direction. Always push forward. If you must return, use your lume-markers.

## VI. Sustenance and Resupply

- **Water:** The standing water is potable after filtration — warm, slightly saline, with a copper tang from mineral content. Not pleasant. Not harmful if filtered. Do not drink unfiltered standing water; the biological load is high and your gut biome is not adapted to this biome.
- **Food:** Bring everything you need. Do not eat anything native. The arthropods are theoretically edible but carry parasites and microorganisms that are not cross-compatible with human biology. The bio-sludge in the conduits is nutrient-rich but no one has analyzed it well enough to call it safe. Carry sealed, shelf-stable rations. Eat in your shelter or on elevated rest points. Crumbs become infrastructure.
- **Caloric expenditure will be high.** The humidity, the constant low-grade acoustic stress, the uneven terrain, and the need to stay moving will burn more energy than you expect. Pack 1.5x what you'd normally carry for an equivalent duration trip.

## VII. Communication and Emergency Extraction

- **Radio works, but unreliably.** The conductive infrastructure creates interference patterns. Line-of-sight comms are best but limited by visibility. A relay drone — waterproof, fungus-resistant — placed at elevation can extend your effective comm range.
- **Signal flares are effective.** The fauna and infrastructure respond to sudden bright stimuli with a visible clearing effect — a brief bubble of space opens around a flare as the local system reacts to the disturbance. Fire a flare and something *will* come to investigate. Whether that something is rescue or the Flats' version of an immune response depends on context.
- **Pre-arrange extraction coordinates and timing.** The Flats are navigable if you have discipline and the right equipment, but they are not navigable casually. Set a hard extraction window and a hard extraction point. If you miss your window, have a secondary and know that the secondary may require an extra day of travel because the environment shifted.

---

## VIII. Final Notes

The Resonance Flats are one of the most extraordinary locations in the known multiverse. They are beautiful in the way a living body is beautiful — overwhelming in scale, indifferent in attention, and humming with a ten-thousand-layered vitality that makes you feel, for the first time in a while, how small a thing a person is.

Respect the place. Move with it, not against it. Keep your equipment dry, your skin treated, your ears protected, and your body in motion.

And if, at some point during the night cycle, you're standing in the amber glow of the mats with the deep hum of the columns vibrating in your chest and a billion small drops of condensation falling around you in absolute silence — stand still for just a moment. Not too long. Just a moment.

Listen. The Flats are trying to tell you something in a language made entirely of water.

You won't understand it. But you'll remember it.
