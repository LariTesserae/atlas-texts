# Preparation Guide: Visiting the Splay

---

## I. What You're Walking Into

Before you pack a single thing, understand this: the Splay is not a place you visit. It is a place that happens around you. The basin is twenty kilometers across, acoustically alive, atmospherically hostile to human senses, and occupied by things that are not hostile to you specifically — but are also not *aware* of you in any way you would recognize. You are not entering a wilderness. You are entering an ecosystem that has been running, uninterrupted, for at least two centuries, and it does not have you in its plans.

An ordinary human walking into the Splay unprepared will last, conservatively, a few hours before the sound, the spores, and the scale of the emptiness make them want to sit down and never stand up again. So let's get you ready.

---

## II. Hearing Protection — Your Single Most Critical Piece of Gear

The Splay is a resonance chamber. The young, smooth rock walls are concave in places and function like the interior of a cello. Every sound generated on the basin floor is caught, amplified, redirected. The parametric callers — the fauna whose vocalizations shift in mathematical frequency progressions — create a three-dimensional cage of overlapping sound that has been described as "deafening" even in open air. At full chorus, the volume would be painful in a room. Here it is worse, because there are no walls to absorb it, and the basin's geometry ensures it *never stops.*

Then there are the bone-conducted hums from the structures. These bypass the ears entirely and vibrate directly through whatever material they contact — stone, your boots, your trekking pole — into the bones of your forearm and up through your skeleton. Earplugs do not help with this.

**What to bring:**

- **Industrial-grade active noise-cancelling headphones or in-ear defenders**, rated for low-frequency attenuation. You need something that handles subsonic groans and sustained high-volume midrange. Standard consumer ANC earbuds will not be sufficient. Look for aviation-rated or heavy-machinery-rated hearing protection with a high NRR (Noise Reduction Rating of 30+).
- **Bone-conduction dampening pads** — if you can find or fabricate them. These are foam or gel pads designed to be pressed against the mastoid processes (the bony protrusions behind your ears) or strapped to the wrists and shins. They won't eliminate bone-conducted vibration, but they'll reduce it enough to keep your teeth from rattling and your forearms from going numb.
- **A backup pair of simple foam earplugs.** If your electronics fail — and at the Splay, assume all electronics will be *tested* — you need mechanical hearing protection that doesn't depend on batteries.
- **Headband audio with a decibel meter overlay**, if available. You want to know when ambient sound crosses into the range that causes permanent hearing damage, because between the parametric callers and the basin resonance, it will.

**Psychological note:** Even with protection, the sound of the Splay does not go away. It becomes a texture you feel more than hear. Experienced multiverse travelers describe it as "living inside someone's throat." Accept this before you arrive. Fighting it makes it worse.

## III. Respiratory Protection — The Spore Haze Is Alive

At shoulder height across the entire basin, there is a permanent layer of airborne spore-matter. It is the remains (or the means) of the cestral groves' canopy, which hangs three hundred meters overhead and filters all incoming sunlight. It gives the Splay its smoke-stained-glass appearance. It is what breathes life into this ecosystem.

It is also a particulate cloud of unknown biological composition suspended in the air you need to breathe.

**What to bring:**

- **A sealed respirator with P100 particulate filters, minimum.** The spore-matter is described as "airborne" and "hanging at shoulder height," which means you will walk through it continuously. A simple dust mask will not cut it — you need a proper seal with replaceable filters. If you have access to a respirator rated for organic vapor cartridges, bring those instead. Better to over-filter than to find out what these spores do to human alveoli two days in.
- **Spare filters.** Bring at least three sets. The Splay is twenty kilometers across. You may be breathing this haze for hours or days.
- **Eye drops (saline, sterile).** Even if you're wearing eye protection, the haze gets everywhere. Your eyes will feel gritty, dry, or over-lubricated depending on your sensitivity. Saline drops will help.

## IV. Vision and Lighting — Perpetual Twilight

There is no direct sun in the Splay. All light has passed through three hundred meters of living haze and arrives as a diffuse, sourceless glow — muted colors, long violet shadows, edges softened. It is as though everything is viewed through ancient, smoke-colored glass. During the "deeper cycle" (local analog of night), the vathines glow faintly through their bioluminescent sap, casting the basin in what can only be described as "columns of fire seen through frosted glass."

**What to bring:**

- **Amber or rose-tinted lenses** (either as clip-ons, a separate pair of glasses, or inserts for your respirator/goggles). These will restore contrast in the Splay's flattened light. Normal sunglasses will make things worse — you're not cutting glare, you're trying to amplify what little definition the environment provides.
- **A headlamp with a red-light mode.** Your night vision will be useless in the Splay regardless — the violet shadows are too pervasive. But red light preserves what dark adaptation you can maintain and does not interfere with the bioluminescent glow of the vathines, which you will come to rely on as landmarks.
- **A physical compass and a printed map.** GPS signals are unreliable in the Splay due to the spore canopy, the acoustic interference, and (in some reports) the presence of the structures, which seem to generate localized electromagnetic effects. Do not rely on electronic navigation. Mark your entry point. The vathines are spaced at ~100-meter intervals and are your best landmarks, but they all look alike after a while.
- **Watch with a luminous dial or GPS timepiece.** Without sunlight, your circadian rhythm will collapse within hours. Know what time it is. Know how long you have been walking. Set a turnaround time before you enter and do not override it.

## V. Footwear and Terrain — The Chalk Is Deceptive

The basin floor is covered in pale sedimentary chalk (called "ite" by the few who have cataloged its geology) that is still soft enough to gouge with a thumb. Rivers of recent origin — some appearing only within the last decade — have carved through the calciumite and created channels, silt beds, and slippery crossings. The terrain is flat and the visibility is extraordinary — you can see for sixty meters or more in any direction — but the ground is uneven, chalky, and wet wherever rivers have been.

**What to bring:**

- **Sturdy, waterproof mid-calf boots with aggressive tread.** The chalk is soft and will cake on regular soles, reducing grip. You need something that sheds silt and channels water away. River crossings are likely — the streams are shallow but fast-carving, and the calciumite beds are treacherously smooth when wet.
- **Gaiters.** Chalk silt is fine enough to infiltrate everything. It will get into your boots, your clothing seams, your respirator's valve mechanism. Gaiters keep it out of the places it will cause the most problems.
- **Trekking poles.** Not optional. The chalk surface looks stable but can be slippery or give way unexpectedly, especially near the river channels. Poles also help you probe the terrain ahead in the haze. And if you encounter a bone-conducted hum from a nearby structure, planting your poles on the ground will tell you through your hands whether the vibration is increasing or decreasing — which is information your muffled ears cannot provide.

## VI. Clothing — Layered, Sealed, Visible

The climate is temperate to cool (no direct sun, perpetual haze), with periodic warm rain. The air is consistently humid from the spore layer and river evaporation.

**What to bring:**

- **Moisture-wicking base layer, synthetic or merino.** Cotton will absorb the humidity and never dry. You will be damp the entire time you are in the Splay. Accept this and plan for it.
- **Wind- and water-resistant outer shell.** The rain, when it comes, is "brief, warm, and heavy." It will soak you in minutes and then stop, and the chalk will absorb the water almost instantly. You need a shell that dries fast and blocks the fine mist that hangs in the lower air.
- **High-visibility markings or panels.** The Splay's palette is muted — pale chalk, dark vathine bark, translucent haze. You want to be as visible as possible to any fauna (or other travelers) that might be nearby. Bright orange or reflective tape on your pack, your helmet, your poles.
- **Gloves.** Not for warmth — for gripping the chalk and the strange smooth surfaces of the structures. Thin, tactile, durable.

## VII. Nutrition, Hydration, and Medical

The rivers in the Splay are fresh but should be treated with suspicion. The chalk sediment is extraordinary, and the water has been in contact with materials (vathine root systems, spore-matter, unknown mineral deposits) that your body is not prepared for.

**What to bring:**

- **All water and food for your entire trip.** The Splay has not been determined to be biologically safe for human consumption of any kind. Do not drink untreated water. Do not eat any plant matter (the spadera blossoms are lovely and probably toxic). Do not eat fauna. Bring high-calorie, lightweight provisions: energy bars, dried meals, electrolyte supplements.
- **Water purification tablets and a portable filter** as a backup, but understand that they may not address the unknowns in Splay water. Boiling is the safest option if you have fuel.
- **A basic trauma and allergic reaction kit.** Epinephrine auto-injector. Antihistamines (oral and topical). Broad-spectrum antibiotics. You have no way of knowing what you will be exposed to. A grazer was described as having opaque, recessed eyes that "spin like lighthouse lenses" — it is not hyperbole to say that contact with an organism from the Splay could trigger an immune response you have never trained for.
- **Sunscreen.** There is no direct sun, but the UV spectrum is not fully blocked by the spore haze. Long exposure in diffuse UV conditions can cause burns that you don't notice until hours later.

## VIII. Psychological Preparation

This is not optional and is arguably the most important section of this guide.

**The Splay does not feel empty, but it is profoundly empty.** You will be able to see for hundreds of meters in every direction, and you will see almost nothing. The vathines are so far apart that each one is a solitary monument. The structures are separated by distances that feel deliberate. The fauna are audible always, visible almost never. The effect of this is cumulative and disorienting — you will feel observed, but you are not. You will feel too visible, but you are not. The acoustic saturation creates the impression of proximity where there is only distance.

**What to prepare for:**

- **Claustrophobic openness.** The Splay is the inverse of a tight space, but it produces tight-space anxiety. The sound presses in from everywhere. The sightlines are too long, the emptiness too conspicuous. It is agoraphobia in a cathedral made of fog and bass.
- **Loss of navigational confidence.** The spore haze erases the horizon. The violet shadows lie about the direction of the light source (there is no light source). Your compass will work but it will feel wrong. Trust your instruments even when your instincts say otherwise.
- **The structures will fascinate you.** This is a warning. The built things in the Splay are masterworks of incomprehensible design. They are beautiful. They are familiar enough to look *intentionally* so. You will want to go inside them. You will feel invited. **Do not enter the subterranean structures.** The sound coming from beneath them is described as "machinery, or breathing, or machinery that has been breathing for so long the distinction has eroded." That is not an invitation.
- **The parametric callers will make you feel intelligent in the wrong way.** Their vocalizations follow mathematical progressions. You will recognize intervals. You will catch patterns. You will think you are understanding something. You are not. You are a primate auditory cortex pattern-matching in the presence of something that does not pattern-match back. Stop. Breathe through your respirator. Move on.
- **Set a firm turnaround time** and share it with your team. The Splay is the kind of place where you can walk for hours and feel like you have gone nowhere, only to realize you have been watching the same vathine from different angles while the sound rearranged your sense of distance.

## IX. Conduct in the Splay

A few rules, derived from the reports of the very few people who have entered and returned:

1. **Do not cross the invisible lines the fauna observe.** You will not see them. You will notice a grazer's path turn abruptly for no reason, or a flying thing bank away from an open space that offers no physical obstruction. Respect those boundaries. Walk around them. They are not for your understanding.

2. **Do not touch the structures without purpose.** They hum. The hum enters your bones. This is a real physical interaction. Touch the fused vathine roots of a structure's base and you will feel a pulse — not your pulse, not the tree's pulse — and it will oscillate for minutes after you remove your hand. No one knows what this means for the structure or for you. Minimize contact.

3. **Do not follow the fauna.** They are large, slow, and apparently unbothered by your presence. This is not tameness. This is indifference. A grazer fifteen meters from you will regard you with eyes that spin like lighthouse lenses and then return to cropping chalk-dwelling organisms you cannot see. It has done this before. It will do this long after you leave. Do not interpret its calm as consent to approach further.

4. **Do not stay after dark.** The "night" version of the Splay is described as the "deepening of an already-dim cycle." The vathines glow, the parametric callers intensify (some of them calling from *above*), and the sound becomes the weather, the ground, the light. If this is starting to sound like a description of transcendence, understand that it has been described as a migraine by everyone who has experienced it unprepared.

5. **Leave no trace.** The Splay has been integrating its structures with the landscape for two centuries. The chalk is soft. The roots are patient. Anything you leave behind will begin to be accepted by the place. Do not give it anything to accept.

---

## X. Final Notes

The Splay is not malevolent. It is not benevolent. It is a system of enormous, ancient, precise biological and possibly technological processes operating on a scale and timeline that dwarfs human presence. You will walk through it and it will not register your passage. The sound will press against you and the spores will thicken your breath and the vathines will stand like exhausted gods against a sky that isn't there.

But the chalk holds your weight. The rivers are carving new paths this very season. Something enormous is moving north, very slowly, and you can feel its footfalls in the stone before you hear them. The Splay is happening.

Go prepared. Come back with stories. Do not stay too long.
