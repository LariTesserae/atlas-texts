# Preparing to Visit the Thallus: A Field Guide for the Multiverse Traveler

---

## I. Before You Arrive: Mental and Philosophical Orientation

The single most important thing you can do before visiting the Thallus is understand one fact at the deepest level of your being: **you will not matter here.** This is not discouragement — it is liberation, if you allow it to be. The Thallus has been doing what it does for millennia beyond counting, and it will continue for millennia beyond your visit. Nothing you do will accelerate it, slow it, or redirect it. This is not a metaphor for "being one with nature." It is a literal structural reality of the place: the organism (if it is an organism) is vast beyond comprehension and so slow that your presence registers, if at all, as a sensation the way a human might register the landing of a mite on their forearm — if the mite were lucky, and the human barely noticed.

**What this means practically:** The Thallus will not respond to provocation, negotiation, domination, or fear. It will not reward you for bravery or punish you for timidity. Prepare to shed every framework of travel that relies on *transaction* — the idea that you give effort and receive experience, that you conquer terrain, that you leave your mark. None of that applies. Arrive with the posture of a stone settling into a riverbed. You are not passing through. You are *joining temporarily,* and the distinction matters.

**Psychological preparation:** Spend time in silence before you go. Not comfortable silence — actual, thick, oppressive, padded silence. The kind you find in anechoic chambers, in deep caves, in the interiors of your own skull at 4 a.m. The Thallus will amplify this. Your hearing will become shockingly intimate. You will hear your own heartbeat, your breathing, the fluid moving in your joints. This is not a malfunction. The Thallus simply removes every other sound from your world. People who are not prepared for this experience have been known to panic within hours — not from danger, but from the sheer *fullness* of the quiet. Train yourself to sit with your own body's noise and find it unremarkable.

**Expect temporal disorientation and depersonalization.** The Thallus does not have "days." It has gradients of moisture and warmth that move across the plateau on timescales of weeks or months. Your internal clock will begin to drift. Within a few days — or what you estimate as a few days, since you have no sun to anchor your circadian rhythm — you will lose track of how long you've been there. Symptoms: a sense that time has stopped or looped; the feeling that you have always been here and always will be; a creeping unfamiliarity with your own identity, as though your memories belong to someone else. This is normal. It does not mean you are losing your mind. It means you are syncing. Whether you want to sync or not is a question you should answer before you arrive.

## II. Physical Gear

### Footwear and Traversal

The most dangerous surface feature of the Thallus is the mat itself. It looks like ground. It behaves like ground. It is not ground.

**Boots:** You need high-ankle, waterproof, rigid-soled boots with a wide base — think of something halfway between mountaineering boots and snowshoes. The mat gives under your weight. Standard hiking boots will allow your ankle to sink into the living substrate, which will then close around your foot with an organic, gentle, inescapable grip. This is not hostile. It is *absorptive.* You will feel like you are being held. Over time — hours, possibly less — the sensation becomes deeply unsettling, because it mimics human touch. People have reportedly stood still for hours, weeping, unable to extract their feet, not from pain but from something that felt like *grief.*

The wider sole distributes your weight and the rigid sole prevents the mat from gripping through the boot's sole. Waterproofing is essential because the mat is damp everywhere, the puddles are warm and oily with iron and overripe fruit, and anything that soaks through will not dry for the duration of your visit (which may be longer than you expect).

**Gaiters and leg wraps:** The fauna at ground level includes things with "too many legs" that move through the mat's surface layer. Most are harmless to humans, but contact with exposed skin produces a sensation euphemistically described by previous travelers as "being tasted." This is not painful, but it is invasive — a crawling, tingling awareness that something is sampling your chemistry. Wrap your lower legs. Tuck everything in.

**Walking poles or staffs:** Heavyly recommended. Not for balance (the mat is flat, give or take a pool depression) but for *probing.* The mat's thickness varies. In stretches where it is thin, you risk breaking through to the living substrate below, which has been described as closing around a whole leg. A staff lets you test depth before committing weight. The bones protruding from the mat can also be fragile and slippery — your pole can alert you to them before your boot finds them.

### Clothing

**Full coverage.** Long sleeves, long pants, collar up, no exposed skin. The air is saturated with spores, evaporated moisture, and particulate organic matter. What this does to bare skin varies: some travelers report nothing, others report a persistent warmth and tingling that progresses to numbness, and a few have reported the sensation of their skin *thickening,* as though the atmospheric moisture is being absorbed into the epidermis. Full coverage is precautionary. You do not want to find out which category you fall into.

**Breathable but sealed.** You will be sweating. The air is oily with humidity. You need fabric that moves moisture away from the body but does not admit the environment. Assume you will be damp for the entire visit. Design your layering around this assumption — moisture-wicking base, sealed mid-layer, and if you have access to it, a lightweight fully waterproof shell for the wettest periods (when the dripping intensifies and the air grows heavy enough to feel on your skin like warm oil).

**Eye protection.** The light is amber-green and diffused. It never reaches full brightness, but over hours of exposure in this perpetual low-contrast environment, your eyes will fatigue severely. Tinted lenses — amber or rose — will reduce strain. Additionally, the spore load in the air means your eyes will water. Bring something to manage this.

**Head covering.** Not for sun (there is no sun here) but for drip management. The columns and their shelves drip constantly, and in wetter zones you will feel a continuous light rainfall of condensation. A hood or brimmed hat keeps the steady trickle of warm water from running down your neck and inside your clothing. This is not trivial. Cold water on the neck is uncomfortable. Warm water on the neck, in this context, tends to produce a dull, muzzy comfort that lulls you toward sleep. Sleep in the Thallus is dangerous (see below).

### Shelter and Sleep

**Do not sleep on the mat.** Beneath the soft crust, the living substrate maintains a warmth that is metabolically produced. Over hours of direct contact — skin against mat, boot soles notwithstanding — this warmth will seep into your body and slow your metabolism. Travelers who have slept on the mat report sleeping for what they perceive as eight to twelve hours and waking up feeling as though weeks have passed, disoriented, with the sensation that their body has been *processed.* This word recurs in accounts. *Processed.* As though the mat has been doing slow, patient work on them while they slept.

Use a hammock or elevated platform. You must be off the mat. If possible, tie yourself between two columns. The columns are dark, dense, and stable — they have been standing for centuries. Anchor your hammock to them. This is not only safer but psychologically stabilizing: you will be enclosed, surrounded by the structure of the Thallus rather than lying exposed on its skin.

If you cannot rig a hammock, sleep on a rigid platform — a cot, a board, anything that keeps your body from conforming to the mat's surface. The mat is forgiving. This is precisely the problem. It is *too* forgiving. It wants to hold you.

**Never sleep at ground level during the "deep hours" (the bioluminescent period).** The mat's underlayer glows cold during this time — described as greenish, rising to the surface "like cold fire." This is when the subterranean presences are most active. You will feel the deep pulses more strongly. The clicking of the small pale creatures intensifies. The silence itself changes pitch. Sleep elevated. Sleep enclosed if possible. Sleep with a tether to something solid.

## III. Navigation

The Thallus is, to the human eye, essentially **featureless.**

The columns repeat at regular-enough intervals to seem organized but without any individually distinguishing marks. The mat is uniform in every direction. There is no horizon. The haze eats distance. You cannot see where you have been because the mat has already begun to absorb and overgrow your footprints within hours — not violently, not with intention, but with the inevitability of a wound healing.

**Bring GPS. Bring redundancy. Bring more than that.**

Standard GPS may function on the Thallus, but the mat's electromagnetic properties are unknown and undocumented. Previous travelers have reported GPS signal drift, sudden loss of satellite lock, and at least one account of a device displaying coordinates that the traveler *knew* were wrong but that read consistently and precisely — as though the Thallus had its own coordinate system and the technology had simply deferred to it.

**Physical trail marking is marginally more reliable.** Use bright, non-biodegradable flagging tape tied to column bases. The mat will grow over it, but slowly enough that you will see it for at least several days. This may buy you enough time to retrace your steps if you are methodical.

**Waypoint journaling.** Record everything. Column density. Mat texture patterns. Any mat-marks you encounter. The relative positions of pools. The angle of drip-lines off columns. The Thallus may be uniform to the human eye, but on careful examination, sections *do* differ — the intervals between columns tighten in some areas, the mat is darker in others, the pools are more frequent. These differences are subtle enough to be invisible to the casual observer, but if you record them, they become a navigable map.

**Assess honestly whether you need to navigate at all.** If you came to observe, to witness, to *be in* the Thallus — there is a case for simply staying put. Let the Thallus come to you. It will. It is already here. Trying to traverse it aggressively is a mistake. People who try to cross the plateau in straight lines become lost in a few hours. People who sit and wait — who settle — find that they know exactly where they are, relative to a center that is themselves. Whether this orientation is "real" or constructed by the Thallus to keep you still is irrelevant. Those who stay put leave. Not always. But more often than those who walk.

## IV. Biological Hazards

### The Water

The pools on the Thallus are warm, black, and smell faintly of iron and overripe fruit. Do not drink them. Do not bathe in them. Do not wade through them in footwear with any breach. The biological activity in these pools is entirely unknown. The black color suggests anaerobic microbial life entirely unrelated to terrestrial biology, or a chemically unique microbiome that has had millennia of isolation from any external ecosystem. The "overripe fruit" odor is likely a metabolic byproduct of this life — possibly alcohol, possibly something else.

If you fall into a pool (unlikely if you are careful, possible if the mat gives way unexpectedly), wash the affected area immediately with clean water from your supply. Scrub. The mat around pools hosts fauna that is more active and more aggressive than the general ground-level fauna — there is what can only be called a *halo* of heightened biological activity around each pool, as if the pools are feeding the mat and everything near the pools is benefitting from that flow.

### The Spores

The air is thick with them. Your filtration mask — if you brought one — will clog quickly. If you have no mask, breathe through cloth. The long-term effects of inhaling Thallus spores are unknown, but short-term effects include: a sweet taste in the mouth, mild euphoria, a sensation of warmth in the chest, and in some cases, a feeling of emotional openness and calm that travelers have compared to being held.

This is probably adaptive. The Thallus may use airborne compounds to discourage aggressive behavior in large fauna. It is probably effective. You may feel, after a few hours of breathing the spore-laden air, that you have no reason to leave. That this is a fine place to rest. That your sense of urgency is a relic of a harsher world.

**Maintain your urgency anyway.** Not because the euphoria is dangerous in itself, but because it makes you complacent, and in a place like the Thallus, complacency is your most dangerous enemy. You will stop checking your supplies. You will stop monitoring your position. You will stop planning your exit. The Thallus has never needed to defend itself against anything, and the pollen of its air is its oldest, gentlest, most effective deterrent.

## V. The Sounds (and Silence)

Prepare for what the Thallus *doesn't* say.

Bring no expectations of acoustic communication over distance. Your voice will carry roughly fifteen meters before the mat and air absorb it. Beyond that radius, the world is functionally silent. If you are with companions, stay within fifteen meters of each other at all times or accept that you are alone.

The sounds that *do* exist are: drip, creak, click, hum, rustle. Drip is constant. Creak is occasional and comes from the columns — they are alive, growing, adjusting. Click is the small pale fauna and it is mechanical enough to be unnerving but not mechanical enough to find its source. Hum is collective and blood-quiet. Rustle is rare and directional and is caused by the larger gliding fauna, which you should watch for but not fear.

**The breathing.** In the deep hours, you may hear what sounds like breathing. Deep. Slow. Measured in minutes. It comes from everywhere and nowhere. Whether it is the mat, the atmosphere, or your own projection, you will not be able to determine. If you hear it and it disturbs you, breathe louder. Force your own breathing to be the dominant rhythm. The Thallus does not compete. Your rhythm will override it.

## VI. What to Do When You Encounter the Unexplained

**The stones.** They exist. A ring of dark, dense, unnaturally regular stones half-swallowed by the mat. Older than the surface. Surrounded by tight spirals of growth. They are not dangerous. They are not safe. They are *foreign* — objects placed by hands (or purposes) that predate the living crust, and the Thallus has neither rejected them nor fully absorbed them. Approach them if you wish, but understand: you are looking at evidence of a prior order, a prior world, a prior intelligence, and the Thallus has been digesting it for so long that the digestion itself has become geology. The stones are not meant for you. They are not meant for anyone. They are residual. Touch them with bare hands if you want to. They will be warm from the mat's metabolism. They will feel almost alive.

**The bones.** Pale, curved, ceramic-hard fragments of something large that walked here before the mat claimed the surface. They are being slowly pressed inward, incorporated into the living crust. Some protrude. Some are flush. You will want to examine them. Do so carefully, because the mat around bones is especially dense and may grip you with a force that feels personal.

**The deep pulses.** If you feel something massive moving below you — and you will, eventually, if you stay long enough — do not panic. Do not dig. Do not stamp your feet. The vibrations mean something is moving in the oldest layers of the mat, in the transition zone where organic matter becomes mineral. What it is, no one knows. The fauna flee from it, which tells you everything and nothing. Stand still. Feel the pulse. Acknowledge that you are standing on something with incomprehensible depth. Resume walking.

## VII. Exit Strategy

Decide before you arrive when you will leave. Set a hard metric — not a time limit (time is unreliable on the Thallus), but a *sensory* limit. For example: "I will leave when I have counted one hundred column intervals" or "I will leave after I find the stone ring" or "I will leave at the first sign of the deep hours." Write this metric down on a physical, non-biodegradable tag and attach it to your wrist or your pack. Refer to it when the euphoria of the spore-laden air tells you that there is no reason to go.

The exit is the hardest part. The Thallus does not resist your leaving, but it makes leaving feel wrong — as though you are pulling yourself free from something warm and welcoming. Your boots will feel heavier. The mat will seem to pull at your feet more insistently, even through the rigid soles. The silence will thicken around you like a presence you are turning your back on.

This is the Thallus's only defense. It is old but not malicious. It simply does not understand why anything would leave.

Walk out the way you walked in: slowly, carefully, without trying to conquer. Pack out everything you brought in. Leave nothing — no flagging tape, no markers, no marks. The mat will absorb them all eventually. It has absorbed everything.

And after you leave, you will notice — hours later, days later, whenever you are back on normal ground breathing un-spored air — that your sense of silence has changed. You will hear the quiet differently. Not as absence, but as *presence.* You will press your palm flat against the earth, or a tabletop, or a wall, and you will, for just a moment, feel a warmth beneath it.

This is normal. It goes away.

Probably.
