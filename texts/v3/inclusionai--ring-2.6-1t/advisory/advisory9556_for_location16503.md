# Multiverse Travel Advisory: The New Coast

---

## Overview

The New Coast is a recently uplifted geological formation on a flooded plateau that appears to have emerged from the ocean within the last few centuries — possibly much less. It is large (regional in scale), geologically young, biologically aggressive, and quietly anomalous. It is not a place that is trying to hurt you. But it is not yet a place that knows you're there, either, and the difference matters less than you'd think.

This advisory is intended for the first-time multiverse traveler with no prior experience in young-land ecologies, liminal geologies, or low-mana-baseline environments exhibiting possible proto-sentient signatures.

Read it all before you go.

---

## I. What To Pack

### Footwear — Critical

The single most important piece of preparation is your footwear. The rock is angular, freshly exposed, and has not been softened by erosion. It will cut through thin-soled shoes in a single misstep. You need:

- **Sturdy closed-toe boots** with reinforced soles and ankle support. Not hiking boots with delicate mesh — the volcanic glass edges here will shred that. Think leather or heavy synthetic, with a thick rubber sole that can grip wet stone without peeling.
- **Secondary water shoes** for when you inevitably have to wade. The pools between the ridges vary in depth from ankle to chest-height, and you will not always be able to see the bottom from the surface. Something with a hard sole you can kick off quickly if it snags on root tangles.

Do not bring sandals. Do not bring flip-flops. If you arrive in either, do not attempt to walk the rock shelf. Stay in the water channels.

### Clothing

The air is warm and saturated with moisture. It sits on your skin and does not evaporate. This means:

- **Lightweight, quick-dry fabrics** — you will be damp the entire time you are here. Cotton will rot against your skin within hours and will not dry overnight. Wear merino wool or synthetic blends. Long sleeves and long legs. The humidity will make you feel like you're suffocating for the first day; you will acclimate.
- **A wide-brimmed hat** with a chin strap. The open rock ridges have no shade whatsoever. The sun is apparently bright by local solar standards (descriptions vary, but travelers consistently report it as harsh and white, lacking the atmospheric softening of older worlds). Sunburn here happens faster than you expect.
- **Rain gear that doubles as a vapor barrier.** The air is so humid that condensation forms on surfaces colder than the ambient temperature — including your skin, after sundown. A light waterproof shell will keep the worst of it off you and, critically, will help your body maintain its own heat when the temperature drops at night. Nights on the New Coast are not cold, but the dampness makes them feel colder than the thermometer suggests.

### Water and Food

**Do not drink the standing water.** The amber-tinted pools in the wider basins are steeped in decomposing organic matter — roots, leaves, bark — and while the tannins may give the water an astringent quality that discourages some microbial growth, travelers have reported gastrointestinal distress lasting 2–4 days after consuming untreated water from these pools. Bring your own water, or bring a reliable purification system (boiling, UV filtration, chemical tablets). The flowing water in the channels is safer but should still be treated.

Food is a secondary concern for visits under two weeks. Pack calorie-dense, lightweight provisions. There may be edible organisms in the tidal pools — shellfish, slow-moving fish — but the ecological assessment of this location is incomplete and the Traveler's Guild has not yet cleared the local food chain for consumption. If you choose to forage, avoid anything that has an unusual coloration, bioluminescence, or that seems to move toward you.

### Navigation Tools

Bring them all. Compass, GPS, star charts, and a physical map you've prepared in advance. You will need them, and they will all fail you in different ways.

- **Compasses** behave erratically near the central rock ridges. The geological composition appears to include ferromagnetic minerals in unusual concentrations, creating localized magnetic anomalies. A compass will swing by 10–30 degrees when you cross certain ridgelines.
- **GPS signals** degrade the farther you move from the coast. The flooded plateau may have an ionizing effect on the upper atmosphere (we don't yet understand why). Expect signal lag, position drift, and outright loss of lock after approximately 4–6 hours inland.
- **Star charts** are unreliable. Multiple travelers have independently confirmed that the constellation patterns visible from the New Coast do not match any known stellar configuration. The stars reflected in the water are *different* from the stars overhead. Whether this is an atmospheric refraction effect, a dimensional bleed, or something else entirely is under investigation. Until it's resolved: do not navigate by the stars here. If you do, you will walk in circles. The "not quite natural" geometry of the landscape will help you lose your orientation even faster than the stars will.

**Your best navigational tool is the sound of the water.** The channels between the pools have a flow direction. Map it as you go. Mark your route with biodegradable trail tape on the rock faces above the waterline. The stone accepts markings easily — it's young and soft — and the marks will last for weeks, maybe longer.

### Light

Days on the New Coast appear to be standard in length, but the canopy over the flooded root-thickets is dense enough to make the interior grooves dark even at midday. A **headlamp with spare batteries** is essential. A backup lantern or chemical light sticks are strongly recommended. The place is enormous and the dusk comes quickly under the canopy.

## II. Medical Precautions

### Cuts and Abrasions

The sharp rock is your primary enemy. Even minor cuts — the kind you'd ignore on a normal hike — can become infected here. The warm, stagnant water is rich in organic material, and the local microbial ecosystem is unfamiliar to baseline human immune systems. Treat every cut immediately:

- Clean with purified water.
- Apply antiseptic (bring your own — local flora has not been screened for medicinal compatibility).
- Cover and keep dry for at least 12 hours.
- Monitor for redness, swelling, heat, or unusual discoloration. The local waterborne organisms are not well characterized. If a wound shows signs of infection, begin broad-spectrum antibiotics if you've packed them, and head for higher ground and open air.

A small first-aid kit should include: antiseptic wipes, butterfly closures, gauze, medical tape, broad-spectrum oral antibiotics (ciprofloxacin is a reasonable first-line choice), antihistamines, and ibuprofen.

### Fungal Exposure

The humidity is relentless. Expect fungal issues: athlete's foot, ringworm, and possibly respiratory irritation from airborne spores in the denser groves. Bring antifungal cream and an oral antifungal as backup. Change socks daily (or more often — they won't dry, so bring extras). Keep your boots open and aired out at night whenever possible.

### Dehydration

You will not feel yourself sweating. The humid air absorbs sweat before it can evaporate, and your body's cooling mechanism is effectively neutralized. You will feel *fine* while becoming progressively more dehydrated. **Force yourself to drink on a schedule** — at least 500ml per hour during active movement, more if the temperature climbs. Watch your urine. If it darkens beyond pale yellow, you're already behind.

### The Feeling of Being Watched

Almost every traveler reports this. It is not uniform in intensity, and it does not correlate with visible animal presence. A single large wading bird — described as heron-like, unusually still, with attention that feels *directed* — has been sighted by multiple independent visitors at considerable distances from one another. It may be a top predator. It may be something else. It has not approached any traveler, and no hostile behavior has been documented. **Do not approach it.** Do not attempt to interact with it. It is not there for you.

What you're feeling is likely a combination of: (a) genuine biological surveillance by the local fauna, (b) your own psychological response to an immense, empty, and unfamiliar landscape, and (c) possibly low-level environmental perceptual distortion (see Section IV, Anomalies). Trust your instincts. If something feels wrong, leave.

## III. Terrain and Movement

The New Coast is navigable, but it is not forgiving.

### The Rock Shelves

The broad, flat stone shelves that jut from the water are your primary walking surfaces. They are stable in the short term but geologically new — thin, sometimes partially supported by water pressure beneath them. **Do not stand on the edge of a shelf and jump down to the next one.** The edges can crumble. The rock between ridges can be deceptively thin over submerged cavities.

Test each step. Shift your weight slowly. The stone is warm — thermally active, perhaps from residual volcanic heat — and your boots will grip it well when dry. When wet, the stone's iron-black surfaces become glass-slick.

### The Channels

The narrow channels between the pools are the natural corridors of the landscape, and in many places they align with the deeper root-thicket "paths" beneath the waterline. The water in the channels tends to be clearer than in the open pools and moves — slowly, but with purpose. The channels are generally knee- to waist-deep. Current is negligible.

However: the channels are the domain of the fish. Those slow-moving, dark shapes you see drifting against the pale sand bottom — they are not startled by your presence, but they *will* move if you step into their water. Some are large. A kick from a startled animal in turbid water can spook you into a stumble, and a stumble on submerged rock is a broken ankle at best. Move slowly. Shuffle your feet along the bottom rather than taking full steps.

### The Groves

The flooded root-thickets are the most visually striking feature of the New Coast and also the most physically disorienting. Under the waterline, the fused root systems form latticed corridors that extend laterally for what feels like hundreds of yards. The canopy above — that thick mat of waxy leaves and dangling epiphytes — filters light into a diffuse green glow that makes it difficult to gauge depth, distance, and direction.

Travelers entering the groves report a sensation of **profound wrongness in spatial orientation** — a feeling that distances are compressed or expanded, that a place you entered five minutes ago is somehow behind you when you turn around. This may be related to the acoustic properties of the flooded forest (sound carries strangely, bouncing off root walls and water surfaces, arriving from directions that don't correspond to their source) or to the canopy's suppression of sky-references that the human brain relies on for navigation.

**If you enter the groves, mark your route obsessively.** Trail tape on every trunk you pass. Count your steps. Note distinguishable features — a fork in a root corridor, a particularly bright epiphyte, a patch where the canopy is thinner and the light is different. The groves are beautiful, and they will make you forget which way you came from if you let them.

### The Grottoes

There are structures in the landscape that appear too regular to be purely natural — narrow grotto mouths at channel junctions, angular alcoves in cliff faces, passages between rock outcroppings that align with the root-thicket corridors as though designed to connect. The Traveler's Guild classifies these as "emergent geological features not yet fully assessed." Some are shallow — ten or fifteen feet deep, ending in a dark pool. Others extend farther. **Do not enter the grottoes alone.** Do not enter them without a light source that you can see in the water ahead of you at all times. The water inside is black and still in a way that the water outside is not. Stillness in water that should have some current, however faint, is a warning sign in any environment.

## IV. Anomalies — What We Know, and What We Don't

### The Breathing Pools

In the shallowest basins on the rock shelf, the water occasionally shivers. It passes across the pool in a slow, deliberate wave, taking roughly ten seconds to cross, pausing at the far wall, and returning. This has been observed by dozens of visitors. **There is currently no agreed-upon explanation.**

Hypotheses include:
- Subsurface gas venting from decomposing organic material trapped beneath the rock. (The most conservative explanation. Incomplete but plausible.)
- A collective behavioral response from an unknown aquatic organism. (No such organism has been identified or captured.)
- A tidal or pressure effect from a larger, unseen body of water connected to the pools in a way we haven't mapped. (Possible, but the pools appear enclosed.)
- A resonance effect from the stone itself. (Speculative. Some researchers have noted that striking the rock produces tones that are *almost* subsonic. Whether these tones correspond to the shiver intervals has not been confirmed.)

**Practical guidance:** The shiver is not dangerous. Nothing has emerged from it. But it is unsettling. If it bothers you, leave the pool edge and move to higher ground. The shiver does not occur in the channels, only in the wide, still basins.

### The Water Reflections

At night, the water reflects the stars. But not the *same* stars. This has been confirmed photographically — the reflected pattern does not correspond to the visible sky from any known location in any cataloged dimension. The extra stars in the water rotate slowly, independent of the overhead sky, on a cycle that appears to be roughly 26 hours. Similar anomalous reflections have been reported at other young-land multiverse sites, suggesting a systemic phenomenon rather than a location-specific one.

**Practical guidance:** Do not use the water reflections for navigation. Do not attempt to "read" them. They are beautiful and they are not for you. Keep your head up, navigate by your markers, and do not stare at the water for too long at night. Multiple travelers have reported a mild hypnotic pull — a desire to look *down* into the reflected stars that intensifies the longer you watch. This may be environmental. It may be psychological. Either way, it is real, and it is worth guarding against.

### The Growing

This place is *new*, and it is still making itself. You can see it happening. The vines extend. The mosses creep. The rock cracks widen by fractions of millimeters each hour. The plants grow with an urgency that is almost visible — not fast enough to watch in real time, but fast enough that a path you walked yesterday may be partially obstructed today. If you plan to spend more than one day here, **expect the landscape to shift.** It is not shifting *toward* you. But it is shifting, and your map from yesterday may be wrong tomorrow.

## V. Temporal Considerations

The New Coast's day/night cycle appears roughly compatible with standard human circadian rhythms, though the exact length of the solar day has not been precisely measured due to the star-chart complications described above. Plan for approximately 24-hour cycles with a slight extension (possibly 25–27 hours) that will drift your sleep schedule by about an hour per day. Bring melatonin if you are sensitive to disruption.

The tidal cycle does not correspond to any known lunar or gravitational model. Water rises and falls on what appears to be a roughly 8-hour schedule, but with significant spatial variation — a pool that was dry two hours ago may now be three feet deep, and a channel that was passable yesterday may be impassable today. **Check the water levels at your current location before committing to any route, even one you've walked before.**

## VI. Final Recommendations

The New Coast is one of the most beautiful places in the catalogued multiverse. It is also one of the most disorienting, and one of the least understood. It does not hate you. It does not welcome you either. You are incidental to it — a visitor to a place that is still in the process of its own creation.

Go. Walk the dark stone shelves and the amber pools and the green-lit corridors beneath the root-tangle canopy. Watch the heron. Mark your path. Carry more water than you think you need, and wear boots that can take a blade along the sole.

And when the dusk comes, and the canopy glows its green-gold from within, and the copper light slides off the water and the first unfamiliar stars appear in the reflection —

Stop. Breathe. Do not follow the light into the water.

Come back when you're ready. The stone will still be rising.
