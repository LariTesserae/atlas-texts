# Preparing for the Drowned Making

---

### I. Understand What You Are Entering

Before anything practical, you must understand the single most important thing about the Drowned Making: **it is not dangerous. It is not hostile. It is indifferent.** This is worse. Every hostile environment gives you something to push against — a predator to evade, a temperature to resist, a terrain to conquer. The Drowned Making offers none of that. It will not fight you. It will not notice you. You could die here and the bioluminescent filaments would, over decades, close over the shape your body left in the silt. You must prepare not for resistance but for *meaninglessness*. That is the real hazard.

The place is a constructed ocean world — a continent of hand-fitted stone, built by a civilization so patient it turned masonry into instinct and instinct into landscape. It has been submerged for longer than language has tracked the drowning. The makers are gone. The architecture is colonized. What remains is a cold, dark, silent world of slow vegetation and ancient stone that is halfway to becoming geology. You are visiting the aftermath of someone else's entire world.

---

### II. Physical Preparation

**Thermal Management**

Do not prepare for surface cold. Do not prepare for winter plunge. Pack for the kind of cold that doesn't announce itself — the cold of depth, which enters through cloth and skin and settles into the architecture of your bones until you do not shiver because you have simply *become* cold, the way a stone becomes wet. You will need:

- A thermal regulation suit rated for sustained deep-water immersion. Not wetsuit material — something heavier, something that attempts to hold a boundary between your body heat and the ambient temperature. You will lose that boundary within the first hour no matter what you wear. Accept this.
- If your physiology allows it, consider biological cold-adaptation protocols before departure. Not temporary acclimation — you need changes at the mitochondrial level, changes to how your body metabolizes energy in cold. The Drowned Making does not cycle between warm and cold. It is cold the way the ocean is salty: totally, and as a permanent condition.
- Bring backup heating elements, chemical or mechanical. They will fail faster than you expect. The water is mineral-rich and will corrode anything with an electrical contact within days.

**Respiration**

The Drowned Making is water. You cannot breathe it. The description is ambiguous on whether you arrive with some capacity to extract oxygen from water or whether you need to carry your supply — assume the latter, and assume a long-duration supply. This is not a place you visit for an afternoon. Corridors take hours. Basins take days. Bring:

- A rebreather system rated for extreme duration, with redundant backup units. The particulate load in the water will clog standard filters quickly — bring replacement cartridges or learn field-cleaning protocols before arrival.
- The sealed chambers deeper in the architecture may still contain air, but *do not rely on this possibility.* The air inside, if it exists, has been sitting for millennia. It will be high in carbon dioxide, low in oxygen, saturated with the breath of ten thousand years of slow decay. If you find an air-filled chamber, treat it as a geological curiosity, not a resupply point.
- A pressure stabilisation protocol — you will be moving through architecture at various depths, and the pressure differentials are unpredictable given the erosion of what were presumably once deliberate structures.

**Mobility and Navigation**

You will need fins or equivalent propulsion assistance. The water is dense with suspended particulate — translucent threads, pale spores, fragments of frond and membrane shed from the vegetal mats. This is not silt. It will not sting your eyes, but it will reduce visibility unpredictably. One moment you may see (or *think* you see) the architecture below, the next you will be wrapped in a soft haze that erases everything beyond arm's length. Prepare for:

- Tactile navigation tools. Sonar or echolocation devices, if you have access to them. The architecture is vast and has been softened by erosion into geological-looking formations — you will not find edges, corners, or any visual cue that signals "structure" versus "natural formation." The builders' hand has been erased by ten thousand years of patient water.
- Marking systems. Leave markers. They will drift in the particulate suspension, but without them you will have no sense of return path and no way to communicate your position to anyone attempting to find you.
- Do not trust your depth perception. Distance warps in water this dense with suspended matter. A faint glimmer could be ten meters away or a hundred. The bioluminescent vegetation pulses on timescales so long you may convince yourself it is static — watch for hours, for days, and you will see it: a slow contraction, then an expansion, like the breathing of something vast and vegetal and asleep. This is hypnotic. It will disorient you.

**Sustenance**

There is no food here. Fauna score: 0.4 out of 3. "Not quite zero but close enough to empty that the absence becomes a presence of its own." There are no fish, no invertebrates, no drifting larvae, no scavengers. The water is depopulated in a way that registers as *wrong*, as a room that is too empty. You will not find anything to eat, and you will not find anything to drink except water that will, without extensive processing, make you sick.

Bring everything you will need. Calculate for more time than you think you will spend. The architecture's proportions — designed for comfort, for passage, for gathering when they were inhabited — become a wilderness when emptied of the civilization that gave them meaning. A corridor you expect to traverse in twenty minutes will take six hours. A basin you think you can cross before nightfall will take three days.

### III. Psychological Preparation

This is where most travelers fail. Not from drowning. Not from cold. From the silence.

**The Silence**

You must understand that silence exists on a spectrum. The silence you know — a held breath before a bird calls, the pause between waves on a shore, the moment in a forest when the wind stops — is *potential* silence. It is the expectation of sound, the held space where sound might arrive. The silence of the Drowned Making is not potential. It is *ontological*. There was never anything here that was going to make a sound. Except the water itself, and the things in it, and they make almost none. What they make is texture. The sensation of particulate drifting past your skin. The murmur of your own bubbles. That is the whole of the acoustic world.

Humans do not tolerate this well. You will want to create sound — cough, hum, tap your tank, speak aloud. This is normal. Do it. But understand that your own sounds will be absorbed with shocking completeness by the water, and beyond the reach of your own noise, the ontological silence remains, vast and patient and total.

**The Groaning**

The only sound that is not yours is the groaning. It comes from everywhere and nowhere — a low, subsonic creaking that is the sound of a built world remembering its own mass under the pressure of an ocean it was perhaps never designed to hold. This will begin to sound like a voice. You will begin to parse it as language. It is not language. It is the settling of stone, the slow adjustment of ten thousand years of fitted blocks finding their final positions as mortar dissolves and pressure redistributes. But you will hear it in your sleep. You will hear it when you are not here anymore. Beware of this.

**The Scale**

From within the water, you can perceive vast distances — the far glow of vegetation on a distant platform wall, the suggestion of a ceiling or surface far above — but you cannot gauge how far anything is, and the distances are always larger than you estimate. The architecture was built to a human scale: corridors sized for walking bodies, chambers sized for gathering, basins sized for crossing. Now emptied of the civilization that animated them, those proportions become an inhuman immensity. You are an insect in a cathedral that has been flooded and left to rot for ten thousand years, and the cathedral does not know you are there.

This will produce a specific kind of dread that is not fear of death but fear of *scale* — the realization that your body, your lifespan, your entire framework for understanding significance is irrelevant here. The Drowned Making was not built for you. It was not built for anyone still alive. It outlasted its makers the way a mountain outlasts a rainstorm, and you are standing in the rain.

**The Vegetation**

The plant life covers everything. Every wall, every slope, every surface. It moves in currents so gentle they are almost theoretical. It glows faintly in shades of dim amber and old green, and it is beautiful, and it is alive, and it does not care about you. None of it is known to you. None of it belongs to any taxonomy you can access. It simply *is*. This is important. If you expect to find evidence of the makers — murals, inscriptions, artifacts, something that says *people were here* — you will find vegetation. Vegetation is what happens when the evidence of human intention is returned to the patient chemistry of water and time.

### IV. Equipment Checklist

- Thermal regulation suit (deep-water rated, with backup heating elements)
- Extended-duration rebreather with redundant backup units and filter replacements
- Echolocation or sonar navigation system
- Tactile marking system (sonic beacons, physical tethers, luminescent trail markers)
- Full sustenance supply calculated for 3x your intended visit duration
- Pressure stabilisation equipment
- Communication system (underwater-capable; note that particulate suspension may interfere with signal propagation)
- Psychological support framework: a contact on the other end of your comms who knows what silence does to people, and who will call you back by name on a regular schedule

---

### V. The Most Important Thing

Do not go to the Drowned Making expecting to find something. Do not go expecting discovery, or insight, or the thrill of alien landscape. Do not go expecting meaning.

Go knowing that you are visiting a world that finished itself long before you arrived. That the makers built it stone by stone by hand across generations so long the knowledge became instinct, and instinct became ritual, and ritual became landscape, and landscape became ocean floor. That the end state of every made thing is this: slow dissolution into the made-looking geology of moss and mineral and cold water. That you are standing in someone else's completed work, and the completion looks like emptiness, and the emptiness looks like peace, and the peace is the most terrifying thing you have ever felt because it has nothing to do with you.

The cold will become part of you. The silence will become part of you. The groaning will become the voice in your head that you cannot identify. This is normal.

You are in a place that outlasted its makers. You will outlast your visit. The plants will outlast you.

Pack accordingly.
